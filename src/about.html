This archive holds the work of Stephen Gordon Lawton, a retired Canadian businessman based in Toronto, who spent 18 years in the Office of the President at Rogers watching how institutions behave when a complaint reaches the top. Since May 2025 he has published long-form political analysis, investigative journalism, historical musings, a nerdy jump into AI, science and technology, and the occasional satire, across about 130 series, at roughly four to five essays a day, now well past two thousand published pieces.

The subject matter is wide but the perspective is consistent: Canadian, outsider, evidence-based, and sardonic in the tradition of Stephen Leacock, who understood that the view from slightly outside the room is often the clearest one available.

The core preoccupation is American politics: its institutions, its corruptions, its capacity for self-renewal, and the consequences it generates for everyone who lives within its considerable gravitational pull, which is most of the world. But the series range from AI governance and energy policy to foreign affairs, Canadian trade, space exploration, consumer advocacy, and the long history of democracies under pressure. Some are analytical. Some are investigative. Some are satire sharp enough to require a label.

Major ongoing series include the Trump Accountability Project, The Asimov Gap (AI governance through Asimov’s lens), The Great Canadian Trade War, the Iran Crisis, The Middle Kingdom, The Blanche Files, The Numbers Game (history told whenever my essay count lands on a year that matters), and Your Phone Bill and Mine, an ongoing campaign against telecom advance billing that has taken me from a $1.22 late fee to a formal intervention before the CRTC. Code Blue (America’s 80-year failure to fix healthcare) and The Third Rail (Social Security’s 2032 shortfall) both end in a draft bill. I don’t expect my bills to pass. I’d settle for starting a conversation that is long overdue. New series begin when the news requires them. None ends until the story does.

On Sundays there is also Sketches of Elsinore: weekly sketches about a fictional Ontario city on the Avon River, written in Leacock’s tradition, with a Swan Warden, a volunteer named Gerald who has copies of everything, and something always, gently, rotten. Season Two opens in April.

Several series keep public ledgers, running records of what was undone, what can be repaired, and the draft bills offered from north of the border. They’re gathered on the Ledgers page.

There is no algorithm here, no brand strategy, and no advertiser. There is a writer who reads widely, thinks carefully, and believes that the best thing long-form journalism can do in this particular moment is refuse to be brief about things that are not brief.

A note on process, for those who feel entitled to one

This publication is written in open collaboration with Claude, Anthropic’s AI. Yes, the AI drafts. It’s fast, well-read and tireless, and it suggests ideas too, some of them good. Most ideas start with me: the subject, the angle, the series. But wherever an idea comes from, the judgement is mine. The AI doesn’t decide which series runs today, or what the argument should be. Every draft gets checked, cut, corrected and illustrated. Some get thrown away.

When something gets past me, it gets fixed. Code Blue was drafted in late 2025 with an earlier version of Claude, and going back through it with a newer one caught errors we both missed. Those have been corrected. Standing behind the work includes standing behind the corrections.

At five essays a day across about 130 series, I could not do this volume any other way. That is not an apology. A retired 75-year-old Canadian with opinions about American democracy and access to a powerful drafting tool is not the internet’s problem. He is, if anything, a mild corrective to it.

The useful question isn’t whether AI touched the work. It’s whether a person stands behind every word of it. I do, and that includes the mistakes. The tools will keep getting better. My responsibility for what goes out under my name doesn’t change.

The work is mine. The disclosure is voluntary. Draw your own conclusions about which of those two facts is more telling.

— Stephen Gordon Lawton, Toronto
