Most candidates in a close race lie awake wondering what they will say if they lose. Ken Paxton has a different problem. He already knows what he would do, because he has done it before, on a larger stage, in an election he was not even running in.

Early voting in Texas opened on October 19, 2026, and closes on October 30. Election Day is Tuesday, November 3. By every public measure the United States Senate race between Paxton, the Republican attorney general, and James Talarico, the Democratic state representative from Austin, is close enough that the counting will matter as much as the voting. A Texas Public Opinion Research survey of 969 likely voters, taken from October 15 to 18 and reported by FOX 7 Austin on October 23, 2026, put Talarico ahead by 51 per cent to 47, inside its 3.9-point margin of error. A CBS News poll reported by CBS Texas on October 25, 2026, had it closer still, at 51 to 49. Turnout is running at a record pace. As NBC DFW reported on October 27, 2026, more than 4.5 million Texans voted in the first seven days, and Democrats held an early-vote edge of about 115,000 ballots, according to Derek Ryan of Ryan Data and Research; at the same point in the last midterm, Republicans led by about 400,000. Republicans have not lost a statewide race in Texas in three decades. Control of the Senate may run through Austin.

Which brings us to the question nobody in Paxton’s campaign wants asked out loud, and which J. David Goodman and Alexandra Berzon asked anyway in the New York Times on October 19, 2026: what does a man who once asked the Supreme Court to throw out four states’ presidential votes do if Texas votes against him?

The Big One

Begin in December 2020. Joe Biden had won. Donald Trump’s lawyers were losing in court at a rate that would have embarrassed a minor-league goalkeeper, and his allies were canvassing Republican attorneys general for someone willing to do something more dramatic. Most declined. Paxton did not.

On December 7, 2020, Texas filed an original action in the Supreme Court against Georgia, Michigan, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin, asking the justices to stop those states’ electoral votes from being counted. The theory was that pandemic-era changes to voting rules in other states had somehow injured Texas. Trump called it “the big one.” As American Oversight has documented, the suit drew support from at least 125 Republican members of the House and the attorneys general of 17 other states.

It lasted four days. On December 11, 2020, the Court dismissed it in an unsigned order, ruling, as the Houston Press reported that night, that “Texas has not demonstrated a judicially cognizable interest in the manner in which another State conducts its elections.” In plain English: mind your own business.

One detail from that episode deserves to be remembered, because it returns at the end of this story. Texas’s solicitor general at the time, Kyle Hawkins, the lawyer whose job it was to argue the state’s cases before the Supreme Court, did not put his name on the filing. Paxton signed it himself, alongside Kurt Olsen, an outside lawyer who had helped draft it. According to the Times, Paxton and his deputy Aaron Reitz then went door to door among Republican attorneys general to collect the supporting brief.

The suit failed. The career it launched did not. On January 6, 2021, Paxton stood on the stage at the Ellipse before the crowd that went on to storm the Capitol and boasted that, unlike Georgia, which had certified its results, Texas had kept fighting. Fighting, in this context, meant losing in the Supreme Court and saying so with pride.

Carlos Sanchez saw it coming. Writing for The Atlantic on December 15, 2020, four days after the dismissal, he argued that Paxton had lost in court and won everywhere else. The suit raised his national profile just as his own legal troubles were mounting. “Trump might have lost at the Supreme Court on Friday,” Sanchez wrote. “But Ken Paxton? He won, big.”

In May 2026, after eleven weeks of indecision, Trump endorsed Paxton for the Senate over the incumbent, John Cornyn. As Elaine Godfrey reported for The Atlantic on May 22, 2026, the president’s explanation was that Cornyn “was not supportive of me when times were tough.” Paxton had been, on December 7, 2020, in writing.

The Habit

A single lawsuit can be a stunt. What makes Paxton worth an essay in the closing days before November 3 is that the stunt became a method. Since 2020 his office has announced dozens of election-related investigations and lawsuits, and a few of them show the pattern clearly.

In 2021 he tried to prosecute Dana DeBeauvoir, the long-serving election administrator of Travis County, which includes Austin, for allegedly obstructing a poll watcher. A grand jury declined to indict. DeBeauvoir told the Times this month that she is on high alert.

On Election Day in 2022, after a Harris County court kept some Houston polling places open an extra hour because they had opened late, Paxton went to the Texas Supreme Court to stop the late provisional ballots being counted and to have them discarded. The court ordered them counted. They did not change any result, and the votes of people who had stood in line stayed votes.

In 2024 he opened an investigation into Jolt Initiative, a group that registers young Latino voters, claiming it had been registering non-citizens. Jolt sued. Federal Judge Robert Pitman in Austin blocked the investigation, found that the attorney general had acted in bad faith, and noted that he could offer no plausible proof. Jolt won. It also went from 35 staff to five.

In 2025 he sued Beto O’Rourke and his voter-registration group over alleged fundraising violations. An all-Republican appeals court sided with O’Rourke, and the all-Republican Texas Supreme Court declined to step in. O’Rourke won too, after spending more than $400,000 and, by his account, nearly going bankrupt.

Look at that ledger and a pattern emerges that is easy to misread. Paxton loses in court with remarkable consistency. Mimi Marziani, who represented O’Rourke, told the Times that this is what gives her hope. But losing in court is not the same as losing. The grand jury took months. The Jolt case cost thirty jobs. The O’Rourke case cost $400,000 that would otherwise have registered voters. In the Paxton method, the process is the punishment and the verdict is almost beside the point. Readers of The Enemies List will recognize the technique; it is the one the federal Justice Department has since adopted at scale.

The method predates the attorney general’s office. Sanchez traced it to Paxton’s years in the Legislature, when most of his bills failed and he used the failures to build a following. In 2016, under indictment for securities fraud, he briefly disappeared from public view. Then he hired two crisis-management firms and began seeking out cameras. In Sanchez’s account, the more often he appeared as the attorney general, the less he was known as the attorney general under indictment. He went on to sue the Obama administration more than a dozen times and the Biden administration more than a hundred, and Godfrey notes that both figures are applause lines in his stump speech. Brandon Rottinghaus, a political scientist at the University of Houston, summed him up for her in four words: “He has no brakes.”

The Toolkit

So suppose Talarico wins by something like 30,000 votes out of eleven million. What can the attorney general of Texas actually do about it?

Less than he would like, and more than he should have.

Start with prosecution. In 2021 Texas’s highest criminal court, the Court of Criminal Appeals, a court made up entirely of Republicans, ruled 8 to 1 that the state constitution did not let the attorney general independently prosecute election crimes; that power belongs to local district attorneys. Paxton’s response was not to accept the ruling but to campaign against the judges. In the March 2024 Republican primary, three of the judges who had ruled against him, including the court’s presiding judge, lost their seats. Then, according to the Times, the Legislature passed Senate Bill 12 in 2025 to give him the prosecutorial power the court had said the constitution withheld.

Whether a statute can grant a power the constitution was found to deny is precisely the sort of question courts exist to answer, and Paxton has not yet tested it. University of Houston law professor David Froomkin told the Times that the attorney general’s power to enforce those criminal laws remains quite limited. Paxton may find out in November.

Then there is the evidence. If Texas’s secretary of state, an Abbott appointee, asks him to, the attorney general’s office can help secure election materials after a vote, although any examination of them is directed by a state court. It can also investigate allegations of criminal conduct at polling places, of which there are always some, real or imagined, in a state with 254 counties.

Then there is certification. Under Texas law, Governor Greg Abbott certifies most statewide results. Election lawyers describe the role as ceremonial, a matter of signing what the counties have counted. Ceremonial roles stay ceremonial only as long as the people holding them treat them that way. This year Abbott named Nate Schatzline, a former state representative who has questioned the 2020 results, as a senior adviser on elections. Nobody needs to rig a count to make mischief; delaying a certification while “questions are examined” can be enough to create the story that something is wrong.

Put together, the toolkit amounts to this. Paxton probably cannot overturn a Talarico victory. He can almost certainly delay it, muddy it, litigate it, and give a national audience weeks of footage of an attorney general announcing investigations into the election he just lost. For a party trying to hold a narrow Senate majority, weeks may be worth something. For a man who has built a career on the idea that fighting matters more than winning, they may be worth everything.

The Successor

There is a second race on the same ballot that bears directly on this one. Paxton is not seeking re-election as attorney general, so on November 3 Texans also choose who inherits the office. As Adam Serwer reported for The Atlantic on October 21, 2026, that contest pits Nathan Johnson, a Democratic state senator from Dallas, against Mayes Middleton, a Republican state senator and oil-and-gas heir whose own advertising calls him “MAGA Mayes.” The Texas Politics Project’s October poll had Johnson ahead by 48 per cent to 47. A later Texas Public Opinion Research survey, taken from October 15 to 18, had Middleton ahead by 50 to 48. Either way it is a coin toss, and in the earlier poll nearly half of voters held no opinion of either man.

The timing matters. The new attorney general does not take office until January. Through the canvass, the certification and any contest that follows, Ken Paxton remains attorney general of Texas, including in the event of his own defeat. The man whose toolkit is described above keeps the keys to it until his successor arrives.

And the successor decides whether the method outlives him. Johnson says he would return the office to consumer protection and checks and balances, and calls Paxton’s suits against out-of-state abortion providers “legally ridiculous.” Middleton says there is no such thing as separation of church and state. The head of the Republican Attorneys General Association has called the Texas office the largest legal power in the conservative movement nationally. Texas Republicans’ complaints about Paxton have been mostly about ethics, not method. A Paxton without the baggage, as Serwer’s headline puts it, would inherit the method intact.

So Texans will cast two votes about Ken Paxton on November 3: one on whether he goes to Washington, and one on whether his way of running the office stays behind in Austin.

The Conflict Nobody Mentions

There is something a Canadian notices here that Americans seem to have stopped noticing.

In most democracies, the official responsible for enforcing election law does not run in the election. In Canada, the Chief Electoral Officer is an officer of Parliament who does not even vote. In Texas, the attorney general is a partisan elected official, and this year he is also a candidate, which makes him the referee, the striker, and the person who decides whether the goal counts.

Paxton’s campaign sees no difficulty. His spokeswoman, Madison Cercy, told the Times that he has never apologized for enforcing Texas election law and never will.

At a Trump rally in San Antonio this month, Paxton explained the stakes in three words: “We have to win.” In Fort Worth on October 19, the first night of early voting, he put it another way: “When Republicans vote, we win.” Everyone in a close race feels that. Few of them hold an office that could be pointed at the result if the arithmetic comes out differently.

Five days earlier, on The Daily, Michael Barbaro put the same question to James Talarico: what if you lose? Talarico said the work would go on, that he was one runner in a long relay of Texans, and that whoever came after him would carry the baton. It was the answer of a man who intends to hand something over. Paxton, who did not take Barbaro’s invitation, has never had to give his answer in an interview. He gave it on December 7, 2020, in a filing to the Supreme Court.

The 2020 Team, Reassembled

The cast from December 2020 has done well since. Aaron Reitz, the deputy who collected those 17 signatures, is now the United States attorney for the Southern District of Texas, the chief federal prosecutor for Houston. He told the Times he would use all of his legal and law-enforcement resources to make sure federal law is upheld in the 2026 election, while conceding that elections are mostly a state matter. That is a reassuring sentence or an unsettling one depending on which side of Harris County you live on.

Kurt Olsen, the co-signer, went on to be sanctioned by the Arizona Supreme Court over a 2022 election lawsuit and to serve as Trump’s White House director of election security and integrity. He remains, for now, on the federal payroll.

None of this proves anything about November. It does mean that if there is a post-election fight in Texas, the people who wrote the 2020 playbook will not need to look it up.

The Guardrails

Now the hopeful half, because there is one, and it is substantial.

David Becker of the Center for Election Innovation and Research, who has spent years warning about exactly this kind of scenario, told the Times that the situation could be volatile but that he expects Texas courts and election officials to hold firm. The record supports him. The Supreme Court threw out Texas v. Pennsylvania in four days. A grand jury refused to indict DeBeauvoir. The Texas Supreme Court ordered Harris County’s ballots counted. A federal judge blocked the Jolt investigation. An all-Republican appeals court protected O’Rourke. Paxton’s greatest weakness as a strategist is that judges keep reading the law.

The county officials who run Texas elections, Republican and Democratic alike, have also had six years to prepare. Every lawsuit Paxton lost taught them which procedures to document, which deadlines to defend, which ballots to segregate. Election administration is not glamorous work, but the people who do it in Texas now do it in the expectation of being sued, and that expectation is itself a guardrail.

Courtrooms are not the only place an election can be shaped, though. Polling places are decided months earlier. Over the summer, the Republican-led Tarrant County Commissioners Court cut the number of Election Day polling locations by about a third, including one at the University of Texas at Arlington. The county called it a cost saving. Democrats said the cuts would mostly hit Black and Hispanic neighbourhoods. As J. David Goodman and Jesus Jiménez reported for the New York Times on October 20, 2026, local Democrats now argue the cuts have backfired by energizing their voters, and early turnout in the county has been heavy. Tarrant is the last large urban county Republicans control in Texas, and both candidates rallied there on the first day of early voting. If the Senate race ends close enough to contest, Fort Worth is where the counting will be watched hardest.

And then there is the final irony.

If a disputed Senate result reaches the Supreme Court of Texas, it will be heard by nine Republican justices. The newest of them is Kyle Hawkins, the former solicitor general who in December 2020 declined to sign his name to Ken Paxton’s attempt to overturn a presidential election.

There is a kind of justice that arrives by statute and a kind that arrives by seating chart. If Ken Paxton loses on November 3 and decides to fight, he may find his case landing on the desk of the one man in Texas who has already read his brief and declined to sign it.
