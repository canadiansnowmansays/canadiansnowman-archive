POSITION: Inspector General, Department of the Interior REPORTS TO: In theory, no one. In practice, the same administration this office is meant to oversee. LOCATION: Washington, D.C., within convenient walking distance of a Reflecting Pool currently under review for a botched, no-bid renovation this office may eventually be asked to investigate. START DATE: Pending Senate confirmation.

ABOUT THE ROLE

The Department of the Interior is seeking an experienced, independent-minded watchdog to provide rigorous, nonpartisan oversight of the department’s operations — including, should the need arise, its recent handling of a certain national landmark’s renovation, the one with the algae and the peeling lining and the vandals who turned out not to exist. We are pleased to say we have found our candidate.

QUALIFICATIONS (REQUIRED)

  • Prior experience co-authoring a 900-page conservative governing blueprint for exactly this kind of administration, ideally the one currently in office.

  • A demonstrated ability to be simultaneously extremely well known to senior officials and, when politically convenient, a complete stranger to the President personally.

  • Comfort with the concept of investigating an administration that employed you, appointed you, and may at some point need you to look the other way regarding a Reflecting Pool.

  • Prior service as an adviser to a former Director of National Intelligence, a credential we are including here mostly because it sounds impressive and less because it has anything to do with pool maintenance.

  • No prior experience as an actual inspector general required. We have found that a total absence of institutional memory in this specific role tends to move things along.

QUALIFICATIONS (PREFERRED, NOT REQUIRED)

  • Willingness to occupy a position designed to be independent while remaining, in every practical sense, a member of the team being independently reviewed.

  • A résumé the administration can describe as demonstrating “a long career within government” and “a loyalty to the rule of law,” phrasing this office finds accurate in the first half and aspirational in the second.

A NOTE ON OUR LAST INSPECTOR GENERAL

Our previous nonpartisan inspector general, Mark Greenblatt, was removed last year as part of a broader housecleaning of inspectors general across the federal government — a housecleaning some critics described as removing the people whose entire job was to notice things. Mr. Greenblatt has since said that inspectors general need to be able to investigate the administration that appointed them without fear or favor, a sentence we are including here in full because we could not have written a cleaner description of the job this posting is not, strictly speaking, offering.

A NOTE ON OUR CANDIDATE’S PRIOR STATEMENTS ABOUT THIS ADMINISTRATION’S GOVERNING DOCUMENTS

We would be remiss not to mention that the President himself once described the very policy blueprint our candidate helped write as something he knew nothing about. “I know nothing about Project 2025,” the President said in the summer of 2024. “I have no idea who is behind it.” He has since had a meeting with one of its named authors to determine, among other things, which federal agencies to cut. This office does not consider the President’s earlier unfamiliarity with the document, or his subsequent familiarity with its authors, disqualifying for the role. If anything, we consider it on-brand.

A NOTE ON WHAT, SPECIFICALLY, THIS OFFICE MIGHT ONE DAY BE ASKED TO REVIEW

For context, and purely in the interest of transparency: under Interior Secretary Doug Burgum, the department approved a series of no-bid contracts for the Reflecting Pool renovation, citing tight deadlines as the reason ordinary scrutiny didn’t apply. The renovation subsequently failed in ways that were, by the administration’s own admission, not actually anyone’s fault except “some contractor error.” Congressional Democrats have already called on this office to investigate that contracting process. As of this posting, it is not publicly known whether such an investigation is underway, which this office notes is also true of most things this office does, that being rather the point of the position.

Should our new inspector general ever find himself reviewing the department’s own no-bid contracting decisions, made under the same administration that nominated him, having previously co-authored that administration’s own governing blueprint, this office is confident he will approach the matter with the same rigorous independence he has demonstrated throughout his career, whatever that turns out to mean in practice.

WHY JOIN US

  • Meaningful work. Should this office ever be asked to examine the awarding of no-bid renovation contracts at a certain reflecting pool, you will be uniquely positioned to decide how hard to look.

  • Job security. Independence is optional. Loyalty is not.

  • A built-in answer to any future conflict-of-interest question, which is simply to note that the position was designed this way from the start, so nothing that happens afterward can really be called a surprise.

WHAT CRITICS ARE SAYING

“An attempt by the White House to plant an ally inside an office meant to investigate the administration.” — Representative Jared Huffman of California, on this exact nomination, not exaggerating

“Inspectors general must be able to investigate the administration that appointed them without fear or favor.” — Mark Greenblatt, describing the job this posting is not quite offering

HOW TO APPLY

Applications are not currently being accepted from the general public, on account of the position already having a nominee. The Senate will conduct the traditional confirmation process, a phrase this office uses loosely, understanding that “traditional” and “confirmation process” mean different things in different administrations, and that this one has shown a fairly consistent preference for the second word over the substance of the first.

THE DEPARTMENT OF THE INTERIOR is an equal opportunity employer, in the sense that anyone is equally welcome to apply, provided they already co-wrote the document.
