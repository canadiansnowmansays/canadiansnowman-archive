There is a particular indignity reserved for the satirist who guesses correctly.

One sets out, you understand, to exaggerate. To take the world as it is and stretch it past the point of plausibility, so that the reader laughs at the distance between the real and the absurd. The whole trade depends on that distance. Without it, satire is merely stenography with a grudge.

So when this series ran the June 12 hearing two weeks before it happened — when we imagined Todd Blanche on the stand, the consigliere cornered, the Chorus of thirty-five retired judges with their pens uncapped — we did so in the confident expectation of overshooting. We would invent absurdities. June 12 would arrive, deliver something merely bad, and we would have the small satisfaction of having been wickeder than reality.

Reality, dear reader, declined the arrangement.

June 12 arrived and was wickeder than us.

I. The Hearing We Ordered, As Delivered

Let us be precise about what we asked for and what we received, because the gap between them is the entire subject of today’s sermon, and the gap, on inspection, turns out to run the wrong way.

We imagined a lawyer for the government standing before a federal judge, unable to defend the indefensible. We gave that lawyer the name Blanche, because Blanche is the consigliere, the signer of orders, the man whose name is on the paper. We thought we were being bold.

What actually happened, before Judge Leonie Brinkema in the Eastern District of Virginia, was this. The government sent a lawyer named Andrew Block. And as Roll Call reported, Judge Brinkema asked Mr. Block a simple question: why had Acting Attorney General Blanche not simply rescinded the order that created the Anti-Weaponization Fund? If the fund was truly dead — as the government now insisted — why not put a stake through it?

Mr. Block’s answer, delivered under oath in a federal courtroom, was: “Your honor, I don’t.”

He did not know. He could not speak for Blanche. He had not, it emerged, even attempted to find out.

I want to dwell here, because in fiction this would have been rejected as too neat. As ABC News reported, Judge Brinkema said she “couldn’t believe” that a government lawyer, in a case of this magnitude, had not even tried to learn the answer to the most obvious question in the room. She called it a “huge gap in the record.”

We invented a consigliere who could not explain himself. Reality improved on the conceit: it sent a man who could not explain the consigliere, and had not thought to ask.

II. The Brother in the Gallery, Now Speaking

Students of this series will recall the Pentangeli problem — the scene in which the witness, ready to testify, looks up and sees the brother brought from Sicily sitting silently in the gallery, and promptly forgets everything he knew. The power of the scene lies in the silence. Vincenzo does not speak. He does not have to.

The administration’s difficulty in June was that its Vincenzo would not stop talking.

The choreography was supposed to be simple. As MS NOW reported, on June 2 Mr. Blanche stood before a congressional hearing and announced, with admirable finality, “we’re not moving forward with the fund, period.” The consigliere had spoken. The matter was closed. The lawsuits, the government would argue, were now moot — there being nothing left to sue about.

Then, on June 7, in a pretaped interview that aired on Meet the Press, the Don himself observed that the weaponization fund was “a great idea,” that many Republicans agreed, and that “you have to get it approved.”

You have to get it approved.

The Don Speaks Twice

The consigliere had told Congress the family was out of the business. Five days later the Don went on national television to say the business was excellent and the only problem was the paperwork. As Roll Call reported, Judge Brinkema noted that a sitting president’s continued enthusiasm for the fund was a “pretty good indication that there is some incentive or motive to make it happen.” The brother in the gallery, it turns out, had given an interview.

III. The Chorus, Made Bipartisan and Read Aloud

In Parts I and II, this column deployed a Chorus — thirty-five retired federal judges, appointed by presidents of both parties, accumulating moral authority across the acts until their accumulated weight became the verdict the official court would not yet pronounce. It was a literary device. It was, I confess, a little theatrical.

On June 12 the device walked out of the footlights and sat down in the gallery.

As MS NOW reported, Judge Brinkema read aloud from the bench a brief filed jointly by Senator Bill Cassidy, Republican of Louisiana, and Senator Cory Booker, Democrat of New Jersey. The Fund, the two senators wrote, “presents an immediate and dire threat to our constitutional order and the authority of Congress.” Among its purposes, they noted, was the compensation of the insurrectionists who stormed the Capitol on January 6th — the same roughly fifteen hundred individuals the President had pardoned upon his return to office, now positioned to be paid for the trouble.

A Republican and a Democrat. Read into the record. By the judge. I had invented a Chorus of thirty-five and thought myself generous. Reality supplied two, gave them party labels from opposite ends of the chamber, and had the judge perform them aloud, which is more than my Chorus ever managed.

IV. The Application Arrives by Mail

Every courtroom drama needs its small, human detail — the object that makes the abstraction real. We did not think to invent one. Reality, having now established that it would be doing the heavy lifting, provided.

As ABC News reported, Judge Brinkema mentioned during the proceedings that earlier that very week an unidentified individual had mailed an application for money — addressed not to the Justice Department, not to any commission, but directly to the court. Somewhere in America a citizen had concluded that the fastest route to the $1.8 billion was to write to the judge.

I have nothing to add. I could not have written it. I would not have dared.

Someone actually mailed this to the court. We did not invent it.

V. The Verdict, Such As It Was

So how did it end — this hearing we ran early and reality ran better?

As Roll Call reported, Judge Brinkema ruled from the bench. She extended the block on the Fund. She found the government’s claims of the Fund’s demise insufficient, observing that Blanche’s congressional statement was not enough, and that the administration “should not be able to evade judicial review by temporarily altering behavior.”

There is the whole of it, in fourteen words. The entire architecture of the thing — the lawsuit a man filed against the government he runs, the settlement with no monetary payment, the fund with no oversight, the consigliere’s careful denials, the Don’s careless enthusiasm — all of it answered by a judge who declined to pretend that a problem stops existing because the people who caused it have promised, for now, to behave.

She gave the administration one week — until Juneteenth — to enter a binding declaration, signed by both Blanche and Treasury Secretary Bessent under penalty of perjury, swearing the Fund would never go forward “in any manner, or under any name.” Sign, and she might dismiss the case. The catch was always the perjury part: a thing one says freely to Congress, and freely to Meet the Press, grows remarkably heavy when a federal judge asks for it in writing, under oath, with the President still on tape saying he wants it approved.

On June 19, the family declined to sign.

The refusal arrived, fittingly, over the name of Andrew Block — the same counsel who, a week earlier, could not say why the order had never been rescinded. As CBS News reported, his filing called the sworn declaration “unnecessary,” warning that compelling such testimony from senior Executive Branch officials raised “serious separation of powers concerns.” A consigliere asked to swear, on his oath, that the business was closed — and a lawyer dispatched to explain, with great constitutional solemnity, why he would rather not.

Read the declaration they would not sign and you find the reason in its plainest form. They were asked to swear the Fund would not proceed “in any manner, or under any name.” It is one thing to say a thing is over. It is another to promise it will not return wearing a different hat. They would say the first. They would not swear the second. As the New York Times reported, the judge had pressed precisely because the President’s own enthusiasm made the second promise the only one that mattered.

Even the friendly press saw it. As one conservative outlet put it, refusing to swear the Fund was dead had made the Fund look alive — an unforced error of the most elementary kind. The defense of the thing had become the proof of it.

And in Florida, the other thread held. As ABC News reported, Brinkema noted in her own courtroom the parallel inquiry before Judge Kathleen Williams — the question of whether that court had been the victim of a fraud. The two courts, north and south, now reading from each other’s records. The Chorus, you might say, in harmony.

VI. A Closing Note on the Trade

I began by complaining that the satirist who guesses correctly is robbed of his distance. Let me end by recanting, because the complaint was vanity.

The distance was never the point. The point was always the recognition — the reader’s small, cold start of understanding that the absurd thing on the page is also the true thing in the world. When we ran the hearing early, we were not predicting. We were describing a machine and trusting that the machine would run as machines do. It ran. The lawyer could not explain. The Don could not stop talking. The senators crossed the aisle. The citizen wrote to the judge.

We did not exaggerate. We merely arrived two weeks ahead of the stenographer.

The Fund is blocked. The deadline came and went. And the family, asked to swear under oath that the business was closed — closed in any manner, under any name — chose instead to send a lawyer to explain why such an oath would offend the Constitution. They will say it is dead. They will not swear it. The distance between those two verbs is the whole of the thing, and it is the distance this column has been measuring since the first installment.

The Chorus has capped its pens. For now.
