👤 Personal — the third of the three protected numbers: my country, my voice, and now my family. There is a serious ledger in the middle of it, because the man it is about would have insisted on one. The dog appears once, briefly, because kinship is her department.

I count my essays, and for eighteen hundred and ninety-seven of them I have been catching other people rounding. The chroniclers who called a hundred and sixteen years of war the Hundred Years’ War. The empire that counted its own dead by name and its victims by range. The fleet that booked its victory before it sailed. I have made something of a career, in retirement, out of noticing when a number has been nudged toward the shape someone wanted it to be.

So it is only fair that the third protected number should begin with a confession. Essay 1898 is about the year Hugh Llewellyn Keenleyside was born, and for most of my adult life I have called him my great-uncle. He was not. I rounded him up. And I am the one who did it.

The arithmetic, honestly kept

Here is the family ledger as it actually stands, and I owe the clearest page of it to Hugh himself, who set it down in the first volume of his memoirs.

His grandfather, Anthony Keenleyside, came to Canada as a teenager and married a woman named Hannah Place. She died giving birth to their first child, a son, James — “my uncle James,” in Hugh’s words. Anthony, as men of that century did, soon married again, and it is from the second marriage that Hugh descends. From the first, through that motherless boy James, descends my grandfather Gordon, born in 1882, and from Gordon my mother, and from my mother me.

Do the sum. James and Hugh’s father were half-brothers: one father, two mothers. That makes my grandfather and Hugh half-first-cousins, two grandsons of Anthony through two different wives. It makes my mother Hugh’s half-first-cousin once removed. And it makes me — I have checked this more times than I would like to admit — his half-first-cousin twice removed. Not his nephew at any degree. Not his great-anything. A fraction, and not a tidy one.

Now the part I have to own. Nobody else in the family rounded it. To my mother, my grandmother, my aunt, my cousins — to everyone who knew the true coordinate — he was simply Hugh. No title. It was I, a generation further down the chart, who began calling him “Uncle Hugh,” and then, because I am my own worst editor, “Great-Uncle Hugh,” when I started speaking and writing about him. I promoted a half-first-cousin-twice-removed to great-uncle because the warm word could be said aloud and the accurate one could not, and because a man I revered deserved, I felt, to be brought closer than the arithmetic allowed.

That is exactly what the chroniclers did at essay 1337. They had a figure that was true and could not be held, and they replaced it with a word that could. I spent a whole essay gently mocking them for it. I find, at 1898, that I am them.

I am going to keep both. The honest figure goes in the ledger; the warm word stays on my tongue. The degree is disputed. The reverence is not.

(One footnote, because the family tree insists. Hannah Place, the young wife who died bringing my line into the world, was by Hugh’s account a relative — at some unknown remove — of Francis Place, the radical tailor of Charing Cross, who spent his life agitating for the vote and the right of working men to organise. I cannot prove the remove. I only note that a series about the honest counting of power has, somewhere in its own bloodline, a man who kept the reformers’ books.)

The meetings ledger

Here is the other count that matters, and it is zero.

I never met him. I came close once. In May of 1980, my first wife and I were driving the Pacific Northwest, and we went to Victoria to knock on his door. Family lore held that the house had been designed by Frank Lloyd Wright; the record says Wright built almost nothing in this country, and I have left the two ledgers to argue it out between themselves. A housekeeper answered. Mr. Keenleyside was not at home. This was 1980: there was no telephone in my pocket, no way to arrange a return, and the trip went on without the meeting in it. I always meant to go back. He lived twelve more years, until September 27, 1992. I never went back.

That same trip held a lesson I did not understand until much later. On the morning of May 18, 1980, we were in Tacoma, some hundred and thirty kilometres from Mount St. Helens, when the mountain blew its side out in the deadliest volcanic eruption in American history. We heard nothing. Not a thing. Scientists have since documented a strange “quiet zone” around the eruption — the sound bent up and over the people nearest it and came down on listeners hundreds of kilometres away, while those close by watched, in one witness’s words, “like a silent movie.” I was as near to the loudest event of my lifetime as I would ever be, and the record of it reached strangers before it reached me.

Proximity is not presence. I was a few hours’ drive from Hugh for the whole of my youth and within reach of his door for an afternoon, and the meetings ledger reads nil. Everything I know of him I know the way you know the people in these essays: from records. Which means, I have come to realise, that the third protected number is the most Numbers Game entry of them all. My country I live in. My voice I hear every morning. My family’s most remarkable member I know only through his paperwork.

The man in the records

And what paperwork. Read his résumé and you will think he was trying to visit every chapter of this series in person.

Born in Toronto on July 7, 1898, he went west to British Columbia as an infant. He served in the First World War with the Canadian Expeditionary Force — the war of the timetables, essay 1914, still sixteen numbers ahead. He took his degree at the University of British Columbia in 1920, a doctorate at Clark University in Massachusetts, taught history at American universities, and in 1928 joined the young Department of External Affairs, which was then barely more than a handful of men in Ottawa with typewriters and ambitions.

In 1929 he was sent to Tokyo to help open Canada’s first legation in Asia, and stayed seven years. That is the Japan of essay 1868 — the country that had audited the auditors — and here was my relation, a generation later, auditing it back. Among his papers at Library and Archives Canada are his own amateur films, including Dominion Day in Tokyo in 1929: a man from a sixty-two-year-old country filming its birthday party on the far side of the world.

He was Canada’s first ambassador to Mexico. He was Commissioner of the Northwest Territories from 1947 to 1950 — the same high Arctic, I note with a jolt, where a boarder from my own kitchen table spent this census year knocking on doors at the top of the world, essay 1867’s counted-become-counter. From 1950 to 1958 he ran the United Nations Technical Assistance Administration, sending engineers, agronomists and statisticians to the poorer countries of the world — the Iwakura Mission of 1868 run in reverse, the auditors dispatched outward to help other nations count themselves. It was the plumbing beneath the promises of 1948, when the new Declaration of Human Rights had counted, in thirty articles, what every human being is owed. Somebody had to build the wells. For eight years, that somebody was Hugh.

Then he came home to British Columbia and ran its power: chairman of the BC Power Commission, then co-chairman of BC Hydro through the 1960s, deep in the negotiation of the Columbia River Treaty. He was made a Companion of the Order of Canada in 1969. And in 1981 and 1982 he published his life in two volumes, because — this series has lately had reason to believe — the habit of multi-volume accounting runs in the blood.

My mother’s story

All of that came to me on paper. One thing about Hugh came to me by voice, and it was my mother’s favourite.

In the spring of 1939, King George VI and Queen Elizabeth crossed Canada — Quebec City to Victoria and back to Halifax, a month on the rails, the first time a reigning monarch had ever set foot in the country. The record lists Hugh among the External Affairs officials who prepared the visit. My mother’s version had him travelling with it, coast to coast, in the wake of the royal train. Their daughters stayed home in England; the elder, thirteen years old, followed the journey through her mother’s letters.

Years later — my mother was never precise about the year, and I have stopped wishing she had been — Hugh found himself seated beside that elder daughter at a dinner, by then Queen Elizabeth II, with Prince Philip at the table. And he told her about the trip. All of it, my mother said, with relish: the route, the crowds, the stops, the details of a journey her parents had made across her country while she was a girl at home reading about it. He was, in my mother’s telling, enormously proud to be the one who could hand the Queen a piece of her own family’s story she had not been there to see.

I cannot document the dinner. I can document that my mother told it, often, and that her face did a particular thing when she reached the part where Hugh started talking. And I notice, with this series’ habit of noticing, what the story actually is. The last essay, at 1897, I wrote that I have lived my whole life between the only two sixty-year reigns there have ever been. It turns out a man in my family tree sat at the table with the second of them and gave her the account of her parents’ journey that she had only read in letters. Hugh was proud to tell it to the Queen. My mother was proud to tell it to me. I am proud, now, to tell it to you. Three tellings, each a generation further from the source, each a little rounder than the last. That is how a family keeps its oral ledger, and I would not trade it for a better-documented one.

The count he refused

Now the entry for which, of all of them, I am proudest to share a fraction of his name.

In December 1940 Hugh sat on a federal committee examining the place of Japanese Canadians in British Columbia. He had lived in Japan for seven years; he knew, better than almost anyone in Ottawa, the difference between a country and the people who had left it to become Canadians. After Pearl Harbor, at a conference in Ottawa in January 1942, the British Columbia delegation pressed for the mass removal of every person of Japanese ancestry from the coast. A small group of federal officials opposed them — the vice-chief of the general staff, the RCMP commissioner, and a handful of men from External Affairs. Hugh was one of them. Historians of that injustice record him as one of the few people inside the Canadian government who strongly protested what was being done.

They lost. Some twenty-two thousand people were removed from the coast, their homes, boats and businesses seized and sold, and many were not permitted to return for years after the war ended. The province had counted its neighbours by ancestry and found them to be a threat. Hugh refused that count. He did not win. But this series has spent nearly nineteen hundred essays recording the people who signed the wrong ledger in good faith, and it matters to me — more than I can make sound reasonable — that when the wrong ledger came round in my own country, my relation would not sign it.

The dam with his name on it

And because this series does not round its heroes, and he would not have wanted me to round him, here is the other entry.

On October 10, 1968, BC Hydro commissioned a dam across the Columbia River above Castlegar, built to meet Canada’s obligations under the treaty Hugh had helped negotiate. It had been called the High Arrow Dam. It was named instead for him. The Hugh Keenleyside Dam raised the Lower Arrow Lake by some twelve metres and turned the Arrow Lakes into one long reservoir, and the communities along the old shoreline — Burton, Renata, Edgewood, Needles, Arrowhead and others — were dismantled, moved, or left to the water. More than two thousand people were displaced. The oral histories collected since are hard to read: compensation offers that residents called derisory, consultation that felt like none at all, a sense, in one survivor’s phrase, of being “casualties on the road to prosperity.”

There is a deeper layer. The Arrow Lakes are the homeland of the Sinixt. In 1956, when the last registered member of the Arrow Lakes Band died, the federal government declared the Sinixt “extinct” in Canada — a people counted to zero by an act of administration. Twelve years later the reservoir rose over the shoreline where their villages and graves had been. In 2021 the Supreme Court of Canada ruled, seven to two, that the Sinixt were never extinct at all: the count had simply been wrong.

I will not pretend the arithmetic is comfortable. The man who fought the removal of twenty-two thousand people from the coast lent his name to the agency that removed two thousand from a lake. He did not, as far as any record I have found shows, sign the individual offers; the concrete was the province’s and the treaty two nations’. But his name is on the concrete, and I do not get to keep the protest and leave the dam in the footnotes. At essay 1895 I wrote that after Niagara, power stopped being local. On the Arrow Lakes, it turned out, neither were the people. Both entries are his. I keep both. That, I have come to believe, is what it means to keep honest books about someone you love.

The family convenes at institutions

The lattice continues, and it has a pattern I did not notice until I tried to draw it.

Hugh’s son Miles became a scientist of fish behaviour, a pioneer of watching fish underwater, who spent his career at the University of Western Ontario in London — the son studying the creatures of the rivers on which his father’s name would one day stand. Miles and his wife Hilda raised two sons in London: Eric, who became an actor and began at Stratford, and Joel, who became a family doctor. Both boys went to A.B. Lucas Secondary School, where my mother taught for years. Same building, same corridors; she likely never had them in a class. A generation later, Joel’s son — Hugh’s great-grandson — was at Western at the same time as my son Jim, and the two of them met there: third cousins, introduced by an enrolment list.

That is how my family meets. Not at reunions. At institutions — a high school attendance register, a university registrar’s office — where the record-keeping machinery does the introducing, generation after generation. I met Eric and Joel a few times as boys. I never met their grandfather. The handshake that never happened sits in the middle of the lattice like the one cell left blank on a census form, and he is, of course, the one the records are about.

Dior’s kinship tables

The husky, I should report, recognises no degrees of consanguinity whatsoever. Her kinship tables are simple and unappealable: you are family if you are in the house at dinner, and the closer you sit to the chicken, the closer the relation. By her accounting the boarders are siblings, the mailman is a cousin of uncertain loyalty, and Hugh — who never once dropped anything from a plate in her presence — is no relation at all. I find her method crude, unscholarly, and very nearly correct. Family is mostly a matter of who showed up. My great-uncle, so called, is the one who never could.

On the bridge of time

The two volumes are on my desk as I write. They were my mother’s, and they came to me when she passed. The first is signed on its title page, with an inscription — “From Isabel & Enid, Feb 2nd 1982” — from two women I cannot identify, and it reached our family by the roundabout route of a used-book dealer, who sold it for fifteen dollars and pencilled “signed title page” inside the cover. The attribution of the signature is disputed. The ink is not. Even my relation’s handwriting came to me secondhand.

The first volume is called Hammer the Golden Day. The second is called On the Bridge of Time.

I had owned that second title for years before I truly saw it. This whole series is a bridge of time: one plank per essay, each plank stamped with a number, each number already a year before I reached it. I thought I had invented the conceit. A man I never met, whose kinship to me is a fraction I rounded up out of love, named his own life after it four decades before I wrote essay one. The family habit, it turns out, was never just the two volumes. It was standing on the bridge, counting the planks, and writing down what you saw from each one.

The closer

The count moves on, as it does, and it moves into the century Hugh actually lived — all ninety-four years of it, very nearly the whole of the 1900s, stretched end to end like a span across a river. The first plank on the far side is essay 1,905, and it is the strangest piece of arithmetic this series will ever handle. Hugh’s dam took the weight of a river and turned it into power. In 1905 a patent clerk in Bern, working in his spare time, published a short calculation showing that matter itself is stored power — that a single gram, at the exchange rate he posted, holds enough energy to light a city. The rate was correct. The century would spend forty years discovering what it meant, and pay the invoice in 1945.

Hugh, by the way, was seven.

See you at 1,905, where a clerk posts the exchange rate between a gram and a city.

Some entries are satire. Some aren’t satire at all. A few are the most personal things he writes. This is one of those.
