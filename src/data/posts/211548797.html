A decade ago, a thirty-four-year-old mayor of South Bend, Indiana, sat down with a newspaper columnist and delivered a small lecture on vocabulary. As Frank Bruni recounted for The New York Times, Pete Buttigieg wanted to talk about the word “freedom” — specifically, his frustration that Democrats had let Republicans claim exclusive title to it, as though a government safety net and a personal liberty were natural enemies rather than, quite often, the same thing wearing different clothes. “You’re not free if you have crushing medical debt,” he told Bruni.

That was 2016-ish, in the sense that it was a decade before the column landed in my feed. Buttigieg was a flyspeck then. He is not a flyspeck now, and neither, it turns out, is the argument he was making. Because in August 2026, with the midterms bearing down and a president whose relationship with words like “freedom,” “patriotism,” and “capitalism” has become increasingly theoretical, that argument is no longer a lonely mayoral hobby horse. It’s showing up everywhere Democrats are trying to win something.

I wrote three weeks ago, in the essay that opened this series, about the coalition math problem — the risk that Democratic primary voters mistake intensity for electability, and hand the GOP a ready-made attack ad in the process. That essay was about strategy: who runs, who wins, who alienates whom on the way to November. This one is about something upstream of strategy. It’s about language — who gets to define the words a campaign is fought in, and what happens when a party finally decides to stop conceding them.

The Word Thieves Come Home

Here is the uncomfortable truth Bruni’s column surfaces, and it is uncomfortable regardless of which side of the border you’re reading it from: Republicans didn’t earn “freedom,” “patriotism,” “family values,” and “law and order” through some superior claim on their meaning. Democrats handed them over. For decades, the reflexive move was defensive — question whether a Republican’s patriotism was genuine, litigate the hypocrisy, and otherwise cede the emotional register entirely to the other team. It is very hard to win an argument you have already conceded the vocabulary for.

What’s changed is that Donald Trump’s second term has made the abandonment of those concepts by their supposed owners so blatant that reclaiming them barely requires argument anymore — it mostly requires noticing. Take capitalism. Abdul El-Sayed, fresh off his one-point primary win in Michigan that I wrote about in Part I, has taken to describing himself not as a socialist-adjacent insurgent but as a serious student of capitalism, arguing that unchecked wealth concentration and gutted market competition are themselves the betrayal of capitalist principle, not the defense of it. Whatever you think of the underlying policy — wealth taxation, antitrust enforcement — the rhetorical move is shrewd. It reframes the fight from “how far left is El-Sayed” to “who actually believes in a functioning market,” and it does so against an administration that just extracted a fifteen percent cut of AI chip sales to China as the price of a president’s personal blessing. That is not capitalism. That is rent-seeking with a flag pin.

Representative Ro Khanna made a version of the same move with patriotism, publishing a fourteen-point “Team America” plan on economic growth and social mobility — and publishing it, pointedly, on Fox News’s website rather than somewhere friendlier. That is not a subtle choice. It’s a Democrat walking into the other side’s living room to argue that “America First” belongs to whoever actually delivers for American workers, not whoever says the phrase loudest while emptying the country’s weapons stockpiles into a war most Americans never signed up for.

And then there’s Rob Sand, running for governor of Iowa, opening his town halls by having the whole room sing the first verse of “America the Beautiful.” I confess my first reaction to that image was skepticism — it reads, on paper, like exactly the kind of performative gesture that invites eye-rolling from people who’ve watched two decades of Democratic candidates try on flag-pin patriotism like an ill-fitting jacket. But Sand’s own explanation is more interesting than the gesture itself: he’s using it as a unity ritual for a genuinely mixed-partisan crowd, a way of establishing shared ground before the disagreements start. Mary Peltola’s Alaska Senate slogan — “Fish, Family, Freedom” — is doing something similar with three words instead of one song. None of it is subtle. All of it is, I think, correct.

Why This Isn’t Just Branding

The risk with any essay about political vocabulary is that it starts to sound like advice from a marketing consultant — find the right words, focus-group the slogan, win the news cycle. That is not what I think is happening here, and it’s worth being precise about the difference, because I spent Part I of this series warning against exactly that kind of shortcut: the temptation to treat a coalition problem as solvable through messaging alone.

The distinction is this. A branding exercise tries to make an unpopular substance sound more palatable. What Buttigieg, El-Sayed, Khanna, and Sand are doing is closer to the opposite — pointing out, accurately, that the substance underneath words like “freedom” and “patriotism” has quietly relocated to their side of the aisle while the label stayed put on the other. Medical debt is a freedom problem. A president monetizing his own office is a patriotism problem. A White House taking a cut of semiconductor exports to a strategic rival is a capitalism problem, full stop, under any definition of the word that predates 2025. The words aren’t being repainted. The building underneath them changed hands, and somebody finally read the deed.

That matters for a Canadian reader — and I include myself in that category, watching all of this from the outside — because we have a slightly different relationship to this whole exercise. In a Westminster system, the opposition’s job description includes contesting the story of the nation, not merely its line-item budget. Her Majesty’s Loyal Opposition, whatever its flaws, exists structurally to argue that it, not the government, represents the country’s real values and real interests. American politics has never quite built that muscle into either party’s DNA the same way; the assumption has usually been that whoever holds power gets custody of the national narrative until the next election proves otherwise. What Bruni is describing, whether he’d frame it this way or not, looks like Democrats finally building that muscle — treating the contest over what “America First” or “freedom” or “family values” actually means as itself the campaign, not a decoration on top of the policy fight.

This Has Happened Before, Just in Reverse

None of this is unprecedented, which is itself worth sitting with for a moment. American political vocabulary has changed hands before, and it’s usually happened at exactly this kind of inflection point — when the party currently holding a word stops behaving like it deserves to.

“Liberal” used to belong to nobody in particular; it described a general disposition toward individual rights and limited government that both parties could claim pieces of. It became a Democratic word through decades of use, then became a slur Republicans successfully weaponized in the 1980s and 1990s until Democrats mostly stopped using it about themselves at all. “Family values” was contested territory as recently as the 1990s before the religious right made it a near-exclusive Republican brand. Words don’t have permanent owners in American politics. They have current tenants, and tenancy changes when the current occupant stops paying the rent — stops behaving in a way that makes the word’s meaning credible.

What’s unusual about 2026 is the speed of it. Normally this kind of reclamation takes a generation of patient work — building institutions, training candidates, letting the vocabulary shift accumulate election by election. Buttigieg, El-Sayed, Khanna, Sand, and Peltola are trying to do it inside a single election cycle, which is either a sign of real opportunity or a sign that nobody has the patience to do this properly anymore. I suspect it’s some of both. The opportunity is real because Trump’s second term has vacated these words so publicly and so fast — a fifteen-percent cut of chip sales, an unpopular war, agents shooting protesters — that the normal generational timeline has been compressed into eighteen months of unforced errors. The impatience is real too, because American politics in 2026 doesn’t reward anyone willing to wait a generation for anything.

There’s a version of this story where that speed is a strength: Democrats moving fast enough to capture the moment before the 2026 midterms rather than the 2036 ones. There’s another version where speed without follow-through just produces a slogan season — a few good essays, a few good soundbites, a midterm that goes about as well as midterms for the out-of-power party usually go regardless of vocabulary, and then a slow drift back to the old defensive crouch once the immediate outrage fades. Which version we get probably depends less on the words themselves than on whether the party treats this as a rhetorical adjustment or an actual repositioning of what it stands for and how loudly it’s willing to say so.

The Trap Inside the Opportunity

I don’t want to end this one on a purely admiring note, because the thesis of this series is that Democratic self-correction deserves scrutiny in both directions, not cheerleading in one. Vocabulary reclamation is necessary. It is not sufficient, and there is a specific way it can go wrong that’s worth naming before the midterms arrive and everyone gets too busy counting votes to notice.

The danger is that language becomes a substitute for the coalition-math discipline I wrote about in Part I, rather than a companion to it. It is entirely possible to say all the right words — to sing “America the Beautiful,” to publish the sharpest possible op-ed about real capitalism, to out-patriot the incumbent on Fox News’s own turf — and still nominate candidates who can’t assemble the actual electoral coalition needed to win in November. Words are not currency you can spend instead of doing the harder work of coalition-building; they’re closer to the packaging on top of it, and packaging convinces nobody who opens the box and finds it empty.

The good news, if there is any, is that the two projects reinforce each other when done honestly. Reclaiming “freedom” only works if the policy underneath it — protection from medical bankruptcy, protection from an unaccountable federal presence showing up at a protest — is real and deliverable, not aspirational. Reclaiming “patriotism” only works if the party actually behaves like it has the country’s interests at heart across factions, rather than treating the word as one more applause line for the base that already agrees. Mary Peltola’s fish, family, and freedom mean nothing in Alaska if the party nationally can’t stop treating Alaska as an afterthought between presidential elections.

Buttigieg told Bruni that Democrats are even better positioned to reclaim “freedom” now than they were a decade ago, because the argument has grown a second half: freedom to live the life you choose, and freedom from an administration whose agents have shot protesters and rounded up residents with a carelessness that would have been a five-alarm scandal under either party a generation ago. I think he’s right about the opportunity. Whether the party can hold that vocabulary without letting it become a substitute for the harder arithmetic of actually winning back Congress is the question this series will keep coming back to between now and November.

The words are being returned to their rightful owners. Whether anyone remembers what to do with them once they’re home is a separate matter entirely.
