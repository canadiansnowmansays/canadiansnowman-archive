The memorandum of understanding signed at Versailles on June 17 gave the parties sixty days to negotiate the end of the war and the future of Iran’s nuclear programme.

Sixty days from June 17 has passed.

No extension was sought. As Aaron Boxerman, Zolan Kanno-Youngs and David Sanger reported for the New York Times on August 16, officials inside the White House were perfectly aware the deadline was coming and perfectly aware they would blow past it. Two Americans familiar with the negotiations told the Times as much. The president’s aides did not respond to questions about it.

On the deadline itself, only Tehran said anything at all. As Boxerman reported separately on August 17, the Iranian foreign ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei told reporters that the nuclear negotiations “never began” and that the sixty-day period was “fundamentally no longer relevant.”

Washington’s contribution to the day was to threaten to bomb the mediator.

In fairness, the expiry is largely symbolic. The memorandum provided for the sixty days to be extended, which — given the complexity of what was supposed to be negotiated inside them — was always the likely course. And both governments had already described the deal as dead in all but name.

But a deadline is a promise about time, and the manner of its passing is instructive. The party that spent six months insisting Iran must not have a nuclear weapon let the nuclear deadline lapse in silence. The party accused of seeking one confirmed on the day that the talks had never started.

I. A New Status

Iran’s foreign minister was asked over the weekend whether the ceasefire needed extending, and produced the most remarkable sentence of the month.

“We did not have a cease-fire that now needs to be extended,” Abbas Araghchi said. “We had an end to the war, which has now taken on a new status.”

Consider what is being asserted. The war ended in June. Everything since — the thirteen consecutive nights of bombing, the four transfer cases at Dover, the strikes on Egypt and Iraq, the family killed in their beds on Qeshm Island, the six hundred and fifty-three wounded Americans — has been not a war but a new status. It has no name because naming it would require someone to say when it started, and both governments have excellent reasons not to.

Araghchi added that the sixty-day timeline is no longer relevant, since the June agreement is defunct. This is the same government whose central demand is that the June agreement be honoured. Tehran wants the memorandum implemented and its clock disowned, and has worked out that it can have both as long as nobody insists on consistency.

Washington’s position is a mirror image. The administration wants the strait reopened before it releases Iran’s frozen assets; Iran wants the assets released before it reopens the strait. Ali Vaez of the International Crisis Group describes the stall precisely: neither side is ready to finalise anything, because each believes that within days or weeks the other will be the more desperate party, and that waiting will improve the terms. A game of endurance, he calls it, and notes that we have been here before.

II. The Strategy of Nothing

For the first time in six months of this diary, there is a week to report on in which almost nothing happened. That absence is itself the news.

As Jonathan Lemire reported for the Atlantic on August 11, Washington has quietly de-emphasised both diplomacy and direct military action in favour of a slower instrument — stepped-up sanctions layered onto the existing naval blockade, with the administration betting that time and economic exhaustion will accomplish what five months of strikes and negotiating rounds could not.

It is not obviously a strategy so much as an admission. Three assumptions the administration walked into this war with have each been disproved by its own officials’ account: that the conflict would last a few weeks at most; that Iran, despite Pentagon warnings to the contrary, would not actually close the Strait of Hormuz; and that the Iranian economy would buckle quickly under pressure. The president’s assessment this week was that Iran is broke, totally broke. He has been saying versions of that since 2018.

That third miscalculation should worry the strategy’s architects most, since the new plan is a second wager on a proposition already tested and lost. Lemire’s sources acknowledge that Tehran has repeatedly surprised American officials with its capacity to absorb economic pain, and there is little in the regime’s conduct over five months to suggest a government unwilling to let its own citizens suffer to preserve its grip.

Layered on the analytical problem is a political one. An approach built on patience and grinding sanctions cannot plausibly produce lower energy prices before November. The strategy’s success condition and the domestic electoral calendar are running in opposite directions, and everyone involved knows it.

Two further details from Lemire’s reporting are worth holding onto past this essay. The first is that the president’s own advisers privately regard a single angry post from him as the greatest threat to the plan’s survival — which is, when you sit with it, a White House staff telling a reporter that the administration’s chief vulnerability is the president. The second is Steve Bannon’s old formulation, which Lemire reports has only sharpened with time: that Trump does not try to win a news cycle but a news moment, a news second. A strategy whose entire mechanism is patience, executed by a man who has spent a decade optimising for the opposite instinct, is not a strategy so much as a hope.

We did not have to wait long to see the advisers’ fear tested. As Luke Broadwater reported for the Times on August 17, the president gave a telephone interview to Fox News as the deadline lapsed, and directed his frustration not at Tehran but at Muscat.

“If Oman gets in the way, we’ll bomb the shit out of them,” he told Fox’s Trey Yingst.

Oman has mediated between the United States and Iran for years. It is an American ally. It sits on the far side of the strait from Iran, has spent the past fortnight negotiating the shipping coordinates with Tehran, and insists it remains a neutral mediator. It is, at this moment, the only functioning diplomatic channel the United States possesses.

Nor is this the first time. In May, after it emerged that Oman had discussed partnering with Iran to charge fees for ships transiting the strait, the president told reporters that Oman would behave like everybody else or would have to be blown up — adding, in a flourish worth preserving, that they understand this and they will be fine.

Note what that May episode establishes. The revenue-sharing arrangement that surfaced this month as a novel Iranian proposal — a service fee split evenly between Tehran and Muscat — was under discussion three months ago, and Washington’s response then was the same as its response now. A White House official did not respond to a request for comment.

In the same interview, the president said he was not in a hurry to secure a deal, and maintained that his central goal remains preventing Iran from obtaining a nuclear weapon. His administration has promised crushing new sanctions this week, details unspecified. And Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth has said the United States can maintain the naval blockade of the Strait of Hormuz indefinitely — which is not a statement about capability so much as a concession that nobody has an ending in mind.

Six months ago, administration officials said this war would be over in four to six weeks.

This is TACO’s fourth movement on full display. Having threatened, then partially accommodated, then flirted with capitulation over the reparations exchange in Part XCI, Washington has settled into Obfuscate — not negotiating, not striking, simply declining to be pinned down while the blockade does the talking. Reparations demanded of Iran; reparations counter-demanded by an enraged Trump for American losses and, in the same breath, for Iranian protesters killed by their own government. Both demands can sit on the table indefinitely without anyone having to move.

III. A Region Rearranging Itself Without Asking

While Washington was choosing to say nothing, the region did something.

As Arash Azizi wrote in the Atlantic on August 13, the leaders of Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey met to sign a mutual defence agreement, concluded in and named for Islam’s holiest city. The parties were careful to say it is not aimed at any particular country.

The language is NATO’s: an armed attack on any one of the three shall be regarded as an attack on them all. It comes with machinery — a new organisation, a secretariat in Saudi Arabia, regular meetings of the three countries’ foreign ministers, defence ministers and military chiefs. Turkey’s foreign minister has compared it to NATO and suggested Egypt will likely join. The signatories are the region’s two largest economies and biggest military spenders, plus the Muslim world’s only nuclear power. Two of the three share a land border with Iran.

Iran survived this war by widening it — striking American bases in Jordan, Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar and Iraq, hitting Saudi Arabia directly, sending missiles that were intercepted over Turkish airspace, backing militias that attacked Saudi interests in Yemen and Iraq. The countries it attacked in order to survive have now formed a mutual defence bloc, and named it after Mecca.

There is a notable second absence. Israel is not a party either. The Abraham Accords brought in the UAE, Bahrain, Morocco and Sudan, but Saudi recognition was always the prize, and Riyadh conditions it on progress toward a Palestinian state — which Israel is currently moving away from rather than toward. So the new architecture excludes both states that have spent six months exchanging fire and dragging everyone else into it.

Azizi frames Tehran’s position as a binary: keep backing regional militias and court isolation, or accept the new consensus on development and reconciliation and abandon, in effect, the revolutionary posture the Islamic Republic has organised itself around since 1979.

The frame is tidy and close to unanswerable on its own terms, which should be the tell that it is missing something. Giving up revolutionary ambitions is not a policy adjustment a government makes on a Tuesday. It is the dissolution of the thing the Islamic Republic is. Asking Tehran to choose it voluntarily is asking it to complete by decree the regime change that six months of American and Israeli bombing failed to produce — at precisely the moment the Revolutionary Guard feels most vindicated by its ability to hold Hormuz shut. A choice framed as binary whose second option is national self-abolition is not a choice. It is a verdict dressed as an offer.

One caveat belongs here, and it cuts against the reading above. Saudi Arabia and Pakistan signed a comparable agreement last year and did not invoke it when Saudi Arabia came under attack during this very war, so the precedent for such pacts is that they go unused precisely when they would matter. There is also an entirely different reading available: that a NATO member, Washington’s largest arms customer and a nuclear-armed South Asian state building their own collective defence structure says less about Iranian isolation than about the perceived value of American guarantees. That reading should worry Washington more, and yesterday’s threat against Oman will not have weakened it.

Azizi supplies the detail that gives all of this its depth. Before 1979, Iran was a pillar of exactly this kind of architecture — intervening alongside Saudi Arabia against Communist-backed rebels in Oman and Yemen, helping found the Organization of Islamic Cooperation in 1969. American presidents relied on it. In 1972, Richard Nixon told the Shah two words: Protect me.

Fifty-four years later the United States is at war with Iran, and the regional security structure Iran once anchored has reassembled itself with Iran as its subject rather than its author. What the Mecca pact actually tells you is smaller and more durable than either belligerent’s rhetoric: the region has started planning around Tehran and Jerusalem rather than through them.

IV. Two Claims on One Waterway

At a political event in Garden City, New York, on Friday, the president said he would soon be declaring the Strait of Hormuz a territory of the United States.

It is not clear how serious this was. The White House did not respond to questions about whether any such plan exists. The president has a history of floating unbuilt foreign policy — the proposed American takeover of Gaza last year being the obvious precedent — and such declarations tend to arrive when he wants the subject changed. What is clear is that claiming the strait would require boots on the ground and an indefinite naval presence, which is the precise opposite of what his coalition was promised.

Iran’s deputy foreign minister answered in kind. “Accept reality once and for all,” Kazem Gharibabadi wrote. “So far, you have suffered major strategic defeats. The Strait of Hormuz has been Iranian, is Iranian, and will remain Iranian.”

Before February, the Strait of Hormuz belonged to nobody. It was an international waterway, open and free, transited by roughly a hundred and thirty ships a day without anyone’s permission. Six months of war have produced two governments asserting sovereignty over it and about a dozen ships a day passing through.

That is the war’s product. Not a denuclearised Iran — there has been little indication the nuclear programme has even been discussed at length since June. Not a liberated population. Not a degraded missile force. Two sovereignty claims where there had been none, over a waterway that used to work.

V. The Argument Over Whether Iranians Get to Talk to Each Other

Washington’s silence this week was a choice. Iran’s, more often, is not — and that distinction is the subject of a story with nothing to do with sanctions or Hormuz, and everything to do with what happens after six months of war inside a country whose government has spent decades deciding who is permitted to say what.

As Yeganeh Torbati reported for the New York Times, Azad is a six-person Tehran outlet that hosts long-form debates and posts them to YouTube — itself blocked inside Iran, circumvented by an audience its founder, Mahdi Ahmadi, estimates is roughly seventy percent domestic, reached through VPNs. Ahmadi, 35, describes his role as strictly neutral: a court of ideas in which advocates for outright abolition of clerical rule sit across from officials aligned with the state, and viewers are left to judge. No political group in Iran can be erased, he told Torbati. They must be able to talk to one another. It was Ahmadi who quietly brokered last year's Tucker Carlson interview with President Masoud Pezeshkian — a fact his critics cite as evidence of protection from a powerful patron, and one he flatly denies.

The channel’s survival is a small case study in how much space actually exists inside a government that appears, from this diary’s vantage point, to control everything. Azad’s website is blocked. Ahmadi says he has been interrogated multiple times. During the June 2025 strikes covered in earlier parts of this series, authorities seized the outlet’s equipment for twenty-five days after it aired two programmes on the war. And still, nearly 160,000 subscribers later, with regular six-figure viewership, it airs debates most Iranian institutions would not survive hosting — including, earlier this year, a session in which one guest argued for a constitutional referendum to end clerical rule and another argued for restoration of the exiled monarchy, weeks after security forces killed thousands of protesters who had been chanting for exactly that.

There is a real critique of Azad worth sitting with rather than waving away, and it does not come from the government. The sociologist Fatemeh Hassani notes that of more than a hundred guests hosted over the past year, only four have been women — a gap Ahmadi attributes to the greater political risk women face for appearing and the harsher scrutiny they receive online, not to editorial choice. Hassani’s read is sharper: that Azad’s pluralism is curated to remain politically safe, a platform that can showcase disagreement about clerical rule while structurally excluding the people best positioned to describe, from direct experience, what state violence against women in Iran actually looks like. Both things can be true at once — a channel taking genuine institutional risk, and a channel whose risk-taking has firm, gendered limits it has not yet been forced to confront.

And the state has just staffed up for the decision. As Erika Solomon reported for the Times on August 17, Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei — who has not appeared in public once in the nearly six months since he was named supreme leader — spent this month installing veteran hardliners across the security apparatus: Mohsen Rezaei at the Supreme National Security Council, a new general staff of the armed forces, a new head of the Revolutionary Guards, a new commander of its naval forces, and Hossein Taeb, a cleric and former intelligence chief, to run the Basij — the paramilitary network routinely used to crush protests. Announcing Taeb’s appointment, the supreme leader called on him to strengthen the Basij’s “public intelligence network,” which analysts read as an expanded mandate for domestic surveillance.

Taeb’s own father-figure had let him go: the elder Khamenei dismissed him as intelligence chief in 2022, by which point even Mahmoud Ahmadinejad was accusing him of fabricating charges against political opponents. He is now responsible for watching Iranians at home.

Omid Memarian of DAWN supplies the reason this matters more than an organisation chart. Iranian society, he says, increasingly resembles a boiling pot that could erupt with little warning — and a confrontational strategy abroad becomes far easier to sustain if the leadership believes it can suppress the political consequences at home. The appointments are not only a war footing. They are preparation for the second front, which is domestic.

Set this beside Section II. Washington’s strategy this week was to say nothing and let the blockade do the arguing. Tehran’s approach to a programme like Azad is to permit just enough dissent to dilute the reach of diaspora broadcasters it cannot otherwise compete with, while keeping the state’s hand on how much dissent that is. Neither government has settled the question of who gets to speak. The difference is that in Tehran, six people and a blocked website are still trying to answer it themselves, in public, on camera, in two-hour sessions the state has not yet decided are worth shutting down for good.

VI. By the Numbers

  • 60 days the memorandum allowed. It expired on August 16 without public acknowledgement by either government.

  • ~12 ships a day through the Strait of Hormuz, against roughly 130 before the war.

  • 16% above pre-war levels for Brent crude.

  • 18 American service members killed; 653 wounded, still recorded across two Pentagon webpages.

  • 1,700+ Iranian civilians killed, per the Human Rights Activists News Agency.

  • 3 signatories to the Mecca pact; 2 of them share a land border with Iran.

  • 160,000 Azad subscribers; 70% of the audience inside Iran, reached by VPN; 25 days its equipment was held after it covered the war; 4 women among more than a hundred guests in a year.

  • 2 governments now claiming sovereignty over an international waterway that belonged to neither in January.

  • 0 of the war’s three original objectives achieved.

  • Narges Mohammadi remains in Evin Prison, absent from every negotiating table this diary has tracked, in a country now debating on a YouTube channel it periodically raids whether her jailers should be permitted to rule at all.

VII.

There is a kind of failure that does not announce itself. No surrender is signed, no defeat conceded, no headline written. The bombing simply becomes intermittent, then rare, then absent; the deadline passes and nobody mentions it; the strategy becomes waiting, and the waiting becomes the policy.

Meanwhile the actual work of the region proceeds elsewhere. Three governments met in Mecca and built something with a secretariat and a mutual defence clause, and neither Washington nor Tehran was in the room. Nixon once asked a Shah to protect him from having to do this himself. The current arrangement is that America’s allies are building the architecture and America is not in it, because America is occupied with a war it cannot win, cannot end, and can no longer name.

Araghchi says the war ended in June and this is a new status. He may be closer to right than he intended. Something did end in June. What replaced it has no name, and the two governments most responsible for it are the two least willing to give it one.
