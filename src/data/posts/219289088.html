Every performance has an audience, and every performer knows which seat matters. At Quantico on September 30, 2026, Pete Hegseth played to 600 junior officers and sergeants. On Capitol Hill he plays, increasingly badly, to 535 members of Congress. In allied capitals he plays to defence ministers who have learned to check his social media. But the series has argued throughout that the performance has replaced the job, and a performance needs only one review to keep running.

This instalment is about that review: why Hegseth survives, what could end his run, and what to watch on and after November 3, 2026.

The Performance

“He loves war,” President Trump said of Hegseth at a White House Cabinet meeting on May 27, 2026, slapping him on the biceps. As Andrew Feinberg reported for The Independent in “’He loves war’: Trump slaps Pete Hegseth’s biceps and praises his ‘central casting’ secretary in Cabinet kickoff,” the full line was “the Secretary of War, Pete Hegseth, Central Casting. He loves war.” Hegseth replied by comparing the President to Washington and Lincoln. Greg Jaffe recalled the moment in his September 30, 2026 analysis for the New York Times, “Hegseth Talks Tough as Republican Doubts About His Leadership Grow.” It is the most important job reference in Washington, and it gives this series its name.

Hegseth knows it. At Quantico he told the troops how the President greets him: “Pete, central casting!” There is no better summary of the arrangement. Trump, a man who spent years producing television, hired a defence secretary who looks the part, talks the part and does push-ups on camera. The President has kept saying so. As Time reported on September 2, 2026, even as Republican senators called for Hegseth’s removal, Trump said he was “doing a fantastic job.”

Then, on October 15, 2026, the audience of one spoke up. Hegseth had said the coming firing-squad execution of Nidal Hasan, the convicted Fort Hood shooter, would be made “public,” and the Pentagon confirmed it would be streamed. As Avery Lotz reported for Axios in “Trump cancels Hegseth’s plan to livestream Fort Hood shooter’s execution,” the President ended the idea on Truth Social: “this event will not be streamed.” The plan had drawn condemnation from both parties and from survivors of the 2009 attack. The Pentagon’s spokesman said the two men were “completely aligned.” Perhaps they were. But it was not the first time the President had publicly reversed his Secretary; Part IV described the Poland deployment in May. It was the first time the reversal was about the performance itself.

Todd Harrison of the American Enterprise Institute told the Times what most of Washington believes: “Trump really likes Hegseth, and I think that’s based on style.” Jaffe put the question that follows plainly. Will pressure from Republican lawmakers force the President to find a Pentagon chief who is less politically combative and more willing to make peace with a frustrated Congress?

The Paperwork

A record of survival. Hegseth has survived things that would have ended most defence secretaries.

He was confirmed on January 24, 2025, by 51 votes to 50, with Vice President JD Vance breaking the tie, the narrowest margin for any defence secretary since the post was created in 1947. Two months later, in March 2025, a journalist was inadvertently included in a Signal group chat in which Hegseth shared operational details of strikes in Yemen. As Tom Bowman reported for NPR in December 2025, in “Pentagon watchdog finds Hegseth risked the safety of U.S. forces with use of Signal,” the Pentagon’s inspector general concluded that he had endangered service members and operations and had broken department rules on using a personal device for official business. Hegseth declined to be interviewed and said he had the authority to declassify the information. His spokesman called the report “a TOTAL exoneration.” The national security adviser in that chat, Mike Waltz, was moved out of his job within weeks. Hegseth stayed.

The colleagues who didn’t. The contrast with the rest of the cabinet is striking. In 2026, Kristi Noem was removed from the Department of Homeland Security in March and Attorney General Pam Bondi was fired on April 2. Hegseth, meanwhile, has done most of the firing in his own department (Parts I and III).

The Republican revolt. It has not been a quiet year on his own side. As Chantelle Lee reported for Time on September 2, 2026, in “Republican Senator Calls On Trump to Replace Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth,” Senator Thom Tillis of North Carolina, one of the 50 votes that confirmed him, asked the President to replace him after Army Secretary Dan Driscoll resigned. Tillis accused him of “inept management,” said he was “intimidated by competence,” and said he was “creating a leadership void at the top of our military ranks.” Representative Don Bacon of Nebraska, a retired Air Force general, said Hegseth “would not be on my team.” As Burgess Everett and Shelby Talcott reported for Semafor on September 28, 2026, Senators Susan Collins, Kevin Cramer and John Kennedy have all aired frustrations too.

The impeachment. On September 15, 2026, the revolt became formal. As Matthew Adams reported for Stars and Stripes in “Republican Massie moves to impeach Hegseth,” Representative Thomas Massie of Kentucky introduced eight articles of impeachment. They accuse Hegseth of violating the War Powers Resolution by waging an undeclared war in Iran, defying Congress, causing unnecessary civilian casualties (including the February 28, 2026 strike on an elementary school in southern Iran, which the articles say killed more than 160 people, most of them children; the New York Times reports more than 175 civilians dead), and ordering strikes on alleged drug boats in the Caribbean and Pacific. A Pentagon spokesperson replied that standards and merit were back and morale was at all-time highs.

Democrats had tried twice before. Massie’s resolution matters more because he is a Republican, and because House rules force a vote.

The vote did not happen. As Svetlana Shkolnikova reported for Stars and Stripes on September 16, 2026, in “House delays Hegseth impeachment vote until November,” Speaker Mike Johnson cancelled the House’s last working day before the election recess, which meant the expected vote could not be held. Johnson called the resolution a “publicity stunt” that would “immediately be tabled.” The House returns on November 9, 2026, and the resolution must then be voted on within two legislative days.

Since then the politics have shifted. Massie has lost his own re-election campaign, which makes him a lame duck with nothing left to lose. As AG News Staff reported for American Greatness on October 24, 2026, in “House Dems Target Hegseth for Impeachment if They Win Midterms,” citing the Wall Street Journal, House Democrats now plan to press impeachment both in the lame-duck session and in the new Congress if they win. Hakeem Jeffries, the Democratic leader, told a rally in Pennsylvania that one of the first things a Democratic majority would do is “run Pete Hegseth out of town.” The arithmetic in the current House remains against it: Republicans hold 219 seats to 214, and Republicans including Nancy Mace, Ralph Norman and Warren Davidson have said they oppose impeachment. The retired Special Forces officer Mike Nelson, speaking with David French on the New York Times podcast “The Opinions” on October 7, 2026, in an episode titled “Pete Hegseth’s ‘Vast Overcorrection’,” predicted that Hegseth would not be removed, but would draw more votes against him than he expects.

The electorate. The midterms are now the hinge. As G. Elliott Morris reported on October 21, 2026, in “Poll: Democrats post their biggest generic-ballot lead yet, two weeks out,” his Strength In Numbers/Verasight poll put Democrats ahead 54 percent to 42 among likely voters, with Trump’s approval down to 34 percent and disapproval at 62. Bloomberg, citing Pew, reported that roughly two-thirds of Americans disapprove of the President’s handling of the war. Polls are polls. But Jaffe’s reporting described Republican candidates in Iowa, Nebraska, Ohio and Michigan distancing themselves from the war, and Mike Rogers, the Republican Senate nominee in Michigan and an early supporter of the war, saying it “needs to end, and end quickly.” That is what a party sounds like when it is reading the same polls.

The Bill

What would end it. The list of things that could remove Hegseth is short, and only one item on it is decisive.

The nominations. As of late October, the Army is still run by an acting secretary, Adam Telle; Sean Parnell, Hegseth’s preferred candidate, has not been nominated, and Republican senators including Tillis and Cramer have said they would not support him. The President nominated Hung Cao, the acting Navy secretary, for the permanent job in September; he has not been confirmed. The empty chairs of Part III are, a month later, still empty.

Impeachment. A Democratic House could impeach him with a simple majority. Removal would need two-thirds of the Senate, which no plausible election result supplies. Impeachment would be a judgement, not an ending, though it would make every subsequent hearing and budget request harder.

The budget. Hegseth needs Congress to pass a defence budget, a war supplemental and his nominees. As Parts II and III showed, he has so far secured none of them. A Republican Senate that keeps refusing is a slow-acting form of pressure. A Democratic House that controls the purse would be a fast-acting one.

The President. That is the decisive item. Trump has removed defence secretaries before, and not gently. Jim Mattis resigned in December 2018 after disagreeing with the President over Syria. Mark Esper was fired by tweet on November 9, 2020, six days after an election. The precedent is clear: when a defence secretary becomes inconvenient, Trump acts, and he does not wait for a convenient moment.

Midterms have ended defence secretaries before, under presidents of both parties. Donald Rumsfeld, who had survived Abu Ghraib and repeated calls from retired generals for his resignation, was replaced on November 8, 2006, the day after Republicans lost both houses of Congress. President George W. Bush had said only a week earlier that he expected Rumsfeld to stay to the end of his term. Nelson drew the same parallel on the French podcast, suggesting that a President looking for someone to blame after the midterms might find Hegseth convenient. Chuck Hagel announced his resignation on November 24, 2014, three weeks after Democrats lost the Senate, amid reports of friction with the White House. In both cases the election did not remove the secretary. It gave the President a reason, and a moment, to do what he had been considering anyway.

Hegseth’s position resembles Rumsfeld’s in one respect: an unpopular war, a frustrated party and a President who has, until now, stood by him in public. It differs in another. Rumsfeld had decades of standing in Washington and a network of allies in Congress. Hegseth has the President.

What to watch.

  • November 3. Whether Democrats win the House. If they do, every Hegseth hearing in 2027 becomes an investigation.

  • November 9 and after. Whether the House tables Massie’s resolution, and how many Republicans vote not to.

  • The Army. Whether the Senate confirms a chief of staff and a secretary, or whether the chairs stay empty into a second year.

  • The war. Whether the President resumes bombing Iran after the midterms. He said on October 1 that more bombing was “possible” after the election, and as Magdalena Del Valle, Eric Martin and Samy Adghirni reported for Bloomberg on October 24, 2026, in “Trump prepares for Iran escalation after U.S., Israeli elections,” a third carrier strike group and about 10,000 more sailors and Marines are heading for the Gulf. Hegseth made an unannounced visit to Israel the same week to meet Benjamin Netanyahu, days before Israel’s own election. If the war resumes, it will be run by a Secretary whose standing depends on how it looks.

  • The phone. Whether Trump’s praise stays at “fantastic,” or whether the October 15 reversal on the execution livestream was the first sign of it cooling.

What the troops will watch. There is one more audience, and it is the one the series began with. The 600 junior officers and sergeants at Quantico will spend the coming months watching the same things Washington is watching, but from a different angle. They will see whether a Secretary who removed so many of their generals is himself held to any standard. If he survives the midterms untouched, they will learn that performance matters more than results at the top of their institution. If he falls, they will learn that the system still has limits. Either lesson will shape the officers they become, long after the Secretary has gone.

The cost of an audience of one. The deepest bill is the one the series has been adding up. A Secretary of Defense who answers only to the President, and whose standing with the President rests on looking and sounding the part, has every incentive to perform for him and little reason to do the dull parts of the job. Those dull parts are a budget that explains itself, nominees the Senate will confirm, allies told in advance, munitions counted honestly, and generals who are removed for reasons that can be stated. Hegseth has neglected nearly all of them. He has not been punished for it, because the one person whose opinion decides his future values the performance more.

That is the paradox of the past eighteen months. By almost every measure that Congress, the generals and the allies care about, Hegseth has never been weaker. By the only measure that counts in his own position, he has never been more secure. Until those two facts collide, the show continues. When they do, the review will arrive the way Esper’s did: in a single post, on a weekday, without warning.

It all looks rather like a long-running television series whose critics have given up and whose ratings are falling, but whose producer still likes the lead. Such shows do get cancelled eventually. They are rarely cancelled because they are bad. They are cancelled because the producer has found someone new who looks the part.
