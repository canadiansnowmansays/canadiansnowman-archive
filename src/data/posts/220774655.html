⚖️ Serious — one of the grave ones. It is about a line drawn across a subcontinent in five weeks, by a man who had never seen it, from a set of numbers that had been fought over before they were ever used. The line moved more human beings than any border in history. No wink. The dog stays out of it.

I count my essays, and at essay 1,648 I wrote about the Peace of Westphalia, the treaty that made the map itself the unit of politics — the line on the parchment that tells you which sovereign you belong to. I have been circling that idea ever since: the border as a number, a boundary as a count of who is on which side. Essay 1,947 is where that idea reaches its most catastrophic form. In August of that year the British Empire left India, and on its way out it drew a line through two provinces, and the line turned a census into a migration and a migration into a massacre.

Five weeks

Begin with the man and the deadline, because the deadline is the essay.

In June 1947 the British government announced that it would leave India, and that the provinces of Punjab in the west and Bengal in the east — each with large populations of both Muslims and Hindus, and in Punjab of Sikhs as well — would be divided between two new states, India and Pakistan. Someone had to draw the lines. The task was given to Cyril Radcliffe, a distinguished London barrister. He was chosen, in part, precisely because he had never been to India and had no connection to it: he would be impartial because he knew nothing. He arrived in Delhi on the eighth of July. The date of independence had been fixed for the middle of August.

He had, in other words, about five weeks to divide some eighty-eight million people.

He did it, working from maps that were often out of date, with district reports, with the advice of judges from both communities who largely cancelled each other out, and in the heat of a north Indian summer. The poet W. H. Auden later wrote a poem about him, called Partition, which begins: Unbiased at least he was when he arrived on his mission. The line is a cruel compliment. Ignorance was the qualification. The schedule was the method. At essay 1,914 I wrote that the summer of the mobilisation timetables was what happens when the count is finished before the thinking is. The summer of 1947 is a second edition.

The census that drew the line

What did Radcliffe draw the line from? Largely, from a number.

The starting point for the division was the census of 1941 — the count of India’s population by religion, district by district. Districts with Muslim majorities were to go, notionally, to Pakistan; those with non-Muslim majorities to India; and the commission was to adjust at the margins for “other factors” like rivers, railways, canals and towns. So the border of two nations was, at its foundation, a census rendered as geography: a column of religious percentages, redrawn as a line across the land.

And here is the entry that makes this a Numbers Game year above all others. The 1941 census had been taken in an India where everyone could see partition coming, and everyone understood that the religious count might one day decide which country a district belonged to. Historians have long noted the suspicion — on all sides — that the numbers were pushed and pulled in that census, that communities encouraged their members to be counted and to count their neighbours a certain way, because the enumeration was no longer merely a description. It had become a stake. The count was being fought over before anyone used it, because everyone knew that whoever won the count might win the land.

This series has spent its whole length arguing that a census is never neutral. In 1941, in India, the people being counted knew it as well as I do. And six years later a lawyer who had never seen the country turned their contested arithmetic into a frontier.

Two days late

Now the detail that I cannot read without a kind of vertigo.

Pakistan became independent on the fourteenth of August, 1947, and India at midnight on the fifteenth. Radcliffe had finished his lines a few days before. But the boundary award was not published until the seventeenth of August — two days after independence. For two days, millions of people in Punjab and Bengal woke up in new countries without knowing which ones. Villages celebrated independence under one flag and learned afterwards that they belonged to the other. The timing was, by most accounts, deliberate: the authorities wanted the handover ceremonies to pass off before the anger the line would cause. The count was finished; the people being counted were simply not told the result until the party was over.

The largest movement in history

Then the people learned where the line was, and they moved.

Muslims in what was now India fled west and east toward Pakistan. Hindus and Sikhs in what was now Pakistan fled toward India. They went on foot, in bullock carts, in columns that stretched for tens of kilometres, and on trains so packed that people rode on the roofs. Somewhere between ten and twenty million human beings were uprooted in a matter of months — the figure most often given is about fifteen million — making it the largest mass migration in recorded history.

And they were killed as they went. Columns of refugees were attacked; villages were burned; trains arrived at their destinations carrying only the dead. The violence ran in every direction, by every community against every other, and it has left wounds that the subcontinent still carries. How many died? You know the answer this series always gives, because the same answer keeps coming back. Estimates run from about two hundred thousand to two million. A range of ten to one. The people who drew the line counted its population to the district. Nobody counted the dead the line produced.

The women in the ledger

I have to enter one more column, because I write another series about the women of India, and I cannot leave them out of this one.

In the violence of Partition, tens of thousands of women — the figure usually given is seventy-five thousand to a hundred thousand — were abducted by men of the other community: taken, forcibly converted, forcibly married. And afterwards, the two new governments agreed to a programme to recover them and return them across the border to the families and the religion they had been taken from. Some women were desperate to go home. Some had been rejected by their families as dishonoured, or had children, or had built lives they did not want torn up a second time, and were returned anyway, because the state had decided which column of the national ledger they belonged in. A woman was counted, twice over, as the property of a religion: once by the men who took her and once by the governments that took her back. I wrote at essay 1,942 about the fraction of grandparents that decided a person’s fate. Here the deciding number was simpler still. It was one: the faith a woman had been born into, entered on a form, enforced by a border.

The line that has never closed

What happened next is still happening. The line Radcliffe drew in Bengal made East Pakistan, which broke away in a war in 1971 to become Bangladesh. The line he did not draw at all — in Kashmir, whose fate was left unsettled — has produced wars, insurgencies and standoffs between two nuclear powers ever since. Radcliffe went home to England. He is said to have refused his fee for the work, and to have burned his papers. He never went back to India. The man who drew the line in five weeks spent the rest of his life declining to explain it.

The closer

So 1947 is the year a census became a border. A contested count from 1941, a lawyer chosen for his ignorance, five weeks, two days of silence, fifteen million on the roads, a death toll that is once again a range, and women counted as the property of a column. Of all the maps in this series, it is the one where the arithmetic on the parchment and the blood on the ground are most directly the same thing.

And now, after three of the gravest numbers on the whole route, the count gives us a breath. The next landmark is essay 1,948, and it is a year of quieter but no less enormous counting — the year engineers at the Bell Telephone Laboratories announced a tiny device called the transistor, and a mathematician down the hall published a paper that made information itself a thing you could count, in a unit he called the bit. It is, I should warn you, the most telecom essay of the century, and its author spent eighteen years in that industry. The same year, a Canadian law professor drafted a declaration that counted, in thirty articles, what every human being on earth is owed. We have counted the dead for three essays. At 1,948 we count something else.
