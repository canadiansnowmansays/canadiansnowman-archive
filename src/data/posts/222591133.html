For a quarter of a century, Israeli politics has relied on a three-word phrase. After the Camp David summit collapsed in 2000, Prime Minister Ehud Barak told his country there was “no partner” for peace on the Palestinian side. The phrase outlasted Barak, Arafat and the peace process itself, and it has excused a great deal since.

On Wednesday, October 28, the day after Israel’s first election since October 7, 2023, Thair Abu Rass, a scholar of Arab politics in Israel, used it the other way round. “There’s a shift in Arab politics, an attempt to get closer to the Jewish Zionist mainstream,” he told Aaron Boxerman of the New York Times. “It’s just that the other side is not willing to do so. There’s no partner on the Jewish side to create an alternative reality.”

In a hair salon in Jaffa, Saado Zorob, 48, an Arab citizen of Israel, put it more plainly. Jewish Israelis, he said, seem happy to be treated by Muslim doctors and to eat in Arab restaurants, but not to give Arab citizens a say in where the country goes. “I feel like garbage.”

Part XIX of this series described the Palestinians who would have no vote in this election, the millions in the West Bank and Gaza whose futures it would help decide. This part is about the Palestinians who did vote, in numbers not seen in generations, and found that their votes might count for less than anyone else’s.

An Election of Fears

The campaign was fought almost entirely on fear. In July, as David M. Halbfinger reported for the New York Times, an opposition candidate asked a room of voters in Jerusalem what they feared most if Benjamin Netanyahu’s government won another term. “I’m afraid my children would leave,” one said. Another said, “Civil war.” A third said, “Collapse.” In one recent poll, 39 per cent of Israelis said they feared violence between Israelis, the same share that feared an Iranian bomb. Michael Oren, Netanyahu’s former ambassador to Washington, described the economic choice in one sentence: “We’re going to have a choice of whether we’re going to build in E1 or have an economy.” E1 is the tract outside East Jerusalem that would cut the West Bank in two.

Netanyahu’s closing argument made the fear explicit. Two days before the vote, he posted an AI-generated image of a mushroom cloud over Tel Aviv. The text, written by a supporter and shared approvingly by Netanyahu, told voters that “your hand should tremble” if they chose anyone else. In 1996 Netanyahu first won office promising a “safe peace,” as Graeme Wood noted in The Atlantic. Now he was promising that his opponents would bring annihilation. He told Israelis to prepare for the end of American military aid and floated cryptocurrency as a way around future sanctions. The Bank of Israel issued a rare public warning in reply, and a former governor, Jacob Frenkel, said turning away from the world’s financial system would make Israel “a leper.”

The campaign also had a hidden side. In the final weeks, according to three senior Israeli defence officials and others briefed on the matter, Netanyahu pressed for a new strike on Iran before the vote. The military’s chief of staff, Lt. Gen. Eyal Zamir, found “no immediate or urgent need” and refused, as Halbfinger, Ronen Bergman and their colleagues reported for the New York Times. Avigdor Lieberman, Netanyahu’s former defence minister, asked the attorney general to investigate the prime minister for treason, for trying “to drag Israel into a war on the eve of elections.”

The Day of the Vote

Israelis voted in record numbers. By 6 p.m. on Tuesday, October 27, 66.7 per cent of eligible voters had cast ballots, nine points more than at the same hour in 2022 and the highest in decades. Final turnout was the highest since 1999.

The most striking change came in Arab towns. Arab citizens make up about a fifth of Israel’s population and have long voted at lower rates than Jews, out of alienation, boycott or a sense that their ballots changed nothing. This time they came out. In Rahat, a Bedouin town in the Negev, turnout before 5 p.m. had already matched the town’s final total in 2022. “The stakes are too high to sit this one out,” said Sireen Jabareen, 30, who voted for the first time after years of boycotting a system she considers discriminatory. “I voted for an Arab party because this is about our very existence,” Sara Abu Sayum, 24, told the New York Times‘s live coverage in Lod.

The prime minister responded by attacking them. “There are buses heading to the polls and long lines in Arab towns,” he posted. “Meanwhile, right-wing strongholds have yet to wake up! If we don’t go vote now, we will find ourselves with a government of Arab parties.” He told his base that Arab citizens were “trying to steal the elections.” Likud released a poster of women in hijab captioned “Right-wing voters, don’t stay home,” and asked the elections committee to keep the polls open beyond 10 p.m., a request never made before. The committee refused. Election officials said there was no sign of widespread irregularities. In 2015 Netanyahu had warned that Arabs were voting “in droves,” and he apologised afterwards. This time he used nearly the same words several times in one day and did not apologise.

His national security minister, Itamar Ben-Gvir, recorded his warning about Arab turnout at the entrance to Kafr Qasim, a town in central Israel. On October 29, 1956, Israeli Border Police there shot dead 48 villagers who were returning from their fields, unaware that a curfew had been imposed while they were out. Ben-Gvir filmed his video two days before the anniversary.

Yousef Jabareen, leader of the Joint List alliance of Arab parties, answered the prime minister directly: “The Arab community knows how to punish you. Today we’re sending you and your bloodstained government packing.”

While Israel voted, the West Bank was under military lockdown.

Reply Hazy

The Arab voters did their part. The overall result was less clear.

The final results, released on Thursday, October 29, confirmed what the partial count had suggested. Netanyahu’s alliance fell from 68 seats to 53, eight short of the 61 needed for a majority, as the New York Times reported. Eisenkot’s opposition bloc won 48. That leaves 19 seats to the two Arab parties, Raam and the Joint List, almost double the 10 they held in the outgoing Parliament and probably their best result since the state was founded. Exit polls reported by the BBC on election night had shown the two main blocs level, with Likud the largest single party. President Isaac Herzog will now consult the party leaders. He will choose whoever he thinks has the best chance of forming a majority, not necessarily the leader of the largest party.

Netanyahu stays in office in the meantime. He remains caretaker prime minister until a new government is sworn in or new elections are held, and no Israeli government has been sworn in less than 50 days after an election. “Netanyahu is still in the driver’s seat,” Natan Sachs of the Middle East Institute told Isabel Kershner and her colleagues at the New York Times. “There’s a general sense that this was the opposition’s big chance. From Netanyahu’s perspective, he can throw the dice again and again.” In 2019 two inconclusive elections in a row left him in power as caretaker throughout.

On the morning after the vote, he declared that “we must form a strong right-wing government.” Likud called on the police and intelligence services to investigate the Arab vote. In Rehovot, Shira Barkan, a therapist, said she had gone to bed hopeful and “woke up in the morning with Bibi again.” In Ramla, Yakov Matzlawi, a Likud member who runs a market stall, was more cautious: “What’s important is that we remain in the game.”

Graeme Wood, writing in The Atlantic, summed up the result as a Magic 8 Ball would: “Reply hazy, try again.” He set out three ways to a government, each requiring someone to abandon something they had called non-negotiable:

  • A unity government of Netanyahu and parts of the opposition. In one version, Netanyahu would serve two more years and then hand over to Naftali Bennett, “just as Donald Trump finishes his term.” The opposition would have to keep in office the man it campaigned to remove.

  • The opposition governing with the Arab parties. The left would welcome it. Lieberman’s secular-nationalist party would refuse.

  • Netanyahu governing with the Arab parties. Wood thinks this so unlikely that it would require “immediate public-health announcements to assure the population that the water supply has not been spiked with hallucinogens.” Yet Mansour Abbas of Raam says “the right wing” has already approached him.

Wood thinks the most likely outcome is that the Arab parties are left out.

No Partner

The arithmetic is simple. Eisenkot cannot reach 61 seats without the Arab parties, and Raam alone is not enough. He would also need the Joint List, which won the larger share of the Arab vote and includes a Palestinian nationalist faction that many Jewish Israelis regard as hostile to the state.

The barrier is political rather than mathematical. During the campaign Eisenkot avoided saying whether he would work with Arab parties, and said none met his conditions. On Wednesday he did not mention them. He promised instead “a stable, Zionist government” and said “the public opted for change.” Shalom Yerushalmi, a veteran political writer, explained why he was being careful: “After Oct. 7, Israeli society will not accept that. There’s no chance.” Jacob Abolafia of American University gave Wood the question underneath: “Are Arabs full citizens of Israel, or are they not? There are internal contradictions in Israeli society that are too great to solve right now.”

The Israeli Jewish side of this deserves to be stated fairly. October 7 was the deadliest day in Israel’s history. Raam and some Joint List members condemned the attack, but many Jewish Israelis still do not want lawmakers who see themselves as part of the Palestinian people shaping security policy in wartime. As Gal Beckerman notes in The Atlantic, some Arab parties are openly anti-Zionist and include a Palestinian right of return in their platforms, and even Mansour Abbas has not explicitly renounced Hamas as a terrorist organisation. That fear is real, even if it is not the whole story.

The Arab side has also changed. Mansour Abbas has accepted Israel’s Jewish character as a fact, discussed national service for Arab citizens, and placed Yoav Segalovitz, a Jewish former senior police commander and centrist Zionist, second on Raam’s list. In 2021 his party became the first independent Arab party to join an Israeli government. On Wednesday he said Arab voters had turned “frustration and despair” into “hope,” and that his first priority was a government that would replace Netanyahu’s. Jabareen of the Joint List made a similar offer: “The ball is now in Eisenkot’s court. Whoever wants to remove Netanyahu and change his policies — we are ready to cooperate to topple his government.”

There is a precedent. In the 1990s, Yitzhak Rabin kept a minority government alive with the votes of Arab lawmakers who were not in his coalition. The Oslo Accords passed in that parliament. Analysts say the Joint List could do something similar for Eisenkot, supporting him from outside the government by voting against no-confidence motions. Whether he takes that route will show whether “change” in Israel includes the citizens whose votes made it possible.

In Taibe, an Arab town of 47,500 in central Israel where more than 80 per cent of residents voted, fireworks went up on election night, as Adam Rasgon and Natan Odenheimer reported for the New York Times. Ahmad Tibi, a Joint List leader who has sat in the Knesset since 1999, said his party was “ready to swallow frogs to take down Netanyahu’s government” and would do “almost anything” except “convert to Judaism.” By the next day the mood had cooled. Some opposition figures had already ruled out governing with Arab parties, and television commentators were warning against giving them any say over security. “What value does my vote have?” asked Noreen al-Nashef, a lawyer in the town. Others were still confident. “When they need the Arabs, they’ll come to us,” said Reda Hajj Yahya, 40.

The numbers are not in doubt. Eisenkot’s 48 seats plus the Arab parties’ 19 make 67, a clear majority against Netanyahu, as Beckerman points out. In Abu Ghosh, an Arab village outside Jerusalem, turnout rose from 55 per cent in 2022 to 78 per cent. A man in Jaffa told Beckerman that a Palestinian friend in Jenin, who has no vote, phoned to make sure he used his, “if only to help stop the settler violence.” Liberal Israelis are starting to say openly what that means. “You can’t be liberal and deny the Arab Israeli voters,” the screenwriter Avner Bernheimer said. “If liberals keep boycotting them, we will lose to the extreme religious right, the most violent groups, and the cult of Netanyahu.” A long list of Israeli legal scholars, former judges and academics has petitioned President Herzog to take account of what Arab citizens said with their votes. The Palestinian-Israeli jurist Raef Zreik made the self-interested case: “Sometimes it doesn’t matter what you think; what matters more is the way you act.”

Saado Zorob in Jaffa named the risk if Eisenkot does not. If the deadlock leads to another election, he said, “many Arabs will stay home.” The citizens who were told this time that their vote mattered would learn it did not. Netanyahu would face an electorate without them.

The Ones Who Could Not Vote

Whichever bloc forms the government, the Palestinians of the West Bank and Gaza will have had no say in it. Their situation in the weeks before the vote was not reassuring.

On Monday, October 26, the day before the election, a United Nations commission of inquiry reported that Israel had continued to commit “genocidal acts” in Gaza since the ceasefire of October 10, 2025, as Nick Cumming-Bruce reported for the New York Times. By the end of September, Israeli strikes and gunfire had killed 1,431 Palestinians in Gaza since the ceasefire, 300 of them children. The commission rejected the claim by President Trump’s Board of Peace that the truce had “largely held.” In the West Bank, it counted 1,080 Palestinians killed in the same period, 95 per cent of them by Israeli security forces rather than settlers. It said more than 34,000 had been driven from their homes since January 2025, the largest displacement in the territory since 1967, and called it a crime against humanity. Israel rejected the report as misinformation, and the genocide case brought by South Africa is still before the International Court of Justice. Part XVI of this series set out why the word “genocide” is contested. The figures do not depend on the word.

The candidates offered the Palestinians little. Bezalel Smotrich, the finance minister who also administers the West Bank, was explicit about his aim: “We are deepening our roots in all parts of the Land of Israel and burying the idea of a Palestinian state.” The man most likely to replace Netanyahu offers something milder, but not different in kind. Eisenkot rejects Palestinian statehood in the near term, supports “a more restrained expansion” of settlements, has promised to curb settler violence, and has criticised Netanyahu for once having supported a Palestinian state. Only Yair Golan’s Democrats back two states, and they would carry little weight in any coalition.

The opposition’s best offer to the Palestinians, then, is to restrain the settlers while keeping the settlements. Even that depends on a coalition that may never form, because the opposition will not rely on the Palestinians who are Israeli citizens.

The View From Washington

This election also tested what Part XIV of this series called the domestic political architecture of the U.S.-Israel relationship, and the results came in on both sides of the Atlantic.

President Trump endorsed Netanyahu, calling anyone else the “wrong leader” for Israel, and his Defense Secretary visited the prime minister eight days before the vote. Analysts doubted it moved many votes. Among Democrats, as Annie Karni reported in the Times’s election coverage, support for Israel is changing faster than at any time in memory. Almost half of House Democrats voted in the past year to cut off aid. Senator Chuck Schumer, the minority leader and the highest-ranking Jewish official in the country, has called on the Senate floor for new leadership in Israel. Senior Democrats have proposed sanctions over settlement building. The 10-year agreement that guarantees billions in annual American military aid is about to expire, and Democrats want to renegotiate it. In a Times/Siena poll, 74 per cent of Democratic voters opposed further military and economic aid to Israel. In a Pew survey, eight in ten viewed Israel unfavourably.

When Netanyahu warned his voters that American aid would be “over,” he was blaming the change on others. A week later, on the morning of the American midterms, the President confirmed it. “We were giving them $4 billion,” he told Fox News as polls opened on the East Coast, as David E. Sanger reported for the New York Times. “We are. But it gets cut off at the end of the year.” Under what he described as a deal reached with Netanyahu months ago, Israel would go on buying American weapons but would pay for them itself. The President called the aid “a bad talking point,” and asked why the United States should “suffer the slings from lunatics like an El-Sayed,” a reference to Abdul El-Sayed, the Democratic Senate candidate in Michigan and one of Israel’s sharpest critics. He did not mention Gaza. Netanyahu has his own reasons to agree: aid controlled by Congress has become a lever for those who want to change Israeli policy, and ordinary arms sales are harder to hold hostage. His office did not comment.

A Republican president ended a mainstay of American policy for decades on Election Day, to take an issue away from the Democrats. Netanyahu was right about the trend. He has also done more than anyone to bring it about.

Changing the prime minister would not change much abroad either. As Steven Erlanger reported for the New York Times, the world sees little difference between Netanyahu and Eisenkot on security or on a Palestinian state. Britain and France have held back the sanctions they announced last month on West Bank settlement business, hoping an Eisenkot government would curb the settlers. Gina Abercrombie-Winstanley, a former American diplomat now at the Atlantic Council, said the vote shows the world that Israel’s direction “is not just a Bibi problem. It represents today’s Israel writ large.” Rachel Brandenburg of the Israel Policy Forum described how it would look if the Arab vote were set aside: “if you then go ahead and say, ‘Oh, we have to go into new elections again because they don’t count,’ that’s not a good look for Israeli politics and Israeli government and frankly, Israel as a democracy.”

Hazy, With a Chance of War

Until a government is formed, Israel will be run by a caretaker who campaigned on annihilation, who tried to start a war before the vote and was refused by his own chief of staff, and whose party now wants the police to investigate the citizens who voted against him.

The danger now runs past polling day. The same defence officials who described the pre-election push warned that a return to war in the weeks after the vote could stop the opposition forming a government within the legal deadline, or push it into a unity government that includes Netanyahu. Across the Atlantic, the United States has drawn up plans to strike Iran after its own midterms. The Iran Crisis diary is following that thread. For this series, the point is narrower. Netanyahu’s most dependable route back to a majority is a crisis large enough to make the arithmetic of this election irrelevant. “He will have greater incentive to escalate, whether with Iran after the U.S. midterms on Nov. 3, or in the West Bank or Gaza,” said Beth Oppenheim of the European Council on Foreign Relations. Sanam Vakil of Chatham House was blunter: “The ticking clock of a war with Iran is speeding up and seems all but inevitable.”

Erlanger also noted the simplest fact about the result. Taken together, a majority of Israelis voted for parties opposed to Netanyahu’s coalition. He keeps power because that majority includes Arab citizens, and his opponents have not yet decided whether those votes count as part of it.

The novelist Assaf Gavron, writing in The Atlantic, reminded readers that Israeli liberals have predicted the end of Israel at every election since Menachem Begin’s victory in 1977. They were wrong each time, but Gavron sees a difference now. Begin gave back the Sinai, and Ariel Sharon pulled every settler out of Gaza. Both men “operated within a political world in which power encountered boundaries.” Today’s right, he writes, treats restraint itself as illegitimate. Courts, prosecutors, journalists and bereaved families have all become a “deep state,” and even before the count was finished, Likud was claiming the election had been “stolen” by Arab parties whose seats had risen “from 10 seats to a record 19.” Gavron, 57, says he will not leave Israel. “But I do not expect my two teenage daughters to build their futures here — and I cannot honestly tell them that they should.”

Israel’s democracy produced something this week that it had not produced in a generation: Arab citizens voting at scale, for themselves, to choose who governs. Israel’s political class now has to decide what to do with those votes. For 26 years, Israeli leaders have said they could not make peace because there was no partner. The Arab citizens who turned out on Tuesday are now waiting to see whether there is a partner for them.
