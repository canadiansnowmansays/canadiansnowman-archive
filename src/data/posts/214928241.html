Every essay in this series has, in one way or another, been arguing for addition. Acemoglu’s big-tent framework in Part VII was about widening a coalition without losing its edge. Jolly, Stelson, and Bennett in Part XI were literal additions — converts brought in from the other side. El-Sayed’s apology in Part X was an act of repair aimed at keeping a coalition intact rather than fracturing it further. The implicit assumption running underneath nearly all of it has been that the party’s problem is a construction problem: build the tent right, invite the right people in, and the coalition holds. Sarah Longwell’s essay in The Atlantic this week argues that the voters this series has been building the tent for don’t actually want a tent. They want a wrecking ball.

As Sarah Longwell wrote in The Atlantic on 8 September 2026, adapted from her new book How to Eat an Elephant: One Voter at a Time, the single most consistent finding across more than four hundred focus groups conducted since the early Trump era is that voters — Democratic voters specifically, in this instance — are not asking for ideological recalibration. Longwell ran an experiment that should unsettle anyone who has spent this series treating the moderate-versus-progressive debate as the party’s central strategic question: she convened focus groups of self-identified moderate Democrats and self-identified progressive Democrats separately, and found that if she hadn’t told you which group was which, you would not be able to tell from the transcripts. Brandon from Georgia, a moderate, wants Democrats to “fight fire with fire.” Jeffrey from Georgia, a progressive, describes the party as “a turtle that’s been turned on its back... just kind of flailing around.” Different ideological self-descriptions, the same underlying complaint: not enough fight.

This is where Longwell’s essay lands a direct hit on the thesis this series built in Part VII. Acemoglu’s big-tent populism, recall, distinguished between an adversarial politics that unites and one that divides — Roosevelt welcoming the hatred of “organised money” while still building the broadest coalition of his era. Longwell’s voters are not asking for that kind of careful, structurally sound adversarialism. They are explicit that what they admired about Trump’s 2016 takeover of the Republican Party was the willingness to knock the existing structure down entirely, and they are equally explicit that they want their own version of it. “That tension — absolutely not, but also yes — is the defining mood among Democratic voters right now,” Longwell writes. “They don’t want to become what they hate. But they are tired of losing to it.” A wrecking ball, by its nature, does not ask whether the load-bearing walls are worth preserving. It is a strange and uncomfortable thing to have to report, nine essays into a series about coalition discipline, that the coalition itself may not currently want discipline. It wants a demolition crew.

It would be a mistake, though, to read Longwell’s essay as a simple repudiation of the big-tent thesis, because her own diagnosis of what voters are actually rewarding is more specific than raw aggression, and it maps almost exactly onto the messengers this series has already flagged favourably. Mike, a Massachusetts Democrat, praises Jon Ossoff — cited in Part VII as the source of this series’ favourite line about a wealthy country in which no one should go without care for lack of money — precisely because “he’s not apologetic. He’s not defensive. He simplifies things.”

Bryan, from New York, praises Pete Buttigieg for going “on as many right-wing areas and interview formats” as possible, which is nearly a paraphrase of the venue-crossing thesis this series built around Ro Khanna’s Fox News appearance in Part III. What voters are describing as fight, in other words, isn’t incoherence or pure aggression for its own sake. It’s confidence delivered without hedging, in hostile rooms, without flinching from the questions asked there. That is compatible with Acemoglu’s big tent. It just requires a sharper, less polite version of the invitation than this series may have been imagining.

There’s a specific mechanism in Longwell’s essay that explains why “fight harder” and “big tent” aren’t actually opposites, even though they sound like they should be, and it concerns what she calls the transformation of the relationship between a leader and the public. Trump, she argues, made that relationship “direct, constant, intimate, and unavoidable” through a firehose of tweets, rallies, and Truth Social posts — a form of omnipresence voters have since come to expect and, in her account, have not yet been offered by anyone on the Democratic side. Julie, a Democrat from Arizona, put it in a register that should sound familiar after Part IX’s discussion of the missing elevator pitch: “They didn’t make people connect to them emotionally enough... If you look at the MAGA party, they have, albeit a bit psychotically, a very emotional connection. And we need that.” Parker’s swing voters in Part IX couldn’t name the candidates on their own ballot. Longwell’s Democratic voters are describing the same absence from the opposite direction — not “I don’t know who these people are” but “I don’t feel anything about who these people are,” which may be the more dangerous version of the same problem, since a slogan can eventually fix the first and nothing but genuine emotional presence fixes the second.

Longwell’s own position in this argument is worth pausing on, because it connects directly to Part XI’s discussion of David Jolly, Janelle Stelson, and Rebecca Bennett. She discloses plainly that she is “a former Republican who left the party after Trump took it over,” making her, structurally, exactly the kind of convert this series examined at length two parts ago — someone whose credibility with a skeptical audience rests partly on having crossed the aisle rather than having been born on the correct side of it. But where Part XI’s converts are running as candidates, asking voters to trust an individual biography, Longwell is doing something closer to translation: she is telling the Democratic Party what its own voters are already saying, in their own words, largely because the party’s elites, in her account, are too far from the people politics is supposed to serve to hear it directly. Her line about Washington being “less a swamp than a bubble” is doing real work here. It suggests the coalition-math problem this series has spent thirteen parts diagnosing from various angles may not be primarily a design flaw in the tent. It may be that the people responsible for designing it aren’t in the room where the actual complaints are being made.

Longwell’s essay also forces a reconsideration of Part VIII’s argument, and not entirely a comfortable one. Bruni’s “gotcha piñata” thesis held that Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, and by extension El-Sayed, absorb a disproportionate share of scrutiny for positions that read, to a cable-news audience, as evidence of untrustworthy inconsistency. Longwell’s focus groups complicate that picture rather than confirming it, because the voters she quotes praising Ocasio-Cortez aren’t crediting her despite the inconsistency Bruni flagged — they’re crediting her for the same bluntness that generates the inconsistency in the first place. Adrien, a Pennsylvania Democrat, says “she actually gives a crap about people.” Petra calls her “not afraid to tell it like it is.” Neither voter is weighing her past positions against her current ones the way a cage-fight interview format, discussed in Part VIII, is designed to force a candidate to do. They’re responding to the manner of the delivery, independent of its content, which suggests the piñata dynamic Bruni described may be a media-class preoccupation more than a voter-level one. If that’s right, it reframes the stacking problem this series opened with in Part I: the risk to a candidate like Ocasio-Cortez or El-Sayed may not be that voters will punish inconsistency, but that the press will keep insisting voters care about inconsistency long after the voters themselves have moved on to a different metric entirely — whether the candidate sounds like someone actually willing to fight for them.

Longwell closes with four specific areas of Republican vulnerability her focus groups keep surfacing — immigration enforcement that’s gone further than even reluctant Trump voters wanted, the self-enrichment optics around the Qatar jet, the still-unreleased Epstein files, and an economy that’s failed to deliver on the “greatest economy in the history of the world” promise for the young voters who believed it hardest. None of these are new discoveries; this series’ companion pieces have tracked most of them individually elsewhere. What’s new is Longwell’s insistence that voters already know all four of these things and are simply waiting for someone to say them with the same bluntness Trump used to say things about his opponents. “Why aren’t you fighting harder?” is, by her account, not a question about which four vulnerabilities to target. It’s a question about tone, delivered by voters who have already identified the targets themselves and are annoyed their own party hasn’t caught up.

I want to end where this series’ recurring caution usually lands, because Longwell’s essay, for all its force, doesn’t actually resolve the tension it identifies. “Absolutely not, but also yes” is an honest description of a genuinely conflicted electorate, not a strategy. A party that tries to build Acemoglu’s big tent while also becoming the wrecking ball its own base is asking for risks being neither — too aggressive to hold the coalition-expanding voters Part VII described, not aggressive enough to satisfy the voters Longwell is quoting. But the alternative, ignoring what four hundred focus groups are saying because it complicates a tidier theory, is its own kind of failure, and it’s the specific failure this series was named to guard against. A loyal opposition that refuses to hear what its own voters actually want, in favour of the coalition-math argument it finds more intellectually satisfying, is not practising loyalty to anyone but its own prior thesis.
