The MS St. Louis, 1939. 937 Jewish refugees turned away by Cuba, the United States, and Canada. Many later died in the Holocaust. Western doors closed at the worst possible moment.

By Stephen Gordon | CanadianSnowmanSays

There is a moral trap at the centre of the Israel-Palestine conflict that no honest account of it can avoid, and that most dishonest accounts of it exploit relentlessly.

It works like this: the Holocaust happened. Six million Jews were murdered by a European nation in the middle of the 20th century using the full apparatus of a modern industrial state. This is the most documented, most systematically executed, most morally unambiguous act of mass murder in recorded history. Its victims were innocent. Its perpetrators were guilty. Its scale was unprecedented. Any person of conscience looking at the 20th century must reckon with the Holocaust as a foundational fact about what European civilization is capable of doing to a people it decides to designate as other.

From this foundational fact, a political conclusion is regularly drawn: that Israel’s existence is the Holocaust’s necessary answer, that criticism of Israeli state policy is therefore morally equivalent to Holocaust denial or antisemitism, that the security concerns of a Jewish state created in the shadow of genocide supersede the normal frameworks of international law and human rights, and that anyone who applies those frameworks to Israel’s conduct is either naive about history or motivated by antisemitism.

This conclusion does not follow from the foundational fact. The leap from “the Holocaust happened and was an atrocity” to “therefore Israel’s conduct in Gaza is beyond criticism” is not a logical inference. It is a political manoeuvre, deployed with increasing aggression by those whose interest is in foreclosing the conversation rather than having it.

The honest position is harder and more important: the Holocaust was real, its trauma is real, the Jewish people’s need for security is real and legitimate and historically grounded in documented catastrophe — and none of those facts grant any state, including Israel, immunity from the laws that govern the conduct of states, or from the moral obligation not to visit on another people the logic of collective punishment and dispossession that was visited on the Jewish people in Europe.

The British Mandate period, from 1920 to 1948, is where this trap was built. Understanding how it was constructed is essential to understanding why it is so effective and why dismantling it without dismissing its genuine moral content is the most difficult analytical task this series faces.

The Mandate: Britain’s Impossible Assignment

The League of Nations formally awarded Britain the Mandate for Palestine in 1922, incorporating the terms of the Balfour Declaration into its text — which meant that the contradictions built into that 67-word letter became the official policy of the international body charged with managing the post-war world.

Britain was now legally committed to facilitating the establishment of a Jewish national home in Palestine while simultaneously protecting the civil and religious rights of the existing non-Jewish communities. These commitments were not compatible. As Jewish immigration increased — driven first by persecution in Europe and later by the catastrophe of the Holocaust — the Arab majority population’s political and demographic position deteriorated in direct proportion. Every Jewish immigrant who arrived represented, in the arithmetic of Palestinian Arab politics, one more step toward minority status in their own homeland.

The British response to this structural impossibility was to produce a series of reports, white papers, and policy statements that attempted to balance the incompatible commitments by satisfying neither side completely. The result, predictably, was that both sides became increasingly convinced that Britain was betraying them, leading both to escalating violence against British forces and against each other, until the British finally concluded that the cost of the Mandate exceeded its value and handed the problem to the United Nations.

This is a compressed description of 28 years of administered catastrophe. Let us look at the specific decisions.

The Immigration Waves and the Arab Revolt

Jewish immigration to Palestine under the Mandate came in waves that historians call aliyot — the Hebrew word for ascent, used by Zionist tradition to describe immigration to the land of Israel. The First Aliyah had begun in 1882, before the Mandate, driven by Russian pogroms. The subsequent waves were driven by a combination of Zionist ideology, European persecution, and the practical availability of Palestine as a destination.

Jewish population growth in Palestine under the Mandate, 1917–1947. From ~10% to ~32% of the total population — a transformation enabled by British policy and driven by European persecution.

The numbers tell the story of the demographic transformation. In 1917, when Balfour wrote his letter, Jews constituted roughly 10 percent of Palestine’s population — perhaps 56,000 to 85,000 people. By 1931, the Jewish population had grown to 175,000, roughly 17 percent of the total. By 1936, it had reached 370,000, approximately 28 percent. By 1947, on the eve of the UN partition vote, the Jewish population was approximately 630,000 — roughly 32 percent of a total population of just under two million.

This demographic transformation was the product of deliberate political effort, Zionist institutional organization, and European persecution. It was entirely legal under the terms of the Mandate. It was also, from the perspective of the Arab majority population, an existential political threat that the British had sanctioned and were facilitating.

The Arab political response escalated through the 1920s and 1930s from protests and petitions to organized strikes and ultimately to armed revolt. The 1936-1939 Arab Revolt was the most sustained Palestinian political and military effort of the Mandate period — a general strike that lasted six months, followed by armed guerrilla resistance that tied down significant British military resources. It was brutally suppressed by the British military using tactics that included collective punishment, house demolitions, and the arming of Jewish paramilitary forces to assist in pacification.

The arming of Jewish paramilitaries — the Haganah in particular — during the Arab Revolt is a fact of considerable importance that rarely appears in popular accounts of the Mandate period. Britain, fighting an Arab insurgency, trained and armed the Jewish military organizations that would subsequently fight the 1948 war. The institutional military capacity that made Israeli independence possible was built, in significant part, with British weapons and British training, during a counter-insurgency campaign against the Arab population. The ironies are considerable and the consequences were permanent.

The 1936–1939 Arab Revolt. Brutally suppressed by British forces using collective punishment and house demolitions — while Jewish paramilitary groups were armed and trained to assist.

The Arab Revolt also produced something that its participants could not have intended: it destroyed the Palestinian Arab political and military leadership. The primary Palestinian political figure of the period, Amin al-Husseini, the Grand Mufti of Jerusalem, fled into exile — eventually, catastrophically, to Nazi Germany, where his collaboration with Hitler has served as a propaganda gift to Israeli hasbara ever since. The secondary Palestinian leadership was killed, imprisoned, or exiled during the Revolt’s suppression. By the time the 1948 war began, Palestinian Arab society had been decapitated of its most experienced political and military figures by a decade of British counter-insurgency.

The Jewish community, by contrast, emerged from the Mandate period with functioning institutions — a proto-government in the Jewish Agency, an experienced military in the Haganah, an economy, a press, a university, a political culture. These institutions were the product of decades of Zionist organizational effort, and their existence was the decisive asymmetry of 1948. We will come to that in Part VI. The point here is that the asymmetry was in significant part the product of British policy during the Mandate — which simultaneously suppressed Palestinian Arab political organization and facilitated Jewish institutional development.

The 1939 White Paper: The Cruelest Timing

In May 1939, having spent two years suppressing the Arab Revolt and alarmed by the prospect of Arab alignment with Nazi Germany in the approaching war, the British government issued a White Paper on Palestine that represented the sharpest reversal of the Balfour Declaration’s intent in the Mandate’s history.

British White Paper of May 1939. Issued months after Kristallnacht, it closed Palestine to most Jewish immigration exactly as Europe descended into war and genocide

The 1939 White Paper limited Jewish immigration to Palestine to 75,000 over five years, after which further immigration would require Arab consent. It restricted Jewish land purchases. It proposed an independent Palestinian state within ten years in which Jews, who would by then constitute no more than one-third of the population, would be a permanent minority.

It was, in short, a document that closed the gates of Palestine to Jewish immigration at the precise moment in history when the Jewish people of Europe most desperately needed somewhere to go.

The timing bears repeating because it is not incidental. The White Paper was issued in May 1939. Germany had already absorbed Austria and Czechoslovakia. Kristallnacht — the organized pogrom that destroyed thousands of Jewish businesses, homes, and synagogues across Germany and Austria and killed hundreds of Jews — had occurred six months earlier, in November 1938. The systematic persecution of German and Austrian Jews was not a secret. The direction of Nazi policy toward the Jews of Europe was legible to any informed observer.

The British government chose this moment to restrict Jewish immigration to the one territory that the Balfour Declaration had designated as a Jewish national home.

The reasons were purely strategic. The approaching war made Arab loyalty in the Middle East — particularly control of the Suez Canal and access to oil — more valuable than Jewish political support. The arithmetic of imperial interest favoured the Arabs, whose territory and resources Britain needed, over the Jews, who had no territory and no army and whose support Britain could take for granted because they had nowhere else to turn.

It was, by any moral standard, an act of abandonment. It was also, by the cold logic of British imperial calculation, entirely rational. The two things are not incompatible. They are both true simultaneously. This is the territory where honest analysis of the Mandate period must operate — where strategic rationality and moral catastrophe overlap so completely that separating them requires more intellectual rigour than most participants in this debate are willing to exercise.

The SS St. Louis and the Doors That Closed

In May 1939, the same month the White Paper was published, the MS St. Louis sailed from Hamburg carrying 937 Jewish refugees who had obtained Cuban visas and were traveling to Havana with the expectation of proceeding to the United States. Cuba refused to honour the visas. The ship sat in Havana harbour for days while negotiations failed. It then sailed to Florida, where it was refused entry by the United States Coast Guard. It approached the Canadian coast, where Prime Minister William Lyon Mackenzie King’s government refused admission — the official who coordinated the refusal reportedly said “None is too many” when asked how many Jewish refugees Canada should accept, a phrase that has haunted Canadian historical memory ever since.

The MS St. Louis, 1939

The St. Louis returned to Europe. The passengers were distributed among Britain, France, Belgium, and the Netherlands. When Germany subsequently occupied France, Belgium, and the Netherlands, many of those passengers were deported to concentration camps. Of the 937 passengers, historians estimate that approximately 254 were killed in the Holocaust.

The St. Louis is not an exceptional story. It is a representative one. Throughout the late 1930s and early 1940s, as the Nazi persecution of Jews escalated toward genocide, the doors of the world’s democracies closed systematically against Jewish refugees. The United States maintained strict immigration quotas. Britain, as noted, restricted entry to Palestine. Canada accepted almost no Jewish refugees during the war years. Australia, South Africa, Argentina — country after country found reasons, some bureaucratic, some openly antisemitic, to exclude the people who were running for their lives.

The Holocaust did not occur because the Jews had no state and no refuge. But the absence of a state and the closure of refuges made it more comprehensive and more lethal than it might otherwise have been. The survivors of the Holocaust — and the Jewish communities that observed it from relative safety — drew the obvious conclusion: statelessness is a death sentence when the states that contain you decide to kill you. The only guaranteed protection is your own state.

This conclusion was correct. It was also, by the brutal logic of history, going to be purchased with someone else’s dispossession. The state that would protect Jewish lives could only be built in Palestine, and building it in Palestine required displacing — or at minimum, subordinating — the people who were already there. The people of Europe who had persecuted and murdered the Jews would not be the ones paying the price of the solution. The Arabs of Palestine, who had no involvement whatsoever in the Holocaust, would be asked to absorb the consequences of European antisemitism.

This is the moral trap at the centre of the conflict. It was not constructed by Israel. It was constructed by Europe — by centuries of antisemitism culminating in industrial genocide, by the specific decisions of specific Western governments to close their borders to Jewish refugees while the ovens were being built. Israel is downstream of European antisemitism in a way that Western discourse almost never acknowledges, because acknowledging it would require Western nations — including Canada — to reckon with their own role in making a Jewish state in Palestine the only available answer.

I am not arguing that European guilt justifies Palestinian dispossession. I am arguing that you cannot understand why the dispossession happened, or why it commands the international support it does from Western governments, without understanding who built the trap and why. The Palestinian people are paying for a crime they did not commit, with a currency — their homeland — that was never Europe’s to spend. That is the original sin’s most devastating compound interest, and it was made possible by decisions taken in Ottawa, Washington, London, and Berlin, not in Tel Aviv.

Jewish Resistance and the End of the Mandate

While the Holocaust was consuming European Jewry, the Jewish community in Palestine — the Yishuv — was developing the military and political infrastructure of a state. The Haganah, the mainstream Jewish paramilitary force, had been building capability since the 1920s. Two more radical organizations operated alongside it: the Irgun, led by a Polish-born revisionist Zionist named Menachem Begin — who would later become Prime Minister of Israel — and the Lehi, known to the British as the Stern Gang after its founder Avraham Stern.

These organizations conducted a sustained campaign of violence against British Mandate authority in the final years of the Mandate. The most consequential single act was the bombing of the King David Hotel in Jerusalem on July 22, 1946. The hotel housed the British Mandate civil and military headquarters. The Irgun detonated a bomb in its basement that killed 91 people — British, Arab, and Jewish. Begin ordered the operation. He later received the Nobel Peace Prize.

This is mentioned not to equate Jewish resistance to British occupation with Palestinian violence against Israeli civilians — the contexts and targets were different — but to establish a point that gets obscured in contemporary discourse: political violence in the service of national liberation has been practiced by virtually every nation that has achieved statehood through struggle against a colonial power, including the United States in 1776, including the Jewish community in Palestine in the 1940s. The characterization of such violence as terrorism or as legitimate resistance depends almost entirely on who is doing the characterizing and in whose interest.

By 1947, Britain had concluded that the Mandate was untenable. The costs — military, financial, and diplomatic — of trying to satisfy incompatible commitments in a territory where both sides were shooting at British soldiers exceeded the imperial benefits. In February 1947, the British government announced that it was referring the Palestine question to the United Nations and would terminate the Mandate on May 14, 1948, regardless of what the United Nations decided.

This was, characteristically, an act of managed irresponsibility. Britain had created the conditions for catastrophe. It now proposed to leave before the catastrophe fully arrived, handing the detonator to an international body with no enforcement capacity and washing its hands of the consequences.

The United Nations appointed a Special Committee on Palestine — UNSCOP — which spent the summer of 1947 in Palestine taking evidence from Jewish and Zionist representatives, who cooperated fully, and from Arab representatives, who boycotted the process on the grounds that the outcome was predetermined. In November 1947, the UN General Assembly passed Resolution 181 — the Partition Plan — dividing Palestine into a Jewish state and an Arab state, with Jerusalem under international administration.

The Jewish leadership accepted the plan. The Arab states rejected it. The plan’s acceptance and rejection were both, in their different ways, predictable — the Jewish leadership understood that international recognition of any Jewish state, however imperfect its borders, was an achievable foundation on which to build; the Arab states understood that accepting the plan meant accepting the legitimacy of the Zionist project in a way that their domestic politics would not survive.

What happened next — May 14, 1948, the Declaration of Independence, the Arab invasion, the Nakba — is Part VI. But the foundations of what happened in 1948 were laid across the 28 years of the Mandate: in the immigration waves that transformed Palestine’s demographic balance, in the Arab Revolt that destroyed Palestinian Arab political leadership, in the 1939 White Paper that closed Palestine’s doors at the Holocaust’s worst moment, in the Jewish paramilitary organizations that Britain had helped train and arm, and in the institutional asymmetry between a Jewish community with a functioning proto-state and a Palestinian Arab community whose political structures had been systematically dismantled.

The Trap, Sprung

When Israel declared independence in May 1948 and was immediately attacked by five Arab armies, the Holocaust was three years in the past. Its survivors were in displaced persons camps across Europe. Its full scale was by then documented and known. The connection between the Holocaust and the new Jewish state was not theoretical — the Mandate’s displaced persons camps in Cyprus were full of Holocaust survivors waiting to enter Palestine. The emotional and political force of that connection was real and overwhelming.

It remains real today. And it remains, today, a trap — for both sides.

For Israel and its supporters, the trap is the belief that the Holocaust’s reality permanently insulates Israeli state conduct from normal moral and legal scrutiny. It does not. States do not inherit immunity from their founding traumas. The United States was founded in a revolution against colonial rule; that does not give it immunity from scrutiny of its own colonial conduct. South Africa’s post-apartheid government emerged from one of the 20th century’s great liberation struggles; that did not give the ANC immunity from scrutiny of its governance failures. The Holocaust was real and its consequences for Jewish statehood were legitimate — and Israeli state conduct in Gaza in 2024 and 2025 must still be accountable to international law.

For Palestinians and their supporters, the trap is the temptation to minimize or instrumentalize the Holocaust — to treat it as propaganda rather than history, or to use it primarily as a counter to Israeli claims rather than reckoning with it as the foundational trauma that drives Israeli security psychology. Hamas’s original charter’s antisemitism was not incidental to its politics. It was constitutive. That has consequences for how the Palestinian national movement is perceived internationally and for the political coalitions it can build.

The honest path through the trap is the one that holds both things simultaneously: the Holocaust was real and its trauma is legitimate and it does not justify the dispossession and destruction of a people who had nothing to do with it.

Both things are true. They have always been true. The 75-year failure to hold them simultaneously — by Western governments, by regional actors, by international institutions — is the compound interest on the original sin accruing at a rate that the current catastrophe represents.

In Part VI, we arrive at 1948. The trap springs. The accounting begins.
