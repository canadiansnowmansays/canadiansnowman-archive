On Tuesday 17 November 2026, the last day the law allowed, Representative Virginia Foxx of North Carolina filed for a recount.

Ms. Foxx, a Republican, has represented North Carolina’s Fifth District since 2005 and chairs the House Rules Committee, one of the most powerful jobs in the chamber. Her Democratic opponent, Chuck Hubbard, a former journalist, was ahead by just over 300 votes. “In a race where no candidate received a majority of the votes, it is necessary that we take every measure to ensure the ballot count is accurate,” she said in a statement the day before, calling the margin “razor thin,” as John Yoon reported for The New York Times on 17 November.

She is not alone. In North Carolina's 10th District, the Democrat Ashley Bell has asked for a recount even though she trails the Republican incumbent, Representative Pat Harrigan, by more than 2,500 votes. In Minnesota's First District, the Republican incumbent, Representative Brad Finstad, said he would seek one after the Democrat Jake Johnson, ahead by about 300 votes, declared victory.

Nothing about this is remarkable, and that is the point of this part. For two years this series has documented attempts to contest elections by other means: lawsuits filed before anyone voted, investigations with no end date, armed monitors, demands for voter rolls, a president who calls a result a disgrace before it is final. Here, in the same month, are three candidates contesting close results in the most boring way the country has. They are asking the state to count again, under rules written long before anyone knew who would be behind.

The rules were there first

The North Carolina procedure is set out in the state’s election rules, and Mr. Yoon walked through it. A candidate in a race below statewide level, such as a House seat, can demand a recount after the county canvass if the margin is no more than one per cent of the votes cast. The county boards pay for it. It begins with a full machine recount. Whoever trails afterwards has 24 hours to ask for a hand count of a random sample of ballots, to check the machines against human eyes. If the sample differs from the machine count by enough to change the outcome, the State Board of Elections orders a full hand recount.

Minnesota’s rules are tighter. The state pays for a recount in a federal race if the gap between the leader and another candidate is less than a quarter of one per cent. Requests can be filed between Thursday and the following Monday, and the recount itself would run from 30 November to 4 December, according to the Minnesota secretary of state. Florida and South Carolina go further still, and order recounts automatically when a race falls under a set margin. Last week two Republicans, Representative María Elvira Salazar in Florida’s 27th District and Representative Joe Wilson in South Carolina’s Second, were confirmed as winners after automatic recounts.

Notice what all of these rules have in common. They were written before 2026. They set thresholds in percentages, not party labels. They apply the same way whichever candidate trails, and they end on a date. A Republican in North Carolina and a Democrat in North Carolina used the same rule this month, in neighbouring districts, from opposite sides of the count.

Compare that with the instrument this series spent October describing. The Justice Department’s lawsuit in Alaska, filed on 22 October, asked a court to stop certification only if the Republican lost, and only if a counterfactual reallocation of another candidate’s votes would have saved him. It was written for one race, eleven days before the vote, with a trigger designed around one possible outcome. A recount rule is the opposite: written for every race, in advance, blind to who benefits. One is a procedure. The other was a contingency plan.

The Alaska trigger, as it happens, never had a chance to fire. Mary Peltola won 50.1 per cent of first choices on 17 November, the same day Ms. Foxx filed, and an outright majority leaves no reallocation to argue about. The Blanche Files takes up that story in Part XV.

What a recount usually finds

A recount is a check, not a second election, and it rarely changes much. FairVote studied every statewide general election in the United States from 2000 to 2023, 6,929 of them. There were 36 recounts. Only three reversed the original result: the Washington governor’s race in 2004, Vermont’s state auditor in 2006 and the Minnesota Senate race in 2008. All three began with a margin under 0.06 per cent of the votes cast for the top two candidates. On average, a recount moved the margin by about 551 votes, or 0.03 per cent.

House districts are smaller than states, so the arithmetic isn’t directly transferable. But the pattern explains why Mr. Johnson, a former high school maths teacher, reacted the way he did. Recounts rarely change election outcomes, he said, and he had already begun his transition. He travelled to Washington last week for orientation for new members, and posted a video from the Capitol saying he was “really looking forward to hitting the ground running starting Jan. 3.”

It also explains Mr. Harrigan’s reaction to a request that, on FairVote’s numbers, has almost no chance of changing anything. A 2,500-vote gap is not the kind a recount typically reverses. He said he respected Ms. Bell’s right to ask.

That sentence is worth a moment. It is what a candidate says when he believes the process will confirm his win and sees no reason to treat his opponent’s use of the process as an attack. It is the sentence missing from Alaska, where Senator Dan Sullivan’s campaign announced on 16 November that it would wait until “every legal vote is counted and certified,” a standard for conceding that it had never mentioned before, and where the president called the race a “disgrace” the same day.

How the first one ended

Ms. Foxx’s recount finished on Saturday 21 November, and the machines confirmed what the first count had found. On Sunday the Associated Press called the race for Mr. Hubbard, and Ms. Foxx phoned him to concede, as John Yoon reported for The New York Times on 22 November. Mr. Hubbard, a former reporter at the weekly Wilkes Journal-Patriot, had lost to her by nine points two years ago. This time he led by 99 votes on election night and by about 300 when the counting stopped, in one of the closest House races in the country.

Then both of them said what candidates are supposed to say. Mr. Hubbard said Ms. Foxx had congratulated him “graciously,” and thanked her for her decades of service. Ms. Foxx said she had offered him her “best wishes as he represents this wonderful district.”

Nobody filed a lawsuit. Nobody asked the Justice Department to look at the ballots. The 83-year-old chair of the Rules Committee, first elected in 2004, lost her seat by a few hundred votes, had the count checked the way the law allows, and accepted the answer. It took five days.

The concession that came first

Ms. Foxx was not the only long-serving Republican to lose by about 300 votes this month. In Tennessee’s Fourth District, Representative Scott DesJarlais, an eight-term Republican, finished behind Victoria Broderick, a Democrat who works in tech support. In the week after the election she led by fewer than 300 votes. On 16 November, before the race had been called, Mr. DesJarlais said in a statement that he had conceded. “All of us hope that the new Member of Congress feels the same level of awe and gratitude for this tremendous undertaking that I felt every day I had the privilege of serving,” he wrote. The Associated Press called the race for Ms. Broderick on Tuesday 24 November, as John Yoon and Emily Cochrane reported for The New York Times. Her final margin was about 270 votes out of more than 195,000 cast.

Almost nobody saw it coming. Forecasters had rated the seat solidly Republican. Mr. Trump carried the district by 24 points in 2024, which makes it the most Trump-friendly seat Democrats have taken so far, and Mr. DesJarlais had beaten Ms. Broderick by 43 points when she ran against him two years earlier. Her campaign spent just over $90,000 to his $508,000, according to Federal Election Commission data, and has no record of airing a single television ad. The day after the election, she told her boss she needed the day off because she might have been elected to Congress.

A margin of less than a fifth of one per cent is exactly the kind of result a careful loser might want checked. Mr. DesJarlais looked at the numbers and accepted them. That makes two Republican incumbents, two margins of roughly 300 votes and two concessions, with not a word from either about a rigged count.

Why this matters more than it looks

It would be easy to treat recounts as housekeeping. They are more than that, because a recount is the system’s answer to the question every losing side eventually asks: are you sure?

If that question has no lawful, predictable answer, people find unlawful, unpredictable ones. Part LVII of this series called the weeks after an election “the weeks nobody is war-gaming”: certification deadlines, seized ballots, the House seating its members on 3 January. In 2020 the machinery held because officials in Georgia, Arizona and Michigan ran recounts and audits, published the results and certified them, while the president and his lawyers tried everything else. Georgia counted its presidential ballots three times. The result did not change, and the recounts are the reason the claim of fraud had nowhere to stand.

The same logic runs through this autumn. Recounts in close House races, run by county boards and paid for by the state, are a safety valve. They let a candidate who lost narrowly test the count without accusing anyone of anything, and they let the winner arrive in Washington with a result that has been checked. Ms. Foxx did what a senior member of Congress should do when she trailed by 300 votes: she asked, waited for the answer and accepted it. Mr. Finstad is doing the same. So, on a much longer shot, is Ms. Bell. None of them has called the election rigged. None has asked a federal agency to intervene. Each has filed a form.

It is worth saying plainly that Republicans are the ones using the process in two of these three races, and that they are using it properly. This series has been hard on the Republican Party's leadership for two years, and it will go on being hard on it. But the party is not one man, and Virginia Foxx asking North Carolina's county boards to run their machines again, then conceding when they confirmed she had lost, is not Donald Trump asking Georgia's secretary of state to "find" votes. One is the system working. The other was an attempt to go around it.

The size of the thing being counted

These recounts are settling the last few seats of a very large majority. By Tuesday 17 November, Democrats had 257 seats, tying the total the party won in 2008. They reached that number when the Associated Press called North Carolina's Third District, where Raymond Smith, a former state lawmaker, defeated Representative Greg Murphy, who had already conceded. Mr. Hubbard's win on Sunday took them to 258, as Tim Balk reported for The New York Times. Two days later Ms. Broderick's win made it 259, the party's largest House majority in three decades. The last time Democrats held more seats was the early 1990s, when they had 267. A few races were still uncalled. Minnesota's First District, where the Democrat leads by about 300 votes, goes to a recount on 30 November. Louisiana's Fifth and Sixth Districts go to runoffs on 12 December. Democrats finished first in both November primaries, with less than a fifth of the vote each, but Republican candidates combined took about 57 and 63 per cent, as Tim Balk reported for The New York Times on 25 November, and the Cook Political Report rates both seats solidly Republican.

That last detail connects to Part LXVII. North Carolina's congressional map was drawn by its Republican legislature in 2023 and partly redrawn in 2025, an effort this series followed in Part LXII. Mr. Murphy's loss, and the defeat of the Rules Committee chair in a district she had held for more than two decades, are what happens to a map drawn for a normal year in a year that wasn't normal. The maps were designed to make Republican seats safe. On 3 November several of them turned out to be merely close, and close seats are where recounts happen.

Tennessee shows the same thing from another angle. After the Supreme Court weakened the Voting Rights Act, its Republican legislature carved up the state's congressional map in an effort to sweep all nine seats, including the reliably Democratic district built around Memphis. To make that work, the mapmakers moved part of Nashville and neighbouring Rutherford County into the Fourth District, which left it less Republican than before. The Memphis seat fell, as Part LXVII recorded. The Fourth went the other way. Ms. Broderick will be the only Democrat in Tennessee's delegation, elected in a district the new map made just competitive enough to lose.

A Note From the Canadian Side of the Border

Canada handles this with almost aggressive dullness. Under the Canada Elections Act, a judicial recount is automatic when the margin between the top two candidates in a riding is less than one-thousandth of the votes cast. A candidate or voter can also apply to a judge for one if there are grounds to think the count was wrong. A judge supervises, and the ballots are counted again by hand. In the federal election of 28 April 2025, the Quebec riding of Terrebonne went to a judicial recount and was decided by a single vote, and the losing side took its complaint to the courts. Nobody went to the RCMP. Nobody’s attorney general sued before the vote. The argument went through the courts, under rules everybody knew in advance.

That is all an election system really needs to promise: that when it is close, you can ask, and that the answer will come from a process that would have treated your opponent the same way. Virginia Foxx asked, and she got her answer. The county boards ran the machines again, the count held, and on a Sunday afternoon she called the man who had beaten her. Scott DesJarlais, down by fewer than 300 votes in Tennessee, did not even wait for the call. Ashley Bell asked too, from the other side, and Minnesota's turn begins on 30 November. Once Louisiana's two December runoffs are done, the country will know the final size of the new House. That is what democracy fighting back looks like on an ordinary weekend, with almost nobody watching.
