The arch would welcome you to Elsinore and, in the same gesture, inform you who had paid for the welcome. Six feet of ELSINORE. Three feet of VOSS FAMILY. Lit from below. In perpetuity.

You enter Elsinore from the west on County Road 8, past the Haliwell farm, past the sign that says WELCOME TO ELSINORE — A COMMUNITY THAT CARES and, beneath it, the newer line that says A SMART COMMUNITY, and then you are in the town, more or less, in the gradual way that you are in a small Ontario town, where the edge is not a line but a softening — a gas station, then a farm-supply store, then the first houses, then you are somewhere rather than nowhere, and the somewhere is Elsinore.

There is no gate. There has never been a gate. A town of thirty-three thousand people on the Avon River does not have a gate, because a gate implies that there is an inside and an outside and a moment of passing between them that someone has decided to mark, and Elsinore has never decided to mark it, because Elsinore has never been the kind of place that thinks of its own edge as an occasion.

In September, Garrett Voss proposed a gate.

He did not call it a gate. He called it a Gateway Arch — a “signature architectural feature” to mark the western entrance to Elsinore, to be built at the point where County Road 8 becomes Ontario Street, to be funded, he announced, entirely by himself, as a gift to the community, at no cost to the taxpayer.

The arch would be substantial. The renderings — there were renderings; there are always renderings — showed a curved structure of pale stone and steel spanning the road, lit from below, bearing the word ELSINORE in letters the renderings suggested were approximately six feet tall, and bearing, beneath the word ELSINORE, in letters the renderings suggested were approximately three feet tall, the words A GIFT TO THE COMMUNITY FROM THE VOSS FAMILY.

The arch, in other words, would welcome you to Elsinore and, in the same gesture, inform you who had paid for the welcome.

Garrett Voss, fresh from the library naming reversal — which he had absorbed with the equanimity of a man who understands that you do not win every round and that the trick is to keep proposing things — presented the arch as a fait accompli of generosity. He had the funds. He had the renderings. He had, he said, already had preliminary conversations with a structural firm. He was offering Elsinore a landmark, at his own expense, and all the town had to do was accept it.

The Tourism and Culture Committee was, initially, receptive.

Bradley Fenn was enthusiastic. He had slides within a week — his own slides, supplementing Voss’s renderings — projecting the arch’s impact on tourism traffic, its photographability, its potential to become, in Bradley’s phrase, “a destination in itself.” He noted that visitors arriving from London would pass beneath it, that the Korean tourist market in particular responded well to “iconic arrival moments,” and that the arch represented exactly the kind of profile-raising infrastructure that a smart community should embrace, particularly when offered for free.

Reg Plimpton was inclined toward it. A landmark at no cost to the taxpayer, gifted by a prominent local family, was the kind of thing Reg’s instincts told him to accept warmly and quickly, his arm already extending toward Garrett Voss in his mind.

The receptiveness lasted until the September committee meeting, at which the floor opened, and Constance Hay, who had been waiting, spoke.

She said she had three questions.

She said the first was about the word gift. She said a gift, properly understood, was given freely and without the giver’s name attached as a condition of the giving. She said an arch that announced, in three-foot letters, who had paid for it was not a gift. It was a purchase — the purchase of the western entrance to Elsinore as a permanent advertisement for a family that built subdivisions — and that the town should be clear-eyed about which of these it was being offered, because the two things looked similar in a rendering and were entirely different in stone.

She said her second question was about permanence. She said an arch was not a banner. A banner could be taken down when the town tired of it. An arch spanning the main road into town, lit from below, six feet of ELSINORE and three feet of VOSS FAMILY, was a structure that would outlive everyone in the room, including Garrett Voss, and would become, for every future generation of Elsinore, the first thing seen on arriving and the last thing seen on leaving, in perpetuity, because someone in 2026 had accepted a free arch without asking how long free lasts when it is carved into the approach to your town.

She said her third question was the one that mattered.

She said: “Why does Elsinore need an arch?”

She let the question sit.

She said: “We have a river. We have a theatre that has been full for seventy years. We have a green that the Horticultural Society is restoring after the last improvement. We have a fountain Eleanor Vance is stripping back to grey after the improvement before that. We have a library that we had to fight to keep the name of. We have swans that no one owns and a Swan Warden who has been on the river at five forty-five every morning for twenty-two years. We have, in short, a town. A specific, improbable, sufficient town. It does not need a gate. It has never needed a gate. The only thing an arch would add to Elsinore is the name of the man who built it, and that is not an addition to Elsinore. That is a subtraction, dressed as a gift, lit from below.”

She sat down.

The committee did not vote on the arch that evening.

Bradley said perhaps the proposal would benefit from further study. Reg, whose instincts had told him to accept the arch warmly and quickly, found that his instincts had been overtaken by the specific gravity of Constance Hay having used the word subtraction, and said he thought further study was wise. The proposal was tabled, which in Elsinore is where proposals go to be quietly declined without anyone having to decline them.

Garrett Voss withdrew the offer in October. He did so in a letter to the Beacon, which Norman published, in which he expressed disappointment that the community had not embraced his vision and noted that the funds he had allocated to the arch would be redirected to other opportunities.

Frances Okafor, reached for comment, said the library was grateful for his past generosity and would welcome his continued support of the roof fund, which did not require anyone’s name in three-foot letters and which still had a shortfall.

Garrett Voss did not contribute further to the roof fund.

This, too, was informative, in the way that the things people do when their name is not attached are always informative about the things they did when it was.

A Note for the Record

The triumphal arch is one of the oldest monuments in the Western tradition. The Romans built them to mark victories — a general would return from a campaign and pass beneath an arch erected to commemorate what he had done, his name carved into it, his deeds depicted in relief, the structure designed to make permanent a moment of personal glory by attaching it to the fabric of the city itself.

The Romans understood exactly what an arch was for. It was not for the city. It was for the man. The city merely provided the location.

To the south, the president is moving forward with a triumphal arch — an actual triumphal arch, in the Roman style — to be sited near Arlington National Cemetery, across the Potomac. He has also, by his own social-media account, personally contributed funds to build fountains and to rebuild parts of the park in front of the White House, improvements he has been careful to associate with himself. The arch, the fountains, the ballroom built where the East Wing was demolished, the reflecting pool painted his preferred colour, the lawn fitted with a structure he compared to the Eiffel Tower and suggested might never come down — these are not, taken together, improvements to a city. They are the attachment of one man’s name and preference to the permanent fabric of a capital that belonged, before he arrived and will belong after he leaves, to the public.

The Roman arch at least commemorated a victory that had occurred. The modern version commemorates the desire to be commemorated, which is a different thing, and a smaller one, and which stone is unfortunately just as capable of making permanent.

You arrive in Elsinore the way you have always arrived. Gradually. Without ceremony. A town that does not need a gate because it knows what it is. No one’s name is in three-foot letters over the road.

In Elsinore, the arch was tabled. Garrett Voss withdrew his offer and redirected his funds. The western entrance to Elsinore remains what it has always been: a gas station, a farm-supply store, the first houses, and then you are somewhere rather than nowhere, and the somewhere is Elsinore, and no structure announces this, because Elsinore has never been the kind of place that thinks of its own edge as an occasion.

You arrive in Elsinore the way you have always arrived. Gradually. Without ceremony. Past the Haliwell farm and the sign that cares and is smart, into a town that does not need a gate because it knows what it is.

The river is there. The theatre is there. The swans are on the south bank.

No one’s name is in three-foot letters over the road.

This is not an absence. It is the town, being the town, which is the only thing it has ever needed to be.
