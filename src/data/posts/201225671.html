On a July afternoon in 2019, a room full of distinguished Americans assembled for the specific purpose of saving their country — and nearly came to blows over a single word.

The word was patriotism.

The room, as Yoni Appelbaum describes it in The Atlantic, contained an assemblage of jurists, Ivy League professors, nonprofit leaders, journalists, and theologians. They had been convened by an independent commission that had spent the previous year studying the dismal state of American democracy. Dozens of focus groups had been held across the country. Participants from across the political spectrum had been quick to identify sources of division. When asked to name the things that united them as Americans, they responded with nervous laughter.

The commissioners believed America needed a shared narrative. They could not agree on what it should be. So they called in outside thinkers, among them Appelbaum, and asked for fresh thinking. The session began with what seemed like a simple warm-up exercise: list words or concepts that all Americans could endorse, even if their definitions might vary.

Founding-era Americans in deliberation — the kind of room where “patriotism” was once assumed, not contested.

Appelbaum, a historian whose grandfathers had arrived from a village above Sparta and a shtetl in what is now Belarus, thought of the only word that captured what he felt about the country that had made his existence possible.

He said: patriotism.

The room detonated.

One participant flinched as if struck. Another said the word made her feel excluded. A third said it connoted violence and racism. A fourth was offended that anyone could be offended by the word. The facilitator declined to write it on the easel. Everyone talked at once. The exercise intended to build trust had instead exposed, in the span of a single syllable, the depth of the fracture it had been designed to bridge.

I want to sit with that scene for a moment, because I think it contains, in miniature, everything this series is about.

I. The Diagnostic

These were not random people pulled off the street. They were not MAGA rallygoers or campus radicals selected for maximum conflict by a cable news producer. They were exactly the kind of thoughtful, credentialed, institutionally connected Americans who, in any previous generation, would have been the people most capable of articulating what their country stood for. They had come to the room voluntarily, for the explicit purpose of finding common ground. They had agreed, before attending, not to be quoted by name — a precaution that speaks volumes. They already knew how badly things could go.

And the word that detonated the room was not reparations or abortion or immigration or any of the words Americans are supposed to fight about. It was not a policy. It was not a partisan position. It was the word for loving your country.

From where I sit — Toronto, just across the longest undefended border in the world — this scene is genuinely difficult to process. Not because Canadians are uniformly patriotic; we are, as a national character, somewhat allergic to flag-waving, somewhat suspicious of chest-thumping, considerably more comfortable with irony than anthem. Ask a Canadian to define patriotism and you will receive a long, careful, heavily qualified answer that arrives, eventually, at something like cautious affection with reservations. We are not a people who put flags on our trucks.

But “patriotism” is not a word that would clear a Canadian room. It would produce debate about what kind of patriotism, and on what terms, and whether the term extends to Quebec, and whether loving Canada requires a full reckoning with what was done to its Indigenous peoples, and whether hockey counts, and whether Tim Hortons still counts now that it is owned by a Brazilian-American holding company. It would produce the usual Canadian negotiation — meandering, inconclusive, and fundamentally good-natured. It would not produce a detonation.

The difference matters enormously. And the difference is this: Canada does not define itself by an idea the way America does. Canada is a country that emerged from negotiation and geography and political compromise and the gradual devolution of colonial authority — messy, incremental, largely untheorized. No one wrote a pamphlet called Common Sense about Canada. No one signed a declaration announcing that Canada self-evidently stood for anything in particular. Canada simply became, over time, a fact.

America is a country that began with a proposition. It announced itself to the world as an idea — specifically, as the idea that all men are created equal, endowed with inalienable rights, governed only by the consent of the governed. It did not merely claim a territory. It claimed a truth. And when the word for loving that truth becomes unsayable in a room of people assembled specifically to defend it, something has gone profoundly wrong at the level of the truth itself.

Declaration of Independence and Constitution — the founding texts of a nation built on a proposition.

II. How “Patriotism” Became a Grenade

The word did not always clear rooms. It is worth remembering how recently it didn’t.

For most of American history, a simplified patriotic narrative prevailed without serious institutional challenge. Appelbaum quotes an 1872 textbook explaining to young students that “Providence designed that on this continent should be seen an example of democratic government — government ‘of the people, for the people, by the people.’” Children were taught this. They largely believed it. The story was incomplete, frequently dishonest about its exclusions, regularly deployed to justify the unjustifiable, and occasionally grotesque in its cheerfulness about what had been done to the people the creed excluded. But it was a story, coherent enough to hold, and it held.

What broke it was not malice. It was, initially, scholarship — honest, overdue, and necessary scholarship. And then, decades later, politics feeding on the vacuum the scholarship had left behind.

In the mid-twentieth century, the dominant historians of America were largely the children of immigrants — many of them Jewish — who had found in America a refuge and wanted to understand its exceptionalism. Louis Hartz argued that Americans shared a common liberal tradition built around democracy, property, and individual liberty. Richard Hofstadter, Daniel Boorstin, and others in the consensus school held a flattering mirror up to the country. They wrote best-sellers. They advised prominent politicians. They shaped a generation’s understanding of what America was and why it was worth defending. Their story was partial. It glossed over race, class, and gender in ways that subsequent scholarship rightly demolished. But it was coherent, and it gave Americans a shared framework for understanding themselves.

Then, beginning in the 1960s and accelerating through the 1970s and 1980s, a new generation of scholars — women, people of colour, those whose stories the consensus school had never bothered to tell — entered the academy in larger numbers and began telling the history that had been omitted. The result was a tremendous boon for history. The explosion of scholarship on race, class, and gender widened our understanding enormously. The voiceless got voices. The forgotten got chroniclers. The full, brutal complexity of American history became, for the first time, academically available.

But — and this is the crucial but — the new historians were deeply suspicious of national narrative itself. Patriotic histories had long been used to buttress the oppressive systems they were now working to unmask. So they decentred the nation. They pursued the separate histories of various groups. They challenged facile assumptions. And they made, as Appelbaum notes, comparatively little effort to assemble the pieces into a comprehensible whole. Scholars largely stopped writing single-volume histories of the United States. The flattering mirror was smashed. No new mirror was built. The consensus school’s coherent story was replaced not with a better, more honest story, but with the studied conviction that any national story was inherently suspect.

The history wars then spilled out of the academy and into politics, and the politics made everything worse. In 1994, when the George H.W. Bush administration tried to develop common history standards for schools — standards that reflected the new, more honest scholarship — conservatives reviewed the guidelines and reacted with fury. Lynne Cheney, who had commissioned the work, denounced the standards for emphasizing America’s failings over its achievements. Rush Limbaugh said they aimed to teach students that America was “a rotten place.” The Senate voted to reject the standards, 99-1.

A decade later, when No Child Left Behind created a federal accountability framework for American education, the architects of the law quietly set history to the side. They tested English and mathematics. They did not test history — less because they didn’t care about it, as Appelbaum observes, than because everyone cared too much to agree on what should be tested. The result was that schools struggling to make the grade on tested subjects cut back on everything else. History took the worst hit.

Into the political vacuum poured the entrepreneurs of grievance, on both sides. The historians who had spent decades deconstructing patriotic myths looked up one day and discovered they had, in effect, ceded the task of telling the American story to Bill O’Reilly and Newt Gingrich. On the left, The New York Times’ 1619 Project argued that the American story began not in 1776 but in 1619, with the arrival of the first enslaved Africans — a reframing that produced important insights alongside serious historical errors, and that handed the right a political weapon of considerable utility. On the right, Trump’s 1776 Commission produced a counter-document so credulous and historically thin that it was repudiated by virtually every serious historian in the country. Between them, they did not produce a debate. They produced a war over whether the story could be told at all.

III. The Three Schools and the Empty Classroom

An American classroom — where the battle over the national story is ultimately lost or won.

The historian Johann Neem, who delivered the 2025 annual lecture to the American Historical Association on exactly this problem — and received furious responses for his trouble, including from one colleague who accused him of “retreat, if not outright capitulation” to the fell logic of nationalism — identifies three competing versions of the American story now in circulation. None is dominant. All are insufficient.

The first, which Neem calls the mainstream school, holds America as a work in progress: genuinely committed to its founding ideals, genuinely failing to live up to them, engaged in an ongoing and unfinished struggle to close the gap. This is still how most Americans think about their country, even if it has largely fallen from fashion in the academy and receives no political constituency on either side. It is the tradition of Lincoln’s “unfinished work,” of King’s “promissory note,” of Obama’s Selma speech. It requires holding two things simultaneously — the ideals are real, and the distance from them is also real — which turns out to be a considerably more demanding cognitive and moral task than either the post-American or hyper-American alternatives.

The second, which Neem calls the post-American school, holds that the United States was built on racism and genocide, is a settler-colonial nation founded in white supremacy, and is irredeemably illiberal and oppressive. Many scholars in this tradition actively seek to decentre the nation in their narratives. They find no common inspiration in the history — only a catalogue of sins requiring atonement. This school has genuine intellectual courage and has produced scholarship of real importance. It has also, as a practical matter, surrendered the American story to its worst interpreters.

The third, which Neem calls the hyper-American school, has been supercharged by the political right in reaction to the post-American turn. It casts America’s founding as essentially providential. Slavery was a deviation from the nation’s immutable founding truths, not a foundation of them. To reckon seriously with the darker chapters is, in this telling, to risk disenchanting the nation — and a disenchanted nation cannot defend itself. Trump’s 1776 Commission called for approaching American history “with reverence and love,” which is another way of saying: with your eyes closed.

What Neem finds most disturbing — and what I find most clarifying — is a strange convergence at the extremes. Both the far right and the far left have arrived, by entirely different routes, at the same conclusion: that the history of the United States has been defined, above all, by whiteness. The post-American school says the national story is a white supremacist story and must therefore be abandoned or radically decentred. The hyper-American school says the national story is a heritage American story and must therefore be protected from those who would dilute or critique it. The mainstream school — which says the story belongs to everyone who has ever fought to make the creed mean what it claims to mean — finds itself squeezed between two versions of the same racial essentialism.

The result in American classrooms is not a debate. It is an abandonment. The hours devoted to social studies in elementary schools have declined by a third — 76 minutes per week lost to the pressure of teaching to standardized tests in English and mathematics, the only subjects that count under No Child Left Behind’s accountability framework. Survey courses in American history are vanishing from universities. More than a third of high-school social-studies educators are coaching sports or teaching physical education — the football coach in the history classroom has become so common that students across the country joke that all their history teachers share a first name: Coach. These coaches have fewer years of experience, rely more heavily on textbooks and worksheets, and engage far less with primary sources than their non-athletic colleagues. Fourteen percent of American students are rated proficient in history on national assessments.

Fourteen percent.

A creedal nation whose citizens do not know its creed is not a nation. It is a flag looking for an argument.

IV. The Canadian Window

American and Canadian flags — two neighboring nations with very different relationships to the word “patriotism.”

I have been writing about American politics for fourteen months, and I want to be honest about something that the Appelbaum commission room scene forced me to articulate for the first time.

When I began this project in May 2025, I thought the primary story was institutional — the assault on courts, the capture of agencies, the degradation of norms, the systematic replacement of public servants with loyalists. Those stories are real and I have covered them at length. The Trump Accountability Project ran to forty-seven parts and four hundred thousand words examining the specific mechanisms of democratic backsliding. When Democracy Fights Back tracks the redistricting wars and voting rights erosion in real time. The Enemies List documents the retribution campaign against individuals who defied the consolidation of power. These are stories about machinery — the specific gears and levers by which a republic dismantles itself from the inside.

But a word cleared a room, and I found myself stopped.

Because it is not a story about institutions. It is a story about a word. And a word is a story. And a story — this is the thing I have been circling without landing on — is what holds a creedal nation together in a way that no institution, however robust, can substitute for. You can rebuild a court. You can restore an agency. You can rewrite a norm into law to make it harder to erode. You cannot legislate a shared narrative back into existence. You cannot mandate, by executive order or constitutional amendment, that a room full of distinguished Americans be able to say the word for loving their country without someone flinching.

Canada is not an instructive example of how to do this right. We have our own unfinished business, our own residential school history, our own convenient national myths that collapse on inspection. But we are, structurally, less vulnerable to this particular failure — because we never staked our national identity on an idea the way America did. Canadian patriotism, such as it is, does not require agreement on a proposition. It requires only a shared address and a willingness to negotiate. That is a much lower bar.

America set the bar extraordinarily high. It said: we are a nation defined by what we believe, not by who we are. Subscribe to the creed, and you belong. Any who subscribes can become a citizen. The Irishman who told President John Quincy Adams he planned soon “to become a native” — a joke Adams reportedly loved — was expressing something genuinely radical: that Americanness was not inherited but chosen. That it was available. That the idea was open.

That openness is what is now at risk. Not primarily because of any single policy or any single administration, though both matter. But because the story that explained the openness — that gave it historical weight and moral authority — has been abandoned by the institutions responsible for transmitting it, attacked by the political forces most invested in closing it, and left without serious defenders at precisely the moment it most needs them.

That is what I have been watching from across the border without quite naming it.

That is what this series is about.

V. What the Word Still Means

I want to end Part I not with the detonation but with what survived it.

Appelbaum did not retreat from the word. He thought of his grandfathers — one from above Sparta, one from what is now Belarus — and what it meant that a life combining their inheritances was possible here, in this particular country, and almost nowhere else. He thought of the liberties he had come to understand, through travel and comparison, that Americans genuinely enjoy, and for which genuine gratitude is an honest response. He thought of the ways the country fails to live up to its ideals — the distance between the creed and the practice that is as old as the creed itself. And having thought all of that, he said the word anyway.

That is the only form of patriotism adequate to a creedal nation. Not the unconditional loyalty of the naval hero Stephen Decatur — my country right or wrong — which is no different, morally, from a gangster’s code. The gangster, too, is loyal to his country, meaning his crew, right or wrong. And not the post-American refusal to use the word at all — which does not neutralize it but simply cedes it to the people doing the most damage with it, the people who have never met a complexity they couldn’t flatten into a bumper sticker.

The adequate patriotism is the one articulated by the Reconstruction-era Senator Carl Schurz, who understood that Decatur’s sentence was incomplete: My country right or wrong — if right, to be kept right; and if wrong, to be set right. That is a sentence that requires a story. It requires knowing enough history — the massacres and the movements, the exclusions and the expansions, the specific texture of the distance between the creed and the practice — to understand what “setting right” actually demands in this particular moment, for these particular failures. It is not available to the student whose history teacher was the football coach. It is not available to the scholar who has decided the national narrative is too compromised to be transmitted. It is not available to the citizen whose 250th birthday party features a UFC bout on the South Lawn and a statue garden in which Davy Crockett stands beside Julia Child beside Kobe Bryant, united by nothing except someone’s vague sense that they were famous and American.

Nikole Hannah-Jones, whose 1619 Project is one of the more contested contributions to this debate, discovered something unexpected while researching it. She had assumed that newly emancipated slaves, given the chance to leave the country that had so brutally wronged them, would have wanted to go. Instead she found them arguing, at the very moment they had finally gained rights and citizenship, that they could not abandon the only land they had ever known. They resolved to stay. To make the nation what it could and should have been. “I actually did find that really inspiring,” she told Appelbaum. “And I think that’s the first time I ever felt I could see a path to patriotism.”

The path runs through the full story. Not the flattering version that cleared the room in 1872, and not the prosecutorial version that cleared the room in 2019. The whole story — the breadth of the early franchise and the depth of the disenfranchisement, the genuine idealism of the Founders and the genuine atrocity of what they built on, the people who fought to close the gap between the creed and the practice and the people who fought to keep it open.

Jill Lepore’s These Truths — one attempt to tell the full American story in a single volume

That story is still available. People are hungry for it. When Jill Lepore published These Truths in 2018 — the first serious single-volume history of the United States by a prominent scholar in a generation — a woman wrote to tell her she had bought it to study for her citizenship exam and kept reading. “I really feel like I belong here now,” she wrote, “because I understand the whole journey that is the story of this land and this people and these ideas and this country.”

The word that cleared the room in 2019 is still the right word.

The question is whether enough Americans still know the story that makes it mean something.

That question is what the next nine parts of this series will attempt to answer.
