I have put off writing this part for five installments now, and I want to be honest about why, because the honesty is the whole point of the exercise. It is considerably easier to write about a Wall Street speculator’s ruined fortunes, a mismatched Victorian marriage, or a young cavalry officer’s improbable escapes than it is to write about the deaths of somewhere between eight hundred thousand and three million people in a famine that a British War Cabinet, chaired by the same man this series has spent five parts building sympathy for, was repeatedly warned about and repeatedly failed to answer with the urgency the warnings demanded. But this series opened with a promise not to write hagiography, and a promise like that is worthless if it only ever gets tested on the easy material.

Start with the episode that complicates the simplest version of the story, because it deserves to be told before the harder one. On April 13, 1919, troops under Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer fired without warning on a crowd of unarmed civilians gathered at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar, killing at least three hundred and seventy-nine people by the official British count and, by most Indian estimates, considerably more. When the massacre came before the House of Commons for debate on July 8, 1920, Winston Churchill — then Secretary of State for War, and one of the only senior figures in the government willing to say so plainly — condemned it as “an extraordinary event, a monstrous event,” and it was in significant part Churchill’s own speech that helped end Dyer’s career and shape the government’s formal repudiation of the massacre. This is worth stating clearly because it does not fit the version of Churchill that gets deployed by his harshest critics as a uniformly reactionary colonial administrator incapable of recognizing imperial atrocity when he saw it. On this occasion, at real political cost among his own party’s more reactionary wing, he saw it.

What Amritsar did not do was convert Churchill into a supporter of Indian self-government, and the following decade and a half make that plain enough that no honest accounting can soften it. Through the late 1920s and into the 1930s, as the British government moved, however slowly and incompletely, toward expanded Indian self-rule, Churchill positioned himself as the most prominent and most vocal opponent of the process within his own Conservative Party. He resigned from the Conservative shadow business committee in January 1931 specifically over the party leadership’s willingness to negotiate constitutional reform with Indian nationalist leaders, and he spent much of the following four years touring the country giving speeches against what became the Government of India Act of 1935 — legislation that expanded provincial self-government considerably short of full independence, and which Churchill nonetheless fought as though it were outright capitulation. His public description of Gandhi, delivered in a February 1931 speech, as a “seditious Middle Temple lawyer” — invoking, in the same breath, an image of Gandhi striding barefoot up the steps of the Viceroy’s palace to negotiate as an equal with the King-Emperor’s representative — was calculated, in its condescension, to shock, and it did, drawing criticism even from within his own party as needlessly inflammatory. This was not a passing rhetorical excess. It was the sustained public position of a senior British politician across the better part of a decade, deployed specifically to slow or prevent Indian constitutional advancement that the rest of his own party’s leadership had already concluded was inevitable and, on balance, wise to negotiate rather than resist.

Which brings the ledger to 1943, and to the episode this part cannot responsibly summarize in a single paragraph, however tempting the brevity would be. A catastrophic famine struck Bengal beginning in the spring of 1943, ultimately killing an estimated eight hundred thousand to three million people — the wide range itself a reflection of how contested the reckoning of this famine has remained among historians for eight decades — from starvation, malnutrition-related disease, and the social collapse that accompanied both. The famine’s proximate causes were genuinely complicated: a cyclone had struck Bengal in October 1942, damaging the rice crop; the fall of Burma to Japanese forces in early 1942 had cut off a significant source of rice imports Bengal had previously relied on; and a British “denial policy,” intended to keep boats and grain stores out of Japanese hands in the event of an invasion that never came, stripped rural Bengal of transport capacity precisely when moving food into famine-stricken districts mattered most. None of this was manufactured from nothing by a single man’s malice, and readers who encounter only the most polemical accounts of this famine, which do sometimes flatten it into a simple story of deliberate genocide, are not being given the full historical record either.

But the causes only explain the famine’s origin. They do not explain the War Cabinet’s response once the scale of the disaster was undeniable, and it is the response — not the weather, not the fall of Burma — that belongs most directly on this series’ ledger.

It is worth being specific about the denial policy in particular, because its mechanics illustrate exactly how a defensive military precaution turned into a civilian catastrophe. Fearing a Japanese invasion of eastern India following the fall of Rangoon, British and Indian authorities in 1942 ordered the systematic removal or destruction of tens of thousands of boats along the Bengal coast and river deltas — vessels that were, for millions of rural Bengalis, not a wartime luxury but the basic infrastructure of local rice distribution, fishing, and trade. The policy succeeded in denying the boats to a Japanese invasion that, in the event, never came, and simultaneously stripped rural Bengal of the transport capacity that would have let surplus rice move from less-affected districts into famine-stricken ones once the crisis began the following year. Separately, the Bengal Chamber of Commerce — an organization dominated by British trading firms — worked with the provincial government to implement a “Foodstuffs Scheme” that guaranteed preferential food access to workers in war industries, the armed forces, and the civil service, formalizing a hierarchy of who would eat and who would not that ran along lines uncomfortably close to imperial administration’s own priorities. None of this required a single deliberate decision to let Bengalis starve. It required a series of individually defensible wartime choices, made by administrators operating at every level from Calcutta to London, that collectively produced exactly that outcome — which is, in its own way, a more damning indictment of imperial administration than a single villain would be, because it means the machinery worked as designed and the design itself was the problem.

The historiographical argument over how much of this belongs specifically to Churchill, rather than to the broader colonial administrative apparatus he sat atop, remains genuinely contested among serious scholars rather than settled in either direction. Madhusree Mukerjee’s research, drawing on War Cabinet papers, argues that London’s wartime shipping priorities and Churchill’s personal indifference were decisive factors in turning a difficult supply situation into a catastrophic death toll. The economist Amartya Sen, who witnessed the famine himself as a nine-year-old child in Bengal, has argued in his own scholarly work that the famine was fundamentally an “entitlement failure” — a collapse in the rural poor’s purchasing power relative to rice prices, driven by wartime inflation and speculation, rather than a simple absence of food in the region overall — a framing that implicates colonial economic policy broadly without resting the causal weight on any single Cabinet minister’s personal animus. Other historians, writing in Churchill’s defense, emphasize the genuine constraints of a global shipping shortage in 1943, with Allied vessels committed to the North African and Italian campaigns and under continuous threat from Axis submarines in the Indian Ocean, and point to the eventual million-ton shipment as evidence that the government responded once it grasped the scale of the crisis, however belatedly. The Cabinet, chaired by Churchill, first formally grappled with the famine’s severity at a meeting on August 4, 1943, and agreed to send 150,000 tons of Iraqi barley and Australian wheat — a decision made only after months of increasingly urgent telegrams from Calcutta had already gone past the point where such a shipment could arrive in time to prevent the famine’s deadliest months. Leopold Amery, Churchill’s own Secretary of State for India and by most accounts a considerably more sympathetic voice on Indian affairs than his prime minister, recorded in his diary an exchange from September 1942 in which an exasperated Churchill told him: “I hate Indians. They are a beastly people with a beastly religion.” Amery’s diary records a similar outburst the following year, in the context of a Cabinet discussion of famine relief, in which Churchill complained about Indians “breeding like rabbits” and asked why food should be diverted to them while the war itself still needed winning. Amery, no radical by any measure, was sufficiently disturbed by his prime minister’s fixation on the subject that he privately compared Churchill’s attitude toward India to Hitler’s, and wondered in his own diary whether Churchill was, on this one subject, “really quite sane.”

It would be its own kind of dishonesty to treat Amery’s diary entries as the whole of the historical record, and serious historians on both sides of this argument have said as much. Amery was recording private remarks secondhand, in the heat of his own frustration with a prime minister he frequently found maddening on Indian questions specifically; some of the most inflammatory phrasing attributed to Churchill exists only in Amery’s account, filtered through Amery’s own considerable capacity for exasperated hyperbole. And the balance of the documentary record shows that once the scale of the disaster was undeniable, the Cabinet did move: over a million additional tons of grain reached Bengal between August 1943 and the end of 1944, much of it on Australian ships diverted specifically for the purpose, and Churchill personally pressed, in a September 1943 message, that “every effort must be made” to relieve the famine — the same man, in the same year, capable of both the shipment and the remark about rabbits. It was also Churchill who appointed Lord Wavell as Viceroy in October 1943, specifically because Wavell’s military logistics background made him likely to prioritize the relief effort more aggressively than his predecessor had, and Wavell’s own insistent telegrams from Delhi over the following year were instrumental in finally securing the shipping that reached Bengal before 1944 ended.

Both of these things are true simultaneously, and a series built around the word “inheritance” rather than “hagiography” has an obligation to hold them in the same hand rather than choosing whichever one flatters the argument it would rather make. Churchill said things about Indians, recorded by his own Secretary of State for India, that are indefensible by any modern standard and were recognized as excessive even by contemporaries inclined to be sympathetic to him. The Cabinet he chaired also, eventually, under considerable external pressure and against the priority he had initially assigned the problem, moved meaningful tonnage of grain to a starving province, at real cost to Mediterranean and European theater logistics he plainly valued more highly. Historians who specialize in this period continue to disagree, sometimes sharply, about how to weigh a wartime leader’s documented racial contempt against the eventual, if belated, relief effort his government did organize — and I am not going to resolve in a single Substack essay a debate that professional historians of the British Empire have not resolved across eight decades of archival research. What I can do is refuse to let this series summarize the famine in the single sympathetic sentence that Scaramucci’s essay, and pieces like it, generally allow it: as an unfortunate wartime footnote, mentioned nowhere at all, in an account otherwise entirely occupied with the special relationship’s compounding returns.

There is a reason this ledger entry belongs specifically in a series about inheritance rather than in a straightforward biography, and it is this: none of what happened in Bengal in 1943 happened to the Churchill family. It happened to Bengal, at the family’s — and the empire’s — hands, and the family inherited none of its direct cost. What the family did inherit, and what this series has to reckon with going forward, is a legacy in which the same man who could write “every effort must be made” about famine relief in September 1943 could also, by his own Secretary of State’s account, treat the people dying as an irritant competing with the war for his attention. Randolph annexed Burma in 1885 with a single clause in a single decision. His son, six decades later, inherited an empire built on exactly that kind of clause — territories acquired, administered, and in this instance catastrophically under-prioritized by men operating at a physical and emotional remove from the consequences their signatures and Cabinet votes actually produced. The dividend Scaramucci’s essay describes, landing in the accounts of people who never read a word Churchill wrote, was real. It was also, this part of the ledger insists on recording, financed in part by a province that received considerably less of Churchill’s urgency than the dividend’s beneficiaries ever have.

Part VII turns to Lend-Lease itself — the term sheet Scaramucci’s essay treats as the series’ central achievement — and to a Canadian diplomat named Hugh Keenleyside, who happened to be standing considerably closer to that particular negotiation than most accounts of it ever mention.
