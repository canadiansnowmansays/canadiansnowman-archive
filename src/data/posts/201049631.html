CanadianSnowmanSays | Sunday Edition

The FIFA World Cup opened Thursday in Mexico City, and I want to say, as a Canadian, that I am very excited.

I am excited because Canada is a co-host. I am excited because Toronto and Vancouver will welcome the world. I am excited because for thirty-nine days, the planet’s most-watched sporting event will unfold across sixteen cities in three countries that are, at the time of writing, in an active trade war with each other, one of which recently bombed a third participant, whose players are being required to enter and leave American soil on the same day as their matches, like very athletic day-trippers.

This has not happened before in the history of the tournament. It may not happen again. It is, in its way, a marvel.

The 2026 World Cup was awarded jointly to the United States, Canada, and Mexico in 2017, back when the three countries shared something called the North American Free Trade Agreement and relations among them were, if not warm, at least not characterized by 25% tariffs on each other’s goods, public suggestions that Canada should become the 51st state, and a running argument about whether the Mexican border is a threat or a venue. Much has changed since 2017. The tournament schedule, unfortunately, has not.

And so we arrive at the singular spectacle of a World Cup co-hosted by a country that has spent the better part of eighteen months economically battering its two partners. Seventy-two of the 104 matches will be played in the United States. Thirteen in Mexico. Thirteen in Canada. The opening match was at the Azteca Stadium in Mexico City — the same Mexico whose goods are taxed at the American border, whose president has been lectured about fentanyl and sovereignty, and whose people have been characterized in ways that did not make the official FIFA press materials. The final will be at MetLife Stadium in New Jersey on July 19th. The three host nations have pledged to work together. They are also currently in a trade dispute of the kind that, in previous eras, tended to precede the cancellation of joint ventures rather than the hosting of them.

President Trump, when asked how he felt about sharing hosting duties with Canada and Mexico given the trade situation, offered what may be the most clarifying statement of his political career.

“I think it’s going to make it more exciting,” he said. “Tension is a good thing. It makes it much more exciting.”

I have read this several times. I made tea. I read it again.

The man who imposed 25% tariffs on Canadian and Mexican goods, who has referred to Canada as an economic burden and suggested its prime minister was the governor of an American state that does not exist, who has spent eighteen months treating his nearest neighbours as adversaries — that man genuinely believes that his ongoing economic hostilities constitute a feature of this tournament rather than an extraordinary diplomatic embarrassment. FIFA President Gianni Infantino was standing beside him when he said it. Infantino’s expression, in the photographs, is the expression of a man who has learned to hear only what is useful to him.

FIFA’s official mantra, for the record, is that football unites the world. One notes this without comment and moves on.

But the trade war, as it turns out, is the least complicated of this tournament’s geopolitical complications. That distinction belongs to Iran.

Iran qualified for the 2026 World Cup. This is their fourth consecutive qualification, a legitimate achievement in the fiercely competitive Asian football confederation. Their group matches — against New Zealand, Belgium, and Egypt — are scheduled in Los Angeles and Seattle. Two American cities. In a tournament co-hosted by the United States. Which, since February 28th of this year, has been at war with Iran.

The United States and Israel began their military campaign against Iran on February 28th. By the time the World Cup opened this week, 1,255 Iranians had been killed and more than 12,000 wounded. Iran has struck back against American military bases in the region. Ayatollah Khamenei was assassinated in the opening days of the conflict. And Iran’s football team — representing a nation in active armed conflict with one of the host countries — was, until days before the tournament opened, uncertain whether it could even participate.

Iran’s Football Federation chief Mehdi Taj was among those initially denied a visa. The federation’s response was measured but pointed: this was, they said, “political interference in sport in its worst form.” Iran boycotted the World Cup draw in Washington last December after the US denied visas to its delegation — an act of protest that received less attention than it deserved, given that it was a co-host nation refusing to attend the organizational meeting for the tournament it was co-hosting, because a different co-host nation would not let its representatives in the building.

Iran’s players arriving at a stadium near Los Angeles on Sunday June 14 .Credit...Andre Penner/Associated Press

The resolution arrived just days before the opening whistle. Players received visas. Staff members did not. The Iranian squad relocated its preparation base from Antalya, Turkey, to Tijuana, Mexico — the city across the border from San Diego, chosen precisely because it allows the team to enter the United States by land on match days and return the same evening. “We can enter in the morning and we must leave the same day,” Iran’s ambassador to Mexico told reporters. The team that qualified for the World Cup is sleeping in Tijuana and commuting to Los Angeles to play.

This is, by any measure, an unusual arrangement for a sporting tournament whose host country has pledged to welcome the world.

From Canada — and here I want to speak briefly but directly as a co-host — this is not the welcome we intended. Toronto and Vancouver have spent hundreds of millions of dollars preparing their stadiums and infrastructure. Canadian taxpayers have absorbed enormous costs to be part of a tournament that was, in conception, a celebration of North American partnership and global inclusion. What they are co-hosting, instead, is a tournament that cannot get one of its qualified teams into a hotel room, that has required a country with which it is at war to base its squad in a border town and day-trip to matches, and that prompted the American president chairing its organizing task force to describe the trade war with his co-hosts as an exciting feature rather than a problem to be managed.

“Tension is a good thing,” he said. One wonders what FIFA’s insurance premiums look like this year.

There is something almost poignant about the tournament itself — the football, the pure spectacle of forty-eight nations gathered on a continent that has so thoroughly complicated their arrival. The sport is genuine. The athleticism is extraordinary. The fans who have made their way to stadiums across three countries, navigating visa regimes and border policies and the general ambient chaos of an administration that treats geopolitics as entertainment, deserve every moment of what they came for. The game itself has not changed. It remains, as it has always been, the beautiful game.

The host, meanwhile, remains a work in progress.

Canada will cheer its team — and it will, with genuine pride, because Canada qualifying for a World Cup it is co-hosting is a genuine national achievement. Mexico will fill the Azteca and remind the world that it knows how to host a football tournament, having done so in 1970 and 1986 with considerably less geopolitical interference. The United States will fill MetLife Stadium for the final and the President will attend several matches and call them tremendous and the most exciting in history, and he will not, in any of those moments, appear to notice the complications his administration created.

Though perhaps that is the point. “Tension is a good thing.” A man who has built a career on conflict, who mistakes friction for energy and hostility for dynamism, chairing the task force for the world’s great festival of unity. The beautiful game played out against the most complicated diplomatic backdrop in World Cup history.

The opening whistle has blown. Eighty thousand people were in the Azteca. Billions watched.

The world has come to North America.

North America, characteristically, could not quite agree on the terms.

The 2026 FIFA World Cup runs June 11 to July 19, across sixteen host cities in the United States, Canada, and Mexico. Canada plays its first match June 12 in Toronto
