For most of this summer, the Houthi advance has been a story about geography — a port, a strait, an island, a province. On Saturday it became a story about the Saudi capital itself. As Vivian Nereim, Ismaeel Naar and Pranav Baskar reported for The New York Times, Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree said the group had fired a barrage of ballistic and cruise missiles and drones at “sensitive sites” in Riyadh and at Saudi Aramco facilities in Yanbu, the Red Sea terminal already familiar to this series as the western end of the East-West pipeline. Two emergency alerts sounded overnight warning of a “hostile aerial threat.” Residents in western Riyadh, home to the diplomatic quarter, reported explosions. King Khalid International Airport saw major disruption; Reuters photographed smoke rising nearby. Early-warning systems activated in nine other cities. Saudi Arabia has confirmed none of it. There were no reported casualties, and by the time of filing, Riyadh had not officially acknowledged it had been hit at all.

Farea Al-Muslimi of Chatham House, a recurring voice in this series since the Mokha advance, called it plainly: a “huge” escalation, and evidence the Houthis “are not going to stop anywhere” while battlefield momentum runs their way.

The Ladder to Riyadh

Saturday did not arrive out of nowhere. It was the fifth rung of a ladder this series can now reconstruct in full. Fighting reignited in July after the Houthis accused Saudi Arabia of bombing Sanaa’s airport to stop an Iranian aircraft from landing — the previously missing origin point for a reescalation this series had tracked only from its effects. On Friday, the Houthis claimed Saudi Arabia had attempted a “criminal” attack on Sanaa itself, which they said they’d foiled without elaborating on how. On Tuesday, Saudi air defences intercepted a Houthi drone approaching Mecca — the second such claim in this war’s short history — which Houthi leader Abdul-Malik al-Houthi dismissed outright as “slander and fabrication.” On Wednesday, the Houthis reiterated their claim to have shot down a Saudi F-15 over Marib province with a locally made missile. By Saturday, missiles had reached the capital. Riyadh alerts had not sounded since tensions first flared in July — the strikes had, until now, stayed concentrated on Saudi Arabia’s southern cities. Saturday broke that pattern, and it did so weeks before Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman is scheduled to host the Future Investment Initiative in the capital, a gathering of the world’s biggest financiers and billionaires.

Saree frames the campaign in explicit reciprocal terms: a policy of “escalation for escalation” until Saudi Arabia halts what the Houthis call aggression in Yemen and lifts restrictions on Yemeni airspace. Saudi Arabia, notably, has not acknowledged conducting any recent airstrikes of its own — officially, it attributes that fighting to the Yemeni government it backs, a distinction this series has flagged before and one worth watching as the claims and counterclaims accumulate.

The World Responds, Carefully

The Mecca claim in particular drew a response this series hadn’t yet logged: as Ismaeel Naar, Hari Raj, Sanjana Varghese and Aric Toler reported for The New York Times, the Organization of Islamic Cooperation, a 57-member bloc that considers itself the collective voice of the Muslim world, condemned both the alleged strike near Islam’s holiest site and the Houthis’ wider campaign against Saudi Arabia, warning that such attacks provoke the sentiments of Muslims everywhere. The stakes behind that language are not abstract: more than 1.7 million pilgrims, most from overseas, visited Mecca this year for the hajj. Maj. Gen. Turki Al-Maliki, spokesman for the Saudi-led coalition, called pilgrim security a “red line.” The United Nations Security Council held an emergency session that same week and called for de-escalation and renewed dialogue — a call that, as this series has now watched fail twice already this month, carries less weight each time it’s repeated. Yemen’s UN ambassador, Abdullah al-Saadi, offered the sharpest framing to come out of that session: the Houthis, he said, are using their grip on Bab al-Mandab as an extension of the same threat-and-blackmail equation already on display at Hormuz — two chokepoints now serving the same coercive purpose in different oceans.

The Yanbu Question

That the Houthis named Yanbu specifically, rather than describing a generic strike on “oil facilities,” matters more than it might first appear. Yanbu is the western terminus of the East-West pipeline this series has followed since the Iraqi drone strike on its pumping station earlier this month — the very route Saudi Arabia built to move oil around Iran’s closure of Hormuz. As Lisa Friedman reported for The New York Times, satellite images from Sept. 10 and 11 already showed damage at two separate pumping stations along that line, not one, and the stakes of a prolonged shutdown are larger than this series had previously quantified: Kpler estimates the world could lose 120 million barrels of crude if the pipeline stays offline a full month, keeping some 4 percent of global oil supply from reaching markets. Goldman Sachs has warned prices could climb as high as $120 a barrel if attacks on both the Gulf and the Red Sea continue. Amena Bakr, Kpler’s head of Middle East and OPEC+ insights, called the compounding picture — two major waterways obstructed, active attacks on multiple fronts, no diplomatic talks underway — simply “unprecedented,” a disastrous situation by any measure of energy security.

Not every analyst shares that alarm. Robin Brooks of the Brookings Institution rejected “nightmare scenario” framing, pointing to Saudi Arabia’s own repair record: a 2019 Houthi strike on Aramco facilities was fixed within days, and an April strike on this same pipeline was fully restored within a week. U.S. Energy Secretary Chris Wright told CNBC he expects only “a brief and temporary interruption.” Bakr herself estimated five to six weeks for full repair, though partial restoration could come sooner. The two readings aren’t strictly contradictory — repair speed and strategic vulnerability are different questions — but they do describe a Saudi cushion thinner than its headline numbers suggest: Yanbu’s storage terminal can hold roughly 24 million barrels, yet Rystad Energy estimates it is actually sitting on only three to six days’ worth of crude at any given time. A group that has now claimed strikes on both the pipeline’s midpoint and its western terminus in the same fortnight is testing exactly that margin.

A group that has already shown it can hit a pipeline mid-route, and separately claims it can now reach the capital itself, is advertising that it can threaten both ends of Saudi Arabia’s last working export corridor simultaneously. Whether or not Saturday’s strike on Yanbu caused real damage — and as of filing, nothing has been independently confirmed — the claim alone does real work: it tells markets and Gulf officials alike that the toll-booth logic this series has tracked since Part XCVII no longer stops at the straits. It now reaches inland, to the infrastructure straits were supposed to be a workaround for.

Deciding Mode

The Riyadh strikes landed just as a New York Times investigation by Helene Cooper, Eric Schmitt, Jonathan Swan and Maggie Haberman revealed how close the United States came to entering this fight directly — and how quickly Trump pulled back. For two weeks the question had bedeviled him: whether to yield to Saudi pleas and order U.S. strikes on Houthi targets in Yemen, opening a second front in a war he started but still cannot close. Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman called the president Thursday to implore him, not for the first time, to help blunt the Houthi advance. Trump had told advisers the day before that he opposed strikes. After the call, he reversed course and ordered the Pentagon to prepare an air campaign. By Sunday, he had reversed again: no U.S. strikes, at least for now. The reversal came, according to two people briefed on the operation, after commanders had already approved target lists and troops were loading bombs onto the warplanes selected for the mission — a strike genuinely, mechanically underway, called off at nearly the last possible moment.

Trump told Fox News’s Trey Yingst on Sunday that he was in “deciding mode” on the broader Iran war, and noted, accurately, that the Houthis have so far kept their word not to fire on American ships. That fact is precisely his dilemma: the group has spared the United States while escalating relentlessly against the ally Washington most needs cooperation from. Most of his inner circle opposed joining the fight outright, wary of stretching a military already strained by the Iran war itself. A senior administration official would only say that the U.S. has a defence agreement with Saudi Arabia and will always defend its allies — a line that commits to nothing while closing off nothing either.

Saudi Arabia has leverage of its own and has used it before. In May, Riyadh effectively vetoed the administration’s plan to escort commercial oil tankers through Hormuz, simply by denying the U.S. military use of Saudi airspace and air bases. That episode sits quietly behind Thursday’s phone call: a president who needs Gulf cooperation for one war, being asked to open a second one he isn’t sure he can win, by an ally who has already shown he can withhold cooperation on the first. Some U.S. military officials reportedly argue exactly this — that Washington needs Saudi help against Iran badly enough that it cannot afford to keep refusing Riyadh’s own requests indefinitely, whatever the Houthis do or don’t do to American ships in the meantime. That is the trap Trump’s Sunday reversal has, for now, only postponed rather than escaped: the underlying leverage problem does not go away because one wave of airstrikes was called off with the bombs already loaded.

The Frankenstein Coalition

Any U.S. air campaign was reportedly planned to run alongside a new Yemeni government ground offensive — the same forces that have melted away at first contact with the Houthis all summer, per the coordination failures this series has already traced back to December’s Saudi-UAE rupture. Gregory Johnsen of the Arab Gulf States Institute supplies the sharpest description yet of why that force keeps failing: it is a “Frankenstein of all the leftover parts in Yemen that aren’t the Houthis,” factions with such divergent goals that they spend more time fighting each other than the militia they’re nominally united against.

We’ve Done This Before

Current and former U.S. officials interviewed for the Times piece were openly skeptical a renewed bombing campaign would work, and the record backs them. In spring 2025, the U.S. bombed Houthi targets for more than fifty days, striking over a thousand sites — radar, air defences, missile launch areas, a drone production hub near Sanaa — burning through roughly a billion dollars in munitions in the first month alone. Two $67 million F/A-18s were lost overboard from the carrier tasked with the campaign. The Houthis shot down several American drones and kept firing at naval shipping throughout, having withstood nearly eight weeks of continuous American bombardment without dropping their core demands. Trump ended it abruptly with a ceasefire, claiming the group had simply given its word to stop targeting ships. They mostly did — but in the time since, officials say, the Houthis have rebuilt their arsenal and are now using artificial intelligence to design new missiles, making them steadily less dependent on Iranian supply lines than they were the last time Washington tried to bomb them into submission. A second campaign would very likely end that restraint toward American ships entirely, one official warned — and could destabilize Bab al-Mandab on top of an already unstable Hormuz, doubling rather than easing the strain on global energy transit this series has tracked since Part XCVII. The math the Pentagon would be signing up for is not encouraging: a campaign that failed to dislodge the Houthis at full intensity in 2025 is being weighed now as a supplement to a military already stretched thin by the separate war against Iran, run by an air force and navy that would be fighting on both fronts at once rather than one.

Marib and Taiz, Still Contested

As Ismaeel Naar reported for The New York Times, the ground war that would underwrite any air campaign remains genuinely undecided in the two provinces that matter most. Marib, roughly seventy-five miles east of Sanaa, has eluded the Houthis since 2014 and held even against their last major push in 2021; Ahmed Nagi of the International Crisis Group calls its fall the threshold that would put Houthi forces within reach of the Saudi border itself, something he says Riyadh would treat as an unacceptable threat to its own security. Marib is also home to much of Yemen’s oil and gas production, which means its loss would strike at the internationally recognized government’s finances and its claim to legitimacy in the same stroke — a government that already looks, per multiple accounts this series has gathered, closer to collapse than to counteroffensive. April Longley Alley of the Washington Institute, offering the clearest net assessment available, says the Houthis currently have the wind at their back while government forces remain fragmented and demoralized after the west-coast losses. Taiz, meanwhile, is under a slower siege aimed at severing its last connecting highway to the government-held south — a second chokepoint fight nested inside the larger one, and, per Nagi, a battle whose outcome will matter strategically to both sides regardless of who eventually wins Marib.

Both fronts sit upstream of the question Trump is actually deciding. A ground offensive that cannot hold Marib or break the siege of Taiz is not a force any American air campaign could meaningfully rescue — it would simply be the United States lending its air power to a coalition that, per Johnsen’s description above, cannot agree on what it is fighting for in the first place. That is very likely part of what kept Trump from pulling the trigger on Sunday, whatever the official reasoning offered for the reversal.

By the Numbers

Two straits, one capital now in range. More than 700 Saudi airstrikes claimed by Houthi accounting since July; at least one F-15 claimed downed; zero confirmed casualties from Saturday’s Riyadh barrage, and zero confirmation from Riyadh that it happened at all. One air campaign, in 2025, cost roughly a billion dollars a month and ended in a ceasefire the Houthis mostly honoured — until they didn’t have to anymore. Senator Kennedy of Louisiana remains the lone Senate Republican still willing to say so publicly. Narges Mohammadi remains in Evin Prison, a continent away from a war that keeps finding new capitals to reach.
