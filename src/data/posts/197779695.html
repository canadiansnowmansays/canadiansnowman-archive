A five-part series on the Trump-Xi summit in Beijing, May 2026

The stage was set. The costumes were magnificent. The plot, as always, was beside the point

I have walked through Tiananmen Square. I have walked through the Forbidden City. I have walked the Great Wall. And I can tell you that nothing in those experiences prepares you for the scale of what China puts on when it wants to impress a visitor. The buildings are not built for human beings. They are built for the idea of China — for continuity, for permanence, for the proposition that whoever walks through them is temporary, and the state is not.

On Thursday, May 14, 2026, an American president walked through Beijing again. The first to do so in nearly a decade. He brought with him three cabinet secretaries, a phalanx of corporate chieftains, his middle son, his daughter-in-law, a Fox News host, and a film producer who has faced allegations of sexual misconduct. He did not bring his wife. China noticed. China notices everything.

Xi Jinping met him at the base of the Great Hall of the People (Live Updates: Xi Warns Trump on Taiwan at Beijing Summit). They shook hands. A military band played “The Star-Spangled Banner.” A 21-gun salute echoed across the empty square. Hundreds of children in matching outfits waved American and Chinese flags with the synchronized enthusiasm of young people who have been practising since Tuesday. Donald Trump stopped to applaud them. Xi Jinping gave a short wave and kept walking (The Body Language of the Trump-Xi Summit Mirrored Their Styles).

The Beijing Opera had entered its final act.

One side brought a trade mission. The other brought a state.

I. The Entourage

The delegations told the story before anyone spoke a word.

Trump brought more than thirty people, including Tim Cook of Apple, Elon Musk of Tesla, Jensen Huang of Nvidia, the CEOs of Boeing, Cargill, Goldman Sachs, BlackRock, Qualcomm, Micron, Mastercard, Visa, and Citi. He told Xi, in his opening remarks, that he had invited “the top 30 in the world” and “every single one of them said yes.” He added, with characteristic precision, “I didn’t want the second or the third in the company. I wanted only the top.” Fourteen showed up at the Great Hall. Trump claimed thirty.

Xi brought his government. Cai Qi, his chief of staff. Wang Yi, his foreign policy chief. He Lifeng, his vice premier for economic policy. Dong Jun, his defence minister. The commerce minister, the finance minister, the top economic planner. No corporate chieftains. No BYD. No DeepSeek. No CATL. David Sanger of The New York Times, who has covered five presidents inside the Great Hall (Trump Was Flattering, Xi Was Resolute. The Difference Spoke Volumes), noted the absence with the precision of a man who has been counting chairs for decades: “Mr. Xi did not bring an equivalent group.”

The reason, of course, is that Xi doesn’t need to. In a party-state, the distinction between government and commerce is decorative. Xi is the CEO. The American side advertised the separation by lining up its business leaders like a chorus line on the steps of the Great Hall. The Chinese side didn’t need to. They were already inside the building.

Musk, for his part, brought his six-year-old son, X Æ A-XII, who walked through the Great Hall of the People in a Chinese silk vest (Elon Musk Returns to Trump’s Side in Beijing). When Musk emerged from the first meeting, a reporter asked what had been accomplished. “Many good things,” he said. Tim Cook flashed a peace sign. Jensen Huang gave a thumbs up. These are the men who run American capitalism, and their reviews of a two-hour meeting with the leader of their largest competitor were indistinguishable from a restaurant rating on Yelp.

Eric Trump was also there. He shook Xi’s hand (A Closer Look at Who’s in the U.S. Delegation to China). The Trump Organization issued a statement explaining that Eric was attending “in a personal capacity as a supportive son” and “does not have business ventures in China nor plans on doing business in China.” He does, however, own dozens of Chinese trademarks covering potential hotels, bars, and spas. The parallels to Hunter Biden’s 2013 trip to China with his father — a comparison Trump himself once weaponized — went unmentioned by the White House.

And Melania Trump, who had toured the Forbidden City with Xi and Peng Liyuan in 2017 in one of the most photographed moments of the first term, did not come. No explanation was offered. In Chinese diplomatic culture, where spousal participation signals the seriousness of the visit, her absence left Peng Liyuan without a counterpart. One side choreographed every detail. The other forgot the guest list.

Isn't it great the kids tagged along.

II. The Philosopher King

If Trump came to sell, Xi came to lecture.

In his opening remarks, the Chinese leader invoked the Thucydides Trap (Xi Warned of the ‘Thucydides Trap.’ What Is It?) — the proposition, popularized by Harvard’s Graham Allison, that when a rising power threatens a status-quo power, war tends to follow. “Can China and the United States overcome the ‘Thucydides Trap,’” Xi asked, “and establish a new paradigm for relations between great powers?” It was not a question. It was a thesis statement, delivered by a man who had clearly done the reading, to a man who almost certainly had not.

Xi then invoked “great changes unseen in a century” — a phrase with roots in an 1872 Qing dynasty memorandum about China’s failure to modernize against foreign powers, the failure that produced the century of humiliation. As David Pierson of The New York Times noted (How Xi Jinping Deals with Foreign Leaders in Private), Xi has turned the phrase into a battle cry: a call for China to seize the technological moment and edge out the United States while Trump retreats from global leadership. The phrase was caught on a hot mic in Moscow in 2023 as Xi told Putin: “We are the ones driving these changes together.”

At the Temple of Heaven that afternoon, Xi continued the seminar. He told Trump that ancient Chinese rulers held sacrificial ceremonies at the 600-year-old site to pray for national prosperity and peace, and then added — with the subtlety of a man who controls the curriculum — that “the Communist Party of China inherits and develops this people-centered philosophy of Chinese civilization.” He was drawing a direct line from the Ming emperors to himself. The state endures. The visitors do not.

Trump, for his part, told reporters afterward: “Great. Great place. Incredible. China’s beautiful.”

He declined to answer questions about Taiwan.

The Temple of Heaven, Beijing. Xi lectured. Trump listened. The building has been standing since 1420

III. The Warning

The reviews were barely in (Flags, Flattery and a Blunt Warning on Trump’s First Day in China) before Xi delivered the line that will define this summit.

“The Taiwan issue is the most critical issue in China-U.S. relations,” he said, according to the Xinhua readout. “Handled well, the two countries can maintain stability. If handled poorly, the two countries will collide or even clash, putting the entire U.S.-China relationship in an extremely dangerous situation.”

This was not diplomatic boilerplate. This was a threat, delivered on Day 1, in the first round of talks, inside the Great Hall of the People, while the children outside were still holding their flags. Xi told Trump in February, by phone, to handle Taiwan arms sales with “extreme caution.” The Trump administration responded by delaying a $13 billion weapons package to avoid upsetting Xi before the summit. Taiwan’s legislature, meanwhile, had just approved $25 billion in special military funding. The island is arming itself because it does not trust either of the men in the Great Hall to protect it.

The linguistic knife-edge matters (Here’s Why Taiwan Is Watching What Trump Says in Beijing). American presidents have long said they “do not support” Taiwanese independence. Some Chinese policy advisers — including Henry Huiyao Wang of the Center for China and Globalization — hope Trump will say he “opposes” it. The difference between “do not support” and “oppose” is a single verb. As Nicholas Burns, Biden’s ambassador to Beijing, told Foreign Affairs: shifting to “oppose” would “put all the onus on Taiwan as being the responsible party in this conflict, which is not the case.” It would send shock waves through Taipei and every allied capital in the region.

Trump said nothing. He was asked about Taiwan. He declined to answer. In the TACO framework, silence after a threat is accommodation. The cycle completes not with a word but with the absence of one.

Taiwan’s government responded within hours. “China’s military threats are the sole cause” of instability in the Taiwan Strait, said spokeswoman Michelle Lee, adding that Taipei had maintained close contact with Washington and was “deeply grateful” for longstanding U.S. support. The gratitude had the quality of a prayer.

Tiananmen Gate, Beijing. The portrait has not changed since 1949. The balance of power has.

IV. The Duelling Readouts

The most revealing moment of Day 1 was not spoken aloud. It was written.

The White House released a summary of the two-hour meeting. It mentioned fentanyl, market access for American companies, Chinese investment in the United States, and purchases of U.S. agricultural products. It said both sides agreed that the Strait of Hormuz must remain open and that Iran can never have a nuclear weapon. It did not mention Taiwan, rare earths, or China’s nuclear buildup.

The Chinese readout, from Xinhua, mentioned Taiwan prominently. It discussed the Thucydides Trap, “strategic stability,” and the need for the two countries to be “partners rather than adversaries.” It said the Middle East was discussed. It did not mention Iran’s nuclear programme. It did not mention the Strait of Hormuz.

Ryan Hass, a Brookings scholar and former China director on the National Security Council, wrote on social media: “The two readouts, when read side-by-side, describe almost two different meetings.”

Each side reported only the meeting it wanted to have had. The Americans reported a trade summit. The Chinese reported a geopolitical negotiation. Both versions are, in their way, accurate. But only one side got to set the agenda, choose the venue, design the ceremony, select the landmarks, lecture on philosophy, deliver a threat about Taiwan, and then omit the other side’s priorities from the official record. That side was not the United States.

Rush Doshi of Georgetown identified the deeper play. Xi’s new phrase — “a constructive China-U.S. relationship of strategic stability” — is an attempt to lock in the current détente as the baseline, not just for Trump but for whoever comes after. Any future American effort to challenge China’s manufacturing overcapacity, rebuild military capability in the Indo-Pacific, or enforce export controls could be declared a violation of the frame. Xi is not negotiating a deal. He is building a cage. And the cage has a name now: strategic stability.

The handshake. Xi descended the steps alone. Trump was already waiting.

V. The Price of Stability

Before the summit, Amanda Hsiao of Eurasia Group, who had been meeting scholars and officials in Beijing (Why Xi Doesn’t Need a Deal With Trump), identified China’s core objective in a single sentence: “They just want time and space to fortify themselves for future competition.”

That is what the Beijing Opera is about. Not the trade deals, not the Boeing orders, not the beef licenses that Beijing restored hours before the meeting — a goodwill gesture that cost China nothing and gave Trump something to announce. Not even the rare earths extension, which may or may not come on Friday. All of that is scenery.

The plot is time. China wants time to build its semiconductor industry, time to refine its AI models around American export controls (Spies, Sanctions, Cyberattacks: China and the U.S. Clash Behind the Scenes), time to continue the military buildup that the Pentagon documents in annual reports no one in the White House appears to read. And the price of that time is a performance — a pageant of red carpets, honour guards, coordinated children, imperial porcelain, Peking duck, and a philosopher king who quotes Thucydides to a man who quotes himself.

At the state banquet Thursday evening, Xi delivered the line that may prove the most durable of the entire summit: “The great rejuvenation of the Chinese nation and making America great again can go hand in hand.” Notice the syntax. The rejuvenation of China comes first. MAGA is the supporting act.

Trump raised his glass. “Gan-bei,” said Xi. The cameras were ushered out. The dinner began.

Gan-bei. The toast was warm. The warning had already been delivered.

VI. Intermission

As I write this from Toronto on Thursday evening, the first act is over. Day 2 — Friday — will bring smaller meetings, the kind Trump prefers: leader to leader, behind closed doors, where deals are made or announced. The rare earths question remains open (How China Could Wield Its Control of Rare Earths Against Trump). The Boeing numbers are expected. The Taiwan language has not yet been tested in a joint statement, if there is one.

But the reviews of Day 1 are already in (Chinese Media Cast Trump’s Visit as a Sign of Beijing’s Rising Stature). Zhang Weiwei of Fudan University, speaking to the state-owned Beijing Review, delivered the verdict that Chinese state media permitted the world to hear: “At this particular moment, I think Donald Trump needs China far more than China needs Donald Trump.”

D&C Think Tank in Beijing was more precise: “The Sino-U.S. rivalry has moved beyond the stage of unilateral pressure and entered a stage of two-way dynamic balance.”

And in a Fuzhou nail salon (‘Quite Brutal,’ ‘Not Friendly’: What People in China Say of Trump), Yang Saixiang, a 47-year-old worker whose customers have been hurt by tariffs and the economic slowdown, offered the review that will not appear in any readout from either capital: “I think at his age, he doesn’t need to be president anymore.”

The curtain will rise again tomorrow. The costumes, as always, are magnificent.
