In Ottawa, a minority Parliament runs on deals. A government short of a majority trades policy for votes, and the opposition parties trade votes for policy. In March 2022 the Liberals and the New Democrats signed a supply-and-confidence agreement that kept Justin Trudeau’s government alive in exchange for a national dental-care programme and the first steps towards pharmacare. Nobody in Ottawa thought it scandalous. It was how the arithmetic worked.

Part XVII of this series noted that the United States has invented something Westminster has no word for: an opposition that wins both houses of the legislature and remains the opposition. Within a week of the election, a serious argument had appeared for treating that strange position the way a Canadian party treats a hung Parliament. Make a deal.

The case for a bargain

Chaim Saiman, a law professor at Villanova, made it in The Atlantic on November 11, 2026. He wrote it as a direct reply to Robert Garcia’s election-night message, “Lawyer up,” and he called Garcia’s approach a mistake.

His proposal is blunt. Donald Trump is the most transactional president in modern history, and after the midterm rout his own party is beginning to see him as a liability. So Democrats should use that. They should offer him a legacy as the dealmaker who fixed things: a Medicare expansion, a reversal of the Medicaid cuts, restored Affordable Care Act subsidies, limits on dark money, lower energy costs, a cap on credit-card interest, perhaps even immigration reform. Reforms to disclosure, self-dealing, pardons and emergency powers could be written to take effect in 2029, after he leaves, so that his successors would be bound by rules he never had to follow.

In return, Democrats would hold back from investigating the president and his family, and let him build his ballroom and his arch. Saiman calls the monuments “largely harmless” and points out that anything built can be changed later. The people around Trump, the law firms, tech companies and crypto exchanges that went along with him, would remain fair game. Only the family would be spared.

He has precedents. In his first term Trump cut a budget deal with “Chuck and Nancy.” He has since been charmed into working with Zohran Mamdani on New York housing. Swiss executives brought a gold Rolex and an engraved gold bar to the Oval Office and saw their tariffs fall a week later. If foreign governments and chief executives can work Trump’s vanity to their advantage, Saiman asks, why can’t the Democratic Congress?

What it gets right

The argument deserves better than the reception it got, because several of its premises are correct.

The first is that investigations have limits. The Supreme Court has granted Trump broad immunity for official acts. His Justice Department will not prosecute contempt of Congress, and the companion series When Democracy Fights Back has set out in detail how hard enforcement will be. He can pardon his associates. And no hearing will claw back the money the family has made.

The second is that time is short. Saiman thinks the tension between Trump and his party will peak in the next few months, after which everyone’s attention turns to 2028. Whatever Democrats intend to pass, they need to move early.

The third is that voters asked for results. Corruption and the cost of living topped the list of concerns among the people who gave Democrats their House majority, as Isaac Stanley-Becker reported in The Atlantic on November 10. A majority that spends two years on hearings and passes nothing could lose the next election just as surely as one that ignores corruption.

And the fourth is that even a large Senate majority does not pass much on its own. With 55 Democratic seats and Alaska still being counted, the party needs Republican votes to break a filibuster on anything substantial.

And there is one signal from Trump himself that cuts Saiman's way. Days after naming Stephen Miller chief of staff, the president told 60 Minutes that his own immigration policy "might be too tough," and allowed that immigrants can be good for the country "if they're good people working in a landscaping company, or working in a hotel," Adam Kirsch noted in The Atlantic on November 12. Immigration reform is on Saiman's list of possible trades. If Trump's instincts are drifting, a deal on it is not unimaginable.

What the readers said

The Atlantic’s readers were not persuaded. Within hours, the comment thread under Saiman’s piece had grown to hundreds of posts, nearly all of them hostile, and the objections fell into three groups.

The most common was practical: Trump breaks deals. Commenter after commenter pointed to the contractors he stiffed, the trade partners whose agreements he tore up and the allies who discovered that a promise from this White House lasted until the next mood. The second was political. Voters had just put Democrats in charge after an election in which corruption ranked among their top concerns, and here was a proposal to trade away the one thing they had most clearly asked for. The third was historical. Several readers named the precedents Adam Serwer listed in the Atlantic earlier this year: Gerald Ford’s pardon of Richard Nixon, the Iran-Contra pardons, Barack Obama’s decision to “look forward.” Each time, the argument went, the country chose healing over accountability and got a bolder offender next time. Part LXV of When Democracy Fights Back made the same case.

A minority defended Saiman as a realist, and one of them made a sharp point. Trump’s only formal power over legislation is the veto. If he signs a bill and later regrets it, the law still stands. In that narrow sense, his unreliability matters less than his critics think.

Why Miller matters

The strongest objection to the deal, though, came from a former Republican speechwriter rather than from the comment thread.

On his podcast the same day, David Frum warned in The Atlantic that the last two years of the Trump presidency could bring a series of constitutional crises. His evidence was a single appointment. You do not make Stephen Miller your chief of staff, Frum argued, if you mean to work with Democrats on immigration or the budget. You hire Miller if you mean to refuse subpoenas, deny information and fight on every front.

Frum pointed to history as well. When Trump lost the House in 2018, he did not behave better. He behaved worse, and the attempt to overturn the 2020 election followed. Frum listed the tools a president still has when Congress turns against him: tariffs, war powers, control of ICE, and even the debt ceiling, which Republican Congresses have used against Democratic presidents but which a president could, in principle, turn against Congress. He raised the possibility of investigations and indictments aimed at members of Congress, along the lines of the search of Ilhan Omar’s home in October.

Within days the threats were being made out loud. The White House's top lawyer has warned congressional Democrats that investigating the administration could be criminal, and Trump has suggested that any Democrat who tries could find themselves in jail, Jonathan Lemire reported for The Atlantic on November 16. Asked whether he could work with the new chief of staff, Hakeem Jeffries said no.

The day after Saiman's essay appeared, Trump showed what his dealmaking currently looks like. On November 12 he announced that he had pulled Vice President JD Vance off the effort to restart peace talks with Iran and told him to give "his complete and total attention" to "the Massive Corruption and Crime taking place in Democrat run Cities and States," as Luke Broadwater reported for The New York Times. "We don't need a deal with Iran," the president wrote. "Those days have long since passed." His vice president had been offering to negotiate. The president reassigned him to investigating the voters who had just elected this Congress.

None of that sounds like a man preparing to trade. It sounds like a man preparing for a siege. A deal requires a counterparty who wants something you can give him. What Trump appears to want from this Congress is for it to fail.

The fissure that isn’t there

Saiman’s plan rests on a second premise: that Republicans are starting to break from Trump, so that a deal could pull a few of them across to a bipartisan majority. The week’s evidence pointed the other way.

On November 10, as Part XVII recorded, Thomas Massie forced a vote on impeaching Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, and not one Republican joined him. Massie had pitched it as a life raft for colleagues looking to put distance between themselves and the president before 2028, Catie Edmondson reported in The New York Times. Nobody climbed aboard. Trump posted his support for Hegseth, and took the result as proof that he still controlled the party he had just led to defeat. The new majority did not cover itself in glory either: eleven Democrats missed the vote, many of them saying they were stuck in traffic. On paper, their votes would have been enough to defeat the motion that shelved the articles. The next day, House Republicans chose Jim Jordan as their leader, one of the president's most combative allies, according to The New York Times.

Bob Inglis, Frum’s guest, explained why. Inglis represented South Carolina’s Greenville-Spartanburg district for twelve years, lost a 2010 primary for insufficient loyalty to the party line, and endorsed Kamala Harris in 2024. He described losing a primary as the political equivalent of a divorce: the people who hugged you and promised their prayers turn up next time wearing the other candidate’s sticker. That fear, he said, is why Republican members listen so carefully to the party faithful, and why so few have broken with Trump. He estimates that 35 to 37 per cent of Republican voters still like the Trump style. Any Republican who wants a future still has to get past them in a primary.

Inglis is more hopeful than the vote count. He quoted Fritz Hollings’s old line that there is “no education in the second kick of a mule,” and he sees signs of learning. Greenville County, his old base, voted for the Democrat Annie Andrews in the Senate race against Darline Graham, who had been appointed to the seat after her brother Lindsey’s death. At a county Republican dinner in Georgia, he heard two College Republicans say the party had to bring the state together. But his timetable for the change is two years of Trumpism souring on the shelf like old bread, not two months.

And Inglis said one more thing that cuts straight through Saiman’s argument. His main objection to Trump, he told Frum, has always been Trump’s transactional view of life, the belief that everything is a trade. Saiman’s plan treats that trait as the opening. Inglis, a lifelong Republican, treats it as the disqualification.

A deal worth having

So is there no bargain to be had? I think there is, but it is not the one Saiman proposed.

Democrats can legislate in the open. They can pass the health-care bills, the energy measures, the credit-card cap and the anti-monopoly bills, put Trump’s name on them if that helps, and send them to his desk. If he signs, the country gets the laws. If he vetoes, the country learns who stood in the way, and the 2028 campaign has its first issue. That is not a deal. It is governing, with the president given the choice.

That is where most of the party has landed. Most of the Democrats Lemire spoke to after the election want to send Trump a series of economic bills and dare him to veto them, betting that an already unpopular president would take the blame, and that they could pin it on any Republican who runs in 2028, JD Vance above all. Trump says he would consider "reasonable" ideas, without saying which. The White House may even meet them partway. Michael Scherer and Ashley Parker reported in The Atlantic that his advisers have begun talking quietly about "populist" measures Democrats could support, such as building on savings accounts for children, an idea that began with the Clinton administration. If a bargain comes, it will probably look like that: a bill with his name on it, not a promise about his family.

They can also do what Saiman himself concedes is fair: investigate the companies, firms and officials who profited from the last two years. That is where the money trail runs anyway, and as When Democracy Fights Back argued, it is where congressional subpoenas are hardest to resist.

Democratic strategists are already thinking that way. With so many allegations in play, chasing all of them could look like overreach, so one told Lemire the party might tell the larger story through a few that anyone can follow, such as the Qatari jet. Shane Harris showed in The Atlantic why it would make a good one. A $400 million plane came from a government that had already put money into Jared Kushner's private-equity firm and paid $623 million for a hotel belonging to Steve Witkoff. The retrofit reportedly cost more than $1 billion. And the security guarantee Trump gave Qatar was worth nothing when Iran struck its gas plant and closed the Strait of Hormuz. Those are not abstractions about the Constitution. They are a gift, a price tag and a broken promise.

What they cannot do is the one thing at the centre of Saiman’s bargain, which is to promise the president and his family that they will not be looked at. The problem is not that it is cynical. Canadian minority governments make cynical bargains every session. The problem is what is being traded. In Ottawa, parties trade votes for policy. Nobody trades immunity. The 2022 agreement bought dental care. It did not buy the government a pass from the ethics commissioner.

The opposition’s real choice

Saiman ends by telling Democrats that if Trump believes everything is a deal, they should take him at his word. It is a clever line. But the voters who elected this Congress did not send it to Washington to take Trump at his word. They sent it to check him.

That leaves the new majority where Part XVII left it. Garcia wants to fight, Talarico and Hamilton want to govern, and Sanders wants the time for something else. Saiman has offered a way to make the second group's wish come true by giving up the first group's. The better answer is the one that serves both. Pass the laws that a Kansas pastor and a Texas seminarian can vote for, and dare the president to veto them. Follow the money through the people who handled it. And leave the question of the president's own conduct open, because closing it by contract would be the clearest message this Congress could send that it is a negotiable matter after all.
