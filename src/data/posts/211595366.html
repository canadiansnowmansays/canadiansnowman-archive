Ghulam Kehar rose before dawn in early June 2026 to pray at the Valley Ranch Islamic Center in Irving, Texas, before shipping out for a months-long U.S. Coast Guard deployment. He had deployed before. This time he arranged for his wife and four children to join him near the Canadian border rather than leave them behind in Texas — not because of anything the enemy abroad might do, but because of what he feared might happen to his family at home while he was gone. As J. David Goodman, Lauren McGaughy, Jesus Jiménez and Ruth Graham reported for the New York Times on August 17, 2026, Mr. Kehar’s calculation was shaped by a rising tide of rhetorical attacks on Muslims from the state’s top elected officials — attacks that, by his account, had begun trickling down into ugly comments directed at worshippers in parks and parking lots.

A member of the United States Coast Guard Reserve, prepared to deploy in service of the country, did not trust that country to protect his family in his absence. Sit with that for a moment, because it is the whole argument of this series compressed into a single household decision.

I have spent fourteen months now tracing a pattern that keeps insisting on its own novelty every time it resurfaces: a minority group grows visible enough to be noticed, prosperous enough to be resented, and organized enough to be legible as a target — and the state discovers, usually within a single legislative session, that the group in question was never really American to begin with. Irish Catholics were not really American; they answered to Rome. Chinese labourers were not really American; they could never assimilate. German immigrants during the First World War were not really American; their loyalty was suspect by birth. Japanese Americans were not really American; forty years of citizenship dissolved overnight into an internment camp. Jewish refugees fleeing extermination were not really American enough to be let in the door in 1939, when the St. Louis was turned back to Europe and to the gas chambers.

Texas Republicans have now found their new group, and they are refreshingly candid about the method. “One of the first things we’re going to try to do is define what a religion is,” State Representative Alan Schoolcraft told the Times, previewing a legislative strategy for the session ahead. “Islam itself is not just a religion. It’s much more than a religion.” This is not a stray comment. It is the opening move of a project: if you cannot argue your way around the First Amendment, you argue your way around the definition of what the First Amendment protects. Asma T. Uddin, a religious liberty lawyer quoted in the piece, named the strategy precisely — “a way of winning the religious liberty fight before it starts, by defining the other side out of the category.”

That is the mechanism this series exists to document. Not persecution that announces itself as persecution — that variant is rare and it is not what wins elections in a constitutional democracy — but persecution that arrives dressed as definition, as classification, as a technical question about what counts. Whom does the law protect? Who qualifies as a religion, a citizen, an officer, a threat? Redraw the boundary of the category, and you can exclude a group while insisting, with a straight face, that you have not touched their rights at all. You have simply clarified what a right is.

The Numbers Behind the Fear

Texas’s Muslim population is not large — somewhere between 300,000 and 500,000 people, one or two percent of the state, according to Pew Research Center estimates cited in the Times report. But it is growing, and growth is precisely what activates the pattern. In the four largest Dallas-Fort Worth counties, the number of mosques nearly tripled between 2000 and 2020, from 28 to 76, according to the Association of Religion Data Archives. Census data on international migration from Muslim-majority countries shows the Dallas-Fort Worth metro area’s population from those countries growing to more than 159,000 by 2024, up from roughly 79,000 a decade earlier — five times the growth rate of the region’s population as a whole.

None of this makes Texas unusual as a destination. Families like the Kehars came for the reasons families always come to Texas: good schools, affordable housing, community. Tammam Alwan, who moved from Detroit to Irving after discovering the community online, put it to the Times in the register of pilgrimage rather than economics: “I made a prayer: Please, God, send me to Irving.” What makes Texas unusual is the speed and specificity with which the state government converted that ordinary migration story into a security question.

The turning point, per the Times account, arrived in early 2025, when the East Plano Islamic Center unveiled plans for a mosque-anchored real estate development east of Dallas — a project that came to be known as EPIC City. Anti-Muslim activist Amy Mekelburg wrote online, “This is how the West falls.” Governor Greg Abbott, whose consultant told the Times that “anything he does is poll-driven,” opened state investigations into both the development and the mosque community behind it. Attorney General Ken Paxton designated the Council on American-Islamic Relations a terrorist organization — a step even the Trump administration has not taken nationally, though Florida has since followed Texas’s lead. Abbott threatened to strip funding from airports offering ritual washing stations to Muslim travelers and ordered a private pool party celebrating a Muslim holiday cancelled outright.

This is not rhetoric anymore. It is administrative machinery, running.

The Assimilation Trap

The series has returned again and again to a particular cruelty embedded in the assimilation demand: it is not actually satisfiable. Dr. Farhat Shaikh, a physician in Frisco, told the Times she serves on the PTA, volunteers in her community, and practices medicine — by any ordinary standard, a model of civic assimilation. But visible participation, she noted, gets read by critics as evidence of a “takeover” rather than proof of belonging. “I’m part of the P.T.A., I volunteer for my community, I’m a practicing physician. I am assimilating,” she said — and it changes nothing about how she is perceived by the loudest voices in her city’s politics.

This is the same trap Irish Catholics fell into in the nineteenth century, when nativist cartoonists drew them as apes regardless of how quickly they anglicized their names or joined municipal fire brigades. It is the same trap Japanese American soldiers of the 442nd Regimental Combat Team fell into, becoming the most decorated unit in U.S. military history while their own families sat behind barbed wire in Manzanar. Assimilation, in the American Contradiction, has never actually been the price of admission. It has been a moving goalpost that lets the state claim its exclusion is conditional and reasonable rather than categorical and permanent — because if the newcomer could just try harder, surely the failure is theirs.

State Representative Schoolcraft made the terms of the bargain explicit to the Times: “I’m not saying totally abandon your culture. But try to become a citizen where you’re living. Otherwise there’s going to be conflict.” Dr. Shaikh already is a citizen, in every sense the word carries outside of Mr. Schoolcraft’s legislative imagination. The conflict he warns of is not a prediction. It is a threat wearing the grammar of a forecast.

The Documentary and the Movie Theater

About 300 people gathered at a Dallas-area movie theater in late May 2026 to watch a film billed as revealing “the disastrous effect of Islam in America.” The sponsors, per the Times, included a Christian ministry, a group of Jewish conservatives, two county Republican parties, and several Republican women’s organizations — a coalition, in other words, assembled specifically for the occasion of watching a documentary about a religious minority most of its members will never actually meet. “Most of the people that are speaking up are Christian,” the film’s director, Frank X. Panico, told the paper, apparently as a point of pride.

Erick Stakelbeck, a host on the Trinity Broadcasting Network, told the crowd, “This is the battlefield right now.” Barbara Isaacs, a Collin County activist, distributed pamphlets urging attendees to get involved in local government specifically to slow or prevent mosque construction. “Share this with your friends, your church members,” she urged.

I want to be precise about what this event actually was, because the language of “battlefield” invites a misreading. It was not a response to any documented act of aggression by the Muslim families of Collin County against their neighbours. It was a theatre full of people organizing themselves, county by county, around the demographic fact of a minority’s growth — treating the presence of new mosques the way earlier generations treated the presence of new synagogues, new Catholic parishes, new Chinatowns: as an invasion to be repelled through zoning boards and city council meetings rather than through anything resembling an actual grievance.

This is worth naming because the American Contradiction has always depended on the fiction that exclusion is defensive. Nobody in this series’ long catalogue of nativist episodes has ever described themselves as an aggressor. The Know-Nothings did not think of themselves as bigots; they thought of themselves as patriots protecting the republic from Rome. The architects of Japanese internment did not think of themselves as jailers; they thought of themselves as prudent men managing a wartime risk. The organizers behind a Sharia-Free Caucus booth at the state Republican convention do not think of themselves as persecutors; they think of themselves, per their own rhetoric, as the last line of defense for a Texas that would otherwise dissolve into the Europe of their imagination — a continent they invoke without apparent awareness that most European Muslims have been there for generations, are citizens, vote, and have not caused the civilizational collapse the pamphlets promise is coming.

The Ledger, Updated Again

Regular readers of this series will recognize the shape of what comes next, because we have watched it unfold in the military officer corps, in preschool inspections, in the erosion of birthright citizenship protections, in the doctrine of a Supreme Court increasingly unwilling to see the pattern it is asked to rule on. The Texas Legislature convenes next year, and the item at the top of the agenda, per multiple Republican officials quoted in the Times piece, is defining Islam out of the category of “religion” as American law understands it.

The Justice Department, under a previous administration, once settled this exact question. When Tennessee residents tried in 2010 to block a mosque on the theory that Islam was not really a religion, the department’s brief was unambiguous: “Islam is plainly a religion,” protected like any other under the First Amendment. Whether the department under the current administration would say the same thing today is, per the Times reporting, an open question — which is itself the most important sentence in the entire piece. A settled constitutional fact has become, once again, a live political question. That is what it looks like when the ledger reopens.

Dr. Omar Suleiman, the Muslim scholar interviewed at Valley Ranch, described his community’s evolving posture after years of trying quiet outreach: “We tried that, and it did nothing for us.” His prescription now is legal combativeness — “We have to actually make lying legally and politically costly to these people.” It is not a hopeful sentence. It is a tired one, arrived at by people who have watched patience fail to purchase them safety, and who have decided, correctly I think, that dignity does not get negotiated from a position of silence.

Canada is not a paradise on this question — no country that has ever hosted a religious minority has managed that — but it is instructive that the closest most Canadians come to a national debate over “defining religion” involves arguing about whether Tim Hortons counts as a secular sacrament. We do not have a Sharia-Free Caucus with a convention booth. We have not asked our attorney general to designate a civil rights organization a terrorist group. The difference is not virtue. It is that we have, so far, declined to reopen a ledger that history keeps warning us never actually closes.

Ghulam Kehar is somewhere near the Canadian border right now, on deployment, with his family finally beside him. He did not leave the country he serves. He simply moved his family to the part of it that felt, this year, less like a threat. That a Coast Guard reservist had to make that calculation at all is the entire American Contradiction, restated in the smallest and most devastating terms: the state asks for your service and denies your family’s safety in the same breath, and calls the arrangement patriotism.
