Taipei, the vibrant capital of a thriving democratic Taiwan — a distinct political community charting its own course.

The case for Taiwan’s independence does not rest on sentiment, though sentiment is present in abundance. It rests on three pillars that can be examined and tested independently: the legal argument, the democratic argument, and the argument from identity. Each is substantial on its own. Together they constitute, in this writer’s view, a position that any honest assessment of the evidence must take seriously — regardless of what Beijing prefers, what Washington finds convenient, or what the KMT’s leadership is willing to say in Nanjing.

The Legal Argument

Begin with the law, because the law is where Beijing is weakest and where Taiwan’s advocates are strongest, even if that strength is not yet translated into international recognition.

On the left cover and contents page of a Japanese colonial-era atlas of Taiwan (Formosa), published during the fifty-year administration that followed the 1895 Treaty of Shimonoseki. On the right, when Japan surrendered in 1945 and the San Francisco Peace Treaty of 1951 formalised its renunciation of "all right, title and claim to Formosa," no recipient was designated. The island was put down and no one was legally designated to pick it up.

The Treaty of Shimonoseki ceded Taiwan to Japan in 1895. Japan held Taiwan as a colony for fifty years. When Japan surrendered in 1945, it relinquished all claim to the island. The San Francisco Peace Treaty of 1951 formalised that relinquishment: Japan renounced all right, title, and claim to Formosa and the Pescadores. It did not designate a recipient. Sovereignty over the island was not legally transferred to the People’s Republic of China, which was not a party to the San Francisco treaty and has explicitly stated that it does not consider itself bound by that treaty. Sovereignty was not legally transferred to the Republic of China either — the bilateral Treaty of Taipei of 1952 acknowledged Japan’s renunciation but similarly failed to specify a transfer of sovereignty, and Japanese officials subsequently clarified that the exchange of notes accompanying that treaty was about administration, not sovereignty.

Japan put Taiwan down. No one legally picked it up. The territory’s status, in the careful formulation of British and American legal opinion at the time, remains undetermined.

This matters because it means Taiwan’s current status is not a deviation from an established legal order that must be corrected by reunification. It is an open question that has not been legally closed. And under the principles of international law that have governed territorial questions since decolonisation — specifically the right of peoples to self-determination, enshrined in the United Nations Charter and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights — an open question about the sovereignty of a territory with twenty-three million inhabitants is a question that those inhabitants have a right to answer.

Beijing argues that the Cairo Declaration and the Potsdam Proclamation settled the matter in 1943-45, before the San Francisco treaty was drafted. But declarations of wartime intent are not treaties, and as Taiwan’s legal scholars have pointed out, the consistent practice of international law holds that changes in territorial sovereignty must be effected through treaties with explicit provisions. Unilateral declarations do not constitute transfers of sovereignty. The wartime agreements expressed intent. The peace treaties failed to execute that intent with legal clarity. The gap between intention and execution is precisely where Taiwan’s legal argument lives.

Taiwan’s own president has been building this case in public. In 2025, President Lai Ching-te delivered a series of pointed speeches elaborating the legal foundations of Taiwan’s de facto independence, emphasising cross-strait cultural and historical differences, and describing China as a “foreign, hostile force.” These were not casual remarks. They were a deliberate assertion of legal status, made by an elected head of government to an electorate that chose him. Beijing condemned them as provocations. They were also, on the merits, an exercise of exactly the kind of self-determination that the legal argument supports.

The independence camp does not need to argue that Taiwan is already legally independent under existing international law — though some scholars make that case. It needs only to argue that existing international law has not definitively assigned Taiwan’s sovereignty to the People’s Republic of China, and that the principle of self-determination therefore applies. On that narrower claim, the legal case is strong.

The Democratic Argument

Voters casting ballots with Taiwan flag elements: Peaceful transfer of power through free and fair elections: the democratic foundation of Taiwan’s legitimate authority.

The strongest argument for Taiwan’s independence is not legal. It is democratic, and it is simple.

Taiwan has been a functioning multiparty democracy since 1996, when it held its first direct presidential election. It has since conducted eight presidential elections and multiple legislative elections, in all of which power has changed hands peacefully between competing parties. Its press is free. Its judiciary is independent. Its civil society is vigorous. Its citizens organise, protest, vote, and hold their government accountable through democratic means. By every measure that liberal democracies use to define legitimate political authority — consent of the governed, free elections, rule of law, protection of individual rights — Taiwan’s government has it.

The People’s Republic of China’s government does not have it, at least not in Taiwan. The PRC has never governed Taiwan. Not one of Taiwan’s twenty-three million current residents has ever been a citizen of the People’s Republic of China. No one on the island has ever voted in a PRC election, been subject to PRC courts, or received PRC government services. The PRC’s claim to Taiwan is a claim of intent — the intent to govern, grounded in a theory of historical sovereignty — not a claim of fact.

The democratic principle holds that governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed. On any honest reading of that principle, the question of whether Taiwan should be governed by the PRC is a question that the people of Taiwan are entitled to answer. And the answer, consistently returned by decades of polling, is that the overwhelming majority of Taiwan’s population prefers either the status quo (functional independence without formal declaration) or formal independence to unification with the mainland — and that this preference strengthens, not weakens, with each new generation of Taiwanese born into the island’s democratic culture.

The Beijing counter-argument — that the Taiwanese people’s preferences are the product of manipulation by the DPP, by American influence, or by historical revisionism — is paternalistic in the precise sense: it holds that the people do not know what is good for them, and that an external authority is better positioned to determine their interests than they are themselves. It is the argument that colonial powers have made about colonised peoples throughout history, and it carries the same moral weight now as it did then.

Taiwan’s people have been asked, repeatedly and through functioning democratic institutions, what they want. They have answered. The argument for independence holds that their answer should be respected.

The Argument from Identity

By the time of the most recent comprehensive surveys, more than sixty percent of Taiwan’s population identifies as Taiwanese, not Chinese — a figure that has risen steadily from below thirty percent in the 1990s and continues to rise most sharply among the young. Among Taiwanese citizens under thirty-five, the proportion identifying as exclusively Taiwanese approaches eighty percent.

Historical photo / memorial reference to the 228 Incident: The 228 Incident (1947) - a foundational trauma that shaped modern Taiwanese political consciousness and identity.

This is not a manufactured identity. It has roots in four centuries of distinct historical experience — the Dutch colonial period, the Koxinga era, the Qing administration, the fifty years of Japanese colonialism that shaped the island’s infrastructure, language, and institutional culture more profoundly than anything the mainland government has done before or since. It was deepened by the trauma of the 228 Incident of 1947, in which the Nationalist government that arrived from the mainland in 1945 massacred tens of thousands of Taiwanese civilians in a suppression of political dissent that is the foundational event of contemporary Taiwanese political consciousness. It was further shaped by thirty-eight years of martial law, lifted only in 1987, which suppressed Taiwanese cultural and political expression while privileging the mainland Chinese elite that had arrived with the KMT.

Taiwan’s democratic transition from 1987 onward was also, inevitably, a cultural liberation — the emergence into public life of a Taiwanese identity that had been systematically suppressed for four decades and that has deepened and consolidated in the decades since. The Taiwan that exists today is not a fragment of China waiting to be reattached. It is a distinct political community with its own democratic culture, its own historical memory, its own relationship to the island it inhabits, and its own views about its future.

The argument from identity does not require the claim that Taiwanese people have no cultural connections to China — they do, many of them deep and genuine, particularly among the older generation that came from the mainland in 1949 and their children. It requires only the claim that shared cultural ancestry does not determine political belonging, and that a people who have built their own political community over generations have the right to define that community on their own terms.

The Synthesis

The case for Taiwan’s independence, stated plainly, is this: that a territory whose legal status was deliberately left undetermined by the post-war peace settlement, governed by a functioning democracy with the consent of its people, whose population has developed a distinct identity over four centuries of separate historical experience and has clearly expressed a preference for self-determination, has the legal standing, the democratic legitimacy, and the moral right to determine its own future — and that determination is not Beijing’s to make.

The obstacles to this outcome are real and serious. They include the military balance across the strait, the diplomatic isolation that the one-China framework imposes on Taiwan, the ambivalence of Taiwan’s principal security partner, and the calculation that a formal declaration of independence would trigger a Chinese military response that the world is not prepared to prevent. These are not arguments that the independence case is wrong. They are arguments that it is, for now, dangerous to act on.

There is a difference between a right and the ability to exercise it safely. Taiwan’s independence advocates understand this. Most of them are not calling for an immediate declaration that would invite war. They are calling for the world to acknowledge that the Taiwanese people have a right to self-determination, and that any future resolution of Taiwan’s status must be arrived at with the consent of those people rather than imposed upon them by external powers negotiating over their heads.

That is not an extreme position. In any other context — in any context not immediately adjacent to a nuclear-armed great power with a stated willingness to use force — it would be the obvious position. It would be the position that every liberal democracy would endorse without hesitation.

The silence with which liberal democracies have largely greeted Taiwan’s democratic achievement is itself a measure of how much the Beautiful Problem distorts the international order around it.
