Every national party eventually discovers that its state-level officials are better at politics than its national ones, usually right around the point when the national ones stop being able to win anything. Democrats appear to be having that discovery now, in real time, across two very different states, and the contrast between how Iowa’s Rob Sand and Michigan’s Gretchen Whitmer are talking to voters says more about the party’s actual capacity for self-correction than any op-ed or convention speech has managed this cycle.

The Auditor Who Sings

I flagged Rob Sand’s town hall ritual briefly in Part II, but it deserves fuller treatment on its own terms, because it isn’t a gimmick that happened once — it’s now a structural feature of his entire campaign, repeated at essentially every stop of a hundred-town-hall tour that’s taken him to all ninety-nine of Iowa’s counties.

The ritual itself is simple and, on paper, almost too on-the-nose to work: Sand asks the room to raise hands by party — Republican, independent, Democrat — has them applaud one another across those lines, and then leads everyone in singing the first verse of “America the Beautiful.” He’s done it in churches, breweries, VFW halls, and a distillery in Sioux City that drew roughly two hundred and fifty people. In Ellston, Iowa — population nineteen — he reportedly drew forty-five. His explanation for the ritual, offered consistently across more than a year of these events, is pointed rather than sentimental: “So many elected officials from both parties want us to be divided from each other,” he told Iowa Starting Line. “It works great for them to amass power if we hate our neighbors and hate our family.”

That’s not a unity message in the soft, both-sides-have-a-point sense that usually earns Democratic candidates criticism from their own base for false equivalence. It’s closer to an argument about who benefits from polarization — an argument that the division itself is the product being sold to voters by people in both parties who profit from it, and that singing a patriotic song together before the disagreements start is a small, deliberate act of refusing to buy it. Whether or not that argument holds up as governing philosophy, it’s a coherent theory of the electorate, tested empirically at more than a hundred stops rather than asserted once from a podium.

The state auditor framing matters here too. Sand isn’t running as an ideological standard-bearer; he’s running as the guy whose actual job for eight years has been catching government waste and fraud, on both sides of the aisle, which gives the unity ritual a credibility a career legislator’s version of the same gesture might lack. When he tells a crowd that Iowa’s system is “broken by two private clubs,” he’s making an institutional argument, not a partisan one — and Iowa Republicans in his own audiences, including at least one sitting GOP state senator who’s shown up to a town hall, seem to be listening to it as exactly that.

The Math That Makes the Ritual Necessary

What CNN’s reporting from a late-August stop in Independence, Iowa adds to this picture isn’t a new ritual — it’s the arithmetic behind the one Sand’s already been running for a year, and the arithmetic is considerably starker than a colourful anecdote about a distillery in Sioux City might suggest. Iowa Secretary of State registration data from this August shows 712,087 voters registered Republican against 528,758 registered Democrat, with another 559,741 registered with neither party. That’s a Republican advantage of more than 180,000 voters heading into a governor’s race Sand needs to win outright, not eke out on a one-point margin the way Abdul El-Sayed did in the Michigan Senate primary this series covered at length in Parts I, VI, and VIII. A Democrat cannot draw an inside straight in a state with that registration gap. He needs Republicans and independents to actually vote for him, in numbers large enough to overcome the deficit outright, which means the singalong ritual this series has been treating as a charming retail-politics flourish is closer to a mathematical necessity dressed up as a folk custom.

That necessity appears to have pushed Sand somewhere Whitmer and Beshear, in their own ways, haven’t gone: he has started downplaying his own party affiliation almost entirely. CNN’s profile describes him, accurately, as “a bow-hunting, scripture-quoting, plaid-shirt wearing father of two” who “seldom dwells on” the fact that he’s a Democrat, campaigning in a way that seems almost designed to draw attention away from the letter next to his name on the ballot. In Independence, he told the crowd plainly that “our political system is broken... mostly because it’s run by two private clubs and they’re more interested in beating up on each other than they are in solving our problems” — the same institutional argument I described in the previous section, but delivered, this time, alongside an even more explicit statement of the unity thesis: “left-wing, right-wing — one bird! We’re all still in this together, so let’s work together.” Asked afterward whether a simple act of shared patriotism could really soften a partisan divide this wide, Sand didn’t hedge: “It’s leading us to hate people that we used to love and we need to change it.”

This is a meaningfully different posture from what Part VII of this series, drawing on Daron Acemoglu’s big-tent populism framework, described as the model for holding an adversarial economic message without alienating the voters caught in its blast radius. Acemoglu’s big tent still has a party label on the canvas; it just makes room for more people to stand under it. Sand’s version comes closer to taking the label off the tent altogether, at least in how he presents himself to a room. Whether that’s a bigger tent or simply a different structure built on the same ground is a genuine question, and Iowa Republicans have already supplied their answer to it. “A liberal with a better brand is still a liberal,” said Jade Cichy, a spokesperson for the Republican Party of Iowa, dismissing the entire de-emphasis strategy as marketing rather than substance. That’s a sharper and more direct rebuttal than anything Mike Rogers has managed against El-Sayed in Michigan, discussed in Part VIII, precisely because Cichy isn’t attacking Sand’s record — she’s attacking the premise that downplaying a party label changes anything real about what a candidate would do in office. It is, in effect, the Republican Party of Iowa accusing a Democrat of practising the exact vocabulary-reclamation strategy Part II of this series described favourably, and naming it as a trick rather than a genuine repositioning.

I don’t think Cichy’s attack line settles the question, but it does sharpen it in a way this series should sit with. A voter deciding whether Sand’s unity ritual is sincere retail politics or a “better brand” wrapped around an unchanged liberal core is deciding, in miniature, the same question this entire series has been circling since “Wake Up, Dems”: whether vocabulary and tone genuinely widen a coalition or merely disguise the same positions in more palatable packaging. Sand’s own answer, offered without much hedging, is that the packaging isn’t separable from the substance — that refusing to lead with the party label is itself the governing style, not a marketing layer sitting on top of a different one underneath. Whether Iowa’s 180,000-voter registration gap closes enough in November to test that answer at scale is, unlike the single-point margin that decided Michigan’s Senate primary, going to produce a result too large to explain away as noise either way.

The Governor Who Won’t Apologize for the Tent

Whitmer’s version of message-testing looks almost nothing like Sand’s, and the contrast is the point. Where Sand performs unity as ritual, Whitmer has spent 2026 relitigating, in public and under direct questioning, whether her party’s inclusiveness is itself a political liability — and refusing to concede the premise.

Asked by NPR’s Steve Inskeep in January whether Democrats had “tilted the playing field” by focusing too much on inclusion, Whitmer didn’t hedge or reframe. She rejected the question’s premise directly, using an analogy about saving a rainforest — recognizing one ecosystem’s unique vulnerability, she argued, doesn’t mean declaring war on every other forest — while adding a real concession alongside the rejection: that the party’s messaging is sometimes “too obtuse” for people to follow. That’s a genuinely difficult needle to thread in a single interview — defending the substance of the party’s coalition-building instincts while admitting the packaging has failed — and it’s a needle Whitmer threaded from a position most national Democrats don’t have: a ninety percent approval rating within her own party and, more strikingly, thirty-five percent among Michigan Republicans, in a state Trump carried in two of the last three elections.

It’s worth remembering that Whitmer was the person national Democrats wanted running for this cycle’s most closely watched Senate seat in the first place, before she passed and the party fell back to the Stevens candidacy this series covered at length in Parts I and IV. Her decision to stay in the governor’s mansion rather than run for Senate looks, in hindsight, like it preserved her as the party’s most credible messenger on exactly the question — how do you defend an inclusive coalition without sounding “obtuse” doing it — that the Michigan Senate primary ended up failing to answer cleanly at all.

“Talk Like a Normal Human Being”

The clearest distillation of what Democratic governors think Washington keeps getting wrong came not from Whitmer but from Kentucky’s Andy Beshear, speaking alongside her at the Michigan Democratic Party’s Legacy Dinner in April. Beshear — a Democratic governor who’s won and held office twice in a state Trump carried by roughly thirty points — urged the room to “talk like a normal human being” heading into the midterms, a line that landed less as a soundbite than as a direct rebuke of how the party’s national figures typically communicate. Beshear and Whitmer both co-chair the Democratic Governors Association this cycle, which puts two of the party’s most electorally successful red-and-purple-state messengers in charge of coordinating exactly the kind of plain-spoken, non-obtuse messaging Whitmer had defended as necessary and Beshear had just demanded outright.

That pairing is worth sitting with. The DGA, unlike the DSCC that Schumer effectively runs and that Part IV of this series found struggling to coordinate two of its most important Senate nominees, is being co-led by two governors who’ve each demonstrated, repeatedly, that they can win over voters their party’s national figures generally can’t reach. If there’s a functioning model anywhere in the current Democratic apparatus for combining “loyal opposition” vocabulary reclamation with actual electoral results in hostile territory, the DGA under Whitmer and Beshear is closer to it than anything happening in the Senate campaign arm right now.

A Third Model, For Contrast

It’s worth naming a third governor briefly, if only because he illustrates that “talk like a normal human being” isn’t the only viable theory currently being tested at the state level. California’s Gavin Newsom has spent 2026 experimenting with something closer to combative mirroring — matching Trump-aligned rhetoric with sharper, more confrontational counter-rhetoric of his own, a strategy built less around reclaiming vocabulary gently than around refusing to let provocations go unanswered. When Newsom and Whitmer appeared together in Michigan this August, conservative commentary framed the meeting as two pandemic-era governors reassuring each other about decisions that remain unpopular on the right — a reminder that both governors are still carrying baggage from 2020 and 2021 that neither Sand’s singalongs nor Beshear’s plain talk have to contend with, since neither of them ran a state through the most contentious phase of COVID-era executive action.

The point of naming Newsom isn’t to adjudicate whose approach is superior — this series has been consistent that it isn’t in the business of picking a house style for the whole party. It’s to note that “governors as message labs” doesn’t mean the labs are all running the same experiment. Sand is testing whether ritual and retail politics, backed now by an almost total de-emphasis of his own party label, can lower the temperature enough to win over Republicans directly, one county at a time, against a registration deficit that leaves him no other mathematical path. Whitmer and Beshear are testing whether plain-spoken defense of an inclusive coalition, backed by a genuine record of red-state and purple-state wins, can hold up under hostile questioning. Newsom is testing something closer to the opposite theory — that Democrats have historically been too restrained, and that matching an opponent’s combativeness is itself the more honest and more effective form of communication. November will tell you a great deal about which of these theories, if any, actually moves persuadable voters, because unlike a Senate primary decided by a single point, gubernatorial and Senate general elections in these states will produce results at a scale too large to explain away as noise.

The Trap That Comes With Success

None of this is uncomplicated, and it’s worth naming the cracks in the governors’ model before treating it as a template. First, both Whitmer and Beshear are widely discussed as 2028 presidential prospects, which means every message they test carries a second, less charitable read: that they’re building a national brand for themselves as much as fixing the party’s midterm messaging problem. Second, Whitmer’s own standing took a real hit within her party’s activist wing after an April Oval Office meeting with Trump that many Democrats saw as legitimizing an administration the rest of this series has spent months cataloguing as corrosive to democratic norms — a reminder that “talk like a normal human being” and “meet with the person your base considers an active threat to the country” can pull in genuinely opposite directions, not just stylistically different ones.

Sand’s model carries a different risk, and the Iowa GOP’s “better brand” attack line has now made that risk explicit rather than merely theoretical, as it was when this series first flagged it with skepticism in Part II. A unity ritual only works if it’s backed by substance, and if de-emphasizing the party label becomes indistinguishable, in the minds of the very voters Sand is courting, from hiding an unchanged liberal record behind folksy packaging, the strategy collapses into exactly the “liberal with a better brand” caricature Cichy is already selling. So far, by most independent accounts of his town halls, Sand pairs the ritual and the low-key partisan branding with concrete policy discussion on data centers, education funding, and carbon pipelines rather than letting the song, or the plaid shirt, stand in for substance. Whether that discipline holds for the remaining months of a genuinely contentious general election against a Trump-aligned opponent, and whether it can actually close a 180,000-voter registration gap rather than merely soften the room in the moment, is the open question this series will need to revisit as November approaches.

What both governors share, and what Washington’s Senate leadership conspicuously lacks right now, is a coherent theory of the audience they’re trying to reach and a willingness to test that theory in rooms full of voters who don’t already agree with them — Republicans in Grundy County, Republicans across the aisle from Whitmer in Lansing — rather than in a primary electorate that, as Part I of this series argued at length, keeps mistaking its own intensity for a representative sample of the state. If “Loyal Opposition” as a governing instinct is going to be built anywhere in this party before November, the evidence so far suggests it’s being built in state capitals, not in Washington.
