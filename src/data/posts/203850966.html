The coating was called Heritage Teal. It was not teal and it was not heritage. For eleven days it commanded attention. On the twelfth day, the algae arrived.

The fountain in the Elizabethan Gardens has been the colour of fountains since 1965.

This is to say it has been the colour of water in a stone basin under an Ontario sky — which is grey when the sky is grey, blue-ish when the sky is blue, green at the edges where the algae lives the controlled life that algae lives in a well-maintained ornamental fountain, and brown for approximately three days every autumn when the maples drop their leaves into it faster than the Horticultural Society can fish them out. It is not a dramatic fountain. It is a modest stone basin with a single central jet, installed by the Horticultural Society in 1965 to mark the Gardens’ tenth anniversary, and it has performed its function — which is to make a pleasant sound and give the eye somewhere to rest — without complaint or incident for sixty-one years.

In May, Bradley Fenn proposed to improve it.

The proposal arrived, as Bradley’s proposals arrive, with slides.

The slides showed the fountain as it was — grey stone, clear water, the central jet — and the fountain as it could be, rendered by a design consultant from Mississauga in a colour the slides described as “Heritage Teal,” which was not teal and was not heritage, but was a bright saturated blue-green of the kind that looks magnificent in a rendering under rendered light and looks, in reality, like a swimming pool that has made a decision.

Bradley explained that the fountain, in its current state, was “underperforming as a visual asset.” He said that the Gardens drew significant tourist traffic, that the fountain was a focal point of that traffic, and that a focal point should command attention rather than merely existing. He said the Heritage Teal coating would make the fountain “Instagram-forward,” a term he used without apparent awareness that he was the only person in the room for whom it was not a small death to hear.

He said the coating was a proven marine-grade product. He said it would last for years. He said the application was straightforward.

Gerald noted the word straightforward. He had heard it before, in connection with the green, and he had since developed a settled relationship with the word, which was that when Bradley used it about a physical alteration to a public asset, the alteration would not be straightforward, and the degree of non-straightforwardness would be proportional to Bradley’s confidence.

Bradley was very confident.

The Horticultural Society opposed the coating.

The Society’s chair — a woman named Eleanor Vance who has chaired the Society for nineteen years and who maintains the Gardens with the combination of expertise and stubbornness that all good gardens require — explained, at the committee meeting, that the fountain’s stone was porous, that the original 1965 installation had been designed to let the stone breathe, that a marine-grade coating would seal the surface, and that sealing a porous stone fountain in an Ontario climate that freezes and thaws forty times a winter was, in her professional assessment, “a way to destroy a fountain slowly and at considerable expense.”

She said this clearly. She had the 1965 specifications. Gerald had given her a copy from his files, because she had asked, and because the specifications were exactly the kind of thing Gerald keeps for exactly the kind of moment that had now arrived.

Bradley said the marine-grade product was specifically designed for harsh conditions.

Eleanor said the harsh conditions the product was designed for were saltwater and sunlight, not the freeze-thaw cycle of a Perth County winter, and that she would welcome the consultant’s data on freeze-thaw performance if the consultant had any.

The consultant, attending remotely, said he would follow up with the data.

The consultant did not follow up with the data.

The committee approved the coating anyway, on Bradley’s recommendation, by a margin of one vote.

The coating was applied in June.

It looked, for approximately eleven days, magnificent. The fountain, rendered in Heritage Teal, did command attention. Tourists photographed it. It appeared on the Elsinore tourism Facebook page, and on several visitors’ own feeds, and Bradley circulated a note to the committee describing the coating as “an early and visible success” and attaching the photographs as evidence.

On the twelfth day, the algae arrived.

Not the controlled edge-algae of a well-maintained fountain. A bloom. The marine-grade coating, it emerged, had sealed the stone, and the sealed stone had changed the water chemistry, and the changed water chemistry, in the warm still water of a June fountain under a sealed surface, was precisely what an algal bloom requires in order to become an algal bloom. The Heritage Teal disappeared beneath a film of vivid green. The film thickened. The fountain, which had made a pleasant sound and given the eye somewhere to rest for sixty-one years, now made the same sound while looking like something the Horticultural Society would, in any other context, have been called in to remediate.

Eleanor Vance did not say she had predicted this.

She had predicted this. She had the meeting minutes in which she had predicted it. But she did not say so, because Eleanor Vance is a gardener, and gardeners understand that being right about a plant’s death is not the same as having a living plant, and that the satisfaction of the former is no substitute for the loss of the latter.

She simply began, quietly, the work of remediation, which would involve draining the fountain, stripping the coating — a process considerably less straightforward than the application had been — and restoring the stone to the breathing, porous, modest grey condition in which it had successfully been a fountain since 1965.

The sealed stone changed the water chemistry. The bloom was a consequence of the coating. There was no vandalism. Eleanor Vance had predicted it, in writing, in the minutes. The swans would not go near it.

The blame, when it was assigned, was not assigned to the coating.

Bradley’s report to the committee described the algal bloom as “an unforeseen environmental event” and noted that “vandalism could not be ruled out,” a sentence that Eleanor Vance read twice and then set down with the care of a woman setting down something she would otherwise throw.

There had been no vandalism. The fountain had done exactly what Eleanor had said a sealed porous fountain would do in exactly the timeframe a biologist would have predicted. But “vandalism could not be ruled out” appeared in the official report, and once it appeared in the official report it existed, in the way that things in official reports exist, available to be cited later by anyone who preferred the vandalism explanation to the sealed-stone explanation, which was anyone who had voted for the coating.

Norman Stalker covered the fountain in the Beacon. He quoted Bradley’s report, including the vandalism sentence. He then quoted Eleanor Vance, who said: “The fountain was sealed. Sealed stone holds water differently. The bloom was a consequence of the coating. There was no vandalism. I have the specifications.”

He quoted the 1965 specifications.

He let the two accounts sit beside each other on the page, which is what Norman does when the facts are clear and the official version is not, and trusted Elsinore to do its arithmetic.

Constance Hay did her arithmetic. She cut out the piece. She placed it on the refrigerator, beside the others.

A Note for the Record

There is a particular sequence that public improvements sometimes follow. First, a thing that works is declared to be underperforming. Then it is altered, dramatically and at expense, in a way that experts warn against and that proceeds anyway. Then the alteration fails, in precisely the way the experts predicted. Then the failure is attributed to anything other than the alteration — to vandals, to saboteurs, to unforeseen events, to anyone who can be blamed without admitting that the thing that worked should have been left alone.

To the south, the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool — which had reflected the Memorial and the sky and the monument since 1922, and which had last been renovated, competently, in 2012 — was recently resurfaced and coated, at the president’s direction, in a colour described as “American Flag Blue.” The renovation faced setbacks. An algal bloom appeared. The basin developed problems. And the president attributed the failure not to the renovation but to “leftist” vandals, who had, in this telling, sabotaged the pool, rather than the pool having responded to its resurfacing in the way that water responds to having its chemistry changed by people who wanted it to be a more dramatic colour than reflecting pools are.

A reflecting pool is not supposed to command attention. It is supposed to reflect. Its whole function is to disappear, to become the sky and the monument and the space between them. Painting it a bold colour does not improve it. It defeats it. A reflecting pool that you notice is a reflecting pool that has stopped working.

In Elsinore, Eleanor Vance is stripping the coating from the fountain. She has the 1965 specifications. She had predicted the bloom, in writing, before it happened. She has not said so, because she would rather have the fountain back than be proven right about its destruction.

The fountain will be grey again by autumn. It will make a pleasant sound. It will give the eye somewhere to rest. It will not command attention, because that was never its job.

The swans, who had given the Heritage Teal fountain a wide berth throughout — they would not approach the new colour, which the Swan Warden noted in her log — will return to its edge once it is grey.

They knew. They always know. They are not consulted, but they observe, and they had observed enough to stay away from a fountain that had stopped being a fountain and become, for one expensive summer, a statement.
