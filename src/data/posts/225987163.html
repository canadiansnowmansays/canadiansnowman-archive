The last part of this series ended in Strasbourg, in September, with Mark Carney declining a word Brussels had offered him. Two months on, the trade war with Washington is still running. Since this series last looked at it, the talks collapsed in August, the tariffs went up, and in early November the talks quietly started again. On Wednesday, November 18, on the edge of the Asia-Pacific summit in Shenzhen, the two leaders sat down together for the first time this year.

Nobody had planned the meeting, or at least nobody admitted to it in advance. What came out of it is less a deal than a set of positions, each stated more plainly than before. One of them is the best line Carney has delivered in this fight.

I. Twenty minutes in Shenzhen

The meeting was not on Carney’s public itinerary, The Canadian Press reported, and his aides had said beforehand that they did not expect one. A year earlier in South Korea, the two men had managed only an informal word over dinner, Ian Austen noted in The New York Times. This time they sat down with their teams for about twenty minutes, according to CBC News.

Only one side said what happened. Carney’s office reported that the two leaders “discussed the work underway to reach a mutually beneficial trade agreement.” His own post on X, quoted by The Tribune, added the phrase that has been in every Canadian statement since the August collapse: any agreement must fully respect Canada’s languages, culture and sovereignty. The White House said nothing. Readers of The Shenzhen Opera will know that this was no special snub to Canada. On the whole trip, the White House issued no formal readout of Trump’s meetings.

At a news conference beforehand, Carney had played down what Washington supposedly cared about most. In Trump’s “hierarchy of issues,” he said of Canada’s dealings with China, “it’s not the top issue at all.” Afterwards, he called the progress “two steps forward, one step back type thing,” but insisted “we have been making progress.” Talks were possible, he said, “in the context of the new U.S. trade policy,” and a deal would happen “only if that deal is in the interest of Canadians.”

The most accurate summary came two days later: beyond a commitment to keep talking and stay in close contact, the meeting produced no progress at all, as Tiago Ventura wrote in Time.

II. Where the fight stands

It helps to remember how much has happened since Part XXXI.

In August the talks broke down, each side accusing the other of last-minute changes. Carney said the American auto proposals would “gradually dismantle” Canadian production, The Associated Press reported, and Trump answered with 50 per cent tariffs on Canadian vehicles, parts and steel, due to take effect at the start of next year. Part XXV of this series called that the price of having said no.

In October Ottawa stopped waiting. Carney and Doug Ford announced an $8.7 billion federal-provincial plan for Ontario’s steel, auto and critical-minerals industries, including a new Joint Steel Action Fund with five-year commitments from both governments, according to the Prime Minister’s Office. Carney’s explanation was a single line, reported by The Associated Press: an integrated North American auto industry would benefit both countries, but Washington’s priorities had moved. “Their approach has changed,” he said. “So must ours.”

In early November the talks resumed, after Trump backed off some of the secondary irritants, including the streaming rules that require Canadian and French-language content, Austen reported. Goods that qualify under the continental trade agreement remain effectively exempt. But autos, steel and aluminium still face tariffs of up to 50 per cent. The day before Shenzhen, Dominic LeBlanc, the minister responsible for Canada-U.S. trade, said he expected the American tariffs on autos and steel to stay in place, according to Newsquawk.

That is the real shape of the fight going into Shenzhen. About four-fifths of the trade between the two countries flows tariff-free. The fight is concentrated in a handful of industries where the damage is greatest, and those are the ones Washington will not move on.

III. “We’re busy”

A few hours after the meeting, Carney appeared by video from Shenzhen at The New York Times DealBook Summit, and gave Andrew Ross Sorkin the fullest account he has offered of where Canada stands. The transcript repays reading in full, and Ian Austen covered it for The New York Times. His summary: any resolution needs a major change of policy in Washington.

He opened with a joke at Washington’s expense. Asked where the trade negotiations stood, he said they were going “exceptionally well”: an agreement had been reached, with tariffs cut on more than 90 per cent of the irritants, and the talks had been professional and orderly from start to finish. Then he explained that he was talking about China. With the United States, he said, “we continue to have a series of issues.”

On the evening’s meeting, he was careful. “We had a very nice discussion this evening. So I’ll hang on to that.” But, asked whether anything had changed in Canada’s view of the United States, he said no. What would change views, he said, would be “policy decisions that are taken and that stick.” Sorkin quoted Trump’s claim last month that Washington holds “all the cards” and asked what Canada’s were. “Our cards is that everyone else in the world wants to deal with us,” Carney replied. He listed the rest: the strongest fiscal position in the G7, an investment boom in energy, and non-U.S. exports at their highest share of Canadian trade in 40 years. And he named a population united behind the strategy.

He described Trump in private more warmly than many Canadians might expect. “He’s very different inside the room,” he said. The president listens far more in private, he said, and is “interested genuinely in your opinion.” But warmth was not the issue. Asked for a timetable, Carney said, “Look, it should have been done six months ago,” and added that Canada would not sign something that did not make sense for it.

Then came the line that will outlast the summit. He listed what Ottawa had been doing while Washington hesitated: two trade agreements in the past two days, one with the ten-member ASEAN group and one with the Philippines, a deal with India expected within three months, and free trade with two billion consumers outside the United States. And he finished: “So, we would like to do the deal, but if the U.S. doesn’t want to do the deal, we’re busy.”

It is worth being clear about what that sentence is and isn’t. It is not a threat. Carney did not say Canada would walk away, retaliate or seek a new partner. It is a description of opportunity cost, delivered in the voice of a central banker. Every month Washington spends not making a deal is a month Canada spends making them with someone else. The leverage it claims is not that Canada can hurt the United States. It is that Canada can do without it in more places than it could a year ago.

IV. The Navarro problem

Not everyone in Washington took it as Carney meant it.

The week before Shenzhen, Carney had said again that without relief on autos, Canada would look to China, Japan and Europe. Peter Navarro, Trump’s trade adviser, answered on social media: “Are u really threatening to play China off the U.S.? This is offensive in the extreme. You should be far more worried China is going to eat Canada alive.” Pete Hoekstra, the American ambassador to Canada, shared the post, Austen reported.

Navarro’s reading is the one the administration finds useful, and it rests on a misreading of what Canada is doing with China. In Shenzhen, Carney contrasted the “very professional” Chinese negotiations with the still-elusive American deal, as The Globe and Mail reported. But the deal he was praising rolls back Chinese tariffs on about $400 million a year of Canadian pork and seafood. That is a rounding error beside the hundreds of billions that cross the Canada-U.S. border. Carney told Sorkin that Canada trades no part of its intelligence infrastructure with China, and that if Canada sells canola to China, “I don’t think that’s compromising our sovereignty.” Selling China a satellite system for military use would be a different matter, he said, and Canada is not doing it. On the electric vehicles Canada now admits from China at a 6 per cent tariff, he said the arrangement is capped at 49,000 cars a year, below 3 per cent of the market.

His own public will not let him go much further. The Angus Reid Institute found that only 14 per cent of Canadians favour closer trade ties with China, against 58 per cent who want them with Europe. Carney’s answer to that poll is that the deepest new partnership he is building is with Europe, which is where Part XXXI left off. China gets canola, pork and seafood. Europe gets the defence industry, the research, the regulatory alignment and the satellites.

Navarro’s warning is therefore aimed at a strategy Canada is not pursuing. Its real effect is on the sequence. Every time Carney mentions any market other than the United States, someone in the administration hears a threat. That makes the obvious response to American pressure — find other customers — something Washington chooses to treat as an insult. Asked by Sorkin whether he trusts Xi more than Trump, Carney gave the only answer a Canadian leader can give: “We look for situations where there’s alignment, and we trust alignment.”

V. The sorting variable, again

Part XXV of this series argued that the variable deciding how Trump treats a country is not whether it is an ally or an adversary. It is whether it has pushed back. Countries that accepted his terms got the 15 per cent deals. Canada said no and got 50 per cent on the industries that matter most. Shenzhen fits the pattern.

It also shows the pattern’s limit. In September, Tom Nichols predicted in The Atlantic that Trump would eventually back down, “perhaps after declaring victory.” Something like that has begun. The streaming climbdown that Part XXVI recorded in August, when Jamieson Greer gave ground on French-language content, became a broader retreat in early November, when Trump dropped the secondary irritants to get talks going again. Nobody in Washington called it a concession. It was simply no longer mentioned. That is how this administration retreats, as this series has noted before: not repudiated, just quietly not repeated.

What has not moved are the sectoral tariffs on autos, steel and aluminium. Those are the ones that matter to Ontario, and they are the ones LeBlanc now expects to stay. The secondary irritants were always the part of the American position easiest to trade away, because they cost Washington nothing to drop. The core is different. Those tariffs reflect a decision, now visible in Carney’s own “their approach has changed,” that the White House would rather build cars in Michigan than share a continental industry with Ontario.

That is why Carney’s line about being busy matters more than his warm words about the room. If the sectoral tariffs are policy and not leverage, there is no concession Canada can make to remove them. The only rational answer is the one Ottawa has been giving since October: fund the industries at home, sell them abroad, and keep the door to Washington open without standing in it.

VI. The ledger

What Shenzhen produced, on the record:

  • A twenty-minute meeting, unscheduled, with a Canadian readout and no American one.

  • A commitment to keep talking, and nothing else.

  • A minister who expects the auto and steel tariffs to stay.

  • An American trade adviser who reads every Canadian sale abroad as a threat, and an ambassador who shares his posts.

  • A prime minister who described the president as personable and listening, and the deal as six months late.

And one sentence that sums up the position Canada has reached after more than a year and a half of this. Canada would like a deal. It does not need one on the terms offered, and it is no longer waiting. Washington’s leverage was always the assumption that Canada had nowhere else to go. Carney’s answer, delivered from China to an audience in New York, is that the assumption has been tested, and Canada is busy proving it wrong.
