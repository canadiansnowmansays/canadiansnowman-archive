Every previous instalment of this series has examined a specific cost — mortgage rates, tariffs, groceries, housing, credit — in relative isolation from the paycheck meant to cover it. This instalment closes that gap and asks the question underlying all of them: are wages, for most Americans, actually keeping pace with the costs this series has spent six weeks documenting? For most of the period since the 2022 inflation peak, the honest answer has been a qualified yes. As of this summer, that answer changed.

The number that flipped

The Indeed Hiring Lab’s analysis of the government’s second-quarter Employment Cost Index data found that real, inflation-adjusted annual wage growth for private-sector workers turned negative — down 0.4 percent year over year — for the first time since 2022. Nominal wage growth, the raw dollar figure before adjusting for prices, actually slowed to 3.1 percent from 3.5 percent a year earlier, even as inflation itself reaccelerated over the same period, driven in part by the higher energy costs tied to the ongoing Iran conflict this series has referenced in nearly every prior instalment. The combination — cooling pay growth meeting reheating prices — is precisely the scissor that produces a real wage decline, and it is the first time American workers have experienced that scissor closing in four years.

This is worth setting against the more reassuring headline figures that have circulated this year. A separate, broader measure tracking average weekly wages found real wage growth of just 0.09 percent between July 2025 and July 2026 — technically positive, technically outpacing inflation, but by a margin so thin it amounts to roughly one additional dollar a week for the average worker. The Bureau of Labor Statistics’ own real earnings data for the same period found real average hourly earnings actually decreased 0.2 percent year over year, even as real weekly earnings ticked up fractionally due to slightly longer average workweeks — meaning some of the reported “gain” reflects people working more hours rather than earning more per hour. Different methodologies, different vintages of data, and slightly different conclusions — but all of them describe an economy where the traditional expectation of the post-2022 recovery, that wages would meaningfully outpace inflation and restore lost purchasing power, has stalled out rather than continued.

It is worth holding this against the fuller multi-year picture, because a single-quarter reversal reads differently against a backdrop of prior gains than against one of prior losses. Cumulative nominal wage growth from 2020 through 2024 ran to roughly 22 percent, against cumulative inflation of about 18.9 percent over the same period — a real cumulative gain of a little over 3 percent, which sounds modest but conceals a far bumpier path than the summary number suggests. Real wages fell sharply in both 2021 and 2022, by 2.3 and 2.9 percent respectively, as the initial inflation surge outran pay growth; only in 2023 and 2024 did wages finally regain that lost ground, edging into narrowly positive real growth. The current reversal, in other words, is not occurring from a position of several years of comfortable, uninterrupted gains — it is occurring just as workers had barely finished clawing back the ground lost in the initial 2021–2022 inflation shock, before that ground could be meaningfully built upon.

Why the average conceals more than it reveals

As with the grocery data examined in Part IV, a single national average wage figure flattens an experience that varies enormously by sector, income level, and job tenure — and the variation matters more here than almost anywhere else in this series, because it determines which households are actually exposed to the wage stagnation described above. Nominal average hourly earnings for private-sector workers have risen from a 2019 baseline of roughly $28.00 an hour to $37.64 by June 2026 — a 34 percent increase that sounds substantial in isolation, but which, once measured against cumulative inflation over the same seven-year span, leaves real wages only slightly positive since 2019. Within that average, lower-wage workers actually saw the fastest nominal percentage gains over the period, a genuine bright spot reflecting a historically tight labor market for entry-level and lower-wage positions in the years immediately following the pandemic. Middle-income workers, by contrast, have been the most squeezed cohort throughout — caught between lower-wage gains driven by labor shortages at the bottom of the pay scale and the specialized skill premiums that have continued flowing to the top, without either dynamic working meaningfully in their favor.

The gap between the fastest- and slowest-growing industries by wage growth — utilities at 4.1 percent versus accommodation and food services at 2.6 percent — has actually narrowed this year, from 2.5 percentage points a year ago to 1.5 points now, a modest convergence that sounds like good news until you notice it is converging toward the middle from both directions: faster-growing sectors slowing down, rather than slower-growing sectors catching up.

The starkest divide, though, runs through the labor market’s most basic mechanism for getting a raise: changing jobs. Workers who switch employers see wage gains averaging 8 to 12 percent above inflation, according to ADP Research Institute data, compared with just 1 to 3 percent for workers who stay in place — meaning job mobility has functioned as one of the only reliable hedges against real wage erosion in this environment, and workers without the ability or willingness to switch jobs — those with specialized skills tied to a single employer, caregiving responsibilities that limit flexibility, or simply risk aversion in an uncertain labor market — have been left disproportionately exposed to the stagnation described above. Separately, research on salary negotiation found that only 37 percent of workers negotiate salary even when starting an entirely new job, meaning a meaningful share of the workforce is leaving even the job-switching hedge partially on the table.

Rent and electricity, not the average, define the actual squeeze

There is a further, more granular problem with treating any single wage-versus-CPI comparison as the final word: the basket of goods that determines the headline inflation figure is not the basket any individual household actually buys, and the categories rising fastest are, disproportionately, the ones this series has already spent five instalments examining in detail. Rent has risen roughly 44 percent since January 2019, and electricity roughly 52 percent over a comparable period, according to tracking that compares category-specific inflation against the average hourly wage baseline — both categories rising dramatically faster than the average wage gain over the same window, and both categories that a household cannot meaningfully reduce spending on the way it might cut back on discretionary purchases.

A worker whose personal spending skews toward housing and utilities, which is to say nearly every worker, has very likely experienced an effective inflation rate running well above the reassuring headline CPI number, for the simple reason that the headline number is an average across categories including many — electronics, for instance — that have gotten cheaper over the same period and pull the average down.

This is, in effect, the wage-side mirror of the grocery-basket point made in Part IV: an average is only reassuring to the household whose actual spending resembles the average, and the households under the most financial pressure are disproportionately the ones whose spending is concentrated in exactly the categories rising fastest.

What this means heading into November

Set against the polling data examined in Part II — the dead-even split over which party has better answers on the economy, the majority of voters naming cost of living as their top concern — a real wage decline landing now, six weeks before the midterms, after four years in which real wages had at least nominally kept pace with prices, is about as unfavorable a piece of timing as a governing party could face. It directly undercuts the “I’ve won affordability” framing traced in Part II in the most concrete way possible: it is no longer simply that voters feel their cost of living has not improved. By the government’s own most recent data, their paychecks, adjusted for what those paychecks can actually buy, have started shrinking again.

Whether this proves to be a brief statistical wobble or the start of a sustained trend is not yet knowable from a single quarter’s data, and this series will not overstate a one-quarter reading into a permanent verdict. But the direction, arriving at this particular moment in the electoral calendar, is the kind of fact that tends to reach a kitchen table long before it reaches a campaign talking point — which is, after all, the entire premise this series was built on.
