Autonomous AI agents answering emails, generating content, making decisions. Computers talking to computers to train computers to sound more human. The Inversion, updated.

In 2018, the writer Max Read writing in the New York Intelligencer, argued that the internet had crossed a threshold he called “the Inversion.” Bots had not merely proliferated — they had come to constitute the internet. They outnumbered humans. And in outnumbering humans, they loosened everyone’s grasp on reality. “What’s gone from the internet, after all, isn’t ‘truth,’ but trust,” Read wrote. “The sense that the people and things we encounter are what they represent themselves to be.”

Charlie Warzel, writing in the Atlantic in May 2026, revisited Read’s framework and found it quaint. The Inversion was about fake accounts and bot traffic. What has arrived since is something structurally different: autonomous AI agents roaming the internet, answering emails, sending texts, deleting code repositories, generating music and video and prose, building websites, making transactions, solving geometry conjectures. Those outputs ride the rails of an internet controlled by black-box algorithms. Computers talk to computers, producing information to train computers to sound more like humans. Humans type into the box, scroll, and wait.

Warzel’s name for the condition is precise: a crisis of agency.

Not agency in the Silicon Valley sense — the venture capital hierarchy where “high-agency” individuals sit at the top and everyone else is warned they will become “the permanent underclass.” Agency in the human sense: the feeling of directing your own life, making your own choices, understanding the world well enough to act in it. The feeling that what you do matters. The feeling that is slipping away.

This essay is about that feeling, and why it is the Asimov Gap stated as an individual experience.

The Reversal

Warzel identifies the structural cause with a precision that earns the record.

In 2011, the writer Paul Ford described the internet as a “customer service medium.” Humans, Ford argued, have a fundamental need to be consulted, engaged, to exercise their knowledge. The internet tapped into that need more effectively than any medium before it. Wikipedia, Reddit, comment sections, user-generated content — all of it was built on the premise that people wanted to participate, to contribute, to be asked.

Silicon Valley took Ford’s insight to heart. And then it built the opposite.

Generative AI is, as Warzel writes, “arguably the most impressive customer-service medium in the history of our species — an infinitely scalable, highly personalized answer machine that flatters our insecurities and mimics our idiosyncrasies.” But the system “automates, and even negates, the human need to be consulted.” The chatbot does not ask for your opinion. It provides a canonical answer. The agent does not invite your participation. It performs the task. The efficiency that the companies celebrate is, from the user’s perspective, the removal of the reason to be involved.

The tool that promises to make you more capable makes you less capable of functioning without it. High-agency builders constructing a low-agency world.

The shift is from active to passive. The internet once invited you to explore. AI invites you to receive. The search engine returned ten links and let you choose. The chatbot returns one answer and asks if you need anything else. The social media feed once surfaced content from people you followed. The algorithmic feed surfaces content selected by machines, reacted to by machines, generated by machines. The human in the loop — the industry’s term for a person supervising an AI system — sounds active. In practice, as Warzel notes, what humans are doing is reacting to an interaction between multiple machines.

The writer Sam Kriss described the experience of scrolling through the current internet: “The more I clicked around, the more I started to panic. There was nothing, no human voices anywhere, just thousands of versions of the same cheery demon. Am I alone out here? Something’s happened to the world; it’s all gone flimsy.”

Flimsy. That is the word for what the crisis of agency feels like at the level of daily experience. Not catastrophic. Not apocalyptic. Flimsy. The ground under your feet is not gone. It is less solid than it was. The information you receive might be real. It might not. The song you’re listening to might be human. It might not. The article you’re reading might have been written by someone who cared about the words. Or it might have been generated, humanised by a program called Walter Writes AI, and uploaded for a small fee. You cannot tell. The inability to tell is the condition.

The Backlash as Agency

Warzel makes a connection at the end of his essay that ties the crisis of agency directly to the material this series has been tracking.

People exercising agency in the one place it still works: the physical world. Town halls and protests against AI data centers — slow, inefficient, stubbornly human. The anti-chatbot.

“It is no surprise,” he writes, “that the backlash to AI, particularly the opposition to data centers, has come in the form of protests and public comments at town and city-council meetings across the country. People are taking up their agency in one of the few places they can: the physical world.”

The 142 data centre activist groups documented in Part 39. The 70 percent of Americans who oppose construction in their communities. The $41.7 billion in cancelled projects in Q1 2026. The Indianapolis councilman whose home was shot up. The communities across 24 states showing up at zoning meetings they had never attended before. All of this is, in Warzel’s framework, not merely opposition to data centres. It is the assertion of agency by people who have been told — by the discourse, by the companies, by the products — that their role is to receive, not to participate.

The town council meeting is the anti-chatbot. It is slow, inefficient, procedurally tedious, and fundamentally human. It requires showing up in person, speaking into a microphone, waiting your turn, listening to your neighbours. It does not scale. It cannot be automated. It is, in the most literal sense, a human-in-the-loop system in which the human is actually in the loop — not reacting to machines but directing the outcome.

The irony is now visible enough to be documented. Kaitlyn Tiffany, reporting in the Atlantic in June 2026, found that much of the online anti-AI activism is itself AI-generated. In local Facebook groups opposing data centres across America, she found posts using AI-generated images of pristine farmland — mowed messages against data centre development, nostalgic pastoral scenes with state-specific captions that turn out to be identical across Pennsylvania, Kentucky, Michigan, and Texas, varying only in the state name. One post helpfully left the AI prompt visible: “Here’s a Michigan version in the same style.” The anti-AI AI slop was produced, in many cases, by content farms in Bangladesh monetising Facebook’s engagement algorithm — people with no stake in Indiana’s cornfields generating fake Indiana cornfield sentiment because outrage about AI data centres gets clicks from both the credulous and the angry.

The absurdity is almost too complete to parse. People exercising their genuine democratic concern about AI — the Bad Feeling made political — are having that concern expressed online in their name by AI tools operated by people who don’t care about the issue at all. The crisis of agency has infected the response to the crisis of agency. The one space that the backlash was supposed to be real — the physical world, the zoning meeting, the protest sign — remains stubbornly human. The online expression of it has already been colonised.

Pope Leo, in Magnifica Humanitas, wrote that “most people are watching and waiting, observing from afar and merely hoping for the best.” The people at the zoning meetings are not watching and waiting. They have decided that passivity is not an option. They have decided that their agency — the capacity to direct what happens in their community — is worth exercising, even if the technology arriving in their town was built by companies whose combined market capitalisation exceeds the GDP of most nations.

The crisis of agency and the assertion of agency are happening simultaneously. The chatbot removes the need to be consulted. The town council meeting restores it. The algorithm decides what you see. The protest sign decides what the council sees. Both are real. Both are happening now. The question is which one the political system responds to.

The “High-Agency” Inversion

There is an irony in the agency crisis that Warzel identifies but does not fully develop, and that belongs in this series because it connects the crisis to the governance argument.

Silicon Valley has made “high agency” a status marker. In builder circles, to be high-agency is to be individualistic, ambitious, decisive — a person who marshals people and machines to accomplish things. The implication is clear: those with high agency are insulated from replacement. Those without it are, in the Bay Area coinage Warzel cites, “the permanent underclass.”

But the technology these high-agency individuals are building is systematically removing agency from everyone else. The agent that codes for you removes the agency of learning to code. The chatbot that answers for you removes the agency of forming your own view. The algorithm that curates for you removes the agency of discovering something unexpected. The AI that brainstorms for you removes the agency of sitting with a problem long enough to have an original thought.

The Carnegie Mellon study documented in Part 39 found that ten minutes of AI use measurably degraded problem-solving ability. That is the crisis of agency stated as a cognitive finding. The tool that promises to make you more capable makes you less capable of functioning without it. The high-agency builders are constructing a low-agency world.

This is not a conspiracy. It is an incentive structure. The companies are rewarded for engagement, for usage, for the number of tasks their agents perform. Every task the agent performs is a task the human does not perform. Every task the human does not perform is a capacity the human loses. The incentive to build agents that do more is the incentive to build humans who can do less. The crisis of agency is the business model working as designed.

The Asimov Frame

Asimov’s Three Laws were designed to preserve human agency. The First Law — a robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm — was not merely about physical safety. In the stories, “harm” extended to psychological and social harm. The robots in Asimov’s fiction were constrained from actions that would diminish human capacity, human autonomy, human self-determination. The Laws existed so that the technology served the human — not the other way around.

The AI industry has no First Law. The agents are not constrained from diminishing human capacity. The chatbots are not constrained from replacing human judgment. The algorithms are not constrained from removing human choice. The technology is built to perform tasks that humans used to perform, and it is measured by how many of those tasks it can absorb. There is no structural mechanism — no law, no regulation, no enforceable constraint — that requires the technology to preserve human agency as a design goal.

Warzel’s essay ends with a warning that deserves the last word: “What’s at stake is not just truth or trust, but also a sense of direction, orientation, and purpose for all of us.”

Direction. Orientation. Purpose. These are the words for what agency provides. Without them, you are not oppressed. You are not enslaved. You are adrift. The crisis of agency is not dramatic. It is ambient. It is the feeling of control slipping — not being seized, not being taken, but slipping — “ever so slightly away.”

The Pope said humanity flourishes through limitations. The limitations are what require agency — the effort of confronting a problem, the friction of learning, the struggle of making something with your own hands and mind. Remove the limitations and you remove the need for agency. Remove the need for agency and you remove, as Leo warned, something that “would no longer be human.”

The laws that should preserve human agency in the age of AI do not exist. The crisis is not coming. It is here. The people at the zoning meetings know it. The student who said “I’m just using the human mind” knows it. The question is whether the political system will build the structures to protect what the technology is quietly dissolving — before the flimsy becomes permanent.

Flimsy. The ground under your feet is not gone — it is less solid than it was. You cannot tell what is real, what is human, what is generated. The inability to tell is the condition.
