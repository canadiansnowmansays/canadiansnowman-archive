Stephen Gordon July 17, 2026

Before he was the man not investigating the deaths of Renee Good and Alex Pretti, Todd Blanche was the man who kept Donald Trump out of prison. It is worth understanding him in that order, because the second job explains the first.

Blanche is 51, born in Denver to a Canadian hockey player and a nurse, raised in the suburbs there before college at American University and a law degree earned the hard way — night classes at Brooklyn Law School while working days as a paralegal in the U.S. Attorney’s Office for the Southern District of New York. He clerked for two federal judges who now sit on the Second Circuit. He spent eight years as a prosecutor in that same Manhattan office, working violent crime: rape, murder, the cases that make careers or break people. By every account, he was good at it.

Then, in 2014, he left. Private practice, first at WilmerHale, then in 2017 at Cadwalader, Wickersham & Taft, where he made partner in white-collar defense. This is the ordinary arc of a certain kind of prosecutor: build a reputation putting people away, then spend the second half of a career defending people from the system you used to run. Nothing in it is disqualifying on its own. What makes Blanche’s second act different is who hired him.

The Client Who Became the Boss

In 2019, Blanche represented Paul Manafort, Trump’s former campaign chairman, and won — got a Manhattan mortgage-fraud case against Manafort thrown out on double-jeopardy grounds, arguing it duplicated the federal case that had already sent Manafort to prison. That case is the hinge of Blanche’s whole career, because it is where Trump’s orbit first noticed him.

In 2023, Blanche resigned from Cadwalader to join Trump's defense team directly, just ahead of the hush-money arraignment in Manhattan. He led that defense through conviction — Trump found guilty on 34 felony counts — and he represented Trump as well in the two federal cases brought by special counsel Jack Smith, both of which were dropped once Trump won a second term. Blanche's own gloss on the hush-money verdict, offered afterward, was that voters had gotten to see the case and had decided for themselves. That is a defense lawyer's answer. It is also, unmistakably, a loyalist's answer, delivered by a man who had just watched his client get convicted of a felony and called it vindication.

Nine days after Trump won reelection in November 2024, he named Blanche his nominee for deputy attorney general — not attorney general, not yet, but the operational number two, the job that actually runs the department day to day while the attorney general does the talking. The Senate confirmed him in March 2025, by a bare 52–46, largely along party lines, after a confirmation process in which Blanche was asked directly and repeatedly about his independence from the president who had, until recently, been paying his legal bills. He did not offer much. He did say, on the record, that he believed the department had been weaponized under the previous administration. That framing — weaponization — is not incidental. It is the same word his critics would soon start using about him.

The Deputy Who Ran the Show

As deputy attorney general under Pam Bondi, Blanche became one of the department’s most visible public faces — a frequent presence on Sunday shows, the man who actually managed operations while Bondi handled the politics of the podium. Readers of my Bondi series already know her side of this period in detail: the binders, the survivors left standing, the unravelling that ended with her firing. What matters for this series is Blanche’s side of that same desk. He was not a bystander to Bondi’s failures. He was the operational hand running the building underneath her, and when Trump went looking for someone to replace her, he did not look outside the department. He looked one office down the hall.

Also in this period: a memo, issued after Blanche took office but reportedly before he had divested his own cryptocurrency holdings, that wound down ongoing investigations into crypto businesses and scaled back enforcement against fraud and money laundering in that industry.

And the fuller story behind that $1.8 billion fund, which came into much sharper focus this week and deserves to be told properly rather than gestured at. It began with a lawsuit Trump himself filed against the IRS, seeking $10 billion over the leak of his own tax returns to reporters. In late May, Trump's DOJ — his own Justice Department, defending against his own lawsuit — settled it, agreeing to establish a $1.776 billion "anti-weaponization fund" to compensate people the administration considered wronged by prior investigations, including some convicted in the January 6 Capitol attack, while also granting Trump and his family sweeping insulation from future IRS audits. On July 13, U.S. District Judge Kathleen Williams, an Obama appointee sitting in Miami, threw the entire arrangement out in a 56-page order that does not mince words: the suit was brought for what she called an improper purpose, dressed up in the "imprimatur of judicial legitimacy" for a settlement that had, in her assessment, no viable basis in law or fact. Trump, she found, was suing an agency he himself controlled — the plaintiff and the defendant answering, ultimately, to the same man — which meant there was never any real adversarial relationship for a court to referee in the first place. She barred Trump from ever citing the settlement again in any official proceeding.

Then the order does something rarer still: it names names. Williams referred Trump's lawyer in the case, Alejandro Brito, to the Florida bar for possible discipline. And she directed that a copy of her ruling be sent to the State Bar of New York — of which Todd Blanche is a member — and to the D.C. Bar, of which Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward is a member, flagging both men's roles in signing off on a settlement she had just called collusive. That referral landed two days before Blanche walked into his own confirmation hearing, where, under questioning, he confirmed he still believes he will be vindicated on appeal.

There is one more detail worth adding, because it did not surface until Blanche testified under oath at that hearing. Sen. Sheldon Whitehouse read into the record a second line from Williams's order, describing her reaction to sworn testimony Blanche had given Congress in May, when he'd claimed the settlement had never been submitted to a court and there was "no mechanism" for judicial review. Williams called that answer "at best misleading and at worst disingenuous," noting that the court had in fact been available to review any filing by any party throughout the litigation. A federal judge does not reach for that language lightly, and she did not reach for it about the settlement alone. She reached for it about testimony the man asking to be the country's top law enforcement official gave to the United States Senate.

None of this, individually, is the story this series is about. I include it because it establishes a pattern before we ever get to Minneapolis: a Justice Department under Blanche’s operational control that moves fast and generates controversy when the beneficiaries are allies of the president, and moves slowly, opaquely, or not at all when the people asking for accountability are the president’s critics — or, as we will see, ordinary Americans shot by the president’s own agents.

“The President’s Right and Duty”

The clearest thing Blanche has ever said about how he sees his job did not come during a crisis. It came in an ordinary press exchange, when he was asked about the propriety of the Justice Department pursuing the president's political opponents. Blanche's answer was that it was the president's right, and his duty, to direct investigations into people Trump believed deserved scrutiny. Pressed further on politicization more broadly, Blanche pointed to Article II of the Constitution, arguing it gave the president authority to lead the country, as he put it, in all areas — including criminal justice.

That is not a rogue comment. It is a coherent legal theory, and Blanche is one of its architects. As Trump's defense counsel in Trump v. United States, he co-authored the Supreme Court brief arguing that a former president holds absolute immunity from criminal prosecution for official acts, unless first impeached and convicted by Congress. The Court substantially agreed. Blanche did not simply benefit from that doctrine as a lawyer. He now runs the department that has to decide, case by case, what counts as an official act — including, as it happens, the acts of ICE and CBP officers who report, through the chain of command, to the same executive branch Blanche has spent his career arguing should answer to almost no one else.

It is worth being precise about what that theory implies once you carry it past the president himself and down into the agencies he oversees. If the presidency itself is largely shielded from accountability for official acts, and if — as Blanche has said outright — it is the president's right and duty to direct the department's investigative priorities, then the logic extends naturally to the officers carrying out the president's enforcement agenda on the ground. An ICE agent who shoots someone during a deportation operation the president has publicly championed is, in this framing, exercising exactly the kind of official authority the theory was built to protect. This is not a conspiracy theory about Blanche's intentions. It is simply what follows, mechanically, from the argument he already made to the Supreme Court on the president's behalf. You do not need to guess why his department has been reluctant to investigate Renee Good's death with the urgency the Civil Rights Division once brought to cases like it. The theory of the office he now runs tells you why, in advance, before you look at a single memo.

That theory is not just a talking point. Part III follows it into the department's actual conduct this year — the internal emails that show exactly how the "weaponization" charge cuts both ways, and a parallel campaign against the press that Blanche has defended in his own words.

It also showed up, unprompted, in the hearing itself. Sen. John Kennedy of Louisiana, a Republican inclined to be friendly, lobbed Blanche a soft question: were he and the president friends? Blanche’s answer, verbatim, was that he had been the president’s lawyer and was now his deputy attorney general — present tense sliding toward past and back again in the same breath, the two roles blurred into one sentence by the man being asked to keep them separate. Nobody had to write that line for him. He offered it himself, under oath, on the record, at the exact hearing meant to determine whether he had ever really left the first job behind.

A note in passing: it is a strange thing, watching from Toronto, to see a country’s chief law enforcement officer be a man whose entire prior career was built on the premise that his client should not be prosecuted. That is not a criticism of defense lawyers, a profession democracies genuinely need. It is an observation about what happens when the defense lawyer never really stops being one, even after the client makes him attorney general.
