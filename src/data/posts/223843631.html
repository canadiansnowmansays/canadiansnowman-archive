In the first days of November 2006, President George W. Bush said he was keeping his defence secretary. Then Republicans lost both houses of Congress over a war in Iraq, and on the day after the election Donald Rumsfeld was gone, the first member of the cabinet to fall.

Hakeem Jeffries, who expects to become Speaker of the House in January, made the comparison himself. “Donald Rumsfeld was gone the day after the Bush administration was repudiated by the American people in the 2006 midterm election,” he told Helene Cooper, Greg Jaffe and Eric Schmitt of The New York Times for a November 6 report. “Why is Pete Hegseth still around?”

This series ended its planned run on October 30 with Part X, “The Audience of One.” Its argument was that Hegseth survives because he performs for a single reviewer, the President, and that a performance needs only one good review to keep running. The midterms tested that argument against a much larger audience, and Part XI followed Thomas Massie’s articles of impeachment to the House floor. This instalment records how both came out.

The verdict on his war

The audience voted. On November 3, 2026, Democrats won the House and the Senate. Inside the administration, the question of why was settled within a day. On November 4, Vice President JD Vance wrote that “the reason we lost is the war.” Several Pentagon officials, the Times reported, read that post as a shot aimed directly at Hegseth.

There is no mystery about what the war cost. It drove up the price of gas and groceries and depleted America’s stockpiles of weapons. David E. Sanger reported for The New York Times on November 5 that the Pentagon had told Congress the war had cost $47 billion so far. That figure leaves out the damage to American bases in the region, several of which, Pentagon officials concede, will probably never be rebuilt. Thirty-eight days of continuous bombing in the spring did not collapse the regime, recover Iran’s stockpile of nuclear fuel or produce the “unconditional surrender” the President once demanded. Congress has never passed additional money for the war, and the new Democratic majorities are unlikely to start.

Hegseth was the war’s loudest champion. Early on he told the country it was being fought with “brutal efficiency.” Eight months later it is the main reason his party lost.

The review that matters

The audience of one. None of that has reached the only reviewer Part X said counted. On election night Trump told Fox News he did not intend to change his cabinet. On November 4, asked about Hegseth specifically, he said: “I think he’s doing a very good job.” In private, according to two people who have spoken with him, he has blamed the defeat on his political team, not on Hegseth or Admiral Brad Cooper, the head of Central Command.

On November 6 he acted on that diagnosis. As Maggie Haberman, Jonathan Swan and Tyler Pager reported for The New York Times, Trump removed his chief of staff, Susie Wiles, and gave the job to Stephen Miller, who keeps a running dialogue with Hegseth. Hegseth greeted the news with a run of posts: “Huge CONGRATS,” then “Patriots in control,” then a photograph of the two men together. Miller’s own allies say he understands that the Iran war has been a political disaster. He has nonetheless inherited its loudest champion as a friend.

The chief of staff, in other words, paid for the election. The secretary who ran the war gained an ally in the office next to the President’s.

The performance continues. On November 5 Hegseth posted a video. “We don’t back down,” he said, “and that especially applies now.” He boasted of tearing down Democratic “sacred cows” such as diversity programmes and repeated his opposition to transgender troops. His chief spokesman, Sean Parnell, dismissed the coming investigations as “sham witch hunts” and said Hegseth was “100 percent committed” to leading the department until the end of Trump’s term.

The President kept applauding, if not always in tune. On November 8 he told Fox News that Hegseth was “one of the guys that was most responsible” for the war, as The New Republic reported. The same day he told reporters that Hegseth was “guilty of two things, basically winning two wars.” After the Secretary’s news conference on November 10, Trump graded the performance “A PLUS PLUS PLUS,” according to The New York Times. It is the same show this series has reviewed for eleven instalments, and it is still playing to the same seat.

The other audience

The impeachment. What changed on November 3 was the size of the hostile house on Capitol Hill, though not yet its votes. Part XI followed Massie’s eight articles, which accuse Hegseth of taking the country to war in Iran without authorisation, of extrajudicial killings at sea and of retaliating against Senator Mark Kelly of Arizona. On November 10 the House voted 211 to 204 to send them to the Judiciary Committee instead of debating them. Massie was the only Republican to vote against, as Stars and Stripes reported. None of the Republicans who had spent the week sharing their doubts with reporters crossed over.

Massie predicted the manoeuvre in September, when he said a procedural vote would serve “to insulate the members of this body from any accountability.” That is what it did. Hegseth called the effort “a farce” and said of the operations named in the articles: “I would do it again tomorrow.” The articles are not dead, and Democrats can bring them back in January. But a Republican House declined to cast what the Times had said would amount to a bipartisan vote of no confidence in a sitting defence secretary.

The budget. That leaves the money, which was stuck before anyone voted. Congress has not passed the Pentagon’s $1.5 trillion request, and lawmakers in both parties cite Hegseth among the reasons. The National Defense Authorization Act, which covers everything from troops’ pay to weapons production, has stalled for months amid complaints that the Pentagon would not consult Congress on the war or much else. A $350 billion military package that Trump hoped to pass through a budget bill immune to the filibuster has not moved either.

The Republicans. Even James Comer of Kentucky, the Republican chairman of the House Oversight Committee, told NewsNation on November 6 that the decision belonged to the President, and then went further. “I can tell you with a high level of confidence there’s a lot of questions about Hegseth,” he said. “We have no idea what the strategy is moving forward in Iran.” Four days later, every Republican who voted except Massie helped shelve the articles.

Chuck Schumer, about to become Senate majority leader, was less delicate. Michael Barbaro asked him on The Daily whether Hegseth should go. “I would be for him kicking Hegseth out tomorrow,” Schumer said. Fired and impeached? “Yes.”

The committees. Senator Jack Reed, the expected next chairman of Senate Armed Services, called the climate Hegseth has created “toxic.” Stopping the damage, he said, “probably means the departure of Hegseth one way or the other.” Representative Adam Smith, returning as chairman of House Armed Services, has proposed an oversight subcommittee to investigate “the illegal war against Iran, unauthorized military strikes in the Caribbean and Latin America, and the deployment of the military to U.S. cities.” Democrats also plan hearings on the boat strikes and on the bases damaged in the war.

Peter Feaver of Duke University offered the new majorities a fair warning. They can choose “made-for-social-media-outrage show-trial hearings,” he said, or substantive oversight that repairs the thin information Americans have about defence policy. This series has argued that Hegseth’s tenure was a performance. The worst answer Congress could give him is a performance of its own.

The officers waiting

The purge’s afterlife. The most consequential detail in the Times report is easy to miss. In the past year, Hegseth has blocked the promotions of more than 50 officers selected for general or admiral rank, often without giving a reason. Boards of senior officers had chosen them. Hegseth simply declined to send their names to the White House and the Senate.

Those selections remain valid for 18 months. Many of the officers had planned to retire in the coming weeks. Since November 4, senior military leaders have been quietly telling some of them to wait. A new secretary could release their nominations and save their careers.

That is what the midterms changed inside the Pentagon. Not policy, not yet, but time horizons. Officers who had decided their service had no future under this secretary are now betting that the secretary has no future either.

The exit ramp

Tennessee. Some of Hegseth’s allies have found a door. Senator Marsha Blackburn was elected governor of Tennessee on November 3 and must choose her own successor in the Senate. To Hegseth’s friends, the seat would let him leave the Pentagon on his own terms rather than be pushed. Several Republican congressional aides and Tennessee operatives told the Times it was unlikely, because the party would have to defend another red-state seat in 2028 with one of the most divisive figures in the administration on the ballot. A source close to Blackburn went further, telling the Associated Press that the talk was “baseless chatter.”

That the conversation happened at all is the point. People close to the President say he wants to stand by his defence secretary. Some of his allies caution that this could change, given how many Republican lawmakers are complaining about Hegseth to the White House.

The loyalist’s verdict

The sharpest notice of the week came from inside the company. On November 13, John Ullyot argued in The Atlantic that Hegseth should resign. Ullyot is no critic of convenience. He worked on Trump’s 2016 campaign in Trump Tower, served as chief spokesman for the National Security Council in the first term, and volunteered early in the second to help Hegseth set up his press office. Twenty years ago he was spokesman for the Senate Armed Services Committee, and he watched an earlier defence secretary become the face of an unpopular war and a burden on George W. Bush. His reading of 2006 is the one this instalment began with, told from the other side. Rumsfeld saw the result and stepped aside rather than weaken his president and his party, and that, Ullyot argues, was what loyalty looked like. Hegseth, in his view, has done the reverse.

His charge sheet overlaps with this series at almost every point: the 51-to-50 confirmation, after which a nominee might have kept his voice down; the Signal chat; the second strike on the boat survivors; the purge of generals and of two service secretaries, Dan Driscoll and John Phelan; and a Quinnipiac poll in September that found 32 percent of voters approving of him and 57 percent disapproving. He also records a fresh embarrassment. On Veterans Day, an Army sentinel at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier loudly rebuked Hegseth and his entourage for breaking the silence the Tomb requires. Hegseth awarded the sentinel a medal, but only after a public backlash.

Then there is Ullyot’s account of this week’s news conference, the Secretary’s first in six months, at which Hegseth argued that he should keep his job. The performance, Ullyot writes, was aimed at “his customary audience of one.” Readers of Part X will recognise the phrase; I did not lend it to him. When a man who has served Trump since 2016 reaches for the same image as an essayist in Toronto, the metaphor has stopped being a metaphor. His own verdict is that a defence secretary reduced to arguing in public that he does not embarrass the President has lost already. He treats the 211-to-204 vote as a stay, not an acquittal. On January 3, the gavels and the subpoenas change hands.

The review

Part X asked what would end Hegseth’s run. Its answer was that only one review counted, and that the President’s review had always been good. More than a week after the midterms, that is still true. The President gives him an A plus plus plus. The man who ran the political operation is out, and a friend of Hegseth’s now runs the West Wing. The House Republicans who complained about him in private voted in public to change the subject.

But Rumsfeld’s story has a second half that Jeffries did not mention. Sanger recalled it in his November 5 analysis: after 2006, Bush did not only replace his defence secretary. He replaced his strategy, and within limits it worked. Trump has so far done the opposite. He has kept the secretary, changed the staff, and given no sign of what he intends to do about the war. The Republican chairman of House Oversight says nobody knows.

What happens next belongs to the new Congress. A companion to this series, The Reckoning Ledger, sets out what it can do, entry by entry, from the Minab school strike to the withheld Ukraine money, and will be updated as hearings are held and votes are taken.

The audience of one is still applauding. The rest of the house has started leaving, and on November 10 the Republican ushers voted to keep the doors open a little longer. Three days later, one of the President's longest-serving stagehands wrote that the star should take his bow. Every long run ends eventually. This one may end the way most do: not with a bad review, but with an empty theatre.
