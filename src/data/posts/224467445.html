On November 10, 2026, the House of Representatives voted 211 to 204 not to talk about Pete Hegseth. The vote sent Thomas Massie's eight articles of impeachment to the Judiciary Committee, where they will sit until the Republican majority expires in January. Part XI of this series argued that the first roll call would be the one that mattered, and Part XII recorded how it came out. One detail deserves adding. Eleven Democrats missed the vote, many of them saying they were stuck in traffic, and on paper their votes would have been enough to defeat the motion. Trump, who had spent weeks asking outside advisers whether to replace Hegseth, posted his support after the secretary's defiant news conference that day, and read the result as proof that he still controlled his party, as Jonathan Lemire reported for The Atlantic. One question remains: what happens now?

The articles are shelved, but the facts behind them are not. A school in Minab was still struck by three American missiles on the first morning of the war. More than 200 people are still dead in the boat strikes. Congress still told the Pentagon to withdraw from Iran in June, and the Pentagon still did not. Every one of those questions will still be waiting when the 120th Congress convenes in January, with Democrats in charge of both chambers and the power of the purse in their hands.

They will also arrive with a list of officials they would like to examine, and Hegseth is at the top of it. Even Democrats wary of a season of impeachments have put him first. "I don't think Democrats can spend their time trying to impeach the entire cabinet, and if there are any impeachments, it should start with Secretary Hegseth," Representative Chellie Pingree of Maine told Sheryl Gay Stolberg of The New York Times on November 12. Pingree was arguing for restraint toward Robert F. Kennedy Jr. Her shortlist of one is the measure of where Hegseth stands.

So I have built a ledger. The Reckoning Ledger is a live companion to this series. It is an interactive page, not a printed list, and it will be updated as hearings are held, riders are attached and courts rule. This essay explains what is in it and how to read it.

How the Ledger Works

The ledger holds 24 entries, grouped under seven headings: war powers; civilians and the law of war; command and the ranks; Congress, the press and free speech; money; military education; and the Secretary himself. Each entry answers four questions.

What did Hegseth do? This is drawn from the reporting this series has followed for twelve parts, and from Massie’s 34-page resolution, which is the most complete charge sheet anyone has written.

What can Congress do about it? Every entry proposes something specific. These are editorial suggestions, not drafted bills, but each is the kind of thing Congress does routinely.

Which lever moves it? There are five. Appropriations means money, and binding conditions written into the bill that keeps the Pentagon funded. The NDAA is the annual defence authorisation, which has passed every year for more than six decades and is the main vehicle for telling the Pentagon what it must do. Oversight means hearings, subpoenas and referrals to the Inspector General and the GAO, none of which needs the President’s signature. The Senate means confirmations and holds. The courts mean cases already running, such as Senator Mark Kelly’s suit over his censure.

Will Republicans help? Each entry is tagged “GOP allies likely,” “GOP split” or “GOP likely opposed.” This matters more than anything else on the page. A Democratic Congress can pass almost anything and enact very little on its own, because the President will veto it. What gets enacted will be what some Republicans also want.

Each entry is also stamped with a colour. Red means reverse a Hegseth decision; there are six. Amber means repair or rebuild something he broke; there are eight. Blue means investigate or build something new; there are nine. Green means keep something that works, once it has been checked. There is exactly one green entry, and I will come to it.

Why Not Simply Impeach Him?

Because the arithmetic does not work, and pretending otherwise would be a performance of its own. Conviction needs 67 senators. Democrats will hold 55 seats in January. The two Cabinet secretaries the House has impeached before, William Belknap in 1876 and Alejandro Mayorkas in 2024, were both acquitted.

The ledger includes impeachment as one entry, under the heading “Decide impeachment on the record.” Hold the hearings first, it says, then vote on articles built from the evidence the hearings produce. Peter Feaver of Duke University warned the new majorities, as Helene Cooper, Greg Jaffe and Eric Schmitt reported for The New York Times, that they can choose “made-for-social-media-outrage show-trial hearings” or oversight that fixes the thin information Americans have about defence. The ledger is built for the second choice.

There is a second reason to start somewhere other than impeachment. The vote on November 10 showed that most Republicans will not vote against Hegseth by name. It did not show that they will not vote for a cost-of-war report, a casualty count or the release of money they themselves appropriated. Those are different questions, and the ledger is built around them.

The First Hundred Days

Twelve entries carry a "First 100 days" stamp. Four of them matter most.

The Minab school. Three Tomahawk missiles struck the Shajareh Tayyebeh Elementary School on February 28, 2026. Iranian officials say at least 175 people died, most of them children. Senior American officials have privately said the United States was responsible; the Pentagon has not said so publicly. On November 10 Hegseth called claims that American forces target civilians “slander.” Adam Smith, the likely chairman of House Armed Services, has already asked why Congress has had no report. The ledger proposes subpoenaing the investigation and publishing it. I expect this entry to define the year.

The cost of the war. David E. Sanger reported for The New York Times that the Pentagon had told Congress the war cost $47 billion so far, not counting the bases it says will probably never be rebuilt. Nobody outside the building knows how many munitions are left. The ledger proposes quarterly cost-of-war reports, with part of the Secretary’s own office budget held back until each one arrives. Fencing office funds is an ordinary NDAA tool, and it is tagged “GOP allies likely” because fiscal hawks want the numbers before they approve a $1.5 trillion budget.

The Iran war itself. The war began without authorisation. The 60-day clock in the War Powers Resolution ran out on May 1. Both chambers voted in June to direct a withdrawal, and the Pentagon kept fighting. The ledger proposes a funding condition: no money for hostilities against Iran without authorisation, with exceptions for self-defence. Chuck Schumer described the arithmetic on The Daily: “We needed five Republican votes. We got three, then four. We’ll get more now.”

The Ukraine money. Don Bacon, the retiring Republican from Nebraska, told CNN that what worries him most about Hegseth is not in Massie’s articles at all. It is the firing of generals and admirals, and “withholding appropriated money all year from Ukraine,” as The New Republic reported. The ledger proposes release deadlines and a GAO ruling on whether the delay broke the Impoundment Control Act. Bacon’s own words put it in the “GOP allies likely” column.

Where Republicans May Help

Eight entries carry the green “GOP allies likely” tag. They have something in common: none of them requires a Republican to say that Hegseth committed a crime.

They are the cost-of-war reports, a full accounting of American casualties, an investigation of the deadly drone strike in Kuwait, the Ukraine money, conditions on the $1.5 trillion budget, a requirement that the Pentagon answer Congress’s questions, a study of why mid-career officers are leaving, and a check on the Pentagon’s recruiting claims. Each is framed around Congress’s own prerogatives, or around readiness, or around money. Those are the three things Republican members have complained about in private all year.

James Comer, the Republican chairman of House Oversight, gave the clearest sign of where those complaints lead. “We have no idea what the strategy is moving forward in Iran,” he told NewsNation on November 6. A member who says that cannot easily vote against a law requiring the Pentagon to explain its strategy.

The casualty entry deserves particular attention. Hegseth held no news conference after a Navy commander died in a helicopter crash on July 1, or after three soldiers were killed by an Iranian missile at a base in Jordan on July 17. Smith says Congress has had no clarity on a strike in Kuwait that killed six service members. A separate entry asks why warnings about Iran's Shahed drones, sent up the chain months before that strike, went unheeded. Massie's articles say seven Americans were wounded in the raid that seized Nicolás Maduro, a figure the Pentagon has never acknowledged. No member of Congress, of either party, wants to explain to a constituent's family why the Pentagon would not account for their son.

The Hard Ones

Two entries are tagged “GOP likely opposed,” and the ledger does not pretend otherwise.

The boat strikes. Since September 2, 2025, according to Massie’s articles, the military has struck at least 67 vessels and killed at least 221 people. Hegseth now claims more than 70 boats. Two survivors clinging to wreckage were killed by a second strike on the first day. After an attack on December 30, eight shipwrecked survivors waited about 45 hours for a rescue that came too late. The ledger proposes a rider barring lethal force against vessels without authorisation and returning interdiction to the Coast Guard, which is what the law already provides. The administration calls the campaign a success, and most Republicans will defend it. The survivors are the strongest argument for those who are uneasy about the precedent.

The Maduro raid. On January 3, 2026, more than 150 aircraft and over 200 special forces troops went into Caracas without any authorisation from Congress. The President counts it as a victory. The ledger proposes hearings and the release of the legal opinion behind it. Republican support is unlikely. The record still matters, because the next president will read it.

The largest group of entries, fourteen of them, are tagged “GOP split.” They include the civilian-harm safeguards Hegseth dismantled, protection for the military’s lawyers, the promotion lists he overrode and the punishment of Senator Kelly. These will be decided member by member. Several have an institutional hook. Senators confirm promotion lists, and they have a reason not to let their votes be quietly overridden. Nearly a fifth of Congress are veterans, and the Kelly precedent would put every one of them under the Pentagon’s thumb.

The One I Would Keep

There is one green entry. The Pentagon says recruiting under Hegseth reached levels not seen in more than a decade. The ledger does not take that on faith, and it does not dismiss it either. It asks the GAO to verify the figures, find out which practices produced them, and keep what works.

I included it on purpose. A ledger that finds nothing to keep is a prosecution brief, and readers can tell the difference. The Recovery Ledger I built for the RFK Files credits Robert F. Kennedy Jr. with the food-dye phase-out for the same reason. If the recruiting claim holds, it belongs in the record. If it does not, the GAO will say so, and that belongs in the record too.

Why a Ledger

I spent 18 years handling escalated complaints at a large Canadian company. The lesson I took from it is that accountability rarely arrives in a single dramatic moment. It arrives in a file. Somebody writes down what was promised and what was done, and then checks again the next week.

That is what this ledger is for. Every entry starts at “Not started.” When a hearing is held, a rider is attached, a bill passes a chamber or a court rules, the entry will change and the update log at the top will say so. Readers can filter it by priority, by lever, by whether Republicans are likely to help, or search it for a single word such as “Minab” or “Kelly.”

The House chose on November 10 not to read the charges. The ledger reads them, one at a time, alongside what can be done about each. It will stay open for as long as the questions do.
