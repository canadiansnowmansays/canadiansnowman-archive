Leonard Walter Jerome was born on November 3, 1817, on a farm in Pompey, New York, a small town in Onondaga County near Syracuse, the ninth of ten children born to a farming family with no discernible connection to European aristocracy of any kind. He studied briefly at Princeton, then transferred to Union College to read law, and set up a practice in Rochester, New York, where he also dabbled in the newspaper business, co-founding a paper called the Rochester Daily American with his brother Lawrence. None of this suggested the life that followed. Jerome sold his interest in the paper in 1850, moved to New York City, and stepped into the middle of the most volatile financial market of the American nineteenth century: Wall Street stock speculation in the decade before the Civil War.

He was, by every contemporary account, extraordinarily good at it, and extraordinarily reckless about staying good at it. Jerome made — and lost — at least three separate fortunes across his career, a fact that earned him the nickname “The King of Wall Street,” which sounds like an honor and was, on close inspection, closer to a warning label. He was briefly bankrupt in the early 1850s before establishing what would become a lifelong pattern: catastrophic loss followed by improbable recovery. He partnered with the financier William R. Travers and, at various points, with Cornelius Vanderbilt himself, moving in circles that included the men who were then actively inventing the modern American railroad and telegraph economy. The 1868 Black Friday gold-market crash — engineered by speculators trying to corner the gold supply, though not by Jerome himself — cost him roughly half his fortune by his own later reckoning, forcing him to give up his Madison Avenue mansion, though the family, notably, kept living as though nothing had happened. His three daughters continued ordering gowns by the dozen from Paris. This is worth sitting with for a moment, because it is the first appearance of a pattern that will recur throughout this series: a Jerome-Churchill instinct for maintaining appearances several fiscal quarters past the point where the underlying numbers justified it.

It is also worth noting what Jerome’s speculative instincts were specifically aimed at, because the target reveals almost as much as the method. Jerome held substantial interests in early telegraph investments as well as railroads — betting, in effect, on the infrastructure of information and transportation at the exact moment both were being invented as national systems in the United States. There is a resonance here I do not think is accidental, given my own working life in telecommunications: Jerome’s fortune was built, at least in part, on correctly identifying that the value of the coming decades would run through the wires and rails that connected a fragmented country into a single market. His grandson would spend a career, seven decades later, arguing that the value of the coming century ran through a different kind of connective infrastructure entirely — alliances, treaties, and the “special relationship” itself as a kind of diplomatic wiring linking two nations into a single strategic system. Grandfather and grandson, in their respective centuries, were both in the business of building and betting on networks. Neither of them, notably, was especially good at explaining the risk involved to the people relying on the network holding.

Jerome was not merely a speculator, whatever his nickname suggests. He was a patron of the arts on a scale that mattered to New York’s cultural development — he helped finance the Academy of Music, one of the city’s first serious opera houses, at a time when New York’s claim to cultural sophistication was still very much in question. He was also, more consequentially for this series, one of the founding figures of American thoroughbred horse racing. He established Jerome Park in the Bronx, organized the first meeting of the American Jockey Club in 1866 alongside figures including Ulysses S. Grant and the notoriously corrupt Boss Tweed, and helped launch what became the Belmont Stakes. If you are looking for the exact moment the Churchill family’s later, well-documented enthusiasm for horse racing enters the bloodline, you can stop looking. It starts here, with an American grandfather Winston Churchill never knew well but whose habits he inherited with striking fidelity — the appetite for risk, the recovery instinct, the taste for spectacle, and the racehorses. Winston himself did not take up ownership of racehorses until relatively late in his life, buying his first, a grey named Colonist II, in 1949 at the age of seventy-four — a fact biographers tend to present as an eccentric late-life indulgence, a bit of fun after decades in office, when it reads rather differently once you know Leonard Jerome was the man who helped found the American Jockey Club and put up the purse money for an entire new racetrack in the Bronx. It was not a new interest Winston discovered in old age. It was a dormant one, inherited from a grandfather he barely knew, that finally had the leisure time and disposable income to express itself once the premiership was behind him.

Jerome Park itself is worth a further word, because its physical fate mirrors the larger arc of the family fortune it was built to celebrate. The track operated at Fordham in the Bronx from 1866 until 1890, hosting the first several runnings of the Belmont Stakes before the land was sold and redeveloped — first as a reservoir site, then eventually as part of the borough’s expanding urban footprint. By the time Leonard Jerome died the following year, in 1891, the track that bore his name had already been decommissioned, a detail that fits the pattern of his life almost too neatly: build something conspicuous and consequential, watch it eventually get absorbed or dismantled by forces larger than any one man’s fortune, and trust that your name survives the asset itself. It did. There is no Jerome Park left in the Bronx today. There is, however, a Jerome Avenue, and a Churchill family that still, a century and a half later, gets described in the press as having American blood — which is really just Leonard Jerome’s name surviving in a different, quieter form than the racetrack he built to carry it.

It is worth pausing on exactly how Jerome made his early money, because the mechanics matter for understanding what he passed down. He formed a partnership with William R. Travers and, at various points, Cornelius Vanderbilt’s own circle of allied speculators, in an era before the Securities and Exchange Commission existed to draw any meaningful line between aggressive trading and outright market manipulation. Jerome’s specialty, by contemporary account, was moving fast on railroad stock — buying into companies whose fortunes rose and fell with breathtaking speed as the postwar American rail network expanded, contracted, and reorganized itself through repeated waves of speculative excess. as the New York Times put it in Jerome’s 1891 obituary, his career was defined by an “alternation of enormous gains and losses” that nonetheless added up to success — a formulation that manages to praise a man for his net results while conceding, in the same sentence, that the path there ran through repeated catastrophic failure. It is not, on its face, the kind of epitaph most fathers would want written about their financial judgment. And yet it is close to a word-for-word description of the reputation Winston Churchill himself would eventually earn across his own career: extraordinary net achievement, purchased through a pattern of individually reckless decisions that a more cautious man would never have risked in the first place.

In 1852, Jerome briefly left Wall Street to serve eighteen months as American consul to Trieste, a fashionable summer destination for European nobility, a post secured through a political nomination from President Millard Fillmore. It was a minor diplomatic sinecure, the sort of reward routinely handed to a financially useful political supporter, and it should have been a footnote in Jerome’s career. Instead, it turned out to be the hinge on which the entire rest of this series depends, because his wife Clara — whom Jerome had married in 1849 — became thoroughly enchanted with European high society during those eighteen months in a way that reshaped the family’s entire trajectory. Clara wanted her daughters raised with access to that world, not the mercantile world of New York, however wealthy. It is a small, almost incidental detail in most accounts of Jerome’s life. It is, in fact, the reason his eldest daughter Jennie ended up at a regatta on the Isle of Wight in August 1873, in the social orbit of the future King Edward VII, rather than at a debutante ball in New York.

There is a persistent, colorful rumor — repeated often enough in popular accounts that it deserves a mention, if only to be handled with appropriate skepticism — that Jerome carried on an affair with the celebrated Swedish soprano Jenny Lind, and that his eldest daughter was named in her honor. It is the kind of story that fits a bit too neatly into the Jerome family’s general aesthetic of flamboyance to be entirely trusted at face value, and serious biographers treat it as unconfirmed. I mention it mainly because it illustrates something true about how the Jerome-Churchill story gets told: even the footnotes come pre-mythologized, and any honest series has to keep separating the documented record from the good anecdote, because the two are not always the same file.

Jerome died on March 3, 1891, in Brighton, England — not in the country of his birth, not on the Wall Street floor where he made his name, but in the country his daughter’s marriage had delivered him into, four years before his son-in-law Randolph’s own death and seven years before his grandson would first see combat in Sudan. He never held a title. He never sat in a legislature of any kind on either side of the Atlantic. And yet by the time Winston Churchill stood before Congress in 1941 to make his half-American joke, it was Leonard Jerome’s money, Leonard Jerome’s recklessness, and — I would argue — Leonard Jerome’s specific talent for catastrophic loss followed by spectacular recovery that had actually built the psychological template Churchill would run on for the rest of his life. Scaramucci’s essay treats the American blood in Churchill’s veins as a kind of charming credential, evidence that he understood America “the way you only know a place where you’ve lost money,” in his own phrase about Churchill’s 1929 crash experience. He is not wrong about the effect. He is simply several decades and one generation off about where that pattern actually originated. Churchill did not learn to lose money and recover from losing his own money in 1929. He inherited the entire emotional architecture of doing so from a grandfather who had already run the experiment three times over on Wall Street, decades before Winston was born.

It is worth pausing, too, on what Jerome actually exported to England, because it was not only Jennie. He had three surviving daughters — Clara, Jennie, and Leonie, born between 1851 and 1859 — and all three crossed the Atlantic together in the 1860s under their mother Clara’s direction, entered London and Paris society at roughly the same time, and became known collectively, in the slightly cloying shorthand of the period’s gossip columns, as “the Good, the Witty, and the Beautiful.” Jennie was “the Beautiful.”

Her elder sister Clara — “the Good” — married an English adventurer named Moreton Frewen in 1881, a man so consistently unsuccessful at every business scheme he attempted, from cattle ranching in Wyoming to a string of failed mining and agricultural ventures, that his own family nicknamed him “Mortal Ruin.” The youngest, Leonie — “the Witty” — married into the Leslies, a substantial Anglo-Irish landholding family, over the initial disappointment of her in-laws, and became the mother of Shane Leslie, who later converted to Catholicism and built his own career as a writer. All three Jerome sisters, in other words, married into families with more social standing than liquid capital, and all three spent significant portions of their adult lives managing husbands or in-laws whose finances required considerably more active oversight than their own father’s fortune, at its peak, had ever needed.

I raise the sisters here, rather than saving them for a footnote, because their collective pattern says something Jennie’s story alone cannot: this was not one lucky, or unlucky, transatlantic marriage. It was a coordinated family strategy, executed three times over by the same set of parents, with results that ranged from Jennie’s globally consequential match to Clara’s marriage to a man whose own relatives joked openly about his talent for financial ruin. The Jerome daughters were, collectively, the clearest possible evidence that Leonard Jerome’s approach to risk — bold, confident, frequently catastrophic, occasionally spectacular — was not confined to his own stock portfolio. He appears to have applied a similar appetite for high-variance bets to his daughters’ marriage prospects, and the family’s fortunes over the following century bore out both the successes and the failures of that approach in roughly equal measure.

That is the actual opening entry in this inheritance’s ledger: not a value, but a temperament. The rest of the series is, in large part, a study of what the family did with it.

It is worth being clear-eyed, too, about what the temperament did not include. Leonard Jerome’s recoveries were real, but they were also, by any modern standard, undisciplined — the product of a market with essentially no regulatory guardrails, in which a sufficiently bold operator could recover from ruin by simply making a larger and riskier bet than the one that had ruined him. That is not a virtue so much as an artifact of the era he operated in, and it is worth flagging now, at the start of the series, because Winston Churchill would spend his own career operating in a considerably more constrained environment — parliamentary democracy, a free press, an electorate — where the same undisciplined boldness that built and rebuilt Jerome’s fortunes three times over would, at Gallipoli in particular, produce a catastrophe measured in tens of thousands of lives rather than dollars. The inheritance was not a clean gift. It came with a design flaw built in from the very first generation, and this series will not look away from it simply because the flaw eventually produced, on balance, more triumphs than disasters.
