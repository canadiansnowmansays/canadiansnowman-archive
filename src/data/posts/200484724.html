CanadianSnowmanSays | June 3, 2026

Bottom Line Up Front: On Tuesday night, the Trump administration announced proposed tariffs of 10 to 12.5 percent on 59 countries and the European Union under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 — the third statutory vehicle in four months as it races to rebuild a tariff wall the Supreme Court demolished in February. Simultaneously, the same administration is defying a court order to return $166 billion in tariffs it collected illegally under the very authority the Court struck down — blocking refunds, shielding its customs commissioner from testimony, and telling importers who want their money back to file their own lawsuits. The mole has found a new hole. The mallet is in the same small hands. And the people who paid for the first wall are being told they cannot have their money back while the second wall goes up.

The Third Instrument

The May 8 Addendum to this series ended with a prediction: Section 301 investigations are the replacement tariff architecture, timed to land as the Section 122 bridge expires July 24. That prediction has now been confirmed.

On June 2, U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer released a 98-page report concluding that 60 countries had failed to enact or effectively enforce laws prohibiting imports made with forced labor. The proposed remedy: tariffs of 12.5 percent on most offenders, and 10 percent on Canada, Mexico, the European Union, and other countries with partial regimes deemed insufficiently enforced. Hearings are scheduled for July 7. The tariffs would take effect sometime after that.

Unlike IEEPA — struck down by the Supreme Court in February — Section 301 has genuine legal precedent. The China tariffs first imposed under it in 2018 have survived years of challenge. Unlike Section 122 — struck down by the Court of International Trade in May and expiring July 24 — it carries no built-in clock. If these tariffs survive the hearings and the litigation that will follow, they may prove more durable than anything that has come before.

Steve Okun, chief executive of APAC Advisors, put it plainly: “Following the Supreme Court overturning the IEEPA tariffs, a legal basis was needed to rebuild Trump’s tariff wall, and this was a convenient way to do so. Tariffs are here to stay under the Trump administration.”

The whack-a-mole has found its most defensible hole yet. But the story today is not only about the new wall going up. It is about what is happening to the money collected under the old one.

The Forced Labor Pretext

Let’s be precise about what Section 301 can and cannot do, and what this particular invocation actually is.

Section 301 requires a finding that a foreign country’s acts, policies, or practices are unreasonable or discriminatory and burden or restrict U.S. commerce. The forced labor investigation found exactly that — in 60 countries simultaneously, in three months, completing a process that normally takes closer to twelve.

The speed itself is the tell. The timeline was engineered to coincide with the Section 122 expiration. As trade lawyers at Norton Rose Fulbright noted when the investigations were launched in March, officials had indicated the investigations “are expected to conclude within five months” — meaning the outcome was always designed to land before the Section 122 clock ran out.

The substantive findings deserve scrutiny as well. Canada, for instance, has had its Fighting Against Forced Labour and Child Labour in Supply Chains Act (Bill S-211) in force since January 1, 2024. The law requires major corporations to report annually on forced labour risks in their supply chains, with fines up to $250,000 for non-compliance. The Greer report found that Canada failed to “effectively enforce” a forced labor import ban — a finding that Prime Minister Mark Carney called “not a surprise,” while announcing new strengthening legislation. He wasn’t disagreeing with the finding. He was acknowledging the pretext.

The European Union’s situation is even more revealing. The EU has adopted a regulation banning imports made with forced labor — but it doesn’t take effect until late 2027. The Greer report’s logic: because the EU cannot enforce its measure until then, it “cannot do so ‘effectively’ until then.” The policy exists. The enforcement mechanism is coming. It’s not fast enough.

The administration’s treatment of Brazil is the case study that strips away the remaining ambiguity. In July 2025, Trump imposed 50 percent tariffs on Brazil — not for trade reasons, but because his political ally Jair Bolsonaro was on trial in Brazilian courts. Trump called it a “witch hunt.” The Supreme Court struck those tariffs down in February. This week, using the same Section 301 mechanism, the administration proposed 25 percent tariffs on Brazil for forced labor failures and other trade grievances. The instrument changed. The target didn’t.

The forced labor rationale is not invented. There are genuine forced labor problems in global supply chains, and the Uyghur situation in China’s Xinjiang region is well-documented and serious. But applying the same framework to Canada, the EU, Brazil, South Korea, and Switzerland — simultaneously, at accelerated speed, timed to a statutory expiration — is not a targeted trade enforcement action. It is a tariff wall in search of a legal foundation.

The Money That Isn’t Coming Back

While the administration builds the new wall, it is actively obstructing the return of $166 billion collected under the old one.

The sequence is important. In February, the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs. Judge Richard Eaton of the Court of International Trade ordered the government to begin immediately and fully refunding the duties, with interest. Under law, those refunds accrue interest daily — costing the government millions every day they are not paid.

What has happened since then is a case study in the uses of bureaucratic obstruction.

The government revealed that about 330,000 importers were owed money on roughly 53 million tariff entries. U.S. Customs and Border Protection built a bulk processing system that went online in April, targeting tariff payments that had not yet been finalized through the liquidation process. That universe was estimated at about $127 billion of the total $166 billion.

The remaining $40 billion — payments that were already finalized before the SCOTUS ruling — the government said it would not automatically refund. Instead, each importer would be required to file its own individual lawsuit. Only after a judge issued an “importer-specific order” would that importer see its money.

As of a May 26 court filing, the government had processed about $85 billion in refund requests and repaid almost $21 billion — roughly 13 cents on the dollar of what it collected. The interest clock has been running the entire time.

Judge Eaton, apparently unimpressed, ordered Customs Commissioner Rodney Scott to appear personally at a June 9 hearing to explain the handling of refunds. The government tried to block that order. Judge Eaton denied the block. The Justice Department then filed an emergency appeal to shield Scott from testifying — offering to send a substitute official in his place, and hinting it would seek a Supreme Court reprieve if necessary.

The same day, the government filed a separate notice that it intended to challenge the original refund order itself.

Sara Albrecht of the Liberty Justice Center, which represented small businesses in the original IEEPA challenge, was succinct: “At the end of the day, this money belongs to the businesses that paid it. For the past year, many small businesses were forced to give effectively all of their working capital to the government supporting their illegal tariff scheme.”

Ashley Akers, a trade lawyer at Holland & Knight, warned that the government’s position could result in “a flood into the federal court system” as importers who can’t get automatic refunds are forced to litigate individually. Oxford Economics had projected more than $100 billion in IEEPA refunds would be issued in Q2, with the remainder within a year. That projection now looks optimistic.

The Logic of the Position

Put the two stories together and the administration’s position becomes visible.

It collected $166 billion under legal authority the Supreme Court found it did not have. It is now resisting returning that money through every procedural mechanism available — emergency appeals, demands for individual lawsuits, challenges to the refund order itself. The officials responsible for the return process are being shielded from judicial scrutiny.

At the same time, it is constructing a new tariff regime using Section 301 — a more legally defensible authority — with rates calibrated to approximate what IEEPA produced. The 10 to 12.5 percent proposed Section 301 tariffs are not coincidentally similar to the IEEPA rates that were struck down. As Atlantic Council economists noted this week, these tariffs “closely track the trade deals that the Trump administration made with certain countries before the court invalidated the IEEPA tariffs.” The numbers were reverse-engineered from the desired outcome.

The underlying message is that courts can strike down the legal vehicle, but the tariff levels will be maintained. The revenue will be replaced. The $166 billion that was illegally collected will be returned as slowly as legally possible, or not at all. And the businesses that paid it will be competing against the new tariff wall while they litigate to recover what was taken.

This is not a government that has absorbed a legal defeat and complied with its consequences. It is a government that has absorbed a legal defeat and is working to make that defeat as meaningless as possible — both by obstructing the remedy and by making the underlying policy outcome survive through new instruments.

What Holds and What Doesn’t

Section 301 has a real legal track record. The China tariffs have survived years of challenge. Courts have generally been reluctant to second-guess trade policy findings on forced labor and unfair practices. The administration’s legal team has clearly done its homework this time.

But several vulnerabilities remain.

The speed of these investigations — completed in three months rather than twelve — creates an administrative record that challengers will probe for procedural defects. The targeting of close allies with strong democratic institutions and existing forced labor frameworks, while using findings as a substitute for IEEPA tariffs rather than as genuine trade enforcement, invites arguments that the Section 301 authority is being weaponized beyond its statutory purpose. And CUSMA-compliant goods — goods meeting USMCA content rules — have historically been exempted from new tariff regimes; whether that exemption survives the Section 301 architecture is not yet determined.

The hearings on July 7 will produce a formal record. Litigation will follow. The same plaintiffs who won at the Supreme Court are watching. Whether Section 301 proves to be the durable foundation the administration needs, or another mole that courts eventually hammer down, is not knowable today.

What is knowable is this: the administration has now tried three statutory vehicles in four months. One was struck down by the Supreme Court. One was struck down by the Court of International Trade and is expiring. The third is now on the table, timed to take effect as the second expires.

The mole has found its most defensible hole yet. Whether it holds is the question that will define the second half of 2026.
