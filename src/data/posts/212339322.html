Three days after the federal debt crossed $40 trillion, Paul Vigna — author of a history of money called The Almightier — published an essay in the New York Times proposing that we look back roughly 4,400 years for guidance. As Vigna wrote for the New York Times, the $40 trillion figure is in fact the smallest of several numbers worth worrying about. Add the debt owed by U.S. states, corporations, and consumers, and the total climbs to approximately $77 trillion, set against an annual GDP of roughly $32 trillion. Globally, total debt now stands near $350 trillion — treble the entire planet’s economic output in a given year. Vigna’s metaphor of choice is snowpack: stable-looking, right up until it isn’t.

I have spent two prior instalments of this series describing the mechanism by which that snowpack accumulates — the compounding interest, the failed DOGE promises, the tariff refunds, the Fed’s balance sheet, the bond market beginning to talk back. What I have not yet done, in eight parts and two addenda, is ask the question Vigna’s essay forces onto the table: if the conventional remedies have all failed, or failed to materialize, what does an unconventional one look like? And what happened, historically, to the people who tried it?

A Word Older Than the Problem

The ancient Mesopotamians, Vigna notes, are credited with inventing money itself, along with loans and compound interest. Having invented the disease, they also invented something resembling a cure. Roughly 4,400 years ago, a Sumerian king named Enmetena issued an edict wiping out public debts across his territory. People who had been sold into bondage to satisfy debts were freed. Farmers who had lost land to creditors got it back. The campaign carried a name — amargi — that translates, imperfectly, as something like “return to mother,” and has come down to us as an early word for freedom itself.

This was not a one-time act of royal generosity. It became something closer to an institution. New rulers in the ancient Near East declared these resets with some regularity, treating debt cancellation as a relief valve built into the operating system of the economy rather than an emergency exception to it. The logic, as Vigna describes it, was straightforward and pragmatic rather than sentimental: debt compounds until it destabilizes the society carrying it. Enough farmers driven off their land, enough citizens sold into bondage, and there is nobody left to grow the crops or serve in the army. The amargi did not eliminate debt as a concept. It periodically zeroed the accumulated balance before the accumulated balance zeroed the society holding it.

The concept did not stay confined to Mesopotamia. The Old Testament book of Leviticus codifies a version of it — a “year of the Lord’s favor” every fifty years, an institutionalized jubilee built into Hebrew law. Six or seven centuries before the common era, the Athenian lawgiver Solon instituted reforms that cancelled debts across the city, over the furious objection of the creditor class, and reportedly left town for a decade afterward, having extracted a promise from his fellow Athenians not to unwind the reforms in his absence. It is a detail worth sitting with: the man who fixed the debt crisis judged it safer to leave the country than to stay and watch what the people he’d expropriated might do to him.

They did worse to others who tried the same thing without Solon’s foresight. A Spartan king who attempted to cancel debts by burning the city’s financial records in the public square was arrested by the outraged elite and executed — along with his mother and grandmother, a detail that tells you rather more about the politics of debt forgiveness than any amount of economic theory. Two centuries later in Rome, the Gracchi brothers proposed a set of debt and land reforms and were killed for it, part of a pattern of elite resistance so consistent that the amargi eventually disappeared from practice entirely, buried deeply enough in history that most people alive today have never heard the word.

What the Ancients Understood That We Have Unlearned

The reason the amargi worked, when it worked, is the part of Vigna’s essay that deserves the most attention, because it cuts against nearly everything the modern debate over the American deficit assumes as fixed. Money, in the Mesopotamian understanding, was not a natural substance to be discovered and hoarded. It was a human invention — a record-keeping device for allocating resources among people, no more sacred than a ledger, and precisely as alterable as any other human-made system when it stopped serving its purpose. If the record-keeping device is producing outcomes incompatible with the survival of the society using it, you edit the device. You do not treat the device as though it were bedrock.

This is, I would suggest, the single idea most conspicuously absent from Scott Bessent’s account of this year’s deficit, or Kevin Warsh’s approach to the Fed’s balance sheet, or any of the testimony collected across this series from Blankfein to Bernstein to MacGuineas. Every serious voice quoted in the Epilogue and the two Addenda has approached the debt as a fixed physical quantity to be managed, serviced, refinanced, or grown around — never as a social construct that the society issuing it retains the authority to simply redefine. Blankfein described default not as impossible but as disguised: America defaults by inflating the currency until the dollars owed are worth less than the dollars lent, returning the lawnmower with the blade removed. That is, in its own way, an amargi conducted by stealth and euphemism rather than by edict — the same fundamental act, dressed up so that nobody has to hold a public ceremony admitting what happened.

Running It Against the Cast

It is worth testing the amargi against the specific figures this series has already introduced, because the exercise clarifies exactly how far the modern American predicament has drifted from the conditions that made ancient debt jubilees survivable.

Bessent’s slingshot metaphor — the OBBBA’s expensing provisions as potential energy being stored for future kinetic release — assumes a future in which the debt is eventually repaid by growth catching up to it. An amargi assumes the opposite: that the debt will never be caught by growth, and that the honest move is to stop pretending otherwise and write down the balance directly. These are not compatible theories of the same problem. One is a bet that time solves the equation. The other is a decision that time has already run out and someone has to say so.

Warsh’s dilemma at the Fed is, if anything, sharpened by the amargi frame rather than resolved by it. A central bank shrinking a $6.8 trillion balance sheet is, in its own narrow domain, doing the reverse of debt forgiveness — withdrawing accommodation rather than extending it. But the Mesopotamian kings who declared these resets were not central bankers managing a portfolio; they were sovereigns exercising the one power that made the whole system make sense in the first place, the power to say the ledger itself was provisional. Warsh has no equivalent authority over the debt itself, only over the Fed’s own holdings of it, which is a meaningfully narrower lever — and one more reason the amargi remains, as Vigna concedes plainly, extremely unlikely to recur in anything resembling its ancient form.

And the household mirror this series has tracked since March — the $18.8 trillion in American household debt, the credit cards financing groceries rather than vacations — is precisely the population an amargi was historically designed to protect. The Mesopotamian edicts freed people sold into bondage and returned land to farmers who had lost it to creditors. It is not difficult to imagine a modern equivalent aimed at consumer debt rather than sovereign debt: a targeted cancellation of the $1.28 trillion in credit card balances, the bulk of which, as the Epilogue documented, is now financing non-negotiable living costs rather than discretionary spending. Vigna gestures toward this history without proposing it as policy, and he is careful to note the track record here is genuinely mixed — failed debt relief efforts for developing nations sit alongside the conspicuously successful postwar debt relief extended to the defeated Axis powers, which suggests the mechanism is not inherently doomed so much as inherently political, dependent entirely on who is doing the forgiving and who is being asked to absorb the cost.

The Elite Problem, Restated

What the ancient precedent makes unavoidable is the same structural obstacle Blankfein identified when he named polarization, not the deficit itself, as his primary fear. Every historical amargi Vigna catalogues was opposed, sometimes murderously, by the class of people to whom the debts were owed. Solon survived by leaving. The Spartan king did not survive at all, nor did his mother or grandmother. The Gracchi brothers were killed. The pattern across three separate ancient societies, spanning nearly a millennium, is depressingly uniform: debt forgiveness is popular with debtors, existentially threatening to creditors, and the creditors, as a rule, hold the swords.

Nothing about the current American configuration suggests this dynamic has softened with age. The same 30-year Treasury bondholders whose rising yield demands were the subject of the last instalment are not a natural constituency for having their claims zeroed out by fiat, and they hold considerably more leverage over the modern American state than the Athenian creditor class held over Solon, if only because the modern state’s entire capacity to fund itself day to day depends on those same bondholders showing up at the next auction. An American amargi, attempted today, would not merely anger a domestic elite. It would test, in real time, whether the world’s reserve currency retains its status once the sovereign issuing it has demonstrated a willingness to unilaterally void the instrument. Vigna does not pretend this problem away, which is to his credit; he offers the amargi as a set of principles for reconsidering the relationship between money and debt, not as a policy his readers should expect Washington to adopt this decade.

The Reset Nobody Will Hold a Ceremony For

I began the Epilogue in March with the observation that this series’ own title had become a historical artifact within eight months of its first instalment. I return to that observation now with a slightly different emphasis. The number keeps climbing not because no reset is occurring, but because the reset that is occurring — Blankfein’s quiet, currency-debasing, stealth version of default — lacks the one feature that made the ancient amargi survivable as a social technology: it is never announced, never dated, never attached to a name the way Enmetena’s edict or Solon’s reforms were attached to names. Nobody stands in a public square and declares the debt cancelled. Instead the dollar simply buys less, year over year, and the people holding fixed incomes and modest savings absorb the cost in a thousand small transactions rather than one visible act of state.

Vigna is right that we are unlikely to see a modern amargi in anything like its ancient form, and right that the principles behind it — money as an invented, alterable system rather than an immovable law of nature — deserve to be taken seriously regardless. But the ancient world had one advantage the modern United States conspicuously lacks: a sovereign willing to stand up, put his name on the edict, and accept whatever came next, up to and including a mob with a rope. What we have instead is $40 trillion in federal debt, $77 trillion including everyone else’s, $350 trillion globally, and a political class that has demonstrated, across eight parts and three addenda of this series, an extraordinary capacity to manage the appearance of the problem without ever being willing to write their name on the solution.

The grain silo is still emptying. Somewhere in Mesopotamia, 4,400 years ago, someone figured out you could simply declare the silo full again and start over. Nobody currently holding office in Washington appears prepared to find out what happens to the person who tries that here.
