In the summer of 1966, Red Guards dragged Peng Dehuai — the Defense Minister who had told Mao the truth about the Great Leap Forward and been destroyed for it — from his house in Beijing, beat him in the street, and paraded him through the city in a dunce cap.

Peng was seventy years old. He had fought for the Communist revolution since 1928. He had commanded the Chinese forces in Korea. He was one of the most decorated military commanders in the history of the People’s Liberation Army. None of it protected him. He died in prison in 1974, of cancer that went untreated, alone.

His story is not exceptional. During the decade of the Cultural Revolution, from 1966 to 1976, virtually every person who had the expertise, the education, or the institutional authority to run a modern society was targeted, humiliated, imprisoned, or killed. The revolution had survived the warlords, the Nationalist purges, the Long March, the Japanese occupation, and the Civil War. In the Cultural Revolution, it turned on itself — and the people it destroyed were the people who had built it.

Understanding why Mao did this requires understanding something about the logic of revolutionary power that applies well beyond China.

By 1966, Mao had not fully controlled the party for five years.

The Great Leap Forward had killed thirty million people and damaged Mao’s standing within the leadership. Liu Shaoqi, who had become head of state in 1959, and Deng Xiaoping, who ran the party Secretariat, had managed the recovery through pragmatic policies that Mao found ideologically offensive: private farming plots, market incentives, reduced commune sizes, grain imports. The policies worked. The famine ended. The economy stabilised. And the fact that pragmatism had succeeded where Mao’s revolutionary mobilisation had catastrophically failed was, in Mao’s reading, not a lesson to be learned but a threat to be eliminated.

The threat was not to his physical safety. It was to his theory of history. Mao’s entire political identity rested on the proposition that revolutionary consciousness — mass mobilisation, ideological commitment, the will of the people directed by correct party leadership — could overcome material constraints. The Great Leap had tested this proposition at scale and killed thirty million people. If the party drew the obvious conclusion, Mao’s theory was wrong, and if his theory was wrong, his legitimacy as leader rested on nothing but habit and inertia.

Mao’s solution was to change the question. The party officials who had implemented the pragmatic recovery were not, in his reframing, capable administrators who had cleaned up a mess. They were capitalist roaders — party members who had used the emergency of the famine to smuggle revisionist ideology into the socialist system, steering China back toward capitalism under the cover of crisis management. The Cultural Revolution was the instrument for removing them.

The instrument was the young.

In the summer of 1966, Mao effectively dissolved the normal structures of party authority and summoned the youth of China to make revolution. The Red Guards — students from universities and high schools across the country, most of them teenagers — were given authority to identify, denounce, and punish the capitalist roaders, the class enemies, the holders of bourgeois values. They were given rail passes to travel anywhere in China. They were freed from their schools, which were closed. And they were given a target list that was, in practice, anyone with education, expertise, cultural authority, or party seniority.

The instrument of the movement was a small red book. Quotations from Chairman Mao Zedong — the Little Red Book — was printed in an estimated five billion copies between 1966 and 1971, making it likely the most widely printed book in human history after the Bible. Waving it was not optional. Memorising its passages was not optional. The book became a loyalty test, a weapon, and a mirror — the Red Guard holding it up to the accused and demanding to know why their actions did not reflect its teachings. The gap between the book’s revolutionary idealism and the brutal reality of the struggle sessions happening under its authority was total. That gap did not slow anything down.

The results were predictable to any observer of what happens when institutional authority is abolished and mob energy is sanctioned by the state. The Red Guards held “struggle sessions” — public denunciations in which the accused was paraded before crowds, forced to confess invented crimes, and subjected to physical and psychological abuse. Teachers were beaten by their students. Professors were paraded through city streets in dunce caps. Scientists were sent to pig farms. Writers were silenced or killed. Historical artifacts were smashed as remnants of feudal culture. Temples were destroyed. Libraries were burned.

The violence was not uniform across China. Some regions experienced it more intensely than others, and some local officials found ways to mitigate its worst effects. But nowhere was immune. Between 1966 and 1969, the most intense phase of the Cultural Revolution, an estimated 500,000 to two million people were killed — the range reflects both the genuine uncertainty and the political sensitivity of the accounting. Millions more were imprisoned, sent to labour camps, or exiled to the countryside for “re-education.”

Among the targets were virtually all of the senior party officials who had built the People’s Republic. Liu Shaoqi — the head of state, Mao’s designated successor — was denounced as a traitor, stripped of all positions, and died in a concrete cell in 1969, denied medical treatment, his body wrapped in a bedsheet and buried under a false name. Deng Xiaoping was twice expelled from the party, sent to work in a tractor factory, and survived partly through the protection of Zhou Enlai and partly through the self-effacement he had learned from years of navigating Maoist politics.

Zhou Enlai deserves a note of his own in any account of the Cultural Revolution. As Premier throughout the entire period, he occupied the impossible position of a man trying to moderate a catastrophe while remaining close enough to the leader causing it to retain any influence at all. He protected some targets, negotiated some releases, prevented some executions — all while maintaining the public posture of support for a movement he privately understood to be destructive. Whether this makes him a hero of quiet resistance or a collaborator who kept the machine running is a question Chinese historians have debated with increasing frankness in the brief periods when such debate has been permitted. When Zhou died of cancer in January 1976, the spontaneous public outpouring of grief in Tiananmen Square — which the party attempted to suppress and which eventually had to be tolerated — was a measure of how differently he was perceived from the movement he had nominally served. The Tiananmen incident of April 1976, when crowds gathered to commemorate Zhou and were dispersed by security forces, was a direct precursor to the protests that would shake the square again in 1989.

The destruction of the intellectuals deserves particular attention, because its consequences extended decades beyond the Cultural Revolution itself.

Universities closed in 1966. They would not reopen fully until 1976, and the restoration of genuine academic standards took longer still. An entire generation — those who were of university age between 1966 and 1976 — received no higher education. Some were sent to the countryside. Some worked in factories. Some managed, at risk, to read and study privately. Most lost the formative years of their intellectual development to a decade of enforced ignorance that Mao called re-education.

This generation is known in China as the “lost generation” or the “sent-down generation.” They are now in their sixties and seventies. Some of them rose to prominence in the reform era and became the technocratic elite who built the economy of the 1990s and 2000s — their deprivation made them, paradoxically, ferociously driven. But the institutional knowledge destroyed in those ten years could not be fully recovered. Fields of expertise that had been developing — particle physics, genetics, computer science, economics — were interrupted for a decade during the period when the rest of the world was making foundational advances. China’s scientific establishment in 1976 was not merely ten years behind where it would have been. It was behind in ways that are difficult to measure because the counterfactual — what Chinese science might have achieved in an uninterrupted decade — cannot be recovered.

The attack on cultural heritage was equally systematic and equally irreversible. The Four Olds — old customs, old culture, old habits, old ideas — were the declared targets. In practice this meant the destruction of temples, monasteries, historical sites, family genealogies, and private collections accumulated over centuries. Tibet suffered particularly severely, with the majority of its monasteries destroyed or damaged during the Cultural Revolution — the physical record of a distinct civilisation methodically eliminated in the name of ideological uniformity. China’s own Confucian heritage was targeted with equal thoroughness: the birthplace of Confucius at Qufu was attacked, his tomb desecrated, his temple damaged. The revolution that claimed to represent Chinese civilisation was destroying the artefacts of that civilisation at a rate that no foreign invader had managed.

The Cultural Revolution’s attack on expertise is worth reading against the 141 pages. The quantum communications programme, the AI investment, the robotics industry — all of it depends on the kind of deep technical expertise that the Cultural Revolution systematically destroyed. The engineers and scientists building China’s technological future are working within an institutional tradition that was interrupted for a decade, rebuilt under conditions of intense political pressure, and is now operating within a system that has tightened, under Xi, in ways that limit the academic freedom that produces genuine scientific breakthroughs.

Whether that constraint matters at the frontier of scientific achievement is a genuine question. It is one the 141 pages do not address.

The Cultural Revolution officially ended in 1976, though it had been winding down since Lin Biao’s death in 1971.

Lin Biao was Mao’s designated successor during the Cultural Revolution — the Defense Minister who had organised the military’s support for the movement and whose image, alongside Mao’s, appeared in the propaganda of the period. In September 1971, Lin Biao died in a plane crash in Mongolia. The official account, endorsed by the party after a period of silence, was that Lin had attempted a coup against Mao, failed, and fled toward the Soviet Union before his aircraft ran out of fuel. The alternative accounts — that Mao had already begun to move against Lin before the crash, that the crash was not an accident — have never been definitively resolved.

What the Lin Biao episode demonstrated was that even the people Mao had elevated highest were not safe. If the Defense Minister and designated successor could be posthumously denounced as a traitor, the category of “person safe from the Cultural Revolution” was effectively empty.

The Gang of Four — Mao’s wife Jiang Qing and three associates who managed the Cultural Revolution’s final phase — were arrested within weeks of Mao’s death in September 1976, by a coalition of military and party leaders who had survived the decade through careful self-preservation. Their trial, conducted in 1980 and 1981, was the party’s formal accounting of the Cultural Revolution’s crimes — conducted in a way that focused blame on the Gang of Four and, somewhat more gently, on Mao’s “mistakes in his later years,” while preserving the overall legitimacy of the revolutionary generation.

Jiang Qing, defiant throughout the trial, told her accusers: “I was Chairman Mao’s dog. Whoever he told me to bite, I bit.” It was the most honest description of the Cultural Revolution’s power structure offered by any of its principals.

Deng Xiaoping, who had survived two purges and a decade of internal exile, was rehabilitated for the last time in 1977. He would spend the next two years consolidating power within a party leadership that was largely composed of people who, like him, had survived the Cultural Revolution through some combination of luck, caution, and protection. They shared, as a generation, a bone-deep understanding of what ideological excess could do — not as an abstraction, but as lived experience. They had watched colleagues beaten to death. They had written self-criticisms. They had worked in factories and pig farms while their expertise was declared bourgeois.

What they built with that understanding — the reform and opening that transformed China — is the subject of the next part of this series.

But the Cultural Revolution’s relevance to the present is not merely historical. Xi Jinping spent part of his own adolescence in the countryside, sent down during the Cultural Revolution after his father, Xi Zhongxun — a senior party official who had worked closely with Deng Xiaoping — was purged in 1968. The younger Xi was fifteen years old when he was sent to Liangjiahe, a village in Shaanxi province, where he lived in a cave dwelling, carried manure, and built a biogas digester. He applied seven times to join the Communist Youth League before being accepted. He applied multiple times to study at a Beijing university before being permitted to do so. He has spoken about that period — the hardship, the manual labour, the years of uncertainty — as formative. He credits it with teaching him perseverance.

What he did not emerge from it with is what Deng’s generation emerged with: an institutional horror of the conditions that produced it. Deng had watched colleagues beaten to death. He had been paraded through the streets himself. He had experienced, viscerally, what a system without institutional checks does to everyone within reach of the leader’s suspicion. That experience shaped the informal norms he built into the post-Mao party: collective leadership, term limits, retirement ages, the requirement that major decisions be made by consensus rather than unilateral decree.

Xi’s consolidation of power — the elimination of rivals, the tightening of ideological conformity, the reconstruction of a system in which the leader’s judgment is effectively unchallengeable — has struck many observers of China as a structural recurrence of the conditions that made the Cultural Revolution possible. The observation is not lost on Chinese citizens who lived through it. It is simply not a safe one to make aloud.

Which is, in its own way, the point.
