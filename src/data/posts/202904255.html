There is a hierarchy to American promising, and over the past year your southern neighbours have been ascending it with the steady determination of a man climbing a ladder he is also setting on fire.

It began, as you may recall, with concepts of a plan. This was the celebrated 2024 formulation — the healthcare scheme that existed in the same way a half-remembered dream exists, gauzily, around the edges, dissolving on contact with daylight or follow-up questions. A concept of a plan is to a plan what a photograph of a sandwich is to lunch. You cannot eat it, but you can post it.

From there the Republic graduated to the Memorandum of Understanding, the diplomatic instrument of the present moment, of which more in a minute. And now, watching the news this morning from my Toronto kitchen while Dior conducted her usual dawn inspection of the back fence, I see the ladder has reached its summit. The United States has arrived at the I.O.U. — the purest, most distilled form of promising yet devised, in which the promise sheds the last embarrassing residue of specificity and becomes, at last, only a signature.

Allow me to explain how each rung works, because as a Canadian I have a professional interest in watching large objects fall.

The Memorandum of Understanding is a document whose entire genius is that it is not a document. It is the handshake that comes with a legal disclaimer insisting it is not a handshake. Two parties sign it precisely so that they may later explain, with wounded sincerity, that they signed nothing. It is the bureaucratic equivalent of crossing your fingers behind your back, except the fingers are notarized and there is a press conference.

The MOU has been the workhorse of the current administration’s foreign policy. The Iran “agreement” announced this past week — and I use the quotation marks the way a bomb-disposal technician uses tongs — is, on close inspection, a thicket of understandings about future understandings. The Strait of Hormuz would reopen, the framework said — and has, on paper, several times now, with roughly the durability of a sandcastle at high tide. Negotiations will commence. A sixty-day window will open. Note the verbs: will, will, will, will. This is not a deal. This is a deal’s concept of a plan, which is itself a memorandum of an understanding about an intention. We have nested the promising like a Russian doll, and when you finally pry open the smallest doll there is nothing inside but the word “soon” — or, in the President’s own native dialect, “within two weeks,” a phrase that has now outlived several wars, at least one pandemic, and whatever the original question was.

But the MOU, for all its evasions, still gestures vaguely toward content. It still pretends. And pretending, eventually, becomes exhausting. Which brings us to the summit.

The I.O.U. dispenses with pretense altogether. It is a promise that has achieved enlightenment by abandoning all earthly attachment to detail. And the President, in his wisdom, is now poised to issue two of them simultaneously — one to Tehran, and one to you.

The first I.O.U. is the large one.

The text of the memorandum, read out by U.S. officials, contains this sentence, which I will quote in full because no paraphrase improves on it: “The United States of America undertakes with regional partners to develop a definitive, mutually agreed plan with at least USD $300 billion for the reconstruction and economic development of the Islamic Republic of Iran. The mechanism for the implementation of this plan will be finalized as part of a final deal within 60 days.”

I want you to notice what that sentence does not say. It does not say who pays. It does not say how. It does not say when, beyond “within 60 days of a deal that does not yet exist.” It is, in the truest sense, an I.O.U. — a number with a promise attached and absolutely nothing load-bearing underneath it.

When Vice President Vance was asked directly on CBS whether Iran would have access to such a fund, he replied that it was “the sort of thing they could have access to, funded by the Gulf Coast Coalition” — presumably meaning the Gulf Cooperation Council, though one forgives a man for losing track of acronyms this early in a press tour — “so long as they honour their end of the obligation.” Hours later, the President posted that the $300 billion figure was “Fake News, put out by the Dumocrats.” Two days after that, a Reuters source confirmed the $300 billion figure was real, that it would be a private investment fund rather than government money, and that more than half of it had already been informally committed by companies across several continents.

Confirmed Monday morning. Denied Monday night. Confirmed again by Wednesday.

So: not fake news. Just a fund whose existence the Vice President confirmed, the President denied, and an anonymous source later confirmed again, on background, to a wire service. This is not a deal shrouded in secrecy. This is a deal shrouded in everyone involved saying different things on consecutive days and calling it clarity.

Now. Let us pause here, because we have arrived at the part of the story that requires a calculator, and Canadians are issued one at birth along with our health cards.

For the better part of a decade, the gentleman now occupying the Oval Office built an entire rhetorical career on a single number: the roughly $150 billion he said Iran received in sanctions relief and unfrozen assets under the 2015 nuclear deal. “The dumbest deal I’ve ever seen,” he called it, more than once, in more than one state. “Iran will make another crazy deal where they give them $150 billion,” he warned, repeatedly, on the campaign trail, as though the figure itself were a kind of incantation against future weakness.

Here is the part even his own supporters might find useful to know: the $150 billion was never real. It was the high-end estimate, the most generous possible reading of how much frozen Iranian money existed anywhere in the world, not how much Iran could actually touch. The Treasury Department said, at the time, under the Obama administration, that the true figure Iran could freely access was a little over $50 billion — roughly a third of what would become the standard talking point. PolitiFact rated the $150 billion claim “dodgy.” CNN, fact-checking the same number years later, called it “a high estimate” with “the real figure likely much closer to $50 billion.” Even the Obama White House itself, defending the deal in real time, said the $150 billion figure was “entirely off base.”

So we are not comparing $300 billion to $150 billion. We are comparing $300 billion — a real number, in a real signed memorandum, with at least half of it reportedly already committed by private companies — to a number that was always closer to $50 billion, dressed up as $150 billion for maximum outrage. Measured against the real Obama-era figure, the new fund isn’t twice the size of the deal he spent a decade calling the dumbest in history. It is roughly six times the size. Measured against the inflated figure he actually used in his speeches, it’s merely double. Either way, the man who built a career on getting outraged at someone else’s big number has now signed a bigger one, and it has his own signature on it instead of someone else’s.

There is also a third figure worth knowing, separate from either of those: in 2016, the Obama administration settled a decades-old military equipment dispute with Iran for $1.7 billion, a payment Trump has also, on occasion, folded into his “ransom” framing despite it resolving a dispute that predated the Islamic Republic’s seizure of American hostages by exactly the amount of time it takes to resolve nothing, ever, quickly, in international law.

While we are on the subject of things that are new this time around, here is one more number worth your attention, because it has no precedent at all in the Obama-era version of this story: the toll.

Iran did not charge ships for passage through the Strait of Hormuz before this war. It does now — or, depending on which Iranian official you ask on which day, it most certainly does not, it merely charges “service fees” for navigation, security, and environmental coordination that ships are, in practice, required to pay in order to pass. Reporting this spring put the going rate at roughly $2 million per vessel for what Tehran calls an authorization and what everyone else calls a toll by another name. President Trump has said the strait will be “permanently toll-free.” Vice President Vance told CNBC it would remain “toll-free for the long term.” Iran’s Foreign Ministry spokesman, for his part, has said repeatedly that Tehran does not charge tolls, immediately before describing, in detail, the fees ships will be charged to pass.

This is not a dispute over an old number wearing new makeup, the way the $150 billion always was. This is a genuinely new line item, invented during this war, that did not exist under any previous American administration’s dealings with Iran, and the two governments cannot agree in public on what to call it, let alone who is right.

Which brings us to the second I.O.U. — the one with your name on it.

While a fund worth at least $300 billion takes shape for Tehran’s benefit — administered by parties not yet named, sourced from countries not yet finalized, on a timeline that resets to “within 60 days” every time anyone asks a follow-up question — the President has, rhetorically if not yet literally, issued a promissory note to the American people. I have taken the liberty of reproducing it, since the administration has not yet released the official version and I am confident I have captured the spirit:

I.O.U. The Bearer is entitled to: ______________ Amount: (to be determined) (later) (possibly) (we’ll see) Redeemable: upon the honouring of obligations by parties not yet specified Backed by: the full faith and credit of a man who spent a decade calling $150 billion the dumbest deal in history and has now structured one worth twice that Signed, D.J.T. (very strong signature, everyone says so, the best signature)

This is the masterstroke of the I.O.U. as a form. It promises everything by specifying nothing. It is the concept of a plan’s final, perfected descendant — a promise so unencumbered by particulars that it can never quite be broken, because there is nothing in it that fully commits. You cannot easily be held to an I.O.U. that delivers conditionally, eventually, pending compliance, funded by parties to be named. It is, in this sense, almost an honest document, in the way that a blank cheque is honest about being blank.

There was, this week, no shortage of Republican voices willing to say the number out loud and flinch. Senator Rick Scott of Florida put it plainly: “I can’t imagine how I would support giving them $300 billion.” Senator Kennedy of Louisiana — who has made a small, lonely vocation of being the one Republican in the room willing to say what everyone else is thinking — offered his own verdict on Iran’s trustworthiness with the kind of folksy bluntness that travels well on cable news: that no one, in his words, who wasn’t “homeschooled by a day drinker” could feel confident Iran would honour its end of anything. It is not, strictly, a joke about the money. But it is a joke about the foundation the money is being promised on, which amounts to the same thing.

Most of his colleagues, when pressed for comment on the fund itself, retreated to a posture perfected by men who have suddenly remembered they left the oven on: “I haven’t seen the documents,” said Kennedy himself, with the kind of straight face that takes practice. Senator Wicker withheld comment outright. It is a remarkable thing to watch elected officials simultaneously confirm and decline to discuss the same number in the same week.

And so the ladder is fully climbed. Concept of a plan, memorandum of understanding, I.O.U. — promising refined and refined again until it became pure vapour, weightless, frictionless, beautiful in the way that a soap bubble is beautiful right up until the half-second before it isn’t.

Before the war: free. During the war: $2 million and a change of flag, if Pakistan can spare one.

I should update you, because the bubble did not survive the weekend.

This morning, Iran announced it was closing the Strait of Hormuz again — not as a new grievance, but, by Tehran’s own account, because Israel had continued striking Hezbollah targets in Lebanon, which Iran considers a violation of the memorandum’s very first clause, the one requiring hostilities to end “on all fronts, including Lebanon.” The United States Central Command promptly disputed the closure’s effect, noting that commercial traffic through the strait had, if anything, increased. Iran does not control the Strait of Hormuz, a CENTCOM spokesperson said. Iran, evidently, disagrees, and has disagreed in this particular way several times now since the war began.

So the very first promise in the fourteen-point memorandum — the one that did not require a calculator, did not require a Gulf coalition, did not require a single dollar from anyone — could not survive five days. Vice President Vance, who had postponed his trip to Switzerland on Thursday citing “logistics,” is now airborne toward it, joining Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, who arrived ahead of him. “I can only be there for a day or two,” he told reporters before boarding, which is either an admirable display of confidence in how quickly this can be resolved, or the diplomatic equivalent of telling your dinner guests you can only stay for one course.

I mention this not to pile on — the ceasefire collapsing and re-forming and collapsing again is a tragedy first, for the people in Lebanon and the people in the missile paths on every side of this, and only a satirical footnote second. But it does rather underline the thesis. We are still arguing about whether $300 billion exists, who’s paying it, and to whom. Meanwhile the one clause in the whole agreement that asked for nothing but silence — stop shooting, on all fronts — didn’t make it to the weekend. The I.O.U. for the big number hasn’t even come due yet, and the small, free, already-signed promise is already bouncing.

In Tehran, meanwhile, Narges Mohammadi remains in prison. No I.O.U. has been issued for her release. No memorandum understands her. No concept of a plan contemplates her freedom. The promising, you understand, is reserved for the things that can be counted, and she has the misfortune of being a human being rather than a number, and therefore she does not appear on the ledger at all.

Three hundred billion dollars, real or not, funded by someone or no one, for reconstruction. Nothing for the woman in the cell. That, in the end, is the only line item that was ever real.
