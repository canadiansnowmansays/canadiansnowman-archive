This series has spent parts 100 and 109 documenting AI deployed into consequential systems — Medicare, gambling promotions, air traffic control — with the people affected given no real say before the fact. This entry covers the one place this series has found where that pattern is actually being reversed, not through legislation or corporate goodwill, but through the oldest mechanism organised labour has: the contract.

What one union actually won

As The New York Times’ Rebecca Davis O’Brien reported on September 22, 2026, Local S6 of the International Association of Machinists, representing more than 4,400 workers who build Navy ships at Bath Iron Works in Maine, negotiated new contract language in August requiring the company to inform the union at the “conceptual stage” — not at implementation, not after the fact — of “any proposed addition or modified technology, equipment or process,” AI and robotics explicitly included. Tim Suitter, the union’s president, frames the change in terms this series’ own account of regulatory capture in Part 100 would recognise immediately: “It was the union trying to force the company’s hand about being more transparent when it comes to A.I. The sooner we can be part of that conversation and represent the members’ side of it, the better off we are.” That is, functionally, a private-sector disclosure requirement — the same category of obligation Steve Case proposed as federal policy in this series’ own Part 110, achieved instead through collective bargaining rather than legislation, and already in force at one specific workplace rather than pending in a Congress this series has documented as institutionally incapable of passing anything comparable.

The contract isn’t merely aspirational. When Bath Iron Works introduced a “co-bot” — a programmable welding arm — the union determined the company hadn’t actually followed the new language and filed a grievance, which the two sides were still working to resolve when O’Brien’s report appeared. Devin Ragnar, a younger machinist who pushed for the AI provisions and now handles the local’s communications, described the company’s own reaction to the original request as surprised, “as if they hadn’t put a lot of thought into the matter” — a small, specific data point suggesting the company’s AI rollout planning genuinely hadn’t accounted for worker input as a design constraint until the union made it one by contract. Since ratification, per O’Brien’s reporting, company leadership has begun proactively bringing technology questions to union leaders rather than waiting to be challenged — a reversal this series has not documented occurring through any regulatory or governmental channel covered in its run so far.

The disclosure clause sits inside a contract with real weight behind it. Roughly 4,500 members of Local S6 ratified the agreement in August, Morgan Womack reported for the Portland Press Herald: a five-year deal running from August 28, 2026 to August 2031, with a 17 per cent base wage increase in the first year, 4 per cent a year after that, and what Chris Wiers of the machinists’ District 4 called “stronger job protections.” The length matters for the A.I. language. Whatever the shipyard plans to install between now and 2031, the union now has a contractual right to hear about it while it is still an idea.

Bath is not the first bargaining table to produce this kind of clause, and the earlier ones show how the idea travels. The Writers Guild of America’s 2023 agreement with the Hollywood studios includes a plain disclosure rule: “The Company must disclose to the writer if any materials given to the writer have been generated by AI or incorporate AI-generated material.” On the docks, the International Longshoremen’s Association signed a six-year master contract with the United States Maritime Alliance in March 2025, running through September 2030, which ILA president Harold Daggett said delivered “full protection against automation,” alongside what the employers described as “a framework for implementing technologies that will create more jobs while modernizing East and Gulf Coast ports.” Writers won a right to know; dockworkers won protection against full automation. The Bath machinists won something earlier in the process than either: notice at the conceptual stage, before a machine has been bought.

The broader strategy, and the lawyer building it nationally

Joseph Bonomo, a labour lawyer advising multiple unions on AI bargaining, supplies the tactical framework behind Bath Iron Works’ win and similar efforts elsewhere: “Define the Function, Not the Marketing Label.” His argument is that employers routinely obscure AI deployments behind euphemistic language — a “scheduling tool,” a “safety platform,” a “productivity system,” a “camera upgrade” — specifically to avoid triggering scrutiny the technology would otherwise invite if accurately named. Bonomo’s own prescribed response is interrogative rather than reflexively oppositional: what does the system actually do, does it collect data, does it make predictions, does it influence discipline or staffing or pay, and — a question this series’ own account of AI vendor relationships elsewhere has not previously surfaced — did the vendor consult the union before writing the code in the first place. His blunter framing of the stakes, delivered directly to union clients: “A.I. is the hill to die on. Because if you don’t have a job at the end of that contract, what good was the contract?”

Bonomo’s specific concern about algorithmic discipline connects directly to a thread this series has run since its own coverage of AI-driven decision systems in Part 100 and Part 109: he urges unions to demand contract provisions guaranteeing human review before any algorithm-generated report can be used to discipline a worker, precisely the kind of accountability layer this series has documented missing from DraftKings’ gambling-promotion algorithm and the FAA’s SMART air-traffic tool alike. Richie Johnsen, who leads the machinists’ air transport division and was, at the time of O’Brien’s reporting, negotiating with United and Alaska Airlines ahead of talks with Southwest and American, draws the same line in plainer language: AI improving corporate profits that flow back to workers is acceptable; AI replacing workers outright is not, and “we have to put in language into our contracts that addresses that. Because that’s the only way that happens” — a statement of the underlying theory this entire essay has been building toward: that in the absence of the kind of federal disclosure law this series’ own Part 110 already catalogued Steve Case proposing, contract-by-contract bargaining may be the only mechanism producing binding AI accountability anywhere in the American economy, at least outside the one statehouse where labour has since begun to win it by law.

That statehouse is California. On September 30, 2026, Governor Gavin Newsom signed five workplace A.I. bills backed by the state’s labour federation, according to the AFL-CIO. The one closest to Bonomo’s demand is SB 947, Senator Jerry McNerney’s “No AI Bosses Act,” which requires human oversight before A.I. can be used to fire or discipline a worker. SB 951, from Senator Eloise Gómez Reyes, updates the state’s WARN Act to require notice of A.I.-related layoffs and orders a study of A.I.’s effect on the workforce. Two Assembly bills restrict A.I. surveillance at work, one of them banning emotion-recognition technology, and a fifth requires licensed health-care professionals, not A.I., to make clinical decisions. Liz Shuler, the AFL-CIO’s president, who had joined Lorena Gonzalez of the California Federation of Labor Unions and Chrissy Lynch of the Massachusetts AFL-CIO, co-chairs of the federation’s state A.I. task force, in urging Newsom to sign, called it “a victory for working people in California and a testament to the power of the labor movement and its allies.” The sequence is worth noticing. What Bath’s machinists had to bargain for one shipyard at a time, California’s unions turned into statute for every worker in the state, and the demand they wrote into law is the same one Bonomo has been telling locals to write into contracts.

The tension this essay shouldn’t resolve on the union’s behalf

Not every voice in O’Brien’s reporting agrees on how AI should ultimately be treated once the disclosure fight is won. Ragnar himself avoids using AI personally, on principle: “If we let A.I. do menial tasks for us, we are letting these machines think for us. If welders surrendered some of their work to a co-bot, their skills would erode. It’s one of the best things about being human — creating, thinking, and problem solving.” David Sullivan, the union’s eastern-territory vice general, takes a more open view of the same technology, noting that a machine capable of welding a seam in 15 seconds rather than the time it takes a human, without injury risk, could mean more ships under construction and more jobs overall rather than fewer: “Maybe the new technology would be great. It’s the unknown.” That disagreement, occurring inside a single union local rather than between labour and management, is worth preserving rather than resolving, because it mirrors a tension this series has documented at the industry level since its earliest parts — genuine uncertainty about whether a given AI deployment represents augmentation or replacement, playing out here at the scale of an actual bargaining unit rather than in the abstract.

What this adds to the picture this series has already assembled

Liz Shuler, the AFL-CIO’s president, called AI “the single biggest threat to working people of our lifetime” in April — rhetoric matching the urgency this series has documented from AI safety researchers throughout its run, applied instead to labour displacement and the widening gap between wages and corporate profits rather than existential catastrophe. What O’Brien’s reporting adds that this series hasn’t previously had occasion to document is a genuine, working example of AI governance actually happening — not proposed, not stalled in committee, not deferred to a regulatory agency that doesn’t yet exist, but negotiated, ratified, and already being enforced through a grievance process at one specific shipyard. Whether that model scales beyond a single machinists’ local representing a fraction of one percent of the American workforce — union density sits at roughly 6 percent in the private sector, per Moira Bulloch of the Communications Workers of America, quoted in O’Brien’s reporting — is a separate question this series has no way to answer yet. But as a proof of concept for what binding AI accountability actually looks like in practice, Bath Iron Works’ new contract language is, on the evidence this series has gathered across more than a hundred parts, more concrete than anything Washington has produced.
