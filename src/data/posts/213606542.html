Every backlash story this series has told so far has come from the outside: a councilman recalled (Part IX), teenagers with public-records requests (Part XII), tenants fighting an Ellis Act eviction (Part XV). Pennsylvania offers something different — a sitting governor with an obvious eye on 2028 reversing a policy he personally championed, in a region where the same boom he’s now restraining is also being welcomed as long-overdue redemption for a fifty-year-old wound.

As The New York Times’ David Gelles reported on August 31, 2026, President Trump travelled to Pittsburgh in July 2025 to claim credit for pledges exceeding $90 billion from Google, Amazon, and Anthropic to build data centres and related infrastructure across the region. Governor Josh Shapiro was, at the time, an enthusiastic partner — fast-tracking approval for Amazon’s enormous data centre, offering incentives to steer projects toward old industrial hubs like Pittsburgh, and helping bring more than 120 planned data centres to the state. On August 18, 2026, he changed course. Mr. Shapiro signed an executive order he called the nation’s strictest guardrails on data centres, labelled developers “predatory,” and ended the state’s fast-track approval process in favour of a system requiring local community sign-off before any project can advance. The Philadelphia Inquirer’s Gillian McGoldrick reported on August 18, 2026 that the order, built on what the governor calls GRID standards, removes every data-centre project, Amazon’s included, from fast-track permitting, requires developers to supply their own electricity generation, mandates community-benefit agreements covering local hiring and infrastructure, bars state nondisclosure agreements on data-centre deals, and orders a public map of every proposed project. It stops short of a moratorium. “I have no other choice than but to take this executive action to protect the good people of Pennsylvania from these predatory developers,” Mr. Shapiro said. His Republican challenger, Stacy Garrity, accused him of “gaslighting” voters.

The order’s legal footing is not settled. Robert Swift reported in the Altoona Mirror on September 6, 2026 that John Quigley, who served in the cabinets of Governors Ed Rendell and Tom Wolf, questioned whether a governor can alter the legislature’s tax exemption for data-centre equipment, or direct the independent Public Utility Commission, by executive order. The GRID standards also appear in House Bill 2650, which passed the Democratic House and stalled in the Republican Senate. “The question of the governor’s authority isn’t necessarily set,” Mr. Quigley said. He pointed to the order’s severability clause as a sign that its drafters anticipated parts of it might be struck down.

Same governor, same industry, two irreconcilable audiences

Mr. Shapiro’s reversal fits neatly into this series’ TACO framework, and it is worth being precise about which quadrant it occupies. This is not obfuscation — the executive order is a real, specific policy change, not a rhetorical gesture. It reads instead as accommodation under direct electoral pressure: data centres have become, per the Times’ reporting, a top issue in the November 2026 midterms, and a Democratic governor weighing a 2028 run has calculated that the political cost of continuing to fast-track projects now exceeds the credit he’d get for the jobs. Costa Samaras, who directs Carnegie Mellon’s Scott Institute for Energy Innovation, summarised the underlying problem in a single line: energy use, emissions, water use, and community friction are piling up with nobody managing any of it — “a free for all.” That is a genuine regulatory vacuum, not a manufactured grievance, and it is the same vacuum Part IX documented in Independence, Missouri, and Part XII documented from the teenage-activist side in Colorado Springs and Loudoun County.

But this series would be doing readers a disservice if it treated Pennsylvania as simply “Part IX again, but statewide.” The regional context here cuts the other way, hard, and deserves equal weight.

The redemption narrative, told without irony by the people living it

Western Pennsylvania’s industrial identity did not begin with steel’s collapse in the 1980s — it began with a colonial-era coal mine in 1760, ran through Andrew Carnegie’s Edgar Thomson Works, and continued through Westinghouse Electric’s founding a decade after that. What collapsed in the 1980s was not the region’s identity so much as its economic floor, and Brian Kennedy of the Pittsburgh Technology Council describes the decades since in blunt terms: population fled, manufacturing left, and “a gray cloud” hung over the region for a generation. Rob Bair, president of the Pennsylvania Building and Construction Trades Council, frames the data-centre build-out as the closest thing to a construction boom the Commonwealth has seen in that entire span — one that, done right, could hand a new generation of tradespeople a genuine career rather than a temporary contract. A March study commissioned by a coalition of state business groups projects $12 billion in annual economic output and more than 19,000 additional jobs by 2036. Manufacturing is already following the data centres in: an $86 million Mitsubishi electronics plant, a $70 million Hitachi transformer investment, a $166 million GE Vernova turbine facility. Homer City — once the site of the state’s largest coal-burning power plant — is being redeveloped into a $10 billion, 4.4-gigawatt gas-fired data-centre campus, and Mr. Kennedy’s framing of that specific site is worth quoting because it captures the emotional core of the pro-development case better than any statistic: instead of Homer City standing as a monument to the coal economy that broke this region, it becomes a monument to the most advanced AI industry in the world.

That is not spin invented for a reporter. It is a coherent, sincerely held argument from people who watched steel leave and are watching something else finally arrive, and this series’ prior parts — built mostly around communities resisting the boom — have not yet given that argument its due weight. It deserves to sit in the same essay as the backlash, not as a rebuttal to be dispatched quickly, but as the other half of a genuine regional split.

The bill nobody’s accounting for

The environmental cost is not hypothetical, and it is larger than any single figure this series has cited before. The Environmental Integrity Project found that just seven of Pennsylvania’s proposed data-centre power plants could together emit roughly 68 million tons of carbon dioxide-equivalent annually — comparable to the yearly emissions of about 15 million gasoline cars. Matt Mehalik, who runs the nonprofit Breathe Project and grew up in a steel-mill family, spent summer 2026 testing air quality across the Ohio River from Shell’s petrochemical complex in Beaver County, recording elevated benzene — a carcinogen — and toluene, which damages liver and kidney function. Shell has exceeded its own pollution limits on benzene and nitrogen oxide since 2022, paid a $5 million civil penalty in 2023, and continued violating limits since, according to state records. Mr. Mehalik’s framing is the piece’s most damning line, delivered without heat: this area has suffered poor air quality for a long time, and data centres are simply the next chapter of the same story, not a break from it.

The energy arithmetic at Homer City sharpens the point further. When complete, the plant’s turbines will generate enough electricity to power the city of Pittsburgh more than twice over — and none of that power will reach the grid. All of it is spoken for by the data centre it exists to serve. That is a useful, concrete answer to a question this series has asked in the abstract since Part V’s Piketon lease: when a hyperscale facility claims a dedicated power plant for itself, what exactly is the community around it getting in exchange for hosting the emissions? In Homer City’s case, the answer is jobs and tax revenue, and nothing routed back to the regional grid that residents’ own utility bills draw from.

The senator who won’t be persuaded by either side’s numbers

Katie Muth, a state senator from southeast Pennsylvania, had spent the previous year building the resistance case Mr. Shapiro’s executive order only partially answers — her Senate Bill 1359, introduced on June 1, 2026, would impose a three-year moratorium on hyperscale data centres, which would make Pennsylvania the second state after New York’s Hochul-ordered pause (referenced in this series’ Part IV) to freeze development outright. Her bill had not advanced as of early October 2026. The measures that did move were weaker. The House passed House Bill 2496, which gives municipalities an optional 180-day pause on data-centre applications, and Broadband Breakfast reported on July 13, 2026 that the Senate Rules Committee had approved, 13 to 4, Senator Jarrett Coleman’s Senate Bill 1345, which would let municipalities pause projects for up to 18 months while they rewrite zoning. Ms. Muth’s answer, in a statement on June 29, 2026, was that “OPTIONAL is NOT protective,” and that the House bill’s pause is triggered only by a data-centre application, so a gas plant “proposed, permitted, or owned separately” could proceed untouched. Her rotunda rally in summer 2026 drew more than a hundred protesters carrying signs reading “You Can’t Drink Data,” and her framing of the underlying question echoes almost exactly the language Part XII’s teenage organisers used months earlier, arriving independently at the same challenge to the industry’s own talking points: who is this progress actually for, and who is paying for it. That two campaigns months apart and a generation apart in age landed on the same rhetorical question, without apparent coordination, is itself a data point about how thin the industry’s public case has worn.

“Let Data Reign” has a body count in gigawatts

The scale Pennsylvania is fighting turns out to be modest next to what’s already approved elsewhere, and the numbers are worth setting down precisely because they dwarf even Homer City. As The New York Times’ Hiroko Tabuchi has documented in an ongoing series on the Trump administration’s environmental rollbacks, at least 82 gas-burning power plants are being built or proposed nationwide specifically to power data centres, according to the Environmental Integrity Project — most permitted within the past eighteen months, together capable of releasing as much planet-warming pollution annually as roughly half of all passenger vehicles in the United States. Texas alone accounts for 39 of those plants, and the state’s fast-track permitting system explains why: gas plants tied to data centres had cleared Texas regulators in an average of 18 days over the year before Ms. Tabuchi’s report, against a 285-day average for a traditional power plant seeking a full air permit. Some projects moved even faster — a Meta-linked plant outside El Paso was approved in 20 days, a second project in 3 days, a third in 2 days, none requiring public notice. Texas developers have learned to exploit this deliberately: breaking a single large power project into smaller phases lets each phase individually qualify for reduced scrutiny, a loophole Thomas McGarity, an administrative law professor at the University of Texas, describes bluntly as a way of “building many of these units and putting them together” while ignoring the cumulative environmental effect any one of them would trigger under full review.

Amazon’s own entry into this race outstrips every plant this series has cited so far, Pennsylvania’s included. The company has invested in a gas-burning plant in Pecos County, Texas — permitted to release up to 33 million tons of carbon dioxide annually, more than twice the output of the James H. Miller Jr. plant in Alabama, which the Times identified as the single largest greenhouse-gas emitter in the country at 16 million tons. Amazon co-founded the Climate Pledge, a voluntary commitment to eliminate its emissions by 2040; the company’s own emissions have risen every year since, and Amazon has openly acknowledged that AI data centres are the reason. Michael Thomas of Cleanview, the firm that first reported Amazon’s involvement, frames the plant as a preview rather than an outlier: “We’re going to see an explosion of off-grid gas projects” as data centres increasingly bypass the public grid entirely in favour of dedicated, on-site generation that answers to no utility regulator at all.

El Paso is Homer City’s mirror image, argument for argument

Meta’s $14 billion data centre outside El Paso reproduces almost every fault line this essay has already drawn in Pennsylvania, compressed into a single Texas neighbourhood. The city granted Meta an 80 percent property-tax break running 35 years; the company’s power plant — 813 small gas-burning generators, each producing roughly as much pollution as 13 cars, all permitted to run continuously — cleared regulatory review in 20 days with no public hearing. When residents finally got a City Council hearing months later, Michael Ramirez, a cook who rushed over on a break from his shift, delivered the same complaint in miniature that Katie Muth has been making at the state level: “When something important is taking place, tell us.” The neighbourhood itself splits exactly along the Bair/Mehalik line this essay has already established for Pennsylvania — Winston Colon, a veteran living in the data centre’s shadow, calls it progress (”What do we want? Another Walmart?”), while spray-painted graffiti reading “Fuera Meta” — Get Out Meta — appears on a bridge nearby, and Michael Ramirez has since circulated a recall petition against the local officials who approved the project. Meta says the buildout will employ more than 4,000 construction workers at peak; once operational, the site will support roughly 300 permanent positions, a ratio residents have noted explicitly when weighing the tax break against what the city actually keeps.

The Trump administration’s own hand in accelerating this is a matter of record, not inference. EPA administrator Lee Zeldin has said publicly that cutting regulatory “barriers” to data-centre growth is a top agency priority, and the administration has separately intervened in federal court on the side of one of Musk’s own companies, defending it against accusations that it is illegally running dozens of unpermitted gas turbines in Mississippi. Jen Duggan of the Environmental Integrity Project put the through-line plainly: these plants “carry significant environmental footprints,” and federal and state regulators are “fast-tracking them” anyway. Against that backdrop, Pennsylvania’s Homer City conversion — a single 4.4-gigawatt plant, publicly permitted, tied to a governor now imposing community sign-off requirements — reads less like the leading edge of a national reckoning and more like the one jurisdiction, so far, where anyone with regulatory power chose to slow down.

Why Shapiro’s reversal is swimming against the federal current

Mr. Shapiro’s pullback also has to be read against what Washington is doing at the exact same moment, because the two are moving in opposite directions. As The Atlantic’s Matteo Wong and Charlie Warzel reported on September 1, 2026, President Trump posted on Truth Social that communities failing to “let Data Reign” would be left “backwards and poor,” and his administration was preparing to approve the largest fossil-fuel power plant in American history — one capable of generating as much electricity as nine large nuclear reactors — specifically to power a mega data centre for OpenAI. Heatmap’s Jael Holzman reported on August 21, 2026 that the plant is a 9.2-gigawatt gas facility on federal land in Pike County, Ohio, the same Piketon site whose lease this series examined in Part V, financed by SoftBank’s SB Energy and Nvidia. The Army Corps of Engineers began its review on July 10 and scheduled it to conclude on December 23, 2026, roughly seven months, using an Environmental Assessment, the lighter form of review, rather than a full Environmental Impact Statement. As of early October 2026 the permit had not been issued. That plant would dwarf even Homer City’s 4.4-gigawatt gas conversion, and it suggests Mr. Shapiro’s guardrails are a state-level exception forming inside a federal environment actively pushing the opposite direction, not a sign the tide has turned nationally.

The polling underneath both moves is also worth setting down precisely, because it’s more one-sided than almost anything else this series has measured: roughly three-quarters of Americans oppose a data centre being built near where they live, a level of agreement the Wong/Warzel piece notes is rare on any subject in the country’s current political climate. Gallup’s Jeffrey M. Jones reported on May 13, 2026 that 71 percent of Americans opposed an AI data centre in their area, 48 percent strongly, against 53 percent who opposed a nuclear plant nearby. Strong opposition ran at 56 percent among Democrats and 39 percent among Republicans, which means a Democratic governor in a swing state faces it most sharply inside his own party. That number gives Mr. Shapiro’s move a much broader electoral logic than a single state’s backlash would suggest, and it makes the federal government’s continued fast-tracking — Homer City’s neighbour-in-scale now rising in the pipeline — a more clearly countermajoritarian choice than this series has previously had the data to call it.

What this adds to the ledger

Pennsylvania doesn’t resolve the tension this series has tracked since Part IX — it sharpens it into its clearest form yet, because for the first time the pro-development and anti-development cases are being made, with equal sincerity, by people living in the same towns, watching the same trucks arrive. Mr. Bair is not wrong that rural communities starved of tax revenue could genuinely benefit from a recurring stream of data-centre dollars. Mr. Mehalik is not wrong that a region already carrying decades of industrial pollution is being asked to carry more of it before anyone has finished measuring the last round. And El Paso proves the split isn’t a Pennsylvania idiosyncrasy — Winston Colon and Michael Ramirez are having the identical argument, block by block, that Mr. Bair and Mr. Mehalik are having across the state Mr. Shapiro governs. Governor Shapiro’s executive order does not choose between these two truths so much as it acknowledges, formally, that no one — not the state, not the developers, not the residents — yet has a mechanism for weighing them against each other before the trucks arrive. Eighty-two gas plants and counting, most of them approved in less time than it takes to renew a passport, say plainly that Pennsylvania is still the exception. That mechanism, not the boom itself, is what’s actually missing from this ledger, in Texas, in Pennsylvania, and everywhere else this series has looked.
