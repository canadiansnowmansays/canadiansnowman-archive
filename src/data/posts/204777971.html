We have come a long way from that first image — the British terrace house, built to hold heat against four centuries of cold, turned into an oven by a single record-breaking June. I have used that house, all the way through, as the figure for something larger: a whole civilization living inside infrastructure, physical and legal and political and psychological, calibrated to a climate that is quietly walking out the door. We have toured the rooms of that house, one by one. The melting foundation of the Canadian North. The smoke that crosses borders. The carbon tax that died at home and the beautiful clean coal that lives on south of the border. The insurance that quietly withdraws, the river treaties that fray, the appliance that cools one room while heating the street, the populists with their tractors, the petrostate that is my own country, the republic turning two hundred and fifty in a furnace. It has been, I will not pretend otherwise, largely a catalogue of a house failing.

So I want to end somewhere else. Not in false cheer — I promised in the introduction that I would refuse both the doom that says nothing can be done and the techno-optimism that says someone clever will do it for us, and I will keep that promise to the last paragraph. But after fifteen essays of diagnosis, the honest question, the only one really worth asking, is the constructive one: what would it actually look like to build the house for the summer that is coming? Not to mourn the old house, and not to pretend it isn’t failing, but to build its replacement. Because we can. That is the thing I most want to leave you with. We are not, in fact, helpless. We are merely, so far, unwilling, and unwillingness is a different problem with a different solution.

Let me be concrete, because vague hope is just despair in a nicer coat, and I have no use for either.

We know how to make the electricity. This is the part that has changed most profoundly, and most hopefully, in just the last few years, and I documented it in the essay on China and gestured at it throughout. Solar and wind are now the cheapest sources of electricity in human history — cheaper than coal, cheaper than gas, cheaper to build new than many existing fossil plants are to keep running. The world installed more clean power last year than ever before, and the curve is still bending upward. The single largest emitter on the planet has very likely passed its emissions peak, years ahead of schedule, on the strength of a clean-energy buildout so vast it beggars description — a hundred solar panels a second. The technology problem, the one we spent decades insisting had to be solved before we could act, is substantially solved. The sun and the wind are free, and we have finally learned to harvest them for less than the cost of digging up the past and setting it on fire.

We know how to store it and move it. Batteries, the missing piece that was always the fossil industry’s favorite objection — what do you do when the sun sets and the wind drops? — have fallen in cost as dramatically as the panels, and the grid-scale storage now coming online is beginning to answer the intermittency question that was supposed to be unanswerable. The work of rebuilding and extending the electrical grid is enormous and unglamorous and essential, and it is the kind of project — continental in scale, demanding of coordination and investment — that nations used to take pride in accomplishing.

We know how to keep cool and warm. I spent the last two essays on this. The heat pump heats and cools at a fraction of the energy, and cleans up automatically as the grid does. The ancient wisdom of passive design — orientation, shade, thermal mass, the windcatcher and the courtyard and the thick wall — married to modern engineering, can take a building most of the way to comfort before a single watt is spent. We know how to build the house that handles both the old winter and the new summer. We have simply, mostly, chosen not to.

We know how to move. The electric vehicle, about which I have been honest regarding my own stake, is no longer a curiosity; in the largest car market on Earth it is approaching half of new sales. The internal combustion engine, the defining machine of the twentieth century, is beginning its long retirement, and every electric mile is a permanent subtraction from the demand that drives the oil sands and the wells.

So if we know how to make the power, store it, move it, and stay comfortable, all with technology that exists and works and is increasingly the cheapest option — then the problem was never really the technology. I said this would be the conclusion, and here it is. The barriers that remain are the ones this series has actually been about, and they are not technical. They are the gap between the pledge and the permit, the visible cost and the invisible benefit, the end of the world and the end of the month, the industries and identities built on the old way, the comfort of insisting the old house will do. The climate problem, at this late date, is not a physics problem or an engineering problem. It is a problem of justice, of politics, of honesty, of will — which is harder, in a way, because we have no machine for manufacturing those. But it is also, in a way, more hopeful, because will is a thing that can change, sometimes with startling speed, when enough people decide it must.

What would building the new house actually require? It would require making the transition fair, so that the farmer and the commuter and the Alberta oil worker and the grandmother in the hot apartment are brought along rather than run over — the lesson of the populist backlash, paid attention to at last. It would require making the costs honest and the compensation visible, the lesson of the carbon tax that died. It would require the wealthy, high-emitting nations, my own emphatically included, to stop hiding behind the emissions of others and to count the carbon we export as well as the carbon we burn. It would require treating the people of the dying fossil economy with the dignity we failed to show the coal counties, building them a place in the new house rather than bricking them into the old one. It would require, above all, abandoning both of the comfortable lies — that we are doomed, and that we will be saved without effort — in favor of the uncomfortable truth that sits between them: that a great deal can be done, that we know how to do most of it, and that whether we do it is up to us.

Let me try, just once, to describe the house itself, because I have spent sixteen essays on the one that is failing and owe you a glimpse of the one that could replace it. It is not a fantasy or a utopia; every element of it exists somewhere today. It is a house oriented to its climate the way the Persians and the Romans understood — shaded against the summer sun, opened to the prevailing breeze, built of materials that hold the night’s coolness into the heat of the day, so that it needs little cooling before any machine is switched on. The cooling it does need comes from a heat pump that warms it in winter and cools it in summer at a fraction of the old energy, running on electricity from wind and sun that is now the cheapest power humanity has ever generated, stored in batteries that have fallen in price faster than almost anyone predicted, carried on a grid rebuilt for the purpose. The car in the driveway is electric and charges from the same clean current. The whole arrangement emits a small fraction of what its predecessor did, costs less to run, and — this is the part we forget — is more comfortable, more secure, and more durable than the leaky, fuel-dependent, weather-vulnerable house it replaces. We are not being asked to accept a worse life in order to save the planet. That is the great lie at the heart of the resistance. We are being offered a better house, and refusing to move into it, out of loyalty to a furnace.

The barrier, again, is not that the house cannot be built. It is built, here and there, already — in the Scandinavian town that put the turbine factory where the mine closed, in the Seville plaza cooled by a revived qanat, on the Chinese roofs going up at a hundred panels a second, in the heat-pumped homes of a hundred countries. The barrier is that we have not yet decided, collectively and in earnest, to build it everywhere, for everyone, fast enough — and that deciding requires the political and moral work this series has been cataloguing the failure of: the honesty, the justice, the will. Those are human variables, not physical constants, and human variables change. That is the whole of my hope, and I will not dress it up as more than it is, but I will not pretend it is less either.

I am seventy-four years old. I will not see most of how this turns out, and I have made a certain peace with that. The worst of the heat, the hardest of the choices, the question of whether the house gets built in time — those belong to my children, Maggie and Jim, and to my grandson Sawyer, and to a future I can describe but not inhabit. Dior, lying on the cool kitchen tile through another Toronto summer that her Arctic ancestors would not recognize, will not see it either. When I write about the world that is leaving, I am writing, in part, about my own — the temperate, stable, reliable world I was born into in 1951 and have watched, in my later years, begin to slip its moorings. There is grief in that, and I have not tried to hide it.

But grief is not despair, and an ending is not the same as a defeat. The house we were born into is leaving. That is simply true, and no amount of beautiful clean coal or axed taxes or boarded windows will call it back. The only question — the question I have circled for sixteen essays and will leave you holding — is whether we will use the time and the tools and the knowledge we actually have to build the next house, the one fit for the summer that is coming, before too many of us are left without shelter in the heat. We know how. We have the materials. The sun is free and the wind is free and the old wisdom is recoverable and the new technology is cheaper every year. What we lack is not capacity. It is the will to begin in earnest, and the justice to bring everyone along, and the honesty to admit that the winter we built for is over.

The bricks were never stupid. The people who laid them were doing their best for the world as it was. But the world has changed, and the house must change with it, and that work — patient, unglamorous, contested, and entirely possible — is the only work that matters now. We built for winter. Summer has come. It is time, at last, to build for summer.

That is the end of this series. Thank you for walking through the whole house with me. There is a great deal of work to do, and the morning, for all the heat in it, is not too late to begin.

Degrees

1951 — the year I was born, into a world whose climate had been stable for the whole of human civilization and was assumed to remain so. It will not. The generation now being born will live their whole lives inside the change. The least my generation can do, in the time we have left, is hand them a house worth living in, and the tools to finish building it. We know how. That was never the question. The question was always whether we would.
