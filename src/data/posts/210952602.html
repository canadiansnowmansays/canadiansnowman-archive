Part I closed on a number that deserved more than a footnote: $662 billion. This part gives it one.

A Liability by Any Name Except the One That Counts

According to Moody’s Ratings, hyperscalers have collectively signed roughly $662 billion in data-center lease commitments that have not yet commenced — deals where the paperwork is done, the capacity is promised, and construction is underway or planned, but the lease itself hasn’t technically started under the accounting standard that governs when it must appear on a balance sheet. Until it starts, GAAP doesn’t require it to be counted as debt. It sits instead in a company’s footnotes and forward disclosures, visible to anyone who reads far enough into a 10-K, invisible to anyone comparing headline leverage ratios.

Put that number next to the on-balance-sheet debt these same companies have already taken on — the more than doubled long-term debt at Alphabet, the 81 percent jump at Amazon, both covered in Part I — and the $662 billion in commitments is larger than the combined on-the-books debt of the companies carrying it. That is the sentence worth sitting with. The liability everyone can see is smaller than the liability almost nobody is required to report as a liability at all.

Why the Rule Exists, and Why It Stops Working Here

The accounting logic isn’t sinister on its face. Lease-commencement standards exist because a signed lease for a building that hasn’t been built yet, with a start date years out, genuinely carries different risk than a lease you’re already paying on. Projects get delayed. Plans change. Treating every signed intention as a current liability would overstate risk in the ordinary case. The standard was built for a world of individually modest, individually cancelable commitments — not for an industry signing hundred-billion-dollar commitments in serial, each one a bet that a specific data center will be needed, staffed, powered, and paying for itself years from now.

That's the part the rule wasn't built to flag. A single delayed lease is a rounding error. Hundreds of billions of dollars in delayed leases, concentrated among a handful of companies whose entire investment thesis depends on demand materializing roughly on schedule, is a different kind of risk — the kind Natasha Sarin flagged directly on The New York Times's "The Opinions," the podcast transcript covered in Part I: the physical bottleneck of whether power plants and grid connections can actually be built at the pace being promised. Every quarter those connections slip, the gap between commitment and completion widens, and none of that gap shows up as debt anywhere an ordinary investor would think to look for it.

Who’s Carrying This, and How Much Room They Have

Not every hyperscaler carries this risk identically. The independent research gathered for this series shows real variation: the top five U.S. capex spenders are on track to commit over $600 billion in 2026 alone, part of an estimated $2.1 trillion deployed across 2026-2028, pushing capex intensity to roughly 34 percent of revenue — more than double the 15 percent peak reached at the height of the 1990s internet buildout. One analysis flagged Oracle specifically as an outlier carrying negative outlooks and negative free cash flow, distinct from the broader investment-grade profile of its larger peers. That distinction matters. A company financing this buildout from a position of strong operating cash flow, as JPMorgan’s own research argues most hyperscalers still are, has room to absorb a slipped timeline that a more thinly margined company does not.

This is also where the bull case deserves its full hearing, not a token line. JPMorgan projects hyperscaler operating cash flow will exceed $900 billion by 2027 — a number large enough that even a substantial chunk of unrecognized lease liability could, in principle, be absorbed without crisis, provided the underlying revenue materializes close to on schedule. The counterargument isn’t that the debt is unpayable. It’s that the accounting treatment lets the market price these companies as though the debt barely exists, right up until a quarter when it very visibly does — the same mechanism, structurally, that turned Situational Awareness’s leverage from an asset into a 36-hour emergency in Part I. The lease commitments aren’t leveraged in the hedge-fund sense. But they share the same basic property: a liability that behaves like debt exactly when the market needs it not to.

Where This Sits in the Ledger

The Asimov Gap has spent its recent parts documenting a regulatory vacuum around who governs what AI companies can build and deploy. This is the accounting equivalent of that same vacuum: a framework designed for an earlier, smaller scale of commitment, now being asked to accurately represent a buildout an order of magnitude larger than anything it was written to capture. Nobody broke a rule here. That’s precisely the problem. The rule was adequate for a world that no longer exists, and adjusting it is nobody’s urgent job, because on paper, everything currently reconciles.

Whether $662 billion in off-balance-sheet commitments becomes this cycle’s version of a warning sign or simply resolves quietly as capacity catches up to demand is not a question this series can answer from the outside. What it can do, again, is keep the number visible somewhere the balance sheet currently doesn’t.
