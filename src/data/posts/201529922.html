Postmark: Eastern Ukraine. A contested intersection. Forty-five days. No Ukrainian casualties.

The DevDroid TW 12.7 (also called Droid TW 12.7), a tracked unmanned ground vehicle armed with a .50-caliber M2 Browning machine gun.

For forty-five days, Russian troops could not take the position.

They attacked through fog. They attacked at night. They attacked with infantry supported by armoured vehicles. Every time, they were met with the same thing: precise, sustained machine gun fire from a tracked vehicle the size of a ride-on lawnmower, armed with a .50-calibre M2 Browning, operated by a Ukrainian soldier sitting ten kilometres away in a protected location.

The machine was the DevDroid TW 12.7, built by a Ukrainian defence company. The unit deploying it was NC-13, a recently established strike company within Ukraine’s Third Army Corps specialising entirely in uncrewed ground vehicles. The commander was Mykola “Makar” Zinkevych, who described the logic of the deployment in terms that have since been quoted in military analysis papers, newspaper features, and NATO briefings.

“Robots do not bleed.”

Every two days, a crew would approach the position from 2.4 miles back to restock ammunition and perform maintenance. Other than those brief windows, the intersection was held by a machine. Zero Ukrainian casualties in forty-five days of continuous contact.

This is the postcard from the battlefield. It is the hardest one to write. It requires both eyes open.

The Laboratory Nobody Chose

No military planner designed the Ukraine war to be a robotics laboratory. Russia’s full-scale invasion began on February 24, 2022, with conventional military objectives pursued with conventional military means. What happened in the months and years that followed was not the execution of a predetermined doctrine. It was improvisation at industrial scale, driven by necessity, accelerated by desperation, and observed by every defence ministry on Earth with an intensity that no exercise or simulation could have generated.

A young Ukrainian technician assembles components for an FPV drone in a workshop setting. Ukraine’s rapid drone industry growth — from dozens to hundreds of producers — was driven by exactly this kind of distributed, adaptive manufacturing under wartime pressure.

Ukraine’s situation was stark: it was defending a nearly 1,300-kilometre front line against an adversary with overwhelming advantages in conventional artillery, armour, and manpower. It could not match Russia soldier for soldier. It could not sustain the casualty rates that attrition warfare at that scale demands without eventually losing the war. The equation had no conventional solution. The unconventional solution that Ukraine reached, step by step, engagement by engagement, is now changing the nature of warfare globally.

In the first year of the war, drones were primarily used for reconnaissance — aerial eyes correcting artillery fire, observing enemy positions, transmitting targeting data. By 2023 they were also strike weapons, with first-person-view drones piloted by operators watching through goggles, diving into Russian vehicles and detonating on impact. By 2024 they were both simultaneously, coordinated in hunter-killer pairs where a surveillance drone designated a target and a strike drone executed the engagement, with minimal human decision-making in the interval between identification and impact. By 2025, AI-enabled drones were beginning to acquire and engage targets without human direction at the moment of strike — not fully autonomous in the legal sense, but operating at speeds and scales that made the human in the loop increasingly nominal rather than substantive.

Ukraine established the world’s first Unmanned Systems Forces as a separate military branch in June 2024, under the command of Colonel Vadym Sukharevskyi. By early 2026, the force comprised an estimated 40,000 to 80,000 personnel dedicated entirely to the operation and development of unmanned systems. Russia followed by announcing its own unmanned systems branch in December 2024. Two nations at war were simultaneously reorganising their militaries around robots.

Inside a Ukrainian drone production facility. Rows of partially assembled FPV drone frames illustrate the scale of domestic manufacturing that reached millions of units annually by 2024–2025.

Ukraine produced approximately 2.2 million unmanned aerial vehicles in 2024. In 2025, that number was projected to exceed 4.5 million — more than the entire NATO Alliance combined. Ukraine’s defence ministry budgeted more than $2.6 billion for drone procurement in 2025 alone, with ninety-six percent going to domestic producers. The number of registered aerospace companies in Ukraine grew from 41 at the start of the invasion to more than 290 by early 2025. A Ukrainian startup developed an armed ground vehicle in four days in a shed, at ten percent of the cost of an imported equivalent. This is what industrial warfare looks like when the industry is fighting for its survival.

What the Drones Became

The FPV drone — first-person-view, piloted by an operator wearing goggles connected to a camera on the drone — began the war as a modified hobby quadcopter. By 2025 it had evolved into a precision weapon system with specialist variants for different missions: anti-armour, counter-drone, perimeter suppression, personnel. Ukrainian operators became extraordinarily skilled at their use, their training partly drawn from the gaming culture that had produced a generation comfortable with fast, three-dimensional spatial awareness.

A Ukrainian FPV drone operator wearing goggles, controlling a first-person-view strike drone from a protected position. By 2025, such operators had become extraordinarily skilled at three-dimensional spatial awareness, often drawing on gaming culture.

In July 2025, Ukraine’s 3rd Separate Assault Brigade reported the first documented case of Russian soldiers surrendering to a robotic system. FPV drones and a kamikaze ground robot carrying antitank mines struck a bunker. When a second robot approached the damaged position, two surviving Russian soldiers held up a hand-written cardboard sign in Russian: “We want to surrender.” They were not surrendering to Ukrainian troops. There were no Ukrainian troops present. They were surrendering to a machine.

In January 2026, a DevDroid unmanned ground vehicle captured three Russian soldiers in the Lyman area without infantry support. In April 2026, President Zelensky announced what he called the first time in the history of the war that a position had been captured by robots alone, without any Ukrainian soldiers present in the assault. Defence analysts watching the development coined a term for the emerging doctrine: the Drone Wall — a system in which robotic platforms handle attrition and seizure while human soldiers are reserved for consolidation and holding.

Russia has been learning simultaneously. Between January 2024 and August 2025, the number of Iranian-designed Shahed attack drones launched into Ukraine per month increased tenfold — from 334 to more than 4,000. Ukrainian engineers, examining the wreckage, found NVIDIA chipsets enabling AI targeting and thermal vision modules capable of locking onto targets at night. “They used to be imprecise and pretty stupid,” one Ukrainian defence technology CEO told IEEE Spectrum. “Now they are interconnected, which allows them to exchange information with each other.” The drones that struck Ukrainian cities in late 2025 were not the same machines as the ones launched in 2022. They had learned.

A Ukrainian unmanned ground vehicle (UGV) equipped with a heavy machine gun operating in a contested area, supported by personnel in the background. Systems like these have been documented forcing Russian positions to surrender or be captured without Ukrainian infantry involvement.

The Governance Gap

While Ukrainian and Russian engineers were iterating on autonomous weapons systems through battlefield feedback loops measured in weeks, the international community was meeting in Geneva to discuss the matter.

The UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons has been hosting discussions on lethal autonomous weapons systems since 2014. More than a decade of expert group meetings produced a rolling text — a framework document with areas of convergence — but no binding treaty. In May 2025, 96 states attended the first UN General Assembly meeting specifically on autonomous weapons systems. The UN Secretary-General António Guterres called lethal autonomous weapons “politically unacceptable and morally repugnant” and called for a legally binding treaty by the end of 2026. The International Committee of the Red Cross joined the call. More than 120 countries support some form of treaty negotiation.

The United States, Russia, and China have not agreed to binding restrictions.

This is the Asimov Gap in its most consequential form. The machines that select and engage targets without meaningful human control are not a hypothetical. They are in production. They are being deployed. The legal and ethical frameworks that should govern them were designed for a world that did not have them, and the process for creating new frameworks moves at a pace measured in diplomatic cycles rather than software release cycles. The gap between what is being built and what we have agreed about what should be built is not narrowing.

The UN Secretary-General’s call for a binding treaty by the end of 2026 is significant. The fact that it has taken ten years of expert group meetings to produce a rolling text with no binding force, and that the states with the largest autonomous weapons programs — the United States, Russia, China — have not committed to the restrictions being discussed, is more significant still. The 2026 CCW Review Conference will be the moment of truth for a decade of negotiation. Diplomatic momentum is real. So is the gap between momentum and the enforceable law that would actually constrain the machines being built and deployed while the diplomats negotiate.

Isaac Asimov’s First Law — a robot may not injure a human being — was always a novelist’s formulation rather than an engineer’s specification. It could not survive contact with the real world. The weapons being developed in Ukrainian sheds and deployed on the Kharkiv front are not governed by any law analogous to it, nor by any international framework with the enforcement mechanisms to matter. Asimov himself knew this. His stories were not arguments that the Three Laws would work. They were explorations of the ways they would fail. The Ukraine battlefield is producing the same kind of exploration, at a scale and speed that Asimov never imagined, and with consequences that are not fictional.

The Diffusion Problem

Silhouettes of armed drones against a dramatic sky — a visual metaphor for the scale of Ukrainian drone operations and the emerging “Drone Wall” doctrine in which robotic systems handle much of the attrition.

What is developed in Ukraine does not stay in Ukraine.

The US Army Military Review documented that China intensified its development and integration of FPV and drone swarm systems directly in response to lessons observed from Ukraine. North Korea mass-produced FPV systems with Russian technical support after its troops encountered them in combat alongside Russian forces. Serbia adopted domestically developed FPV variants in 2024. Saudi Arabia and Qatar signed ten-year defence cooperation agreements with Ukraine in March 2026, specifically to acquire Ukrainian drone expertise.

Ukraine produced approximately 290 drone companies by early 2025. Those companies have engineers, intellectual property, and battle-tested design knowledge. When the war eventually ends, that knowledge will not disappear. It will diffuse — into commercial applications, into the defence industries of friendly states, into the hands of actors whose intentions are less clearly defined than a country defending its territorial integrity against an invading army.

The question of what happens when the technology that Ukraine developed under existential pressure becomes available at commercial scale — available to militaries, to non-state actors, to anyone with the engineering capacity to build it — is not a future question. It is a present one, and no governance framework has yet provided an adequate answer.

The economics are alarming in their simplicity. A commercial FPV drone can be purchased for under two hundred dollars. The modifications required to make it a weapon add cost but remain within reach of actors that international security frameworks were not designed to deter. Ukraine built its drone industry in sheds, under fire, with engineers who learned as they went. The barrier to entry for autonomous weapons development is falling faster than the governance architecture is rising.

What the Battlefield Is Not Telling Us

There are things the Ukraine battlefield data cannot tell us that matter enormously.

The performance of autonomous weapons systems in Ukraine is being evaluated against the specific conditions of that conflict: flat terrain, degraded electronics environments, a clearly defined adversary, legal and political context that makes certain ethical questions feel abstract compared to the immediate reality of soldiers dying. Those conditions will not all replicate in other contexts.

A counter-insurgency environment in a dense urban area, where the distinction between combatant and civilian is the central operational challenge, presents a completely different problem for autonomous targeting than an attritional front-line battle in eastern Ukraine. A system that reliably identifies Russian armoured vehicles in a Ukrainian field will not necessarily reliably distinguish between a combatant and a child carrying a backpack in a market in Mosul. The international humanitarian law principle of distinction — the requirement to differentiate between combatants and civilians — is not a constraint that can be programmed into a machine with current AI capabilities and verified to function reliably at the moment of engagement.

This is not a reason to dismiss the military effectiveness that Ukraine has demonstrated. It is a reason to be cautious about the claims made by advocates of autonomous weapons that battlefield effectiveness in one context constitutes proof of safety in all contexts. The test that matters for international humanitarian law is not whether the system works. It is whether the system can comply with the law in the conditions where it will actually be deployed.

The Necessary Postcard

This is the hardest postcard in the series to write because it sits at the intersection of genuine wonder and genuine danger in a way that no other territory does.

The engineering that has allowed Ukraine to hold a position for forty-five days without a single casualty, to force Russian soldiers to surrender to a machine, to build an autonomous weapons industry from scratch under fire — that engineering is extraordinary. The ingenuity is real. The lives saved on the Ukrainian side are real. The reduction in the human cost of a war that Ukraine did not choose and cannot afford to lose at any human price is real.

And the machine that held the position does not know why it is holding the position. The drone that identifies a target does not understand what a target is. The system that is becoming capable of selecting and engaging without human direction at the moment of strike does not have values, does not have judgment, does not have the capacity for the moral reasoning that international humanitarian law requires of those who use lethal force.

We are building machines that kill. We have not built the frameworks to govern them. The gap between the two is the Asimov Gap at its most dangerous — and the Ukraine battlefield has just demonstrated, to every military on Earth, that the machines work.

The turtle at the coral reef leaves nothing disturbed behind it. The DevDroid TW 12.7 held a position for forty-five days with no Ukrainian casualties and an unknown number of Russian ones. Both are robots. Only one of them was built to leave things a little better than it found them.
