On the evening of August 9, 2019, Jeffrey Epstein was escorted back to his cell at the Metropolitan Correctional Center in Lower Manhattan after making a final phone call. He had told his girlfriend he loved her. He had said he wouldn’t be able to call for a month. He had said she should be strong.

The cell was empty. His cellmate had been transferred that morning. No replacement had been assigned, despite a standing order — flagged repeatedly, passed up the chain of command, and ignored — that Epstein was never to be left alone.

A guard plugged in his CPAP machine. The door closed behind her. It was the last time anyone would acknowledge seeing Jeffrey Epstein alive.

Seven years later, as Charles Homans, Steve Eder, Jan Ransom and Michael Rothfeld reported in an exhaustive New York Times investigation published yesterday, we finally have the clearest account yet of what happened in the hours that followed. It is not the story most people have been telling.

I. The Room Where Theories Go to Die

Let us begin with what the new reporting establishes — not as speculation, but as documented fact drawn from the three million pages of Epstein files released under the Epstein Files Transparency Act, from more than forty new interviews, and from a suicide note that sat sealed in a lawyer’s files for nearly six years before The Times won its release in court.

Jeffrey Epstein was suicidal. He had been suicidal since the day his bail was denied, on July 18, 2019, thirteen days into his incarceration. That afternoon, he returned to his cell and asked his cellmate, Nicholas Tartaglione — a former police officer awaiting trial for four murders — a single question: “How do you make a noose?”

In the days that followed, Tartaglione caught Epstein preparing for suicide twice. Once trying to tie a sheet to the cell window grate. Once with a noose hidden under his mattress. Tartaglione reported both attempts to guards. The guards laughed him off.

Epstein’s second cellmate, Efrain Reyes, flushed a makeshift clothesline down the toilet after waking to find Epstein fidgeting with it in the dark. “Don’t try to kill yourself in this cell,” Reyes told him. “I don’t want to wake up and find you dead.” Epstein told him not to worry. “I’m never going to cause you trouble.”

The night he died, Reyes was gone. As he left the tier that morning, he told a staff member: “Get him a good bunkie. He’s not good to be alone.”

No one did.

II. What He Wrote

Among the documents released in the Epstein files — and among the material The Times won access to through separate court action — are pages of notes in Epstein’s own hand, written during his thirty-five days at the MCC. They are elliptical, riddled with idiosyncratic capitalization and punctuation, jottings rather than complete thoughts. But they are revealing.

He wrote about the frustrations of being a wealthy “Pedophile in jail.” He wrote about the incessant noise of the special housing unit — “no sleep, no air, screams.” He wrote that the guards had told his cellmate that “if he beat the shit out of me, they wouldn’t file a report.”

He wrote, in large letters across the top of a page, the words “J’ACCUSE” — an apparent reference to Zola’s famous public letter denouncing the biased prosecution of Alfred Dreyfus. And then, fragmentary and furious: “Me/Too -Jewish-Rich-Politics. Believe the victim = Believe the accuser — CRAZY!”

And he wrote, in what the reporting confirms was a suicide note found tucked in a graphic novel by Tartaglione after the July 23 episode: “They investigated me for month — Found NOTHING!!! It is a treat to be able to choose one’s time to say goodbye. Watcha want me to do — Bust out cryin!! NO FUN — NOT WORTH IT!!”

In a separate note, in the same handwriting, with the same irregular punctuation and the same personal phrase: “ONLY PAIN TO ME & Others in the future. NOT very much fun! Why should people I Lov suffer for my problem. So … Watcha want me to do? …. Bust out cryin!! Best for all.”

The suicide note was not reported to jail officials. Tartaglione’s lawyer kept it sealed in court filings, using it as leverage to counter Epstein’s claims that his cellmate had attacked him. It was not seen by investigators looking into Epstein’s death. It was made public only this May, after Times lawyers petitioned the court to unseal it.

III. What He Was Thinking About Trump

Among the legal pad pages released in the files is one of the more darkly ironic documents in the entire Epstein saga. While meeting with his lawyers in the jail’s legal-visit room, Epstein was preoccupied with what he might have on Donald Trump — who was then serving his first term in office — that he could offer to federal prosecutors in exchange for leniency.

He returned to the president again and again on the legal pad, trying to dredge up anything useful. What he produced: “Trump is a total con artist — smoke & mirrors” and “Never had money.”

That was it. A man who had spent decades at the intersection of wealth, power and predation, who had flown the world’s most powerful people on his private jet, who had socialized with presidents and princes — and the most damaging thing he could write about Donald Trump was that he was a con artist who exaggerated his fortune.

It is a detail that illuminates, in miniature, the entire mythology around the Epstein files. The conspiracy theory assumed that the files contained devastating secrets about powerful men, locked away by a deep state determined to protect the guilty. What they actually contained was a man on a legal pad, running out of leverage, unable to find anything he didn’t already know.

IV. The Institution

The Metropolitan Correctional Center in Lower Manhattan. A chronically understaffed and dysfunctional federal facility where systemic failures — absent cellmates, sleeping guards, and non-recording cameras — allowed Epstein’s death

To understand how Epstein died, you need to understand the Metropolitan Correctional Center. Not the fortress of the conspiracy theories — a facility so secure and vigilant that only an elaborate murder plot could have ended a life inside it — but the actual building: a Brutalist monolith so overcrowded, so underfunded, so dysfunction-saturated that Bureau of Prisons officials had warned it posed a safety threat to inmates and employees alike. It was closed in 2021.

The MCC was stiflingly hot in winter and freezing cold in summer. Corrections officers worked second jobs and exhausting overtime just to pay rent. Many extracted income from the jail’s thriving underground economy of contraband. Six months before Epstein arrived, the president of the corrections officers’ union wrote to top Bureau of Prisons officials: “Quite frankly, at this point, we are one incident away from a staff or inmate fatality.”

The week Epstein died, eleven MCC officers were absent from their regular shifts. The overnight guard, Tova Noel, had been ordered to extend her shift to cover for the shortage — her third consecutive shift. She routinely worked sixteen hours at a stretch and had at times fallen asleep at the wheel driving home to the South Bronx. The second guard present for much of the night, Michael Thomas, was not principally a corrections officer; he handled commissary, laundry and supplies. There was a sardonic sign attached to the computer at the guards’ desk: “MANDATORY ROUNDS MUST BE CONDUCTED EVERY 30 MINUTES ON EPSTEIN #76318-054 AS PER GOD!!!!”

Between 1 a.m. and 2:44 a.m., investigators later contended, Noel and Thomas were asleep at the desk.

Noel’s role in this story runs deeper than a single night’s negligence. She was the last person to see Epstein alive — the guard who plugged in his CPAP machine on her 10 p.m. round, then continued on her way. She and Thomas were both subsequently indicted on federal charges of falsifying jail records: they had signed paperwork certifying that rounds were conducted, when investigators quickly established that no rounds had taken place. The criminal charges effectively removed the last guard to see Epstein alive from the investigation — neither Noel nor Thomas cooperated with federal investigators until two years later, when their prosecutions were deferred on condition that they submit to interviews with the inspector general.

Tova Noel, one of the two Metropolitan Correctional Center guards on duty the night of August 9–10, 2019, leaves federal court in New York in November 2019.

The camera footage added another unresolved dimension. Around 10:40 p.m. — after Noel’s final round — one of the two functioning cameras captured an indistinct orange shape that appeared to move up the staircase toward the locked door to L Tier, where Epstein’s cell was. The footage is too low-resolution to identify the figure with certainty, and it has fuelled years of speculation that it was an assailant entering Epstein’s unit. The inspector general concluded it was most likely Noel herself, carrying inmate linens — a conclusion supported by the fact that she held the tier keys at that moment and that her location was briefly unaccounted for in the video. Noel has repeatedly denied this under oath, including in a closed-door congressional hearing this year, where she also testified that her fellow guard Bonhomme — who had by then worked more than twenty-two consecutive hours — was asleep during the critical period.

Prosecutors ultimately dropped the charges against both Noel and Thomas. Their conclusion: even if the rounds had been conducted as required, Epstein could easily have timed his death between them, given that he had a clear view of the guards’ desk from his cell window. The one truly fatal failure, prosecutors determined, had occurred hours earlier — when multiple supervisors who knew Reyes was leaving failed to assign Epstein a new cellmate. That was the moment the institution’s last line of defence disappeared. Everything that followed was consequence.

Nearly half of the SHU’s surveillance cameras, while working in real time, were not recording that night. A hardware failure had been identified a week earlier. Replacement drives had arrived that day. No one had installed them.

At some point in the early morning hours, an inmate in the neighboring cell named Chad Brown heard a sound through the wall. It sounded like ripping sheets. He tried to distract Epstein, calling out to ask if he had postage stamps. Epstein said he hadn’t ordered stamps. The tearing sound continued for about ten minutes. Then it stopped. Brown fell asleep.

At 6:30 a.m., Thomas began delivering breakfast. He arrived at Cell 220 to find Epstein motionless. He unlocked the door. Epstein was hanging from a noose tied to the bunk bed frame, his body suspended an inch off the floor. Thomas tore him down. “Breathe, Epstein, breathe!” And then, to Noel: “We’re going to be in so much trouble.”

He was right about that. He was wrong about the rest. The trouble was not a conspiracy. The trouble was that a broken institution had been handed a man it could not keep alive, and had failed at every opportunity to prove otherwise.

V. The Wrong Noose

Orange fabric from Epstein’s cell and close-up of the ligature marks on his neck, photographed during the medical examiner’s investigation. The pattern and injuries were consistent with suicidal hanging, though contamination of the scene and the wrong noose being logged as evidence helped fuel years of conspiracy theories.

The scene investigators found when they arrived seven hours later was contaminated beyond reasonable forensic use. Epstein’s cell was filled with heaps of linens he was not supposed to have — a clear safety violation that guards had overlooked because he intimidated them. Multiple nooses and strips of orange fabric were scattered across the floor. And when investigators tried to retrieve camera footage from the hallway outside the cell, they discovered it did not exist.

A significant evidentiary error compounded the chaos: investigators logged the wrong noose.

This single mistake would fuel years of conspiracy theorizing. The noose logged as evidence did not match the marks on Epstein’s neck — a narrow, deep furrow running in a straight horizontal line across his Adam’s apple, branching into a Y-shape on one side. The inspector general’s investigators later identified a different noose in scene photographs: a longer, more narrowly twisted loop, ripped apart and creased in a pattern consistent with having borne weight. It had landed in a far corner of the cell in the chaos of the morning. It was thrown away before anyone recognized its significance.

The pathology battle that followed was genuine. Medical examiner Kristin Roman ruled the death a suicide by self-hanging. Michael Baden, the pathologist hired by Epstein’s brother Mark, pointed to three bone and cartilage fractures in the neck and argued the injuries were more consistent with homicidal strangulation. His argument found enormous traction, particularly in media that had been following the Epstein story for years.

The Times consulted nine independent pathologists. Their collective assessment: neck fractures of this type, once considered definitive indicators of homicide, are now understood to occur in suicidal hangings too. And without knowing the precise configuration of the body and the noose — information that was lost in the contamination of the scene — conclusive determination either way was impossible.

“Forensics is not an exact science,” one board-certified forensic pathologist told the Times. “It really isn’t. It’s interpretive.”

What the forensic evidence could not establish, the behavioral evidence had already settled. The suicide note. The legal pad. Two rehearsals caught by Tartaglione. The clothesline flushed by Reyes. The final phone call. The will update. The remark to his lawyers, while examining a computer cable, that he was too much of a “coward” to kill himself — a protestation that, in context, reads less like reassurance than inventory.

VI. The Conspiracy Theory’s Burden

To believe that Epstein was murdered requires a specific sequence of events.

An assassin would have needed to pass undetected in real time in front of multiple surveillance cameras being actively monitored by the MCC’s control center. They would have needed three separate physical keys — to the SHU’s interior door, to L Tier, and to Cell 220 — keys held by the two on-duty guards visible on camera. They would have needed the active complicity of at least one of those guards, plus coordinated cooperation from multiple distinct operations of the jail and control center, including knowledge of precisely which cameras were recording and which were not. And they would have needed to do all of this while risking a federal murder charge carrying the death penalty.

“The ability for somebody to have killed him — it would have taken a massive conspiracy that I can’t imagine somebody not finding out about it at this point,” said Hugh Hurwitz, who led the Bureau of Prisons at the time. “Too many people would have been involved.”

The conspiracy theory is not impossible. But it requires every institution, every guard, every inmate, every investigator, every forensic pathologist, and every journalist who has spent years on this story to have missed or suppressed a coordinated murder — in a facility so dysfunctional it couldn’t keep its camera systems running.

The simpler explanation: a suicidal man was left alone in a cell. The guards fell asleep. No one heard anything until it was too late. The scene was contaminated. The wrong noose was logged. A suicide note sat in a lawyer’s sealed files for six years. And a broken institution produced exactly the outcome it had been warned, repeatedly and explicitly, it was going to produce.

Not because of a conspiracy. Because of a failure. A massive, systemic, foreseeable, preventable failure — the kind America’s institutions excel at producing when the people inside them are underpaid, overworked, undertrained, and left without adequate oversight for long enough.

VII. The Asimov Frame

Isaac Asimov imagined robots governed by laws designed to prevent harm. The First Law: a robot may not injure a human being. The designers of that law assumed the threat would come from outside — from malice, from error, from mechanical failure in service of a bad actor.

They did not design for institutions.

Institutions injure through neglect. Through bureaucratic inertia. Through the accumulated weight of a thousand small failures — unfilled vacancies, deferred maintenance, falsified paperwork, guards who are too tired and supervisors who are too overwhelmed to enforce the rules that exist to keep people alive. The MCC did not murder Jeffrey Epstein. It simply failed to keep him from murdering himself, in ways it had been specifically warned about, in a cell it had been explicitly instructed to keep occupied, with oversight it had been formally required to maintain.

The harm was not committed. It was permitted.

That is, in the end, the story of Jeffrey Epstein entire. Not murder. Permission. Decades of it. A system of permission that allowed him to abuse hundreds of girls. A 2008 plea deal that handed him thirteen months and a work-release arrangement that prosecutors later called a corruption of the justice system. Years of re-investigation that went nowhere. And finally, a cell in a broken jail where the permissions ran out — not because justice caught up with him, but because the institution couldn’t keep him alive long enough for justice to try.

VIII. A Canadian Observation

There is something almost classically American about the way this story has been consumed.

A man who sexually trafficked children for decades, who surrounded himself with presidents and billionaires and Nobel laureates, who operated a global network of abuse that prosecutors believe implicated dozens of powerful men — and the dominant public question, seven years after his death, is: was he murdered?

Not: why did it take until 2019 to arrest him? Not: who else was responsible, and why have so few been held accountable? Not: what happened to the girls? Not even: how is it possible that the Metropolitan Correctional Center, a federal facility housing the most significant sex-trafficking defendant in a generation, could not maintain functioning surveillance cameras, could not assign a replacement cellmate in eight hours, could not keep its guards awake through the night?

These are the questions the conspiracy theory forecloses. As long as Epstein was murdered, the system was subverted by a shadowy cabal. As long as someone killed him, the failure is a crime committed by identifiable villains. But if he killed himself — if he died because a hollow, understaffed, underfunded institution could not perform its basic function — then the failure is systemic. It is structural. It belongs to all of us, in the way that institutional failures in a democracy always do.

The girls were not in the Situation Room. They were not on the legal pads. They were not in the conspiracy theories. They were barely in the forensic pathology debate. They were the reason all of this happened — the reason Epstein was arrested, the reason he faced forty-five years in prison, the reason he was in Cell 220 in the first place.

As Homans, Eder, Ransom and Rothfeld reported, the discussions in the jail’s legal-visit room were not about justice for the victims. They were about leverage. About what Epstein might have on Trump. About whether he could get bail. About how to mount a defense. His lawyers sat with him for hours at a stretch, a form of “highly compensated companionship,” listening to him seethe about former friends who were distancing themselves.

The girls did not get that.

Virginia Giuffre, who met Epstein when she was a teenage spa attendant at Mar-a-Lago, who became one of his most outspoken victims, who told the world what had been done to her and was subjected to years of legal harassment and public skepticism in return — Virginia Giuffre died by suicide in April 2025, three months after Trump returned to power. She was forty-one years old.

The last image of Epstein, caught on the one functioning surveillance camera at 7:49 p.m. on August 9, 2019, shows him as a silver head of hair, visible for a split second, being escorted back to his cell for the last time. The Times describes him as “little more than a silver head of hair — stripped of his wealth and influence, reduced to something vague, elusive, a blur, a ghost.”

He is there, and then he is gone.

The girls he hurt are still here. Still waiting for the accounting that his death, and the seven years of conspiracy theorizing that followed it, have helped to defer.

The situation room was not convened for them.

It never was.
