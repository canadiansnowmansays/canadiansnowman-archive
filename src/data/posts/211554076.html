There is a specific kind of loneliness available only to the leader of a legislative minority, and Chuck Schumer has spent the summer of 2026 discovering several new varieties of it. In the span of about six weeks, the Democratic Party produced Senate nominees in three of its most closely watched races — Michigan, Maine, and Florida — and in every case, the winning candidate got there by treating Schumer’s preference, and in some cases Schumer’s active intervention, as something to run against rather than toward.

This isn’t a story about Schumer personally, though it will inevitably be read that way by people looking for a scalp. It’s a story about what happens to a party’s chain of command when the base decides, correctly or not, that the chain of command is part of the problem — and about whether “loyal opposition” can function coherently when the opposition can’t agree on who’s actually leading it.

Michigan: The Fortress That Almost Held

I wrote in Part I about Abdul El-Sayed’s roughly one-point primary win over Representative Haley Stevens, and about the sixty million dollars in outside spending — thirty million from AIPAC’s super PAC alone, the single largest outside investment in any primary in the country this cycle — that couldn’t quite close the gap. What that essay didn’t dwell on is Schumer’s specific role in assembling that fortress, because the fuller picture only became clear afterward.

Schumer’s first call, back when Senator Gary Peters announced his retirement in January 2025, wasn’t to Stevens. It was to Governor Gretchen Whitmer, whose approval ratings were the envy of nearly every Democrat in the country — ninety percent among her own party, a remarkable thirty-five percent even among Michigan Republicans. Whitmer passed, telling ABC’s “The View” she might “want to walk the Earth for a little while” instead. National Democrats’ fallback, according to reporting after the fact, was Stevens — the candidate who ultimately became the establishment’s horse, backed by Schumer, by the DSCC, and eventually by Whitmer herself, who stayed out of the primary until late and then jumped in on Stevens’s behalf when the polling tightened.

None of it was enough. El-Sayed’s win didn’t just embarrass a super PAC; it embarrassed a sequence of institutional choices stretching back eighteen months, each one made by people confident that money and incumbency would eventually be sufficient. They weren’t.

Maine: A Nominee Schumer Didn’t Even Pick

The Maine story is stranger, and in some ways more damaging to the idea of centralized party control, because the eventual nominee wasn’t the product of a primary Schumer lost — he’s the product of a crisis Schumer didn’t see coming at all.

The original front-runner was Graham Platner, an oysterman and Marine Corps veteran running as an anti-establishment populist against Schumer’s preferred candidate, Governor Janet Mills. As Axios reported at the time, Schumer had spent months publicly maintaining neutrality in the race before dropping the pretense entirely and declaring Mills “the best candidate to retire Susan Collins” — a rare direct intervention that a Platner spokesperson immediately cast as proof of the exact establishment capture Platner was running against. It didn’t matter. Platner won the primary handily.

Then, in early July, Platner’s campaign collapsed after a woman he’d previously dated came forward with a rape allegation, which he denied but which prompted a wave of Democratic officials — including some of his own allies — to call for him to step aside. He did. What followed was an unusual state convention rather than a second primary: delegates gathered in Bangor and, over a single Saturday, handed more than ninety percent of the vote to Troy Jackson, the former state Senate president and fifth-generation logger who’d finished a distant third in his own gubernatorial primary earlier that same year.

Jackson’s asked-and-answered relationship with Schumer is now a matter of public record, and it isn’t warm. Asked directly whether he intended to support Schumer as Democratic leader, Jackson didn’t equivocate: “I just think that we need new ideas, new people coming out.” At the Bangor convention itself, he told reporters he hadn’t spoken with any Democratic senators, Schumer included, despite Schumer and Senator Kirsten Gillibrand — who runs the DSCC — issuing a statement praising him for having “harnessed the grassroots energy in Maine.” It was, in other words, an endorsement extended to a candidate who hadn’t asked for one and wasn’t in any hurry to reciprocate.

Republicans, for their part, wasted no time exploiting the disorder. The Senate Leadership Fund, the McConnell-aligned super PAC, launched a six-figure digital ad campaign against Jackson the same day he was nominated — part of a planned spend north of forty million dollars — built around tying him directly to Platner’s scandal and highlighting Jackson’s own history of heated confrontations with legislative colleagues. The ad’s framing was blunt: “A volatile personality, anger issues with women, verbal abuse. It’s not Graham Platner, it’s Troy Jackson.” Whatever the fairness of that characterization, the speed of it underscores the cost of the chaos on its own terms — a national Republican operation didn’t need six months to build an attack line, because the collapse of the Platner campaign and the hurried convention that followed handed them one already assembled.

Florida: The One Nobody Fought For

If Michigan was a fortress that almost held and Maine was a crisis nobody could have modeled, Florida — decided on August 18, 2026, the same week this essay was first drafted — is something closer to a clean, uncomplicated data point, and it might be the most revealing of the three precisely because nothing went wrong that anyone could point to afterward.

The DSCC’s candidate was Alex Vindman, the retired Army officer who became a household name testifying against Trump during the 2019 impeachment hearings — a recruit chosen specifically to expand the Senate map into a state Democrats haven’t seriously contested in years. He raised $16.27 million, a genuinely enormous sum for a statewide primary. His opponent, State Representative Angie Nixon, a Democratic Socialists of America member running as a union organizer, raised under $1 million. By any conventional read of how primaries work, this wasn’t supposed to be close.

Nixon won by twelve points, 56 to 44 percent.

There was no scandal here, no collapsed campaign, no convention scramble — just a sixteen-to-one money disadvantage that turned out not to matter at all. What makes the result worth dwelling on isn’t the margin itself so much as how national Democratic strategists greeted it afterward. One told reporters the loss “might be for the best,” on the theory that Florida was never a realistic pickup this cycle and the money would likely have been wasted chasing it regardless of who won the primary. Nixon now moves on to face Attorney General Ashley Moody in a race no serious forecaster rates competitive, so in narrow electoral terms, the strategist isn’t wrong.

But sit with the shape of that argument for a second, because it’s a near-perfect inversion of the case this series made in Part I. There, the warning was aimed at the base: don’t mistake primary intensity for a representative sample of the general electorate, because nominating on enthusiasm alone risks handing winnable seats to Republicans. Here, it’s the establishment making a version of the opposite move — shrugging off a decisive institutional defeat by noting, after the fact, that the seat was probably unwinnable anyway, so the sixteen-million-dollar misjudgment doesn’t really cost anything. That’s true as far as it goes. It’s also the kind of reasoning that only sounds reassuring if you don’t ask why the DSCC recruited and funded a $16-million campaign for a seat its own strategists now describe as never having been in play.

The Part That Actually Matters

It would be easy to read all three stories as proof of the same simple lesson — establishment bad, insurgency good, Schumer should resign — and that reading would be exactly the lazy version of this argument I warned against in Part I. The more useful lesson is narrower and less satisfying: Schumer’s judgment failures in Michigan, Maine, and Florida were three different kinds of failure, and conflating them obscures what each one actually teaches a party trying to function as a coherent opposition.

In Michigan, the failure was conventional — misjudging where the base had moved, on Israel and on donor money both, and backing a candidate whose institutional support couldn’t compensate for that misjudgment. That’s a strategic error, correctable in principle by better polling and more honest internal conversations about where primary voters actually stand.

In Maine, the failure wasn’t strategic at all. Nobody could have modeled a sexual assault allegation collapsing a front-runner’s campaign six weeks before a convention. What Maine actually reveals is something closer to a structural fragility: when the top of the ticket implodes without warning, a party with a genuinely centralized chain of command should be able to coordinate quickly around a credible replacement. What Maine Democrats did instead was let ninety percent of convention delegates coalesce around a candidate who explicitly rejects the very chain of command that’s supposed to help him raise the tens of millions of dollars he’ll need against a Susan Collins campaign already fortified by a Republican super PAC’s forty-two-million-dollar commitment. Coordination happened. Alignment did not.

Florida is the third and, in some ways, the most damning failure, because it wasn’t a misjudgment about where the base stood and it wasn’t an unforeseeable crisis — it was a resourcing decision that turned out to be almost entirely disconnected from the result it was meant to produce. Sixteen million dollars bought Vindman name recognition, television time, and a national media profile. It did not buy him primary voters, and the eventual explanation — that the seat was a lost cause anyway — only raises the harder question of why that assessment arrived after the money was spent rather than before it.

Not Every Race Went This Way

It’s worth resisting the temptation to write this as a uniform story of establishment collapse, because it isn’t one, and the exceptions are as instructive as the failures. In North Carolina, former Governor Roy Cooper entered the open Senate race left by Thom Tillis’s retirement as the clear favorite and stayed there, without anything resembling the Michigan or Maine drama. Cooper’s institutional standing — a popular former two-term governor with a moderate, southern political style — meant national Democrats never had to choose between their preferred candidate and their base’s preferred candidate, because for once those were the same person. Georgia’s Jon Ossoff, defending the only Democratic Senate seat in a state Trump carried in 2024, has likewise spent the cycle focused entirely on the general election rather than fending off a primary insurgency, in part because his own record already carries enough anti-establishment credibility — a first-term senator who unseated an incumbent in 2020 — that no serious challenger from his left emerged to contest it.

The contrast matters because it isolates the actual variable. Cooper and Ossoff didn’t avoid intra-party warfare because Schumer managed them more skillfully than he managed Michigan or Maine. They avoided it because their own political brands already carried the outsider credibility that national Democratic leadership has spent this cycle failing to manufacture on its own in states without a Cooper or an Ossoff already sitting there. Where the party had an organically popular, base-trusted figure available, the machine didn’t need to intervene and mostly didn’t. Where it didn’t have one — Michigan, Maine — the machine tried to manufacture trust through spending and institutional weight instead, and in both cases, that substitute failed.

That’s a sharper diagnosis than “the establishment is out of touch,” and a more useful one for a party trying to fix the actual problem rather than just venting about it. The lesson isn’t that primaries should be abolished or that leadership should never express a preference. It’s that leadership’s preference only holds up when it’s backed by a candidate whose own standing with voters doesn’t need to be purchased at sixty million dollars a race.

What a Functional Opposition Would Do Differently

Here is where the Westminster comparison I leaned on in Part II earns its keep rather than just decorating the argument. A parliamentary opposition leader who loses the confidence of three of his party’s most closely watched campaigns in a single summer would face an actual, structured reckoning — a leadership challenge, a caucus vote, something with teeth. American Senate leadership doesn’t work that way, and it doesn’t need to; caucus leaders survive rough news cycles as a matter of course, and there’s no serious case that Schumer’s position is genuinely at risk before November. But the absence of a formal reckoning doesn’t mean there’s no cost. It means the cost gets paid later and less visibly, in the coordination problems that show up when a party actually needs its candidates and its leadership pulling in the same direction — fundraising asks that go unanswered, national resources that arrive late or grudgingly, a floor strategy in January that has to account for members who owe the leadership nothing because the leadership offered them nothing when it mattered.

Jackson, El-Sayed, and now Nixon will, as I noted in Part I, eventually have to find some working arrangement with Schumer the way Maine’s own convention-selected candidates and Michigan’s insurgents typically do once the general election forces practical cooperation. That’s manageable in the way most intra-party friction is manageable. What’s less manageable, and what this series will keep watching for through November, is whether “manageable friction” curdles into something worse if a fourth battleground nominee this cycle also turns out to have won by running against the man nominally supposed to be coordinating their party’s path back to a majority.

A loyal opposition, almost by definition, has to agree on who it’s being loyal to. Michigan, Maine, and now Florida all suggest that question is currently unresolved — not because the party lacks a leader, but because an increasing number of its most important candidates have stopped waiting for permission to find out whether they need one.

Postscript, October 12, 2026: The Map He Drew

When Part IV was drafted on August 19, 2026, it asked whether anyone in the Democratic Party still took instructions from Chuck Schumer. Michigan, Maine and Florida answered: not reliably. Seven weeks later, the minority leader has an answer of his own, and he would like it on the record.

In an interview on Friday, October 9, reported by Reid J. Epstein in the New York Times roundup “Who’s Winning the Race for Congress?” on October 12, 2026, Schumer took credit for bringing Roy Cooper, Sherrod Brown and Mary Peltola into their Senate races and for helping Josh Turek through his Iowa primary. His argument was structural. The Senate map as dealt to Democrats consigns them to the minority by the plain arithmetic of how the states are arranged, so he set out to build a bigger one. In his words: “I couldn’t accept the map as is.”

On the evidence, he is entitled to some of that credit. On Cook Political Report ratings as of October 9, Republicans have all but conceded North Carolina to Cooper. Jon Ossoff holds a commanding lead over Mike Collins in Georgia. Democrats are leading or level in Alaska, Iowa, Ohio and Texas, four states Trump carried three times and by comfortable margins, and a CNN/SSRS poll has Brown six points ahead of Jon Husted in Ohio. Senate Majority PAC has opened a $5 million advertising campaign in Kansas, which is not a sentence anyone expected to write in 2026.

That is a better map than the one Schumer was handed. Read carefully, it is also the Part IV map with its colours confirmed.

Look first at where the party is comfortable: North Carolina and Georgia. These were the two states Part IV held up as counter-examples, the places where the establishment candidate and the base never came to blows. There was no primary to lose and no convention to rebel. Cooper and Ossoff were never tests of Schumer’s authority, because nobody contested it.

Now look at where, by the Times’ account, Democrats are still nervous about races that ought to be easier: Maine, Michigan and New Hampshire. Two of those three are precisely the states where Part IV recorded Schumer’s authority failing.

In Michigan, his preferred candidate, Haley Stevens, lost the primary to Abdul El-Sayed by roughly a point, and El-Sayed’s race against Mike Rogers remains close. In Maine, primary winner Graham Platner withdrew and the state convention chose Troy Jackson, a nominee who has rejected Schumer’s leadership, to take on Susan Collins. Collins, for her part, is now wearing the cost of the Iran war in her constituents’ heating bills, with a standard tank of heating oil running about 80 per cent above last year’s price, even after calling for the war she backed to end.

The pattern is hard to miss. Where Schumer recruited and nobody objected, Democrats are winning. Where he recruited and the base said no, Democrats are still fighting for seats that a favourable year ought to be handing them. The map Schumer drew is carrying the party; the map he lost control of is where it remains exposed.

New Hampshire is the honest exception: tight, and not one of Part IV’s rift states. A thesis that explained every close race would deserve suspicion. This one explains two of three.

There is a smaller irony in the recruitment list itself. Schumer names Turek as one of his successes, and Turek’s Iowa race is indeed competitive. But Part XII examines Turek as a candidate positioned as opposition while carrying a long record of voting alongside Republicans. Schumer’s broader map is built partly on candidates chosen because they do not look much like an opposition. That may be exactly why they are winning in red states. It is also exactly the tension this series exists to watch.

None of this proves that the establishment’s candidates would have done better in Michigan and Maine. El-Sayed and Jackson may yet win. If they do, the series will have to concede that the base read those states better than Washington did, a possibility Parts X and XIII have already left open. But the October numbers sharpen the question Part IV asked. Schumer’s case is that he built the path to a majority. The weakest links in that path are the places where his party declined to follow him. Whether that is an indictment of the leader or of the led is a question the count on November 3, 2026 will answer.
