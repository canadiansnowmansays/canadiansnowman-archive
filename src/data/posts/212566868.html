Part XXIV closed on the observation that Greer had said there were no new talks scheduled, and that whatever the collapse was, it wasn’t over. Monday, August 24, established what “not over” means in practice. Trump announced that tariffs on all Canadian cars, trucks, auto parts and steel will rise to 50 percent on January 1, 2027. Doug Ford threatened to cut off electricity and critical minerals. Greer went on television and offered a theory of Canadian motives that has nothing to do with anything Canada has actually said. And a Republican senator in a border state broke publicly with her own administration.

This part is about what each of those tells you, and about the one number in Trump’s post that is simply wrong in a way that matters.

I. The Arithmetic of the Punishment

As Ana Swanson and Tony Romm reported for the New York Times, Trump’s Monday post threatened to raise tariffs on all Canadian cars, trucks, automotive parts and steel to 50 percent effective January 1, 2027, writing that Canada was “among the worst Nations in the World to deal with” and that it “will be treated like a State no longer.” He added: “They feel entitled, and yet, WE DON’T NEED CANADA, THEY NEED US!”

Set that against what was on the table five days earlier. Greer’s own account, given to the Times on Saturday and covered in Part XXIV, was that Washington had offered to cut the auto tariff from 25 percent to 15, possibly as low as 7 with the American-content credit applied, and steel from 50 to 25 under a quota. The announced trajectory is now 50 percent on both.

So the cost of Canada’s refusal is not the loss of an improvement. It is roughly double the status quo that existed before the negotiations began — a status quo that was itself the product of last year’s escalation. That is the actual structure of what happened this week: the offer was better than the current arrangement, the punishment for declining it is worse than the current arrangement, and the gap between those two numbers is the leverage.

The January date matters as much as the rate. Four months is not a deadline; it is a countdown designed to be interrupted. It gives Ottawa a period in which to reconsider, and gives Washington something to withdraw in exchange for movement. Whether that is strategy or reflex is a question this series has asked before without resolving.

What should not be treated as a surprise is the doubling itself. Part XXIII noted the precedent; Monday supplied the confirmation. In April 2025, when China answered American tariffs with tariffs of its own, Washington did not treat the response as a bargaining move to be met in kind. It escalated, and kept escalating, until American duties on Chinese goods reached 145 percent and Chinese duties on American goods reached 125 — roughly $600 billion in bilateral trade brought to a standstill, and something on the order of $5 trillion off American equity markets. The spiral broke only in Geneva that May, when both sides came down to 30 and 10 percent, not because either had reconsidered but because the standstill had become unaffordable for both.

Canada retaliated Saturday. The doubling arrived Monday. Same instrument, faster clock.

And this is where the comparison earns its keep, because the two countries on the receiving end of it could hardly be less alike. China is a strategic rival — a one-party state that arbitrarily detained two Canadian citizens for the better part of three years, that Washington has spent two administrations describing as the principal long-term threat to American power. Canada is a NORAD partner, a founding NATO member, the country whose soldiers died alongside Americans in Afghanistan, and the signatory to a free trade agreement this president negotiated himself.

Trade disputes between the United States and Canada are not new, and it would be over-claiming to pretend otherwise — softwood lumber has been litigated across administrations of both parties for four decades, and steel and dairy have produced their own rounds. What is new is not friction. It is the tempo, and it is the sentence “Canada will be treated like a State no longer,” which describes the relationship as a privilege being withdrawn rather than a treaty being disputed.

Neither the alliance nor the rivalry changed the shape of the response. What both countries did was decline to absorb a tariff without answering it, and in both cases the answer was escalation rather than negotiation. On the public record available as of Monday, the variable that predicted the reaction was not whether a country is an adversary or an ally. It was whether the country pushed back.

The differences in scale are real and cut against Canada, not for it. China’s economy is roughly ten times Canada’s, its exposure to the American market a fraction of Canada’s 72 percent, and its capacity to absorb a prolonged standstill correspondingly greater — which is precisely why Beijing could wait for Geneva and Ottawa may not be able to. The question that follows is therefore not whether Washington escalates. It does, reliably, and it does not stop on its own. It is what the equivalent of Geneva looks like for a country with a tenth of the room to wait.

II. The Number That Isn’t Right

Trump’s post makes a factual claim that carries the weight of the whole argument: “They do 95% of their business with the U.S., with us, the exact opposite!”

Canada sent roughly 72 percent of its merchandise exports to the United States last year, per the Associated Press. This series has used the figure 71.7 percent since Part XXII, and has treated it as the central fact of Canadian vulnerability — because it is. Nobody in this dispute has any interest in understating it. Twenty-three points is a lot of rounding up, even for a man who bought a golf course and called it a country club with a golf course.

But 95 is not 72, and the difference is not decorative. It is the entire basis for “WE DON’T NEED CANADA, THEY NEED US.” At 95 percent, Canada has no alternatives and no exit; at 72, it has a difficult but real diversification problem, which is precisely the problem Carney has spent his premiership working on. The overstatement is what converts a lopsided relationship into a total one, and a total one is the only kind in which the demand for submission makes sense.

There is a second problem with the deficit figure. Trump cites a $60 billion deficit as the grievance. The American goods deficit with Canada is substantially a function of energy imports — and the tariffs imposed Saturday pointedly exclude oil, gas, electricity and potash. Washington has exempted the largest single driver of the number it is citing as the injury. That is not an inconsistency this series is inventing; it is visible in the tariff schedule itself.

III. Ford Goes Back to the Weapon That Started This

Ontario’s premier gave an interview to the Associated Press on Monday that was considerably sharper than his weekend remarks.

On critical minerals: “I’ll cut them off. You won’t get a grain of sand out of Ontario.” On what he believes Washington is doing: “He wants to bleed out every single sector and bring them down to the U.S.” To CBC separately, Ford said Trump could “kiss my ass,” and that Canada needs to “throw everything in the kitchen sink at them.”

But the sharpest thing Ford did was reach for Reagan again. He told the AP that Reagan would be “throwing up” over Trump’s trade policies — noting that Trump keeps a Reagan portrait near his desk — and that Reagan would be “disgusted with President Trump.”

Readers of this series will recognise why that is not an offhand insult. Ford’s Reagan anti-tariff television ad last year is what enraged Trump and prompted him to halt trade talks with Canada in the first place. Ford is deliberately picking up the specific weapon that detonated the previous round, and doing it in an interview with an American wire service, which means it lands in American newspapers rather than Canadian ones. He knows exactly what it does.

Whether Carney follows him is the more interesting question, and the honest answer is that the two men are not currently in the same place. Energy remains, by a wide margin, Canada’s largest export to the United States. Carney has resisted weaponising it on the grounds that it would damage Canada’s reputation as a reliable supplier — a reputation that is, not incidentally, the entire premise of the diversification strategy he has spent his premiership building. A country advertising itself to Asian and European buyers as the dependable alternative to American volatility cannot easily demonstrate that by cutting off a customer.

On Saturday he came closer than before without committing: “I don’t think they want us to stop sending it.” That is a reminder of leverage, not a threat to use it. Ford is well past that line, and the gap between them is real. The premier of the province with the most to lose is pushing hardest for the measure that would hurt most; the prime minister holding the file has the most to lose from using it. That tension, rather than the threat itself, is what to watch over the next fortnight — because if Carney does move on energy, it will mean he has concluded the reliability argument is already lost.

IV. Greer’s Theory, Against the Itemised Record

The American trade representative gave an interview to CNBC on Monday that is worth reading closely, because it can be checked against a list.

His account of the timeline: “We progressed to a point Tuesday night where we had enough agreement among the parties to announce that we had found a way to a deal. Then we set about to finalize it. And then in the last hours, I think, there were things that the Canadians just, you know, they wanted more.”

Then the theory of why: “I don’t know if it was political for them. It certainly doesn’t make economic sense, but perhaps for political reasons, they have some by-elections coming up, I don’t know. But they came in and they wanted more. And we weren’t prepared to do that.”

The claim, reduced to its core, is that Canada arrived at the end of a settled negotiation and asked for additional concessions. Set that against what Carney said arrived in those same final hours, which he itemised publicly on Saturday.

By his account, the American side introduced late demands that Canada roll back its promotion of Canadian and French-language content in online streaming services; roll back subsidies for industries including publishing and film production; and abandon mandatory French-language labelling on packaging, in a country where French is an official language. Alongside those sat a requirement that Canada align its tariff policy toward third countries with American tariffs — the item Carney called “a power play” and “a question of sovereignty.”

Those are not concessions Canada was seeking. They are demands Canada was receiving. And they are not adjustments to the rates under discussion; not one of them concerns steel, aluminum, autos, or lumber. They arrived from outside the subject matter of the negotiation, in its closing hours, after both leaders had publicly said a deal was essentially done.

Carney also named what Washington would not move on, which cuts against “they wanted more” from the other direction: the American side refused to count Canadian as well as American parts in the auto content calculation, refused to reduce the rate on medium and heavy trucks, and offered little for the broad category of goods deemed primarily steel or aluminum — everything from antennas to snowmobiles — which remain at 25 percent. Greer himself confirmed on CNBC that Canadian officials had been pressing on heavy-duty vehicles. On his own telling, then, the thing Canada “wanted more” of was a rate reduction on trucks that the United States declined to give.

And set against all of that is what Canada had already agreed to hand over: pulling its retaliatory tariffs on American steel, aluminum and cars, and persuading eight provinces to restore American liquor to government-owned shelves after more than a year of bans.

So the record, as both sides have described it, runs roughly like this. Canada conceded the retaliatory tariffs and the liquor boycotts. Canada asked for relief on trucks and downstream metal products and did not get it. The United States then introduced, late, a set of demands about language policy, cultural subsidies, and Canada’s freedom to set its own tariffs with other countries. Canada left.

“They wanted more” is not a description of that sequence. It is a description of a different one.

This matters beyond the point-scoring, because it is the same move Part XXIV identified: a sovereignty objection cannot be answered with a better rate, so it gets reclassified as something else — first as Canadian greed, then as Canadian electioneering.

I should be plain about what this is and isn’t. Two governments have given incompatible public accounts, no text of the offer has been released by either, and I am reconstructing from what each side has chosen to say. Greer may know things he has not said. Carney’s itemised list is his own characterisation, not a document. What can be established from the public record as of Monday is narrower but still useful: Greer concedes twice in one paragraph that he does not know why the talks failed, supplies a motive anyway, and does not engage the specific demands Carney named. Whether the by-elections theory is a genuine belief or a placeholder is not something I can settle. That it occupies the space where a response to Carney’s actual complaint would otherwise sit is simply observable.

There is also a straightforward numerical problem with Greer’s public position. Asked about Canadian retaliation, he told reporters the duties cover about 5 percent of Canadian trade and “only 0.06% of U.S. overall consumption,” adding: “We don’t actually expect a huge impact,” though consequences “may be different” for Canada. That is the trade representative minimising the significance of the measures on the same day his president escalated them. Both statements cannot be doing the work they are meant to do.

V. The First Republican Crack

The most consequential development Monday may not have come from either capital.

Senator Susan Collins, Republican of Maine, issued a statement over the weekend saying she had heard from businesses, farmers and lobstermen concerned about the cost of navigating the tariffs, the absence of domestic supply chains, and the likelihood of Canadian retaliation. She urged the administration to return to talks, citing the effects on local families and businesses.

The Times notes two things about Collins that give the statement weight beyond its content. She is one of the most vulnerable incumbents heading into the November midterms. And she was not expected to attend a Monday event with Vice President Vance, who was in Maine speaking at a manufacturing facility as part of the effort to rally supporters before the election.

That is a specific picture: a vulnerable Republican in a border state, publicly breaking with the administration’s trade policy, while the vice president campaigns in her state without her. Congressional Democrats attacked the tariffs as expected. Collins is the part that isn’t expected.

This series has argued since Part XXII that Canadian domestic politics would determine how long Carney could hold his position. The mirror question has been comparatively neglected: how long American domestic politics permits this to continue. Tariffs are paid by American importers and passed to American consumers, inflation remains above target, and the administration already relaxed beef tariffs this month over price pressure. November is ten weeks away.

VI. What Hasn’t Happened Yet

Two absences will shape the next part. Canada still has not published its retaliation list — Carney named the sectors, but the schedule that determines who in the United States actually feels this does not yet exist publicly, and September 8 is two weeks out. And Ottawa now faces a transparency fight of its own: Conservative Canada-U.S. relations critic Shuvaloy Majumdar has written to Trade Minister Dominic LeBlanc asking for the American offer to be released, saying that while his party supports rejecting the deal, Parliament is in the dark about what was on the table. Lisa Raitt echoed it.

That is a notable position — supporting the refusal while demanding to see what was refused — and it is the right question. Everything in this part, and in Part XXIV before it, rests on two governments’ characterisations of a document neither has published. Every figure here is drawn from the public record as it stood on Monday, August 24: Truth Social posts, a CNBC interview, an AP interview, a televised address, and reporting by the New York Times and the Associated Press. If the text is released and it contradicts either account, this series will say so.

VII. One Page, Two Countries

One further observation, offered as an observation rather than a thesis.

Greer’s formulation from earlier this month — that if a country retaliates, “we’re obviously not going to tolerate that” — is not the language of trade negotiation. It is the language of sanctions enforcement. The distinction is not stylistic. Sanctions are imposed on parties who have no recognised standing to answer in kind; that is what makes them sanctions. Tariffs, under every framework the United States itself designed and signed, are answerable by counter-tariffs as a matter of ordinary right. To describe a treaty ally’s retaliation as something to be tolerated or not is to apply the wrong category’s grammar.

That vocabulary is not appearing in isolation. The same administration spent Monday preparing further measures to isolate Iran’s economy — a country it is actively at war with — while doubling tariffs on a NATO founding member. I want to rule out the cheap reading before anyone reaches for it: Canadians are not Iranians, Iran is being bombed, people there are dying, and Canada faces duties on plywood and hockey sticks at an effective rate still below Japan’s. Anyone treating those as comparable injuries deserves to be dismissed, and I am not making that argument.

The narrow point is about the instrument, not the injury. Economic isolation is a tool built for adversaries, and its vocabulary is migrating toward a neighbour, with diminishing evidence that anyone in Washington finds the distinction worth preserving. Carney supplied the phrase himself on Saturday: at war, meaning economically, over trade. He was not reaching for the Iran comparison and neither am I. But “treated like a State no longer” is a sentence about reclassification, and reclassification is what this section is about.

VIII. The Ledger

Twenty-five parts in, the pattern has changed shape but not direction.

Canada said no, and the price of having said no was announced within seventy-two hours: double the tariffs, on a four-month clock, justified by a figure overstated by twenty-three percentage points and a deficit driven largely by goods deliberately left off the tariff list. It is the same response China got in April 2025 for the same offence, which was not being an adversary but declining to absorb a tariff quietly. Ontario has escalated toward the one lever — energy and minerals — that would genuinely hurt, and reached again for the Reagan comparison that started the previous round. Washington has answered a documented set of eleventh-hour demands about language, culture and third-country tariff policy by asserting that Canada simply wanted more, and then guessing at by-elections. And for the first time, the political cost has begun showing up on the American side of the border rather than only the Canadian one.

None of that tells you how this ends. It does tell you that the thing Part XXIII called a break in the pattern is holding, at least for now, and that holding is expensive in both directions. Whether either government finds a reason to stop before September 8 is the only question that matters, and neither has given one yet.

If I could offer the President one piece of unsolicited advice, it would be this: he has a war on. A real one, with casualties, an oil market it has already unsettled, a Treasury Secretary spending his week designing further sanctions, and an ally in Seoul that just had a joint exercise cancelled because the resources were needed elsewhere. That war is not going especially well, it is not close to finished, and it is the sort of thing that has historically consumed the full attention of the men who started them.

Canada, by contrast, sells him plywood and hockey sticks and asks for very little. It has never threatened him, never shot at anyone he cares about, and spent eighty years assuming his country would answer the phone. Opening a second front against the neighbour who supplies your electricity, while the first one is still burning, is not strength. It is a failure to count. Whatever satisfaction there is in making Canada say uncle, it is worth rather less than the thing being neglected to get it — and Canadians, who have been paying attention to America for a very long time, can tell the difference between a country that is winning and a country that is busy.
