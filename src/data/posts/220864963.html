👤 Personal, with a serious count at its centre. A boy has the summer of his life in it, a president dies in it, and the boy remembers the afternoon wrongly, and the summer too. The essay is about all of it.

I count my essays, and every so often the count lands on a year that already belongs to someone. Most of the years in this series belong to the dead: kings, generals, the ten million of 1914. This one belongs, a little, to me. Essay 1 963 is the year of the question my generation has been asked more often than any other, by children and grandchildren and the occasional radio host, and that every one of us answers at once, without stopping to think, in full sentences, with the confidence of a man reading from a document.

Where were you when you heard?

Before I answer it, I want to enter something on the other side of the ledger, because in my memory 1963 did not begin in a classroom. It began on a propeller plane.

The summer before

In July of that year, at eleven, I went west for the whole summer with my grandmother and her sister, my great-aunt Stella. My grandmother was Maude Aileen Keenleyside, born a Mann, though nobody ever called her Maude; she went by her middle name, and shortened even that, so to everyone who loved her she was Lena. She was the widow of Gordon Keenleyside, my mother Ruth’s father, the man whose name I carry in the middle of mine and on the byline of this series. Gordon died in 1951, the year I was born. I knew him only as a name, and I knew her as the person who carried it.

The census of 1931 counted her, as censuses do: “Aileen,” forty-seven, home maker, 848 Colborne Street, London, wife of a confectionery salesman, mother of Fulton, sixteen, and Ruth, fourteen. Ruth was my mother. I found the entry this week, and for one evening it corrected me. I had always thought of Lena as past eighty that summer; by the census, she was not quite. Then I found her gravestone, which gives her birth as 1881, two years before the census allowed. She had been past eighty after all. The census was the one that rounded. The boy had it right.

Past eighty, then. Her sister Stella was three years older; she had married late, at forty, and by the time I knew her she was Stella Mann again.. Two ladies past eighty and an eleven-year-old boy, London to Toronto on a prop plane that I suspect was no treat for any of us, and then Toronto to Vancouver on a DC-8 of Trans-Canada Air Lines, which would not be called Air Canada for a few years yet. Whether we stopped on the way, I can’t tell you. Two months. July and August. I am sure I was the heaviest piece of luggage either of them had ever taken across a country, and neither of them once let me feel it.

I can close my eyes and still see it. My cousins in North Vancouver, and the games we played. My other cousins in Tacoma, Washington, the city I would be standing in, years later, when Mount St. Helens blew its side out. The World’s Fair in Seattle, and the Space Needle, brand new and impossibly tall. Mount Rainier. The ocean, and digging for clams. Climbing Grouse Mountain. The ferry across to Victoria. My aunt Jean, and my uncle Jim, my mother’s brother, who died at around fifty, not many years after. The census calls him Fulton; nobody else ever did. In my head he is still on that beach, far younger than I am now. And Lena and Stella, whom I adored, presiding over the whole of it.

It is as fresh to me as if it happened this year. It is also, I thought, the one memory in this essay that nobody could check. No wire service timed it; no commission reconstructed it; there is no film to count frame by frame. I am as sure of that summer as I was of the hour you are about to read. Hold on to that. I’ll come back to it.

Three months later I was back in grade seven, and the question found me. So I will answer it, the way I have always answered it, and then I will do what this series does to every confident count: I will check it.

The count I kept

I was in grade seven, in London, Ontario. We had come back in from recess. It was just after one o’clock in the afternoon. Our teacher, Mr. Blackwell, the first man I ever had for a teacher, stood at the front of the class and told us that President Kennedy had been shot in Dallas, and then he told us to go home. The class was over. I think the whole school was sent home, though I can’t swear to that part. I remember walking out early into a November afternoon. And I remember that when I got home we sat glued to the television, a small black-and-white set, watching the news go round and round. It certainly wasn’t Howdy Doody time; I was twelve, and long past the Lone Ranger. Of the rest of the day, I remember almost nothing.

That is the whole memory. It has been in my head for sixty-three years. I have told it, in some form, perhaps a hundred times. And I want you to notice something about it before we go on, because I didn’t notice it until this week: I could not have told you the date. I had to look it up. November 22. But I could have told you the hour, and I would have sworn to it.

Remember where I was. Not Dallas, not Washington, not New York. A Canadian classroom, in a mid-sized city in southwestern Ontario, in a country with its own prime minister and its own troubles. And still a teacher stopped the lesson and sent the children home, because the news had come through the wires and the radios and the black-and-white sets in the staff room, and it had arrived everywhere at once. Kennedy was not only America’s. He was the first president my generation had really looked at, young and quick and televised, and the grief, that afternoon, did not stop at the border. It was perhaps the first time the whole world heard the same bad news in the same hour.

Which brings me to the hour.

The clock in Dallas

The motorcade turned into Dealey Plaza at about 12:30 in the afternoon, Dallas time. Dallas is an hour behind London, Ontario. The shots were fired at roughly 1:30 our time. The first wire bulletin, from United Press International, moved a few minutes later. The president was pronounced dead at one o’clock Dallas time, two o’clock ours, and the news of his death reached most of the continent over the following half hour; Walter Cronkite, on CBS, took off his glasses and read it on air at about 2:38 in the afternoon, Eastern.

So Mr. Blackwell did not tell us “just after one o’clock.” At just after one o’clock in London, Ontario, the President of the United States was alive, in an open car, waving to a crowd in Texas. Whatever recess we had come in from, it was not the one I remember. The likeliest truth is that it was the afternoon recess, somewhere between two and three, and that the news our teacher gave us was either “shot” or, by then, “dead,” and I no longer know which.

I have carried an hour that did not happen for sixty-three years. I would have sworn to it in court. And I am, as readers of this series will know, a man who checks other people’s counts for a living. I spent eighteen years auditing the gap between what a company told its customers and what was true. I have spent eighteen hundred essays catching chroniclers rounding. I never once thought to audit the clock in my own head.

How many shots

The official count of that afternoon has fared no better than mine, and it has had considerably more help.

The Warren Commission reported in 1964 that there were three shots, all fired by one man from a sixth-floor window of the Texas School Book Depository, with a bolt-action rifle that needed, by the FBI’s tests, at least two and a third seconds between rounds. One shot missed. One passed through the president’s neck and went on to wound Governor Connally, which is the famous “single bullet,” derided ever since as the magic one. One struck the president’s head.

In 1979, the House Select Committee on Assassinations looked again and concluded that there had been four shots, not three, and that the president was “probably” killed as the result of a conspiracy. The fourth shot rested almost entirely on a single piece of evidence: a police radio recording, a Dictaphone belt, on which acoustic experts believed they could hear the gunfire. In 1982 a panel of the National Academy of Sciences examined the same recording and concluded that the sounds had been recorded about a minute after the shooting. The fourth shot was withdrawn by science, but never by the public. The count, in the popular mind, has never settled, and a generation of file releases, the last of the government’s withheld records coming out as recently as 2025, has not settled it either, because the people who need it unsettled are not waiting on paperwork.

And here is the strangest entry in the whole ledger. It is the most thoroughly recorded murder of its century. A dressmaker named Abraham Zapruder, standing on a concrete pedestal with a home-movie camera, filmed the whole of it, and his film runs to some twenty-six seconds. Investigators have counted it frame by frame, because frames were the only clock they had. The fatal shot is known to the world by its frame number: 313. No other death in this series has been measured in frames of film, and no other has been argued over so precisely, and to so little agreement. The more exactly a thing is recorded, it turns out, the more exactly people can disagree about it.

Two days later, on Sunday, November 24, the man arrested for the killing was himself shot dead in the basement of the Dallas police headquarters, in front of the cameras that had come to film his transfer to the county jail. It was the first murder ever broadcast live, a little after noon our time on a Sunday, and this is my second memory of that weekend: we were watching. The same little black-and-white set, a crowded basement corridor in Dallas, a man in a hat stepping out of the crowd, and then the noise and the scramble. I could not have told you which day it was until I looked it up. I can tell you that I saw it. The witness who might have answered the question of how many was gone before the question could be put to him, and a boy in London, Ontario, watched him go.

The count we keep in our heads

In 1977, two psychologists, Roger Brown and James Kulik, gave a name to the kind of memory I have been carrying. They called it a “flashbulb memory”: the vivid, detailed, unshakeable recollection of where you were and what you were doing when you heard shocking news. And the example they built their paper around was this afternoon. Kennedy. Almost everyone they asked could tell them exactly where they had been.

What nobody could tell them was whether it was true, because nobody had written it down at the time.

That test came later. On the morning after the Challenger shuttle exploded in January 1986, the psychologist Ulric Neisser had a class of students write down how they had heard the news. Two and a half years later, he asked them again. Many of the later accounts were simply different: a different room, a different person who told them, a different hour. About a quarter of them gave a completely different account. And the part that has stayed with me is this: when some of them were shown their own handwritten accounts from the day after, they did not simply say “I must have forgotten.” Some held to the memory over the page. It felt truer to them than their own handwriting.

That is what a flashbulb memory is. It is not a photograph. It is a story we tell ourselves so often that the telling replaces the event, and it gets brighter every time we tell it, not more accurate. The vividness is real. The confidence is real. Neither one is evidence.

I find this a humbling entry to make in a series built on catching other people’s numbers. The chroniclers who called a hundred and sixteen years of war the Hundred Years’ War, the empire that counted its own dead by name and its victims by range, the fleet that booked its victory before it sailed: I have been hard on all of them. And the whole time, in my own head, there was a twelve-year-old’s hour, kept faithfully, told with conviction, and wrong.

What the count is for

So here is how I would correct the record, if the record were a page in this series.

I was in grade seven, in London, Ontario. Sometime on the afternoon of Friday, November 22, 1963, most likely after two o’clock, Mr. Blackwell told us the president had been shot, and we were sent home. That is all I can certify. The hour I remember is an hour before it happened.

And yet I notice that the correction changes nothing that mattered. The fact that has survived sixty-three years in my head intact is not the time. It is that a teacher in a Canadian classroom, with no obligation to anyone in Dallas, stopped what he was doing and sent the children home, because something had happened that was too large to teach through. The clock was wrong. The grief was right. Sixty-three years on, I have his name exactly and the hour wrong by sixty minutes, which tells you what memory thinks is worth keeping. I suspect that is true of most of what any of us remember: the numbers drift, and the reason we kept them does not.

Which brings me back to the summer, the memory I told you nobody could check. I was wrong about that too. A summer can be checked, it turns out, if you go looking for the people in it.

I had already found Lena in the census of 1931. So I went looking for Stella. I found her in the census of 1901, a lodger of twenty-two in London.I found what I believe is her signature on Lena’s marriage licence in 1913, a witness at her little sister’s wedding in Lambeth, forty-nine years before the two of them flew west with me. I cannot prove the hand is hers. It is my best guess, and this autumn has taught me to call a guess a guess. I found her in a marriage register of 1919, giving her own age, honestly, as forty. And then I found her in Woodland Cemetery, in London, Section K, Row 22, under a family stone she shares with a sister who died at thirteen, a great-aunt I never knew I had. Stella died in 1962.

Her grave leaves me one question I cannot answer. It names her the wife of Arthur Mann. The man she married in 1919 was a Zurhorst, and he died in 1931. Mann was the name she was born with. Did she marry again, late, a man who happened to share it? Was Arthur a cousin, or someone the family simply never spoke of to a boy of ten? I do not know. I have entered him in the ledger as a question, which is the honest column for him.

So the summer was not 1963. It was 1962, and I was ten, not eleven. The proof had been in my own memory all along. We went to the World’s Fair in Seattle that summer; I remember the Space Needle. The Century 21 Exposition opened in April 1962 and closed that October, and it never opened again. A fair named for the twenty-first century, which I have now lived a quarter of, had the date written on it the whole time, and I never once read it.

The plane, the cousins, the clams, the ferry to Victoria: all of it happened a year earlier than I have told it for six decades. And it was Stella’s last summer. She would have turned eighty-four that August, with a boy in tow, and she was gone before the year was out. I had the hour wrong by sixty minutes and the summer wrong by a whole year, and in all that time I never knew I had been given her last one.

But I notice what did not drift. The year moved; the people did not. The hour was a single moment, told a hundred times until the telling wore a groove of its own. The summer was sixty days, and what it left behind is not a time stamp but people: two sisters on a propeller plane with a boy in tow, cousins on two sides of a border, Uncle Jim on a beach, who did not have many summers left. Neither, as it turns out, did Stella. It is the same lesson the hour taught, from the happy side of the ledger: the clock drifts, and the people stay.

This series will end, at Christmas, on exactly that idea, and I will not say more about it here. I will only note that it began to dawn on me at 1963, in a classroom I can still see, at an hour I had wrong.

Dior, who keeps no such records

The husky has no flashbulb memories, or rather she has only one, and it concerns the precise location of every piece of chicken that has ever fallen from the kitchen counter. These she recalls with total confidence and, I am obliged to report, total accuracy. She has never once remembered a dropped drumstick in the wrong room. I have come to suspect that this is because she does not tell herself the story. She simply goes back and checks the floor.

The closer

The count moves on, six years, and it moves to a promise Kennedy made and did not live to see kept. In May 1961, he stood before Congress and committed his country to landing a man on the Moon and bringing him home safely “before this decade is out.” It was a deadline with a number on it, set by a man with less than three years left to live. The next landmark is essay 1969, and it is the year the people he left behind met that deadline, with five months to spare, on a count that ran backwards to zero and was kept, for once, to the second.

A summer of cousins and clams a year earlier than I’d have sworn, a wrong hour, a contested count, twenty-six seconds of film, a small black-and-white set on a Sunday, and Mr. Blackwell sending us home. I can’t tell you when I heard. I can tell you that I did.

See you at 1969, where a dead man’s deadline is met on time.
