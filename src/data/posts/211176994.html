On August 13, 2026, the New York Times published a piece that, had I not been writing this series since June 4, 2025, I might have mistaken for a summary of it. It was not flattering, exactly — nobody sends a Canadian retiree a byline credit — but it was something better than flattery. It was corroboration, arriving fourteen months late, from an institution with considerably more fact-checkers than I have.

The thesis of that piece, stripped of its restraint, is the thesis of this series: that the machinery running in Washington in 2026 is not new. It is 1924 with better software.

As Hamed Aleaziz reported for the New York Times, the Immigration Act of 1924 set national quotas that gutted immigration from Italy, Poland, and Russia, functionally barred immigration from Asia, and capped total annual arrivals at 150,000. It was, by design, a rejection of the country Emma Lazarus had described on a plaque in New York Harbor thirty-eight years earlier. Representative Albert Johnson of Washington said the quiet part aloud at the time: America had decided to “cease to function as an asylum.” Congress did that. Openly, on the record, with hearings and votes and a bill signed by Calvin Coolidge on the White House lawn.

That is the detail worth sitting with before anything else. In 1924, the country that wanted to stop being an asylum had to say so, in public, through its elected legislature. The shame, if there was any, was distributed and accountable. Somebody’s name was on the vote.

What 1965 Undid, and What 2026 Is Undoing Without a Vote

I have spent nineteen parts of this series tracing the pendulum: the 1924 quotas, the internment of Japanese Americans, the McCarran-Walter Act’s ideological exclusions, the slow dismantling of all of it by the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965, signed by Lyndon Johnson at the foot of the Statue of Liberty on October 3 of that year. The 1965 act did not just raise numbers. It replaced the premise. It said, for the first time in four decades of federal law, that a person’s country of origin was not a proxy for their worth.

Julia Gelatt of the Migration Policy Institute, quoted in the August 13 piece, put the throughline better than I have managed in nineteen installments: both eras, she noted, restrict immigration based on “who is viewed as able to integrate into a desired national culture.” The only thing that changed between 1924 and 2026 is the paperwork. In 1924, the exclusion arrived as a statute with 150,000 written into it. In 2026, it arrives as a proclamation, a travel restriction on 75 countries issued in January, a suspension of asylum processing at the southern border, a selective refugee program that has, in practice, admitted almost no one except a group of Afrikaners whose qualifying trait Secretary of State Marco Rubio described, without apparent irony, as their capacity to assimilate.

Doug Rand, a former senior official at U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services, gave Aleaziz the line I wish I had written first: “Today, Trump is trying to snap them back by fiat.” Not by law. By fiat. That is the entire distinction between 1924 and 2026, and it is not a small one — it is the difference between a democracy making an ugly choice out loud and an executive making the same choice quietly enough that no one has to answer for it at the ballot box.

The TACO Pattern, Applied to a Century-Old Playbook

Readers of this series know the framework by now: Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate. It was built to track how institutions respond to pressure campaigns in real time, and it applies with uncomfortable precision to the rhetorical architecture Stephen Miller has built around immigration policy.

The threat is explicit. Miller has described the post-1965 immigration era, on Fox News’s The Will Cain Show, as an experiment that allowed people to “empty out their entire towns and their entire villages to the United States of America.” The accommodation comes from an administration eager to translate that framing into policy without the inconvenience of legislative debate. The capitulation is institutional — a Supreme Court that upheld birthright citizenship in June 2026 while allowing administration officials to spend the ensuing weeks denouncing the cultures of the citizens it had just protected, and a Congress that has ceded so much of its Article I immigration authority that Rand’s “by fiat” reads less like accusation and more like job description. And the obfuscation is the part I keep returning to in this series: the claim, repeated by Miller on Jesse Watters Primetime, that immigrants from what he called “third world nations” come from places that “would have never invented the wheel, let alone modern technology” — language calibrated to sound like policy analysis while doing the work of the 1923 eugenics pamphlets I quoted in Week 2 of this series, back when I still thought I might run out of historical parallels before I ran out of weeks.

I did not run out.

The Numbers, Which Do Not Care About Framing

Here is what the 1924 act actually did, and it is worth stating plainly because the current administration is fond of insisting its restrictionism is a security measure rather than a demographic one. Italian arrivals fell from roughly 42,000 in 1921 to about 6,000 in 1929. The foreign-born share of the U.S. population fell from nearly 12 percent in 1930 to 4.7 percent by 1970 — a forty-year contraction that Miller has publicly praised as the “cauldron” in which American national identity was forged, as though a cauldron with the heat turned off is still doing any cooking.

Now the present: Census figures show immigration fell by more than 50 percent in the year ending in 2025 compared with the year before. Combined with a birthrate that has been sliding for a decade, that produced one of the slowest population growth rates in American history in the twelve months leading up to July 2025. This is not a controlled experiment finishing early. It is the same experiment, rerun, with the same expected result, and the administration appears to regard the result as the point rather than the cost.

I have written, in earlier parts of this series, about Canada’s structural advantage in exactly this moment — a country that spent the last decade building immigration infrastructure precisely so it could absorb the talent an anxious America was preparing to turn away. I will not repeat the argument in full here except to note that it requires no revision fourteen months later. Every data point in the August 13 piece is an argument for it. A country that voluntarily shrinks its foreign-born population by design is a country handing its competitors a labour force, a customer base, and a research bench, gift-wrapped, no card attached.

Who Gets to Be “Unassimilable” This Time

Part of what made the 1924 act durable for four decades was its precision. It did not simply say fewer immigrants; it said which immigrants, ranked by a formula tied to the 1890 census — a year chosen specifically because it predated the large Southern and Eastern European arrivals the law’s authors wanted to discourage. The ranking was the policy. Assimilability was never a neutral test; it was a conclusion dressed as a diagnostic, calibrated in advance to exclude whoever the drafters had already decided to exclude.

That same rigged diagnostic runs through the current list of the excluded, and the list is longer than immigration alone, which is the entire premise of this series. It includes the seventy-five countries named in the January 2026 proclamation, disproportionately Muslim-majority or African nations, in a restriction that functions like a 21st-century national-origins quota without ever using that phrase. It includes the asylum seekers turned away at the southern border under a suspension that treats the act of fleeing violence as itself disqualifying. It includes transgender service members purged from the officer corps, a story this series covered in “The Stars They Couldn’t Earn” in March, on the theory that they, too, fail some unstated test of who belongs in the institution. And it includes, per Rubio’s own framing, a category of one: a group of Afrikaner refugees admitted not because they were fleeing more urgent danger than anyone else in the global asylum queue, but because an administration official could describe them, without needing to elaborate, as people who would fit in.

The 1924 report I quoted in Week 2 of this series argued that Southern and Eastern Europeans were unfit “by tradition, political background, customs, social organization, education and habits of thought to adjust itself to American institutions.” Substitute “country of origin” for “tradition” and “gender identity” for “customs” and the sentence requires no other editing to function as 2026 policy rationale. The vocabulary evolved. The test — does this group look, to the people writing the rule, like it already belongs — did not.

The Doctrine That Still Cannot See

This postscript follows directly on the heels of July’s installment, “The Doctrine That Cannot See,” which examined the Supreme Court’s June 2026 birthright citizenship ruling and the administration’s response to it. I noted then that the Court’s decision to uphold birthright citizenship was treated by the administration not as a boundary but as a talking point — an opportunity for officials to spend the following weeks publicly disparaging the cultures of the very citizens the ruling had just protected. The August 13 Times piece confirms that this was not a one-off rhetorical lapse. It reports that the administration has, in the weeks since, moved to revive parts of the birthright ban the Court had just rejected, which is not the behavior of an administration that lost an argument. It is the behavior of an administration that regards a Supreme Court ruling as a temporary inconvenience to be re-litigated by other means, the same way it has treated Congress’s exclusive constitutional authority over immigration as a suggestion rather than a limit.

That is the throughline connecting every part of this series since June 2025: institutions built to require consensus — Congress for immigration law, the Supreme Court for constitutional limits — are being routed around, one executive action at a time, in favor of a policy program that could never survive an open vote. The 1924 act survived because its authors were willing to put their names on it and defend it in the next election. The 2026 program survives because, so far, no one has had to.

A Note on Being Right Early

There is a particular, mildly uncomfortable feeling that comes from watching a major newspaper independently arrive, fourteen months later, at an argument you have already spent a year making in installments to a Substack audience of, by industry standards, modest size. It is not vindication, exactly — vindication implies someone was keeping score, and nobody at the Times has read this series, nor should they be expected to. It is closer to the feeling of having set a table for a dinner party and then discovering, well into the meal, that the caterer down the street independently prepared an identical menu. The dishes match. The reasoning behind them, drawn from the same historical record, converges without collusion, because the record only supports one honest reading.

That is, in the end, the argument of this whole series, restated one more time because it apparently bears restating: this is not a novel crisis requiring novel vocabulary. It is the oldest American argument, run through 2026 hardware. The country has had this fight before, resolved it in 1965, and is now relitigating the resolution through executive order rather than through the Congress that would, at minimum, have to put its name on the outcome. Historians did not need the Times to tell them that. The Times, to its credit, did not need me. Both of us were reading the same century.
