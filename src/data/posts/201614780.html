Canadarm2 aboard the International Space Station, capturing a Northrop Grumman Cygnus resupply spacecraft. In April 2026 it turned 25 years old — still operating in the vacuum, radiation, and 300°C temperature swings it was built to endure forever.

Postmark: Low Earth orbit. Altitude: 408 kilometres. Speed: 28,000 km/h. Still operating.

In April 2026, while Canadian astronaut Jeremy Hansen and his Artemis II crewmates were recovering from their journey around the Moon — the first humans to travel that far from Earth since 1972 — a celebrated Canadian space veteran was quietly getting on with the job aboard the International Space Station.

It captured a Northrop Grumman Cygnus resupply ship, pulling it from twelve metres out and guiding it to dock. It had done this kind of thing before. Many times.

Canadarm2 turned twenty-five years old in April 2026. It has never come home. It was never designed to. It was built in Brampton, Ontario by engineers who understood that the machine they were making would spend its entire operational life in vacuum, radiation, and temperature swings of more than three hundred degrees Celsius every ninety minutes as it crosses in and out of Earth’s shadow. They built it to last. An MDA engineer who has worked on the system put the achievement in terms that need no embellishment: How many cars from 2001 are still rolling today?

The arm is still rolling. It will be rolling when most of the people reading this have retired.

The Idea That Started with a Fuel Loader

The story begins not in space but in a CANDU nuclear reactor, which is not where you would expect the history of space robotics to originate.

In the late 1960s, a Canadian company called DSMA Atcon developed a robot to load fuel rods into CANDU reactors — a repetitive, precise, dangerous-if-done-wrong task that was ideally suited to automation. NASA noticed. The Americans were deep in development of the Space Shuttle program and had been looking, since 1969, for international partners willing to contribute specific technologies in exchange for access to the program. Canada’s fuel-loading robot demonstrated something important: Canadian engineers could build robotic manipulators that worked reliably in hostile environments.

In 1975, NASA and Canada’s National Research Council signed a memorandum of understanding. Canada would design and build a robotic arm for the Space Shuttle. The NRC awarded the contract to Spar Aerospace, a Toronto-based company that had grown out of de Havilland Aircraft’s advanced research division. The budget was approximately $110 million Canadian. The deadline was the Space Shuttle’s first operational missions. The margin for error was effectively zero.

At the acceptance ceremony in February 1981, NRC president Larkin Kerwin gave the Shuttle Remote Manipulator System a name. He called it Canadarm. The maple leaf was painted on. It was shipped to Kennedy Space Center.

On November 13, 1981 — the second flight of Space Shuttle Columbia, mission STS-2 — Canadarm was successfully deployed in space for the first time by astronauts Joe Engle and Richard Truly. A footnote worth preserving: the November mission had been shortened due to a fuel cell malfunction. The Canadarm tests were rescheduled. Engle and Truly pulled an all-nighter in space and accomplished ninety to ninety-five percent of their objectives anyway. They said it was worth it.

Canada had arrived in space. Not as a passenger. As a builder.

Canadarm deploys for the first time on Space Shuttle Columbia, STS-2, November 1981. From a Toronto clean room to low Earth orbit — Canada arrived in space as a builder.

Thirty Years. Ninety Missions. One Telescope.

The original Canadarm flew on ninety Space Shuttle missions between 1981 and 2011. It deployed and retrieved satellites. It positioned spacewalking astronauts at workstations they could not have reached otherwise. It helped assemble the International Space Station piece by piece, module by module, across dozens of missions spanning more than a decade. It was 15.2 metres long, weighed 410 kilograms, and could move payloads of up to 266,000 kilograms in the weightlessness of space — the equivalent of lifting a fully loaded Boeing 747 with a machine that weighs less than a grand piano.

But the mission that defined Canadarm’s legacy — the one that demonstrated, irrevocably, what it could do — was the rescue of the Hubble Space Telescope in December 1993.

Hubble had launched in April 1990 with a flaw in its primary mirror so small it was measured in micrometres and so consequential it rendered the most expensive scientific instrument ever built effectively useless. The images were blurry. The physics was embarrassing. NASA was humiliated. Congress was furious. The jokes wrote themselves.

Three and a half years later, Space Shuttle Endeavour arrived at Hubble carrying the tools, the optics, and the astronauts to fix it. Over the course of five spacewalks across eleven days — the most complex shuttle mission ever flown to that point — the crew replaced instruments, installed corrective optics, and restored Hubble’s vision. Canadarm held the telescope. Canadarm positioned the astronauts. When Kathryn Thornton needed to release a damaged solar array — one that had failed to retract properly — she mounted the Canadarm and jettisoned it manually into the void, watching it tumble away into space.

The before-and-after images of galaxy M100 told the story better than any press release. Before the repair: a blur. After: the universe, in extraordinary clarity, as it had never been seen from Earth. The Canadarm had made that possible.

Canadarm on Space Shuttle Endeavour during the 1993 Hubble Space Telescope servicing mission. It held the telescope steady while astronauts performed the most complex repair in orbital history, restoring the universe to clarity.

The Arm That Would Not Die

Canadarm2 arrived at the ISS in April 2001, launched aboard Space Shuttle Endeavour. It is seventeen metres long, has seven degrees of freedom, and can rotate each joint 540 degrees — a greater range of motion than a human arm. Unlike its predecessor, which was stored in the shuttle’s payload bay between missions and eventually retired with the shuttle program, Canadarm2 is permanently attached to the station. It has never returned to Earth. It never will.

It operates as part of a three-component Canadian system. The arm itself handles the heavy work — the cosmic catches, the payload moves, the astronaut positioning. The Mobile Base System allows Canadarm2 to travel along the station’s main truss, making it a station-wide asset rather than a fixed manipulator. And then there is Dextre — the Special Purpose Dexterous Manipulator, launched in 2008 — a two-armed robot that can sit at one end of Canadarm2 and perform the kind of fine manipulation work that would otherwise require a spacewalk.

Dextre is the unsung member of the Canadian family. It has seven degrees of freedom in each arm, force and torque sensors that give it a sense of touch, and camera systems that allow ground controllers to see what it is doing in high resolution. Maintenance tasks that once required astronauts in spacesuits, with all the preparation and risk that entails — the pre-breathing of pure oxygen, the hours of suit-up, the hours of decompression, the fatigue, the ever-present danger — can now be performed robotically while the crew remains safely inside. Since Dextre’s arrival, dozens of operations that would have required full spacewalks have been completed by the robot alone. Canada’s contribution to the ISS was not a single machine. It was a system — and the system has been operating for more than two decades without a mission failure.

It has, however, been repaired in orbit. In 2017 and 2018, astronauts gave Canadarm2 new latching end effectors — its hands, effectively — during spacewalks. In 2021, a piece of space debris punched a hole through one of its boom segments — a strike that would have been catastrophic if slightly differently aimed. The arm kept working. In 2025, astronauts Sunita Williams and Butch Wilmore retrieved a pressurized communications unit that had been operating for far longer than its design life, returning it to MDA Space in Brampton for examination.

Dextre, the Special Purpose Dexterous Manipulator, working alongside Canadarm2 on the ISS. Together with the Mobile Base System they form Canada’s three-part robotic ecosystem that has eliminated dozens of risky spacewalks.

The engineering culture that produced Canadarm2 has a phrase for this kind of longevity: they build things to be refurbished rather than replaced, because replacement in space is either impossible or prohibitively expensive. Every design decision carries the weight of that constraint. You cannot send someone up to fix it easily. You build it right the first time, and you build in the ability to service what you cannot replace.

MDA, the Brampton-based successor to Spar Aerospace, reports a 100 percent mission success rate across more than 24,000 hours of direct robotic operations. In an industry where failure is measured in human lives and billion-dollar assets, that number is not a marketing figure. It is a statement of engineering culture.

When Space Robotics Came Back to Earth

The Canadarm story does not end in orbit. It comes back down.

Dr. Garnette Sutherland, a neurosurgeon at the University of Calgary, spent years working on a problem that surgeons encounter regularly and rarely discuss publicly: the limits of the human hand. Even the steadiest surgical hand trembles, fatigues, and loses precision over the course of a long procedure. For brain surgery — where the margins between tumour and healthy tissue can be measured in millimetres — those limits matter enormously.

Sutherland approached MDA, the builders of Canadarm and Canadarm2, with a proposition. The same engineering that made a robotic arm reliable enough to operate in space — the precision, the fault tolerance, the ability to work in environments where a single failure is catastrophic — could be applied to brain surgery inside an MRI machine. Working together, Sutherland’s team and MDA engineers developed neuroArm: the world’s first robot capable of performing surgery inside a magnetic resonance imaging scanner.

The first image-guided MR-compatible brain surgery using neuroArm was performed at the University of Calgary on May 12, 2008. The patient was a young woman named Paige Nickason, who had a tumour in her brain. The surgery was successful. The technology that had assembled the International Space Station had removed a brain tumour. A robotic arm engineered for the vacuum of space had found its way into a Canadian operating theatre.

This is what a genuine technology lineage looks like. Not a single invention but a branching tree of applications, each one building on what came before, each one solving a problem that the previous iteration was not designed to address. The fuel loader begat the arm. The arm begat the station builder. The station builder begat the brain surgeon.

NeuroArm, the world’s first MRI-compatible robotic neurosurgery system, developed by MDA and the University of Calgary. The same engineering lineage that built Canadarm now performs brain surgery inside a magnetic resonance scanner.

The Canadian Quiet

Here is a thing about the Canadarm story that Canadians tend not to notice because they are, characteristically, not paying enough attention to their own achievements.

The arm that deployed the Hubble Space Telescope. The arm that repaired it when it was broken. The arm that assembled the International Space Station, module by module, over more than a decade. The arm that has been catching cargo ships in low Earth orbit, reliably and without incident, for twenty-five years. The arm whose engineering principles found their way into the first robot to perform brain surgery inside an MRI machine.

That arm was made in Canada. In Toronto and Brampton, by engineers who graduated from Canadian universities, working for Canadian companies, under Canadian government contracts. The most consequential robotic system in the history of human spaceflight is a Canadian product, and most Canadians could not tell you who built it, where it was built, or what it has done.

We put the maple leaf on the arm — quietly, matter-of-factly, as if of course you would put the maple leaf on the arm — and then went back to work. The Americans got the headlines. We got the arm in space.

The Canadarm’s deployment in space was designated a National Historic Event in 2025. Forty-four years after the fact. That is a very Canadian timeline.

To the Moon

Canadarm3 is under construction in Brampton. MDA Space received a one-billion-dollar Canadian contract from the Canadian Space Agency in June 2024 for its detailed design, construction, and testing. It will be the most advanced space robotic system ever built. It will operate autonomously — without human oversight, because the communications delay between Earth and lunar orbit makes real-time human control impossible — using AI-enabled sensors and software to conduct operations around the Moon’s Gateway outpost on its own judgment.

Canadarm3 will think for itself, in the essential sense that it will have to. Gateway’s distance from Earth means that signals travel at the speed of light and still take over a second to arrive. You cannot drive a robot by remote control when the joystick commands arrive a second after you move it. You have to trust the machine to make decisions.

This is a profound shift from everything that came before. Canadarm and Canadarm2 were controlled by humans — astronauts at a console inside the spacecraft, or flight controllers on the ground in Longueuil, Quebec, watching camera feeds and issuing commands in real time. The human was always in the loop. Canadarm3 will be the first Canadian robotic system designed to act autonomously — to assess its environment, interpret its task, and execute without waiting for a human to approve each move. The AI-enabled sensors and software that will govern it represent a different kind of trust than any Canadian robotic system has previously required. Every design lesson from fifty years of Canadian space robotics — the materials science, the fault-tolerance engineering, the operational culture developed by the men and women in Longueuil who have guided Canadian robots through more than 24,000 hours of space operations without a single mission failure — is embedded in Canadarm3’s design. The machine that thinks for itself on the Moon will be built by the same people who built the machine that caught cargo ships in orbit. The lineage matters.

Canadarm3 under construction in Brampton for the Lunar Gateway. Designed for autonomous operation in lunar orbit, it represents the next chapter in a fifty-year Canadian robotic lineage — from CANDU fuel loaders to a machine that will think for itself on the Moon.

Canada exchanged Canadarm3 for two seats on Artemis missions, including the seat that carried Jeremy Hansen around the Moon in 2026. A country of forty million people — ten percent of the population of its neighbour — traded its engineering expertise for access to the most ambitious human spaceflight program since Apollo. The Americans got the arm. Canada got the Moon.

The Lineage

Stand back and look at the full arc: from a fuel-loading robot in a CANDU reactor in the late 1960s, to ninety Space Shuttle missions spanning thirty years, to twenty-five years of continuous ISS operations, to a brain surgery robot in a Calgary operating theatre, to a machine now being built to think for itself in lunar orbit.

That is a lineage. A deliberate, continuous, evolving body of engineering knowledge and institutional capability that Canada has built and maintained across more than half a century. The National Research Council handed it to Spar Aerospace. Spar became MDA. MDA built Canadarm2 and Dextre. MDA’s technology found its way into neuroArm. MDA is now building Canadarm3.

The maple leaf goes on the arm. Then you go back to work.
