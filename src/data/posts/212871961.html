There is a story Kevin Warsh likes to tell about the man he considers his hero. Three years before he became chairman of the Federal Reserve, Warsh recounted a conversation he once had with Paul Volcker, the central banker who broke the back of 1970s inflation at the cost of a brutal recession. Volcker told him the job came down to two things. Get interest rates about right. And make sure you look like you know what you’re doing.

On Friday, at ten in the morning Eastern time, Warsh will stand before the world’s most powerful economic policymakers at the Kansas City Fed’s annual conference in Jackson Hole, Wyoming, and attempt both. It will be his first address there as chairman, and as the New York Times has reported, it arrives with government bond markets on edge, inflation stubbornly above target, and a growing chorus of economists warning that his months of studied silence have curdled into something closer to confusion. “The honeymoon’s over,” one of them put it.

I want to spend a few paragraphs on why this matters to Warsh’s reputation, and then a great many more on why it matters to you — whether you have ever heard of Kevin Warsh or not, whether you can name a single member of the Federal Open Market Committee, whether the words “term premium” mean anything at all. Because underneath the institutional theatre of Jackson Hole sits a plain question that will decide how much it costs you to renew a mortgage, carry a credit card balance, or finance a car this autumn. And it arrives six weeks before an election in which, according to every major pollster now working the question, the economy is the single thing voters say they care most about — at the exact moment the man who leads that economy insists there is nothing to worry about at all.

What Warsh actually has to decide

Warsh inherited a Fed with rates in the 3.5 to 3.75 percent range, an inflation rate that has drifted further from the 2 percent target rather than closer to it, and a president who has made no secret of wanting rates lower to ease borrowing costs ahead of November. He has responded, so far, by saying almost nothing specific. His stated reason is that he wants an unfiltered read on how markets are digesting the data, rather than having the Fed’s own signals reflected back at it — part of a broader project, alongside a small library of task forces, to shrink the Fed’s footprint in markets it believes it has distorted.

The trouble is that the world will not hold still while Warsh keeps his own counsel. Long-term US borrowing costs have surged to multi-decade highs, forcing Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent into surprise interventions to calm the bond market, interventions that markets are now testing rather than trusting. Economists describe what’s happening as a “generational reset” in bond yields — a shift driven partly by a worsening fiscal picture as Washington keeps borrowing heavily, and partly by the artificial-intelligence investment boom I have tracked elsewhere in these pages, which is pulling money away from government debt and into hyperscaler financing at exactly the moment Washington needs buyers for that debt.

This is where the term “term premium” earns its keep, because it is the bridge between a Jackson Hole speech and your own bank statement. It is the extra compensation investors demand to hold a bond for ten or twenty years rather than a few months, and when it rises, it drags every other long-term borrowing cost up with it — mortgage rates most directly of all, since thirty-year mortgage pricing tracks the ten-year Treasury yield far more closely than it tracks the Fed’s own overnight rate. Warsh does not set your mortgage rate on Friday. But what he says, and does not say, about how he reads this dynamic will move it within days.

Some officials inside the Fed already believe the case for raising rates further is straightforward: the current range is not obviously restraining the economy, credit remains freely available, and a gradual increase now beats an ugly, catch-up increase later if inflation reaccelerates or the public loses faith the Fed will act at all. Others still believe patience will be rewarded, that the data will turn in the second half of the year, as it has shown some signs of doing. Warsh has never said which camp he belongs to, only that he is committed to getting inflation back to target after five years of overshoot. Markets, left to read tea leaves, have grown “jittery” — the word used by Deirdre Dunn, Citigroup’s head of global rates — about whether Warsh’s hawkish reputation is more talk than follow-through.

There is also the matter of what is actually driving those yields higher, and here Warsh finds himself in an uncomfortable position, because one of the culprits is a boom he does not want to snuff out. The artificial-intelligence investment surge is drawing enormous sums of capital toward hyperscaler borrowing, and away from the government bonds Washington needs someone to buy. “The trend is our friend here,” Warsh told a congressional hearing last month, when asked about the broader uptick in business capital spending — a chairman rooting for the very phenomenon that is making his own job harder. He remains hopeful that wider AI adoption eventually delivers higher productivity and, in time, lower inflation. But in the meantime, the spending is bidding up the price of chips and other crucial inputs, and the borrowing behind it is competing directly with Treasuries for investor demand. Layer on top of that a protracted, unresolved war with Iran that has already pushed inflation higher the longer it drags on, and a new trade dispute with Canada threatening to revive the tariff-driven price pressures that had only just begun to fade — and the “here and now” Warsh would rather not discuss starts to look considerably more urgent than the “big picture” speech he had reportedly hoped to give.

Why this is not really a Washington story

I have written at length elsewhere in these pages about the financial architecture propping up the artificial-intelligence boom — the circular financing arrangements, the off-balance-sheet leases, the hyperscaler bond issuance now running past two hundred billion dollars. That series, The Capex Ledger, is concerned with who owns the risk when that architecture wobbles. This series is concerned with something narrower and, I think, more urgent: what happens to an ordinary household’s finances while the people responsible for managing the wobble spend months deciding how much to say about it in public.

Because the honest answer, this week, is: nothing good. If Warsh’s silence keeps yields elevated, mortgage renewals get more expensive — and a very large share of American homeowners with fixed-rate mortgages taken out when rates were far lower are going to have to refinance at some point in the coming years, whatever happens Friday. If the Fed does raise rates to fight the inflation the White House insists does not exist, credit card annual percentage rates — already sitting well above twenty percent for most cardholders — climb further, and so do auto loan rates for the roughly one in six vehicles bought with financing. If Warsh instead holds and inflation reaccelerates, groceries and other everyday costs keep drifting upward regardless, a tax paid by everyone, felt hardest by those with the least room in a monthly budget to absorb it.

And there is a third possibility worth naming plainly, because it is the one markets currently seem most worried about: that Warsh neither raises nor holds convincingly, but continues to say too little for anyone to plan around. A central bank that will not commit to a framework does not remove uncertainty from a bond market already unsettled by deficits and AI-driven borrowing — it adds to it. Uncertainty of that kind does not stay confined to trading floors. It shows up, with a lag, in the rate a bank quotes a family renewing a mortgage next spring, because lenders price in the risk of not knowing which way the Fed is likely to move. There is no version of the next several weeks, as things stand, in which the answer to “what does this cost me” is zero.

The gap between the data and the denial

This is the part of the story that turns a Fed governance question into a political one, and it is where this series will spend most of its attention over the coming weeks. Because at the very moment household economics has become the dominant issue in American political life — Pew Research found that by a wide margin, voters heading into these midterms want their congressional candidates talking about prices and affordability above all else, and a Washington Post/Ipsos poll found more than half of registered voters naming the economy and high prices as the single most important factor in how they will vote — the president has spent the better part of a year insisting there is nothing to talk about. He has called affordability concerns a “hoax,” dismissed them as a “con job by the Democrats,” and, in one especially memorable riff at a Georgia steel plant in February, declared that the reason the word “affordability” had disappeared from the news was that he had already won the argument: “I’ve won affordability.”

None of this is a single off-the-cuff remark. It is a pattern that has hardened over the better part of a year. As far back as last December, with fears mounting over a worsening job market, the president travelled to Pennsylvania to discuss what he called ending an “inflation crisis” he blamed on his predecessor, even as he simultaneously insisted the entire premise of affordability worry was a Democratic hoax. Pressed directly by a Fox News host earlier this year on whether affordability concerns were merely a “voter perception issue,” he did not engage with the substance at all. He called it “a con job by the Democrats,” adding, “I don’t know that they are saying that. I think polls are fake. We have the greatest economy we’ve ever had” — this while a contemporaneous Washington Post/ABC News/Ipsos survey found most Americans reporting they were spending more on groceries and utilities than the year before. By February, at that Georgia steel plant, the denial had evolved into something closer to a victory lap: “What word have you not heard over the last two weeks? Affordability. Because I’ve won. I’ve won affordability.”

The data he is arguing against is his own administration’s. His approval rating specifically on his handling of the economy sank to roughly a third of the country by early August — well below the low point of even his own first term, when the same number never dropped under forty percent. Reuters/Ipsos, Gallup, and AP-NORC surveys through the summer told a consistent story, with his overall job approval sinking to the low-to-mid thirties amid growing disapproval even among his own party’s voters. And the country is not narrowly divided on whether there is a problem, only on which party it trusts to fix it: Americans split almost evenly, thirty-seven to thirty-six, over which party has the better answer on the economy. Neither side owns this issue yet. Which means neither side has to lose it, either — and it is precisely that kind of contested, unresolved terrain where a sustained pattern of denial from the top becomes its own kind of evidence.

I will have much more to say in the next instalment about the shape and history of that denial — where it started, how it has hardened from “hoax” to “I’ve won,” and what it costs a governing party to insist a problem voters feel in their own kitchens simply does not exist. And the instalment after that will turn to a trade war much closer to home for readers of this newsletter: the tariff fight between Canada and the United States, and the very unglamorous way a fight over steel quotas and softwood lumber classifications turns into a more expensive dishwasher, a pricier truck, and a slower home renovation on both sides of the border.

For now, the point is simpler. Paul Volcker told a young Kevin Warsh that the Fed’s job was to get rates about right, and to look like it knew what it was doing. On Friday, in Jackson Hole, Warsh will attempt the second half of that assignment in front of the world. The first half — getting it right — is the part that actually reaches your bank statement. And on that question, for now, silence is not neutral. It has a price, and households are already paying it.
