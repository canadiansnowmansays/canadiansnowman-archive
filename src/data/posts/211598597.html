Three parts into this series, I have mostly been asking a banker's question: is the money real, and who actually eats the loss if it isn't. The $500 billion Nvidia-led financing consortium, the $662 billion in off-balance-sheet lease liabilities, the circular financing between Nvidia and OpenAI propping up both companies' numbers at once — all of it has been, at bottom, a question about downside risk. This entry asks the opposite question, and it turns out Washington and dozens of statehouses are already deep into arguing about it: if the upside is real — if this genuinely adds trillions to the economy, as forecast — who is actually entitled to a piece of it?

As Tony Romm reported for The New York Times on August 16, 2026, that question has stopped being an academic one and become one of the more politically live fights in the country, running simultaneously through Congress, state legislatures, and city councils in a way few technology debates manage. Betsey Stevenson, a University of Michigan public policy professor and former Obama-era Labor Department chief economist, distilled the whole fight into five words for the Times: "What does A.I. owe us?"

The Numbers Making the Question Unavoidable

It is worth sitting with the scale of the forecast driving all of this, because the political urgency only makes sense once the size of the prize is visible. Some estimates cited in the Times piece put AI’s potential contribution to global economic output above $15 trillion by 2030. That forecast alone has already reshaped equity markets: AI-exposed companies now make up a lopsided share of the S&P 500, and both Anthropic and OpenAI are reportedly weighing public offerings that could value each company north of $1 trillion. Elon Musk, meanwhile, briefly became the world’s first trillionaire this June when SpaceX went public — a data point the piece uses less as a business story than as a preview of what concentrated AI wealth might look like at the individual level, repeated across a handful of founders and early investors, if nothing intervenes.

Set against that is a second, darker forecast running in parallel: mass job displacement has not yet arrived in any measurable way — the piece is careful to note there have been no large-scale layoffs directly attributable to AI so far — but the fear of it has already become a durable political fact, showing up in protests at multiple levels of government and even surfacing, per the Times, at one of President Trump’s own rallies last month. Brent Orrell, who co-directs a joint AI commission run by the American Enterprise Institute and the Urban Institute — a deliberately left-right pairing — told the paper the political pressure is growing simply because people are worried about their economic footing and see AI as one more threat to it. The fear is running well ahead of the layoffs. That gap, historically, is usually where the most volatile politics live.

The Sovereign Wealth Fund Idea, and Its Stranger Bedfellows

The most structurally ambitious proposal on the table comes from Senator Bernie Sanders, who wants the federal government to take a 50 percent equity stake in major AI companies, funded through a one-time tax paid in stock rather than cash, rolled into a new sovereign wealth fund the Times estimates would be worth roughly $7 trillion at current valuations. Sanders’ own math suggests an immediate payment of more than $1,000 to every American, with ongoing distributions as the fund’s holdings appreciate. The closest domestic precedent is the Alaska Permanent Fund, which has paid oil-revenue dividends to state residents for decades — modest sums, never a wage replacement, but a proof of concept that a state can treat a strategic resource’s proceeds as something closer to a shared inheritance than a purely private windfall. If data is the new oil, as the analogy’s defenders argue, the same logic transfers.

What makes this section of the piece genuinely surprising is not Sanders’ proposal itself — a government equity stake in Big Tech is a predictable ask from that direction — but that Donald Trump has floated a strikingly similar idea from the opposite end of the political spectrum, mentioning at least once this year that he’d like to convene tech executives to discuss the public taking a stake in companies like OpenAI. Even Musk, no one’s idea of a redistributionist, has called for a “Universal HIGH INCOME” funded through direct government checks. On the industry side, both Sam Altman and Dario Amodei have said in general terms that they support some mechanism for sharing AI’s gains with the public — Altman has reportedly lobbied Trump directly on the idea. But that support has stayed rhetorical. Per the Times, neither OpenAI nor Anthropic has endorsed any specific piece of legislation, including Sanders’ proposal, and both companies declined to comment for the article. The pattern across the industry, the piece notes dryly, has mostly been to call for further study.

Not everyone thinks the equity-stake model is even a good idea on its own terms, which is the part of this debate I find most underdiscussed. Ben Harris of the Brookings Institution warned the Times that a government holding a direct financial stake in the companies it is supposed to regulate creates exactly the wrong incentive — a state that profits when a company’s valuation rises has a built-in reason to go easy on the regulation that might lower it. Harris’s preferred alternative is unglamorous by comparison: use the tax code Washington already has, and simply raise the rates on capital. It will not generate a headline the way a $7 trillion sovereign fund does, but it does not require the government to become a shareholder in the industry it’s meant to police.

The Industry’s Own Answer, and Why I Don’t Fully Buy It

Since I first drafted this piece, OpenAI has offered its own answer to Stevenson’s question, and it’s worth taking seriously precisely because it comes from inside the industry rather than from a think tank adjacent to it. Dean Ball — who left a White House AI-adviser role, where he helped write the Trump administration’s AI Action Plan, publicly criticized the administration during its dispute with Anthropic, and then joined OpenAI this July to run a team called “Strategic Futures” — gave The Atlantic’s Matteo Wong an extended interview in early September laying out his view of what AI owes the country, and his answer is close to a direct rebuttal of the entire premise this section of my piece has been built on. “Wealth is not some resource that we divide up,” he told Wong. “It’s something we create.” His claim is that innovators historically capture only a single-digit fraction of the value they generate for society at large, and that OpenAI, ten years from now, will likely be no exception.

I find this argument more interesting than persuasive. It’s true as far as it goes — the food-delivery-app example Ball reaches for, the sheer convenience now available to ordinary consumers, is a real form of value that a wealth-concentration framing genuinely underweights. But it also quietly answers a different question than the one Stevenson actually asked. Nobody in this debate — not Sanders, not Hochul, not Lucas — is arguing that AI creates zero value. They’re arguing about who captures the value once it exists, and pointing to a single-digit-innovator-share statistic doesn’t tell you anything about whether the other ninety-some percent is landing on displaced workers and strained municipal power grids, or on broadly shared consumer surplus. Ball’s own single best idea, buried later in the same interview, is actually a tacit concession that the current arrangement isn’t self-correcting: he proposes a federal body that would set, month to month, the percentage of a lab’s compute that can go toward automating its own AI research — a dial explicitly modeled on how the Federal Reserve sets interest rates, meant to give the public “meaningful political control over the technology” it doesn’t currently have. That’s a genuinely novel governance mechanism, and a considerably more concrete one than anything Sanders, Trump, or Musk has floated on the wealth-sharing side of this debate. It’s also, notably, a mechanism aimed at controlling the pace of AI development rather than the distribution of its proceeds — which suggests that even the industry’s own in-house institutionalist thinks the pacing question and the wealth-sharing question are separate problems, whatever Ball’s own essay implies by discussing them in the same breath.

Where the Fight Is Actually Being Won or Lost: Underneath the Data Centers

The federal debate is still mostly hypothetical — a set of proposals, a handful of speeches, no legislation close to passing. The state and local fight is not hypothetical at all, and it is where the Times piece is strongest. Virginia — home to “Data Center Alley,” the densest concentration of AI infrastructure in the country — became the first state to tax data centers on their electricity consumption, after a year of negotiation that state senator L. Louise Lucas pushed through despite a well-funded lobbying campaign from the Data Center Coalition, the industry group representing Amazon, Microsoft, and Alphabet among others. The tax, expected to bring in up to $600 million in its first year, is nonetheless a fraction of what Lucas originally proposed; an attempt to repeal the industry’s sales-tax exemption on server hardware was defeated outright by the same coalition, which the Times reports also helped fund a dark-money ad campaign — one ad opened with the line “Virginia’s data centers are there for you” — to protect the exemption. Lucas’s own verdict was blunt: the state is still missing dollars it should be collecting.

New York went further. Governor Kathy Hochul enacted the first statewide moratorium on new data center construction, for one year, alongside a promised overhaul of the state’s tax treatment of the industry. Hochul’s argument to the Times was specifically about jobs and power, not ideology: the facilities demand more electricity than the state can readily supply even with new nuclear capacity under construction, while generating comparatively few permanent jobs relative to their footprint — a sharp contrast, she noted, with the state’s earlier, successful courtship of a $100 billion Micron semiconductor investment upstate. “What’s the return on investment,” she asked, “when the jobs aren’t associated with it?” President Trump, notably, demanded Hochul rescind the moratorium and predicted it would simply push data centers into “red states” — a prediction the rest of the piece somewhat undercuts, since red-state governments have been imposing their own restrictions in parallel. Nebraska’s Republican governor, Jim Pillen, suspended a tax-incentive law this July, arguing flatly that Big Tech needs to pay its own way. Texas’s Greg Abbott, who once branded his state the “epicenter” of AI development, ordered a statewide audit of data centers’ compliance with electricity rules this month. Ohio and Illinois — one Republican-led, one Democratic-led — have both paused incentive programs this year as well. The University of Virginia’s Digital Technology for Democracy Lab counts roughly 120 communities that have considered or adopted outright construction moratoriums over the past two years, and roughly 30 states that have taken up data-center-specific energy legislation.

A Backlash Without a Party

What the Times piece captures better than most coverage of this fight is that it does not sort cleanly along the usual left-right axis at all. Josh Hawley, a conservative Missouri Republican, sounds nearly identical to Lucas, a Virginia Democrat, when he complains that the industry claims it would love to share more of the wealth while lobbying hard against any proposal that would actually compel it to. Arizona’s Democratic governor Katie Hobbs and New Jersey’s legislature have both moved on data-center energy tariffs this year for the same practical reason cited across party lines: constituents calling their district offices about rising power bills, not any broader ideological objection to artificial intelligence. This is, in other words, not a fight about whether AI is good or bad. It is a fight — increasingly, in dozens of legislatures simultaneously — about who absorbs the electricity bill and who gets the tax base, conducted by officials from both parties who have each concluded, independently, that the answer currently on offer is “not us.”

Nowhere is that convergence sharper than the pairing Josh Tyrangiel, a staff writer at The Atlantic, drew on PBS's Washington Week this September: Steve Bannon and Bernie Sanders, aligned almost completely on data centers despite agreeing on virtually nothing else, both reading the backlash as, in Tyrangiel's phrase, "100 percent populist fodder" — people who feel they're being asked to host the infrastructure and told to expect no share of what it produces. Senator Mark Warner's warning on the same program cuts the same way from inside Congress itself: the question, he said, is no longer whether AI transforms the economy, but whether Congress helps shape that transformation or spends the next decade scrambling to catch up after the fact. Tyrangiel also offered a useful corrective to one piece of the backlash's own folk wisdom worth noting in passing: most data centers recycle the water used for cooling rather than consuming it outright, which means the more durable and better-founded anxiety, in his account, sits with electricity demand and grid strain rather than water use — a distinction this series' own coverage of the fight should hold onto rather than let blur together.

The Ledger This Series Is Actually Keeping

I titled this series the Capex Ledger because the earlier entries were, in the end, about liabilities — debt structures, lease obligations, circular financing arrangements designed to make an enormous capital outlay look sustainable on a balance sheet. This entry is the other side of that same ledger, and it is worth stating plainly: a $15 trillion forecast is not free money waiting to be collected. It has to come from somewhere, and right now the “somewhere” running the tab is mostly municipal power grids and state budgets, while the eventual profits accrue overwhelmingly to a small number of companies and their earliest investors. Stevenson’s question — what does AI owe us — does not yet have an agreed answer in Washington. But Virginia, New York, Nebraska, and roughly thirty other states have already stopped waiting for one, and started answering it themselves, one electricity tariff at a time.

The Precedent This Actually Resembles

I have written elsewhere in this newsletter about the long history of American industrial transformations that parceled out their costs and benefits unevenly — electrification, the wave of cheap Chinese manufacturing imports that hollowed out entire regional economies before anyone in Washington built a serious policy response to it. The Times piece draws that same line explicitly, and I think it deserves to be taken more literally than a passing historical footnote. Every one of those earlier transformations produced the same three-stage political arc: an initial phase where local governments compete to attract the new industry with tax breaks and incentives, a middle phase where the industry’s actual footprint — jobs, environmental cost, strain on public infrastructure — turns out to be smaller or more damaging than promised, and a final phase, arriving years too late for the communities that bet on stage one, where politicians finally start clawing back concessions the industry has since stopped needing to make. Virginia, notably, is still fighting the stage-two argument even as it locks in stage-three tax collection — Data Center Alley is simultaneously the place that benefited most from the original incentives and the first place organized enough to start reversing them.

What's genuinely new this time, and what I think the sovereign-wealth-fund proposals are groping toward even when they get the mechanism wrong, is the scale mismatch between how fast the capital moves and how slowly the political response usually arrives. Electrification took decades to build out and decades more to regulate. The data center buildout Romm is describing has gone from regional curiosity to a $15 trillion global forecast and a fifty-state legislative response inside about three years. Harris's tax-the-capital-more approach is almost certainly the more institutionally sound answer, precisely because it doesn't require the government to become an industry shareholder — but it is also the answer least suited to the speed this fight is actually moving at, since raising capital gains rates requires the kind of sustained congressional coalition-building that the data-center backlash, running through mayors and governors instead, has simply bypassed. Ball's compute-ratio dial is the first industry-side proposal that's actually built for that speed — a lever tunable monthly rather than legislated once — even if, by his own account, nobody has agreed on who would hold it. The states got there first not because their policy tools were better, but because they were the only actors structurally capable of moving on the same timeline as the industry itself.
