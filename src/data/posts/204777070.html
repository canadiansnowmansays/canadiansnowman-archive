A disclosure to begin, because this essay touches on markets in which I hold a personal stake, and you are owed the knowledge before I offer you a single opinion. I own shares in Tesla. Not many, but enough that honesty requires me to put the fact on the table before I write a word about electric vehicles or the companies that make them. I have written elsewhere, in The Musk Stack, about the man who runs that company and the tangle of admiration and alarm he provokes in me; I will not relitigate all of that here. But you should read what follows knowing that when I praise the electric car, a small and self-interested part of me is praising my own portfolio, and you are entitled to discount accordingly. I would rather tell you and be discounted than stay silent and be trusted falsely.

Now to the hard part, the part I have been deferring since the introduction, where I promised that if I was going to ask hard questions of others, I would start and end at home. I have asked them of Europe and its ovens, of the United States and its beautiful clean coal, of the populists and their tractors. It is time to ask them of Canada, and to do it without the comfortable anaesthetic of national self-regard, because my country presents one of the purest examples on Earth of the gap this entire series is about — the gap between what we say and what we do, between the climate-conscious self-image and the petrostate reality.

Here are the facts, stripped of the flattering narration we Canadians prefer. The oil and gas sector is the single largest source of greenhouse gas emissions in Canada, responsible for about twenty-eight per cent of the national total — up from seventeen per cent in 1990. Canada is the fourth-largest producer of oil in the world. The oil sands of northern Alberta, the vast bitumen deposits that are among the most carbon-intensive large-scale crude operations on the planet — generating something like two times the emissions per barrel of the average North American crude just to extract and process — set a record in 2023 for total emissions, 86.5 million tonnes, and in 2025 oil sands production reached an all-time high of around 3.5 million barrels a day. While my country endured its second-worst wildfire season on record, while the smoke I wrote about poured south into American lungs, while the permafrost I wrote about buckled under northern towns, Alberta was pumping more bitumen out of the ground than it ever had in its history. The independent Climate Action Tracker rates Canada’s overall climate performance as “Highly Insufficient.”

But here is the single fact that should end, forever, any Canadian claim to the moral high ground, and I want to state it as plainly as I can. Canada exports oil and gas at such a scale that the emissions from the final combustion of those exports — the carbon released when someone, somewhere, burns the fuel we sold them — exceed Canada’s entire domestic emissions. Read that again. Everything we emit at home — every car, every furnace, every factory, every oil sands operation, the whole national output that we fret about and measure and occasionally tax — is smaller than the cloud of carbon we generate for other people by selling them the fuel and looking away as they burn it. We do not count those exported emissions as ours. They appear on no Canadian ledger. They are, in the accounting, somebody else’s problem, combusted conveniently over the horizon. But the atmosphere, as I argued in the essay on the smoke, keeps no such books. There is one sky, and we are pouring into it, through the side door of export, more than we will admit through the front.

This is the house built for winter at the scale of a national economy, and it is the most uncomfortable instance in the whole series precisely because it is mine. Canada’s prosperity, its federal budget, the equalization payments that knit the country together, the fortunes of an entire province — all of it is built on the assumption that the world will keep buying our fossil fuels, an assumption calibrated to a twentieth-century climate and economy that is, like everything else in this series, leaving. We have constructed a national house designed to hold the heat of the oil boom, and we are standing inside it insisting the weather will not change, even as we choke on the smoke of the very warming our exports help to drive.

I want to be fair, because fairness at home is the whole discipline of this essay. The defenders of the oil sands are not villains, and they make arguments that deserve a hearing. They point out, correctly, that the emissions intensity of the oil sands — the carbon per barrel — has fallen for several years running, as the industry has gotten more efficient. They point out that the world still demands oil, and that if Canada does not supply it, Saudi Arabia or Russia or Venezuela will, with no better environmental record and far worse human-rights ones. They point out that hundreds of thousands of livelihoods, and the economic security of a province, ride on this industry, and that an abrupt abandonment would visit real suffering on real families — the same dignity argument I credited when I wrote about American coal miners, and it is no less real north of the border. These are not nothing.

But notice the sleight of hand in the intensity argument, because it is the same move I flagged in the essay on the climate TACO, and Alberta’s government performs it with particular brazenness. Emissions intensity — carbon per barrel — can fall while total emissions rise, if you simply produce enough more barrels, and that is exactly what has happened: the intensity drops, the press release celebrates, and the actual tonnage going into the sky climbs to a record. It is a way of reporting a defeat as a victory, of pointing at the efficiency of each cigarette while the patient smokes more and more of them. The atmosphere does not care about carbon per barrel. It cares about total carbon, and the total is going up.

And as I write this, the sleight of hand has just been elevated to the level of national policy, in a deal that crystallizes everything this essay is about. In November of 2025, Prime Minister Carney — the same man whose killing of the consumer carbon tax I dissected several essays ago — signed a “grand bargain” with Alberta’s premier, Danielle Smith. In exchange for Alberta’s cooperation, Ottawa would clear the way for a major new pipeline carrying bitumen to the West Coast and onward to Asian markets, and would scrap the proposed cap on oil-and-gas emissions and the clean electricity regulations that were among the last pillars of the federal climate plan. The fig leaf covering the whole arrangement is the Pathways Alliance carbon-capture project — a sixteen-billion-dollar scheme to bury some of the carbon dioxide produced at the oil sands. “No Pathways, no pipeline,” Carney declared, presenting the capture project as the thing that would make Canadian oil clean enough to keep selling.

But run the arithmetic, because the arithmetic is the entire moral of this series. The Pathways project, in its best-case scenario, hopes to capture something like ten to twelve million tonnes of carbon dioxide a year — and only the carbon released in the extraction, the digging and steaming of bitumen out of the ground. A new pipeline carrying a million barrels a day would, when that oil is finally burned in the engines and furnaces of Asia, release on the order of a hundred and fifty million tonnes of carbon dioxide. The pipeline, in other words, would produce roughly fifteen times more carbon than the capture project it is yoked to could ever remove. The Pembina Institute calculated that you would need something like eight more Pathways projects just to decarbonize the extraction of the oil the single pipeline would carry — and even then, the vast bulk of the emissions, the carbon from actually burning the stuff, would sail off the national ledger entirely, combusted conveniently over the horizon in someone else’s country. This is carbon capture deployed not to reduce emissions but to sell a pipeline — the obfuscation stage of the climate TACO, rendered in steel and subsidy. The environment minister who had built the dismantled climate plan, Steven Guilbeault, resigned from cabinet within hours of the deal becoming public. He understood what he was looking at. So, I think, did Carney, which is the saddest part: the most climate-literate prime minister in Canadian history, signing the document that traded the climate plan for a pipeline, calling it “Canada building.”

The creed is the same on both sides of the border now, north and south: produce more, capture a little, export the rest, and count only the emissions you cannot hide. The United States dresses it in coal smoke and “energy dominance”; Canada dresses it in carbon capture and “co-operative federalism.” But the underlying move is identical, and it is the move this whole series has been tracking — the refusal to renovate the house, performed in the confident language of building it.

I have written at length, in my energy series The Great Energy Challenge, about how carbon capture has become the all-purpose alibi of the fossil age — the technology invoked not to cut emissions but to justify producing more of the fuel whose emissions it cannot keep up with, the fig leaf that lets a government or an oil company say “clean” and “beautiful” while the actual tonnage climbs. The American version routes carbon-capture subsidies toward propping up coal; the Canadian version yokes it to a new bitumen pipeline. The vocabulary differs, the arithmetic does not: in both cases the capture is real but tiny, and the production it licenses is vast. If you take one idea from that series into this one, let it be this — that “we’ll capture it” has become, in our decade, the single most effective phrase for ensuring that we won’t.

And here, at last, is where the electric vehicle re-enters, and where I must be most careful given what I disclosed at the top. The transition away from the internal combustion engine is real and accelerating — I wrote in the essay on China about an EV market closing in on half of all new cars sold there, about BYD becoming the largest electric carmaker on Earth. The technology that threatens the oil sands is not some distant hope; it is on the roads now, in growing numbers, and every electric car sold is a small permanent subtraction from future oil demand. The International Energy Agency and others have projected that global oil demand may peak before the end of this decade, and when it does, the highest-cost, highest-carbon producers will be the first to be squeezed — and the oil sands, expensive and carbon-heavy, sit precisely in that vulnerable category. This is the cruel irony of Alberta’s record production: it may be pumping hardest just as the ground beneath the entire business begins, slowly, to give way, exactly like the permafrost under the northern towns. The petrostate, too, is a house built for a climate that is leaving — an economic climate this time, the long fossil-fuel summer that is finally, belatedly, beginning to cool.

I take no pleasure in this, and not only because of my own modest holdings. The people of Alberta are my countrymen, and the unwinding of a fossil-fuel economy, if it is done carelessly, will hurt them the way the closing of the coal counties hurt the people of Appalachia — and we saw, in the essay on the populist backlash and in the essay on American coal, what a politics of abandoned resource towns produces. The lesson of this series is not that Alberta is wicked and should be punished. It is that Alberta, like the rest of us, is living in a house built for a world that is ending, and that the humane response is neither to pretend the world is not ending nor to bulldoze the house with the people still inside it, but to begin, with honesty and with care for those who will bear the cost, to build the replacement.

We Canadians like to think of ourselves as the good guys of the climate story — the reasonable, multilateral, tree-loving nation, victims of the larger emitters rather than perpetrators. I have spent this series dismantling that comfort, and this essay is its demolition. We are the country warming twice as fast as the world, and we are one of the great carbon exporters to that warming world. We are the burned and the burning, the victim and the dealer, the nation that breathes its own smoke and sells the match. There is no hill here for any of us to stand on, least of all me, with my Tesla shares and my Toronto townhouse and my fourteen hundred miles of comfortable distance from the oil sands whose royalties have helped pay for the country I love. The hardest questions in this series were always going to be the ones I had to ask in the mirror. Here they are. I do not have comfortable answers, and I distrust anyone who does.
