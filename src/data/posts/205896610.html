He has been shot at three times. His friend was shot dead in front of a crowd. And on July 7, 2026, the New York Times reported that the administration he leads has spent eighteen months quietly dismantling much of the federal machinery built, however imperfectly, to make that number smaller.

I want to sit with that sentence, because I don’t think it resolves into irony as easily as it first appears to. Irony implies a kind of cosmic joke, a coincidence too neat to be intentional. What the Times describes is not a coincidence. It is a philosophy, applied consistently, and applied against the evident self-interest of the man enforcing it.

The facts, briefly, because the essay does the rest of the work: the White House Office of Gun Violence Prevention, closed. The Community Based Violence Intervention and Prevention Initiative — the program that funds hospitals to counsel gunshot victims before they become gunshot victims twice — stripped of $150 million, according to the official who ran it under the previous administration. The CDC’s Division of Violence Prevention, hollowed by layoffs. A federally commissioned report on red-flag laws, quietly removed from a government website after the transition, and now surviving only in the corner of a nonprofit’s server that its own lead author didn’t know still existed. Roughly $18.5 million in counterterrorism prevention funding, gone, its architect returned to academia. And the Health Secretary — a man whose father and uncle were both assassinated — telling a House committee in April that he does not consider gun violence a public health matter at all. “A law enforcement issue,” he called it.

I noted in Part X that America’s political violence defies partisan explanation, that the ledger runs through Butler and the Hilton and the Capitol lawn without ever settling into a story either side can tell cleanly about the other. This postscript is not a revision of that argument. It is what happens after the ledger closes and the policy apparatus decides what to do about it. Some of that decision is unremarkable — a new administration closing its predecessor’s signature office is a partisan reflex as old as the transfer of power itself, and I’d be a poor observer if I pretended otherwise. But the reclassification runs deeper than office politics: the roughly 44,000 Americans who die by gun each year are not one population. Most — well over half — die by suicide, a crisis of means and mental health that sits closer to medicine than to policing. The rest, the homicides and the mass shootings that fill the ledger this series has kept, are a different problem again. Folding both into “a law enforcement issue,” as the Health Secretary did before a House committee in April, isn’t a refinement of the public-health framing. It’s an exit from it — for the whole category, not just the part where the argument might hold.

I’ll tell you what I think is happening, because you should know it’s my inference and not the paper’s reporting: I think this is a fall election calculation dressed as a philosophy of governance. A law-and-order frame plays well with a base that the administration needs energized in November, in a way that a public-health frame — which asks people to sit with statistics about suicide, about domestic proximity, about the ordinary American gun in the ordinary American home — does not. But I want to give the other readings their due, because the Times piece doesn’t hand you my theory on a plate. It could equally be read as simple repudiation — undoing the previous administration’s program because it was the previous administration’s program, the same reflex that closed the gun violence office within weeks of the inauguration. It could be read as fidelity to a gun-rights coalition that has backed this president since before he was a president. The electoral read is mine. The Times gives you the receipts and lets you do the arithmetic.

There’s a steelman here worth stating plainly, because it’s an old argument and not, I think, made in bad faith: there is a real case that federal violence-prevention dollars have historically bought more bureaucracy than results, that the marginal dollar does more in a prosecutor’s office than a grant program, and that a government skeptical of its own prevention apparatus is not thereby indifferent to the deaths it counts. I don’t find that case persuasive at the scale of what’s been cut, but it is coherent, and a fair essay says so.

What I can’t get past, sitting here in Toronto, is the tension between the story this White House tells about itself and the direction its policy actually runs. The near-miss, the fist in the air at Butler, the sense of a man spared for a purpose — that is a story about survival, told often and told proudly. And yet the same administration’s Justice Department is now suing Virginia and California to expand gun access rather than restrict it — the first time, a Duke law scholar notes in the piece, that the department’s civil rights division has ever sued affirmatively for looser gun laws rather than tighter ones. The survival story and the policy point in opposite directions, and nobody in the administration seems to have noticed, or minded.

The last prime minister assassinated in this country died in 1868. I have said before that this is not a boast; it is a fluke of history, a small country’s luck. But it does let me watch what’s happening south of the border with a certain clarity that proximity might not afford. What I see is a government that has been shot at and has concluded, not that the wound needs tending, but that the bandages were the problem all along.

The dinner, I wrote in Part X, will go on. So, it turns out, will everything else — the funding cuts, the vanished reports, the reclassification of a public health crisis as a policing matter. The reckoning I described as absent has not simply failed to arrive. It has been actively prevented from arriving, one canceled grant at a time.
