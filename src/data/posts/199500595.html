It is a well-established fact that the Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy has outsold the Encyclopaedia Galactica on every planet where both publications are available, owing largely to the fact that it is slightly cheaper and has the words DON’T PANIC inscribed in large, friendly letters on its cover.

It is a lesser-known fact that the Guide’s editorial department has attempted, on no fewer than thirty-seven occasions, to publish a definitive entry on the entity known as the Musk Stack. Each attempt has been overtaken by events before reaching final proof. The most recent editor assigned to the project resigned after the subject acquired a social media platform between the first and second drafts, merged it with an artificial intelligence company during the third, and filed for an initial public offering while the editor was in the lavatory.

The entry, as it currently stands in the Guide’s conditions-may-change section, reads as follows:

MUSK STACK, The

(See also: TESLA, SPACEX, xAI, X, STARLINK, NEURALINK, THE BORING COMPANY, and DO NOT ATTEMPT TO LIST THEM ALL BECAUSE HE WILL HAVE ACQUIRED SOMETHING ELSE BY THE TIME YOU FINISH)

The Musk Stack is the colloquial term for a portfolio of interconnected enterprises controlled by a single human from the planet Earth — a small, mostly harmless world in the unfashionable end of the western spiral arm of the Galaxy, notable primarily for its inhabitants’ persistent belief that digital watches are a pretty neat idea and that giving one person simultaneous control over rockets, satellites, cars, robots, social media, artificial intelligence, and energy infrastructure is a reasonable organisational structure.

The Stack’s origins can be traced to the late twentieth century, when a teenager from Pretoria, South Africa — having absorbed the complete works of Douglas Adams and concluded, quite reasonably, that the universe was far too interesting to be observed from Pretoria — obtained a Canadian passport and used it to reach the United States, which at the time still accepted immigrants who arrived with ideas rather than capital. He brought both.

How It Works

The principle underlying the Musk Stack is simple. Each company solves a problem that creates a problem that another company in the Stack also solves. The rockets launch the satellites. The satellites provide the internet. The internet trains the artificial intelligence. The artificial intelligence drives the cars. The cars contain batteries. The batteries store the energy. The energy powers the data centres. The data centres train more artificial intelligence. The artificial intelligence improves the robots. The robots build the cars. The cars need chips. The chips require a fabrication facility. The fabrication facility requires a power grid. The power grid requires batteries. The batteries are made by the car company.

It is, in the language of systems theory, a closed loop.

It is, in the language of everyone else, insane.

The Guide’s Department of Corporate Taxonomy attempted to classify the Musk Stack using the standard Galactic Business Register categories. The form requires the applicant to identify the company’s “primary sector of economic activity.” The department tried “Aerospace.” Then “Automotive.” Then “Telecommunications.” Then “Artificial Intelligence.” Then “Robotics.” Then “Energy.” Then “Social Media.” Then “Semiconductor Fabrication.” The form has seventeen boxes. The Stack filled all seventeen and wrote “Also: Mars” in the margin.

The department eventually filed it under “Whose Turn Is It to Be Omnipotent This Century?” — a category previously occupied only by the East India Company and a hyper-intelligent shade of the colour blue from Zarquon’s Singing Fish district.

The Merger Question

In the Earth year 2026, financial analysts on the planet began speculating that two of the Stack’s largest components — Tesla (which builds cars, robots, batteries, chips, and energy systems) and SpaceX (which builds rockets, operates satellites, runs an artificial intelligence company, and owns a social media platform) — might combine into a single corporate entity.

The analysts described this as “complex.”

This is rather like describing the heat death of the universe as “a bit chilly.”

The proposed combination would create an enterprise valued at approximately $2.8 trillion — a number so large that the Guide’s Department of Really Big Numbers was consulted for an appropriate metaphor and suggested: “Imagine every grain of sand on every beach on Earth. Now imagine each grain is a dollar. You are now imagining approximately four percent of the amount of money we are discussing, and you should probably sit down.”

The entity would manufacture the vehicles, build the robots that assemble the vehicles, design and fabricate the chips that operate the vehicles and robots, launch and maintain the satellite network that connects them, train the artificial intelligence that controls them, generate and store the energy that powers them, and own the social media platform on which the CEO announces all of the above, usually at 2:47 a.m.

When asked whether this concentration of capability in a single individual raised concerns, the relevant regulatory authorities on Earth — specifically the United States Department of Justice — indicated that a SpaceX-Tesla merger “likely wouldn’t spark antitrust issues.”

The Guide’s editors would like to note that this is the same species that once prosecuted a company for making its web browser too convenient.

The Compensation Structure

The CEO’s compensation at SpaceX is tied to two milestones: achieving a market capitalisation of $7.5 trillion, and establishing a colony of one million inhabitants on Mars.

A junior researcher at the Guide pointed out that combining the two companies would halve the distance to the first milestone. When asked about the second milestone, the researcher observed that if you already own the rockets, the satellite internet, the autonomous vehicles, the humanoid robots, the power storage, and the chip fabrication — the only thing standing between you and a Martian colony is the minor inconvenience of Mars not being particularly hospitable to human life.

“But,” the researcher added, “he also owns the company that makes the spacesuits.”

The file was stamped PROBABLY FINE and forwarded to the Department of Whose Problem Is This Anyway.

The Laws

The Stack’s expansion has prompted scholars from across the Galaxy to revisit several of Earth’s canonical Laws of technological prediction.

Asimov’s Three Laws of Robotics — which state that a robot may not harm a human, must obey human orders, and must protect its own existence — have proved difficult to implement at Tesla’s Fremont factory, where the Optimus humanoid robot is now being manufactured on the same production line that previously built the Model S sedan. The difficulty is not technical. It is taxonomic. Following the merger discussions, the robot’s firmware was unable to determine which corporate entity constituted its “master,” triggering a recursive loop that was only resolved when an engineer taped a Post-it note to the robot’s torso reading “ELON” and rebooted it.

Clarke’s Third Law — “Any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic” — has been updated by the Guide’s Technology Desk for the specific case of the Musk Stack:

“Any sufficiently advanced stock price is indistinguishable from magic.”

Tesla currently trades at approximately 325 times its earnings. The Guide’s financial analysts attempted to model this valuation using conventional methods (discounted cash flow, comparable company analysis, price-to-earnings ratio) and produced a result that the computer described as “adorable” before suggesting they try astrology instead.

Heinlein’s Observation — Robert A. Heinlein, an Earth author who wrote extensively about lunar colonisation, once proposed that “the Moon is a harsh mistress.” The phrase has taken on renewed relevance following reports that a Tesla Cybertruck — an angular, stainless-steel vehicle that already looks as though it was designed for a planet with no atmosphere — may serve as the design basis for a pressurised lunar rover.

Heinlein would have appreciated the irony. He envisioned a lunar colony that declared political independence from Earth. The Cybertruck colony would more likely declare independence from Tesla’s over-the-air software updates, which have been known to arrive at 3 a.m. and rearrange the dashboard for no discernible reason.

Bradbury’s Caution — Ray Bradbury, in The Martian Chronicles, warned that humanity would carry its worst impulses to new worlds. He imagined colonists bringing their prejudices, their hierarchies, and their strip malls.

He did not imagine they would also bring a $159,420 Signature Edition Model X Plaid, of which only one hundred were manufactured, all sold by invitation, all purchased within seventy-two hours. But he would not have been surprised. Bradbury understood that the first thing humans do upon reaching a new frontier is open a dealership.

The Canadian Footnote

The Guide’s entry on the Musk Stack contains a footnote — footnote 42, naturally — regarding the role of Canada in the Stack’s creation.

Canada is a large, polite country located immediately north of the United States, notable for universal healthcare, an inexplicable devotion to a sport played on frozen water, and a passport that once served as the gateway for a South African teenager to reach the country where all the interesting things happen.

The Canadian who writes about the Musk Stack for a publication called CanadianSnowmanSays has noted, with the weary precision of a man watching his neighbour build a Death Star in the backyard, that no other nation on Earth would permit a single individual to simultaneously control the dominant reusable rocket system, the largest satellite internet constellation, a social media platform with 600 million users, the most advanced civilian AI training infrastructure, the leading electric vehicle manufacturer, a humanoid robotics programme, a chip fabrication joint venture, and the energy storage systems that power all of the above — while holding contracts with the Department of Defence, NASA, and the intelligence community.

The Canadian further noted that this individual obtained his American opportunity via a Canadian passport.

Canada has not received a thank-you note.

The footnote ends: “See also: EMPIRE, ACCIDENTAL; CITIZENSHIP, STRATEGIC USE OF; and REGULATORY CAPTURE, AMUSING EXAMPLES OF.”

The Entry’s Final Line

The Guide’s editorial department, having failed thirty-seven times to publish a complete and current entry on the Musk Stack, has settled on the following permanent concluding line, which it believes will remain accurate regardless of future acquisitions, mergers, IPOs, lunar colonies, Martian settlements, or regulatory interventions:

DON’T PANIC.

But do read the prospectus.
