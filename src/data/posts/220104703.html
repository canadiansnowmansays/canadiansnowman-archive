Part II ended with a question. If diesel prices were really the point, why would the United States issue a sanctions licence that runs to April 7, 2027, five months past the midterms? And why would its envoys refuse to go to Moscow until Ukraine’s president made his peace with Donald Trump?

The honest answer is that we do not know everything. But we know a good deal more than the President has said, and most of it has been sitting in plain sight in reporting from the New York Times, the Wall Street Journal, the Financial Times and Ukrainian outlets for weeks. This essay assembles it. Then it turns to the more hopeful half of the story: what Europe, and the countries Ukraine still counts as friends, did in the same three days.

September 5, 2026

The trail runs back to a room in the Kremlin.

On September 5, 2026, Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Vladimir Putin in Moscow. According to three sources who spoke to the New York Times, Putin personally raised a business matter during that meeting: the sale of the foreign assets of Lukoil, Russia’s second-largest oil producer. As Meduza summarised the Times’ reporting on October 5, 2026, the sale had become part of the U.S.–Russia talks on ending the war.

What is for sale is not small. Lukoil’s international portfolio of oil fields, refineries and petrol stations across many countries was valued by the company itself at about $20 billion earlier in 2026. The Trump administration sanctioned Lukoil in the autumn of 2025, which forced the company to look for a buyer. The first serious bid, from the Russia-linked trading house Gunvor, was turned down by the U.S. Treasury.

The group now leading the bidding is where the story becomes uncomfortable. According to the Times and the Financial Times, it is organised by the American investor Todd Boehly, the former Chelsea co-owner, who gave $2 million to Trump’s political projects. It includes Sheikh Tahnoon bin Zayed, the brother of the UAE’s president, who has invested in ventures involving Kushner, Witkoff and Trump’s sons. It also includes the Qatari Al-Khayyat family, whose brothers Moutaz and Ramez are partners with Kushner and Ivanka Trump in a luxury resort project in southern Albania. And, as WION reported, the U.S. International Development Finance Corporation is seeking a stake as well, with Kushner and Witkoff helping to negotiate terms meant to secure what was described as a substantial upfront payment and profits interest for the United States.

Fairness requires the other side of the ledger. An administration official confirmed to the Times that Kushner and Witkoff were involved in the Lukoil discussions, but there is no evidence either man will personally profit. A spokesman for Witkoff has said he has no conflict of interest and no financial stake in the matter. Those statements deserve to be recorded, and I record them.

But the question for Ukraine was never whether two envoys would get rich. It is what the presence of this deal on the table does to the peace talks.

The catch in the bargain

David Frum, writing in The Atlantic on October 10, 2026, put his finger on it. Russian energy assets are not worth much if Ukrainian drones keep wrecking them. Every refinery Ukraine hits lowers the value of the Russian energy sector that buyers, partners and deal-makers are being invited to invest in.

So the commercial interest and the diplomatic demand point the same way. If Ukraine stops striking Russian refineries, Russian oil is worth more, Russian deals become more attractive, and the people around the table who are discussing those deals have a stronger hand. Frum described the implicit offer bluntly: compel Ukraine to stop, and the rewards will be very large.

None of this was new as of September 2026. In November 2025, the Wall Street Journal reported on secret talks in Miami the previous month between Kirill Dmitriev, the head of Russia’s sovereign wealth fund, and Witkoff and Kushner. Western security officials told the Journal that the Kremlin’s strategy, settled before Trump’s inauguration, was to go around the American national-security establishment and persuade the White House to see Russia not as a military threat but as a business opportunity, dangling rare-earth and energy deals worth billions.

Anne Applebaum, writing in The Atlantic on October 12, 2026, listed what else has been discussed: the Nord Stream gas pipelines, mineral mining in the Arctic, and the Russian state assets frozen in European banks. Even if none of these deals is ever signed, she argued, the fact that American negotiators keep discussing them tells the Kremlin it can keep fighting without paying for it.

Seen from Kyiv, the arithmetic is cruel. Ukraine’s grain sits in silos because Russia sinks the ships that would carry it. Russia’s oil assets, meanwhile, are being shopped to investors with friends in the White House, on terms that depend on Ukraine laying down its drones.

The man who always comes back to Moscow

There is a temptation to see all this as purely transactional, which would at least make it predictable. Applebaum argues it is something older.

Trump first travelled to Moscow in 1987, when it was still the Soviet capital. When he came home, he took out full-page newspaper advertisements attacking America’s alliances in Europe and Asia. Over nearly forty years since, Applebaum observes, he has changed his mind on abortion, on cryptocurrency and on which party he belongs to, but never on his dislike of America’s democratic allies or his preference for Moscow.

I am a Canadian, so I will add one observation that Applebaum makes in passing and that ought to sting south of the border. Canada is a far more reliable energy partner to the United States than Russia will ever be. Frum notes that New England already draws about 154,000 barrels of diesel a day from Canada, much of it from the refinery in Saint John, New Brunswick, because it is cheaper than shipping fuel up from the Gulf Coast. The entire first Russian shipment amounts to about two weeks of what Canada already supplies. If an American president wanted cheaper diesel and a dependable supplier, he could have called Ottawa. He called Moscow.

The summit at Doral

There is one more date on the calendar that explains a good deal. The United States is hosting the G20 in December 2026 at Doral, a Trump-owned golf resort just outside Miami, and Trump has invited Putin, overruling objections within his own party. As Jonathan Lemire and Simon Shuster reported for The Atlantic on October 12, 2026, if Putin accepts, it would be his first multilateral summit in a Western country since the full-scale invasion. Zelensky has said he will attend and hopes to meet Putin there.

Trump, by the account of one of his advisers, would like both sides to arrive at Doral ready to make a deal, so that he can preside over a peacemaking summit. He was passed over for the Nobel Peace Prize again in early October 2026. Read in that light, the pressure on Kyiv this month looks less like energy policy and more like stage management: a Ukraine that has stopped hitting refineries, and a Putin whose economy has been given some air, would make for a tidier photograph in December. The Kremlin, for its part, told reporters after the Friday call that Putin had declined to commit to resuming peace talks at all.

Europe answers

Now the other half of the story, because this series is not only an account of betrayals.

In the days after the diesel announcement, France’s president, Emmanuel Macron, and the President of the European Commission, Ursula von der Leyen, issued statements of support for Zelensky. On Monday, October 12, 2026, EU foreign ministers meeting in Luxembourg heard from Andrii Sybiha directly. Kaja Kallas, the EU’s top diplomat, chose her words with care as she went in, as Michael D. Shear and Jeanna Smialek reported for the New York Times on October 13, 2026. “The U.S. is our biggest ally, but our message is clear,” she said. “If we want to end this war, the only way we can do this is by putting more pressure on Russia.” Estonia’s foreign minister, Margus Tsahkna, was blunter: “We have had this kind of turbulence and also pressure from President Trump’s side to Ukraine before. So we need to focus on what we can do.”

The same day, at the Warsaw Security Forum, two speeches set out a European answer more clearly than any communiqué.

The first came from Donald Tusk, Poland’s prime minister. As Marc Santora and Steven Erlanger reported for the New York Times on October 12, 2026, Tusk said that anyone who wanted to weaken Ukraine’s defence, whether from treason, stupidity or money, was acting against his own nation’s security and sovereignty. He named no one. He did not need to.

The second came from an American. General Alexus Grynkewich of the U.S. Air Force, NATO’s Supreme Allied Commander in Europe, made the unusual decision to disclose the core of the alliance’s classified war plan, which he called its theory of victory. It has three stages: block an invasion at the border, blunt its momentum from air, sea and land, and, if the attack continues, break the aggressor’s supply lines, communications and command. He wanted, he said, to assure the alliance’s billion citizens that NATO knew what it would have to do and how it would do it.

The general made the point that NATO’s new doctrine draws directly on what Ukraine has taught the world: precision weapons combined with what he called affordable mass, large numbers of drones, autonomous systems and electronic-warfare assets built for a long war of attrition. In other words, the alliance’s plan for defending Europe is built on Ukraine’s own innovations, including the drone methods behind the refinery campaign Washington now wants stopped.

The contrast was hard to miss. America’s highest-ranking soldier in Europe stood in Warsaw promising that any attack on an ally would be made futile. Back in Washington, the President was telling the country that is actually under attack to replace its leader.

The European warnings were not abstract. The same day, Michal Koudelka, head of the Czech intelligence service, told his parliament that Russia could escalate within months rather than years to something riskier, possibly including limited military action against a NATO state. Russian sabotage, arson and cyberattacks across Europe are aimed, NATO says, at rattling Europe’s resolve and cutting the supply lines that feed Ukraine.

And Congress, at least, has not entirely gone along. A bipartisan group of senators, including the Republicans Lisa Murkowski, Susan Collins, Thom Tillis and John Curtis, demanded that the administration reverse course on October 11, 2026.

What Europe must also own

It would be a poor kind of solidarity to pretend Europe’s hands are clean. Zelensky, in the same statement in which he condemned the diesel deal, pointed to a European decision to lift sanctions on the Russian oligarchs Alisher Usmanov and Mikhail Fridman, as LIGA.net reported. Hungary and Slovakia have kept buying Russian oil, and, by Zelensky’s account in September 2025, complained to Trump about Ukraine’s strikes on Russian energy infrastructure. The sanctions regime has been leaking on both sides of the Atlantic. Trump simply opened the largest valve. He said as much himself on Monday, October 12, 2026, posting that the European Union had paid Russia “250 Billion Dollars for Energy.” The jab was not baseless; Europe still buys some Russian energy, though it has sharply cut its dependence. But a leak elsewhere is a poor argument for opening another.

The lesson Ukraine is drawing

Applebaum ends her essay with the lesson she thinks America’s allies will draw: do not rely on the United States. She adds a second: democracies can cooperate without the American president. French intelligence, German weapons, and aid packages from Scandinavia, Poland and elsewhere will help Ukraine keep fighting, and probably keep striking Russian refineries.

Ukraine drew that lesson some time ago. It drew it in March 2025, when the last energy ceasefire collapsed within days. It drew it again on February 28, 2025, in the Oval Office. It is drawing it now, from a golf club in Florida, a sanctions licence that runs to 2027, and a $20 billion sale being negotiated alongside a peace process.

It is also drawing a harder one. A Ukrainian official close to the negotiations told Lemire and Shuster that Russia’s war economy, rebuilt around Putin’s war machine, is unlikely to implode under drones and sanctions alone. Kyiv, the official said, needs to “change our strategy, stop fooling ourselves” and find a way out of the war with Europe’s help, which will probably mean a new emphasis on negotiation. Zelensky has already said he will hold an election if Putin agrees to a 60-day ceasefire. That is not capitulation. It is a country under fire thinking clearly about the long game, and it deserves to be heard in its own words rather than through Washington’s.

What Ukraine has not done, across three essays’ worth of evidence, is agree to be traded. It has offered reciprocity. It has offered a package ceasefire covering energy, ports and the Black Sea. It has held off its drones for a night to show good faith. And it has said, through its foreign minister, the one sentence a country under invasion should never have to explain: it will accept a ceasefire that is mutual, and nothing that is one-sided.

That is where the foundation of this series ends, and where the weekly reports begin. Each week, I will follow four things from Kyiv’s side: whether Russia keeps striking Ukraine’s energy and ports during any so-called ceasefire; whether Washington’s deals with Moscow advance; whether Europe, and Canada, fill the gaps Washington leaves; and what is being arranged for Doral in December. If the past three days are any guide, there will be plenty to report.

Postscript, Wednesday, October 14, 2026

As this essay was being readied for publication, the week answered its closing line. Overnight, Russia fired 39 ballistic missiles at Ukraine, the largest such salvo of the war by a New York Times count based on Ukrainian Air Force figures, and hit all three of Kyiv’s combined heat-and-power plants. Some 600,000 people in the capital lost electricity. The same night, as Constant Méheut reported for the New York Times on October 14, 2026, Ukraine’s military discovered that Starshield, the Pentagon-funded military version of Starlink that guides its drones and connects its vehicles at the front, had been restricted without warning for any terminal moving faster than 100 miles an hour. Starlink does not operate inside Russia, so the restriction has nothing to do with refineries. It hobbles Ukraine on its own soil. Neither SpaceX nor the Pentagon, which pays for the service, responded to requests for comment. Kyiv, meanwhile, has kept its drones away from Russian refineries, as Washington demanded. “Apparently,” a senior Ukrainian military officer had told Jonathan Lemire and Simon Shuster of The Atlantic days earlier, “there’s always another level below rock bottom.”
