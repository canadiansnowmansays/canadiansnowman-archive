Canadian Snowman’s Guide to the Most Important Meeting Nobody Expects to Matter

The twenty-one-mile needle’s eye that rewrote the entire summit agenda.

On February 28, 2026, the United States and Israel launched Operation Epic Fury — an air war against Iran that killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, shattered the country’s military infrastructure, and, in a detail that would prove rather more consequential than anyone in Washington appears to have anticipated, effectively shut the Strait of Hormuz.

The summit with China was five weeks away.

I have been chronicling this war in a separate series — the Iran Crisis war diary — and I will not rehearse its full arc here. But the collision between Epic Fury and the Beijing summit is one of those moments when two stories that appeared to be running on parallel tracks suddenly converge, and the resulting wreckage tells you more about both stories than either could tell you alone. The war did not merely complicate the summit. It rewrote the agenda, reversed the leverage, and turned what was supposed to be a choreographed exercise in mutual flattery into something considerably more dangerous: a meeting between two leaders, one of whom had just launched a war that made him weaker at the very table he was supposed to dominate.

The irony is structural, not incidental. And it begins with the strait.

The Strait of Hormuz is twenty-one miles wide at its narrowest point. Through this needle’s eye passes roughly one-fifth of the world’s seaborne oil, a substantial share of the global liquefied natural gas supply, and approximately thirty percent of internationally traded fertilizer. Before February 28, it was open. After February 28, it was not. The closure has been, in the assessment of the International Energy Agency’s Fatih Birol, the worst energy shock the world has ever seen — more severe than the oil crises of the 1970s, the Iran-Iraq war, and Iraq’s invasion of Kuwait combined.

For China, the strait’s closure was both a crisis and a lever. Roughly forty-five percent of China’s oil imports transit through Hormuz. A third of China’s total oil supply comes through it. Beijing was, in the most literal sense, watching its energy lifeline being crimped shut by an American war of choice — and doing so at the precise moment when the Americans were about to arrive in Beijing asking for cooperation on trade, rare earths, and fentanyl precursors.

The diplomatic absurdity of this position is difficult to overstate. The United States had started a war that was choking off energy supplies to the country it was about to visit, was simultaneously sanctioning Chinese companies for buying Iranian oil, had imposed a naval blockade that China called “dangerous and irresponsible,” and was now asking China to use its influence with Iran to reopen the strait that American bombs had helped close. This is the diplomatic equivalent of burning down your neighbour’s garage, then knocking on his front door to ask if you could borrow his lawnmower.

Beijing, to its considerable credit, did not say this out loud. What it did instead was more effective: it built strategic reserves, diversified its supply chains, and let the Americans stew in the consequences of their own war while maintaining just enough diplomatic warmth to keep the summit alive. China’s strategic petroleum reserves — approximately 1.4 billion barrels at the war’s start, enough to cover roughly 220 days of Middle Eastern imports — gave Beijing a cushion that most Asian economies did not have. South Korea, by comparison, had nine days of LNG reserves. The asymmetry was not lost on anyone.

But the strait was only the most visible piece of leverage the war delivered to Beijing. The less visible piece was, in some ways, more devastating.

Every precision-guided weapon the United States fired at Iran — every Tomahawk cruise missile, every JDAM, every Predator drone — required rare earth elements in its guidance systems. Neodymium for the magnets. Dysprosium for the heat resistance. Yttrium for the radar components. And China controls roughly seventy percent of global rare earth mining and ninety percent of separation and magnet manufacturing.

The arithmetic is brutal in its simplicity. Each missile fired made America more dependent on Chinese supply chains. Each sortie depleted inventories that could only be replenished with Chinese cooperation. The South China Morning Post reported — and American analysts did not dispute — that the United States had approximately two months of rare earth supply remaining for military needs. New Pentagon procurement rules banning Chinese-sourced rare earths were scheduled to take effect in 2027, but domestic alternatives would not be ready for years. The gap between the deadline and the capacity was, in the language of defence procurement, a “readiness shortfall.” In the language of ordinary English, it was a hole.

Kyle Chan, a rare earth expert at the Brookings Institution, put it plainly: the Iran war had made the rare earth problem “almost even more dire” because the question was now where the United States would source the specific elements needed for missile systems. The American Prospect noted that the country had entered a war with “a trillion-dollar military without enough bombs to fight for more than a week, and none of the critical components needed to make more.” Fortune reported that Epic Fury had already burned through $25 billion, with a supplemental budget request of $200 billion pending before Congress.

China did not need to threaten to cut off rare earth supplies. The mere existence of the dependency was sufficient. The rare earth controls Beijing had been toggling on and off throughout 2025 — tightening in January and February, easing slightly when diplomacy required a gesture — gave China what strategists call “escalation dominance without escalation.” The weapon existed. Everyone knew it existed. Nobody needed to fire it. The shadow was enough.

Axios published the definitive framing in mid-April: “China is the Iran war’s biggest winner. It never fired a shot.” The headline was reductive in the way that accurate headlines often are. China had indeed won — not through military action but through the patient accumulation of structural advantages that the United States, in its enthusiasm for kinetic solutions, had failed to account for. Every smart weapon expended in Iran had transferred a small quantum of leverage from Washington to Beijing, and the transfers were cumulative and irreversible in the short term.

Every Tomahawk fired in Iran made America more dependent on this. China didn’t even need to threaten — the dependency was the threat.

Into this shifting landscape walked Marco Rubio, the first sitting American Secretary of State to be personally sanctioned by the country he was supposed to visit.

The sanctions had been imposed in 2020, when Rubio was still a senator — once in retaliation for US measures on Chinese officials over the treatment of the Uyghur minority, and once in response to sanctions related to Hong Kong. They typically involved entry restrictions and asset freezes, which raised the obvious question of whether Rubio could legally set foot in China at all.

Beijing handled this with the kind of elegant diplomatic judo that the Chinese foreign ministry deploys when it wants to simultaneously solve a problem and remind everyone of the problem’s existence. The foreign ministry spokesperson said the sanctions “were aimed at Mr. Rubio’s words and deeds concerning China during his tenure in the United States Senate” — a formulation that implied the sanctions did not apply to his current role without formally lifting them, and that simultaneously acknowledged and dismissed his hawkish history in a single sentence. It was the diplomatic equivalent of saying: we remember who you were, we see who you are, and we note the distance between the two.

Rubio’s response, when asked whether China would lift the sanctions for his visit, was characteristically terse: “We’ll find out when I go.” Sources reported that he had been reluctant to participate in the trip. Trade was Bessent’s portfolio, not his. And his entire political identity had been built on being the toughest China hawk in Washington — a brand that does not survive a smiling photograph in the Great Hall of the People without significant damage.

But the war in Iran had changed Rubio’s calculation. With Iran consuming the administration’s bandwidth and the Strait of Hormuz turning into the central foreign policy crisis of the year, Rubio could not afford to skip the most important bilateral meeting on the planet. His own State Department was pressing China to pressure Iran on reopening the strait. You cannot ask a country for help while refusing to show up at its door.

On March 16, the summit collapsed.

Not dramatically — nothing in diplomacy ever collapses dramatically, except in movies and memoirs written by people who wish their careers had been more exciting. It subsided. Trump told reporters he was delaying the trip by about a month because of the war. “Because of the war, I want to be here. I have to be here, I feel,” he said. China had not yet confirmed the postponement, which was itself a signal — the asymmetry between Trump’s public announcement and Beijing’s silence suggested that China had not been consulted so much as informed, and was not pleased about either the process or the implication.

The Brookings Institution’s Ryan Hass later noted that the postponement came “just as the United States and Chinese trade negotiators were finishing two days of intensive talks in Paris to help set up the summit.” The timing was, if not deliberately insulting, certainly not carefully managed. Staff preparation had been inadequate from the beginning — the “worst I’ve ever seen” assessment from February now looked less like an outlier and more like a diagnosis.

The visit had originally been scheduled for March 31 to April 2. It was rescheduled for May 14 and 15. The delay was officially about Iran. But the delay also served Beijing’s purposes. Every additional week that Epic Fury continued was another week of missile expenditure, another week of rare earth depletion, another week of Strait of Hormuz chaos that underscored China’s importance as both energy buyer and potential mediator. Time was not neutral. Time was working for Beijing.

Hugh Keenleyside would have recognized this dynamic immediately. The Canadian diplomat who spent decades navigating between great powers understood that the most dangerous moment in any imperial project is not the initial assertion of force — it is the moment when the costs of that assertion begin to compound. Empire, Keenleyside would remind his colleagues, is a business with very high operating expenses and a tendency to generate liabilities faster than assets. The United States had launched a war that was simultaneously depleting its arsenal, enriching its rival’s leverage, disrupting the global energy supply, and complicating the most important diplomatic meeting on its calendar. These were the operating expenses of empire, and they were coming due.

In the analytical framework I have been developing across these essays, the war’s impact on the summit maps with uncomfortable clarity. TACO — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — was operating at full throttle on both sides, but the war had rearranged who was doing what.

Before the war, the United States held most of the threatening cards: tariffs, export controls, Taiwan arms sales, technology restrictions. After the war, China held leverage it had not asked for and did not need to advertise: rare earth dependency, energy mediation, the ability to simply wait while America’s arsenal thinned and its diplomatic calendar frayed.

The accommodation had shifted too. Before the war, Trump’s visit to Beijing was a gesture of diplomatic symmetry — you come to my country, I come to yours, we demonstrate mutual respect. After the war, the visit had become something closer to a necessity for Trump. He needed Chinese cooperation on Iran. He needed rare earth supplies for his military. He needed a Boeing deal to demonstrate that his foreign policy could produce economic results. The accommodator, in other words, had become the petitioner.

The capitulation was being recalibrated. China was now in a position to offer less and demand more. Before the war, the deliverables were expected to be roughly symmetrical — soybeans for market access, Boeings for investment commitments. After the war, China could offer the same soybeans and Boeings while extracting concessions on Taiwan language, rare earth terms, and sanctions relief that would have been impossible in February.

And the obfuscation continued as before — perhaps even intensified. Trump claimed Iran’s military had been destroyed and the strait was open when neither statement was true. China expressed its desire for “stable and predictable” relations while building alternative supply chains designed to reduce its dependence on the American market. Both sides were telling stories about the relationship that served domestic purposes and bore diminishing resemblance to observable reality.

By late March, the situation had acquired a quality that students of great-power diplomacy will recognize: the feeling of events moving faster than the institutions designed to manage them. The war was not going according to the tidy narrative the White House had projected. The ceasefire announced in early April was fragile, the strait remained functionally closed, Iran was charging tolls in Chinese yuan for ships that were allowed through, and the US had imposed its own naval blockade of Iranian ports — which meant that the strait was now blocked from both ends, by different parties, for different reasons, with both claiming the other was responsible.

Daniel Shapiro, a former deputy assistant secretary of defence for the Middle East, warned that “Trump’s position and leverage at the summit is considerably weaker if he goes to Beijing with the war still unsettled.” This was, by May, the consensus assessment of virtually every serious analyst who studied the relationship: the war had not strengthened Trump’s hand. It had weakened it. The man who had launched Operation Epic Fury to project American power had instead demonstrated the limits of American power, the fragility of American supply chains, and the centrality of Chinese cooperation to American strategic objectives.

And Beijing was not merely waiting. While Washington burned through munitions and diplomatic capital, China was quietly assembling a legal and regulatory counter-arsenal — blocking rules to shield Chinese companies from American sanctions, new authorities to punish foreign businesses that complied with Western decoupling efforts, and the institutional infrastructure to wage a prolonged economic war on terms that favoured Chinese leverage. The war had not only shifted the balance of power. It had given Beijing time to build the tools it would need for whatever came next.

The invitation still stood. The calendar had been reset. The Beast was being loaded onto C-17 transports for the flight to Beijing. But the meeting that was coming was not the meeting that had been planned. The war had walked into the room and rearranged the furniture.
