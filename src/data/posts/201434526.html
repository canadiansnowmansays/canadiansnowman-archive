Stephen Gordon

June 10, 2026

There is a room in the White House basement that carries the weight of history the way old wood carries the smell of smoke. The John F. Kennedy Conference Room, inside the Situation Room complex, is where Barack Obama sat with Joe Biden and Hillary Clinton and watched Navy SEALs kill Osama bin Laden. It is where presidents have authorized the use of force, received intelligence about imminent threats, and made the kinds of decisions that fill the chapters of history books.

On July 17, 2025, around six o’clock in the evening, Donald Trump’s senior advisers filed into that room to discuss Jeffrey Epstein.

The president was not there. He didn’t need to be. The crisis was already beyond him.

I. What They Built

Before we get to the Situation Room, we need to understand how the administration got there. Because this was not a crisis that arrived from outside. It was one they constructed, brick by brick, over years, and then walked into. Maggie Haberman and Jonathan Swan, reporting for The New York Times from inside the most closely guarded rooms of the Trump White House, have now documented exactly how.

Kash Patel had spent the years before taking over the FBI promising his podcast audiences that a second Trump administration would release "everything" in the Epstein files. The government, he repeatedly claimed, was hiding Epstein's black book — a client list of powerful men — to protect the guilty. The FBI, he said, was "deliberately withholding names." On one podcast, he was blunt: "Put on your big boy pants and let us know who the paedophiles are."

Dan Bongino, the former Secret Service agent whose background lent an air of authority to his claims, asked his large MAGA audience: “What the hell are they hiding with Jeffrey Epstein?” The client list, he predicted, “is going to rock the political world. There’s a reason they’re hiding it.” The Washington swamp was “not telling you the truth.”

JD Vance had been equally unambiguous on the campaign trail: “We need to release the files. That is an important thing.” He and Donald Trump Jr. invoked the Epstein files as a campaign message, framing the hidden documents as proof that “powerful people” were concealing the truth from ordinary Americans. Tucker Carlson and Charlie Kirk each insisted the government must release everything. Elon Musk used his social media platform to repeatedly demand the client list.

Trump himself had been more careful, but not careful enough. On the Lex Fridman podcast in September 2024, asked about releasing the client list, he said he’d “certainly take a look at it” and that it “probably will be” made public. In private, he told Marjorie Taylor Greene that releasing Epstein material could hurt some of his friends. He called it all “fake news.” But his posture was overtaken by the frenzy his own allies had spent years stoking.

They had sold a product. Now they had to deliver it.

And there was nothing to deliver.

II. Bondi’s Binder

Conservative political commentator Rogan O'Handley, social media content creator Chaya Raichik and conservative political commentator Liz Wheeler hold up binders with a cover titled "The Epstein Files: Phase 1," with Jack Posobiec at the White House, Feb. 27, 2025, in Washington.

The first catastrophe was Pam Bondi’s.

On February 21, 2025, in a Fox News interview, the attorney general appeared to confirm what the MAGA base had always believed: that a client list existed, that it was sitting in Justice Department files, and that its release was imminent. “It’s sitting on my desk right now to review,” she said.

Six days later, the White House Communications Office organized a gathering in the Roosevelt Room — a who’s who of online MAGA influencers. Mike Cernovich. Liz Wheeler. Collin Rugg. DC Draino. The president brought them to the Oval Office and gave them custom challenge coins. One of them, before things went wrong, would remark that it was “the best day of my life.”

Then Bondi walked in carrying boxes. Her staff began distributing binders. “Watch this,” someone on her team said. “This is cool. This is going to be epic.”

Other officials in the room, who had no idea what was in the binders, began frantically flipping through pages to see if Trump’s name appeared. A few pages in, right in the middle of a page, there it was.

Keir Starmer, the British prime minister, was at the White House that day. If news broke that the Epstein files were being released before Trump’s press conference with Starmer, the journalists would ask about nothing else.

An aide hustled the influencers out, telling them the binders were embargoed until after the press conference. On the way out, they snapped selfies in front of the White House holding their binders and posted them to social media. They had created a wave of anticipation for contents that turned out to be almost entirely recycled — flight logs, contact lists, material from Epstein’s residences after his 2019 arrest, nearly all of it previously public.

Bondi had simultaneously oversold and trivialized the Epstein files. She had made the influencers feel like dupes. And she had promised there was more coming.

There wasn’t.

III. The Situation Room, July 17

By the time Trump’s inner circle gathered in the Kennedy Conference Room on July 17, the administration was in full crisis mode — a fact they were determined to keep from public view.

JD Vance took the seat at the head of the table. “This is a huge problem,” he told the group. Around him: Chief of Staff Susie Wiles. White House Counsel David Warrington. Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt. Deputy Chief of Staff Taylor Budowich. Communications Director Steven Cheung. Deputy Chief of Staff James Blair. Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche. Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward Jr. Attorney General Pam Bondi and FBI Director Kash Patel on speakerphone.

The Wall Street Journal was about to publish a story the White House had spent days trying to kill. Trump had called News Corp. CEO Robert Thomson. He had called Rupert Murdoch. He had called Journal editor in chief Emma Tucker and practically shouted at her that she must “hate America,” threatening to file a lawsuit. None of it had worked.

Vance, who colleagues said appeared genuinely panicked, argued that the only path forward was full transparency. Release everything — including the unsubstantiated allegations about Trump. Get out ahead of the story, take credit for openness, rip the bandage off. Waiting would only produce months of damaging drips, each one renewing the cycle of suspicion and fury.

His arguments fell on skeptical ears.

Blair, a realist in the room, cut through the communications strategy discussion with characteristic bluntness: “With all due respect, the communications strategy of this group got us here. I don’t know that it’s going to get us out.”

Then the Journal story arrived. Cellphones are forbidden in the Situation Room. A staff member brought in printed copies. The group sat quietly reading about how Trump, along with many others, had contributed to a birthday book assembled by Ghislaine Maxwell for Epstein in 2003 — a book that included a card attributed to Trump depicting a hand-drawn nude woman, inscribed with an imagined dialogue between the two men about a “wonderful secret,” signed with what appeared to be Trump’s distinctive Sharpie signature in place of the woman’s pubic hair.

Wiles prepared a public denial. Trump posted it. Then he posted the Situation Room plan: calling for Attorney General Bondi to produce grand jury testimony, subject to court approval. He called it a “SCAM, perpetuated by the Democrats.”

What the post didn’t mention was that Blanche had told the group the court petition was almost certainly going to fail — and that was the point. If the judges refused, which Blanche predicted they would, the White House could shift blame onto the judiciary. Better still if the judges had been appointed by Democratic presidents.

The call for transparency was engineered to produce opacity. The appearance of wanting release was designed to guarantee the opposite.

What was notably absent from every Situation Room discussion, as Haberman and Swan observed, was any conversation about justice for the women. Many of them were minors when Epstein abused them. Not one meeting addressed how to give them a hearing, or a chance to tell their stories. These meetings, from first to last, were about one thing: how to handle the political fallout. How to find a way out.

IV. What the Files Contained

In June 2025, Blanche had briefed Trump directly on the state of the Epstein review. “We’ve gone through the files,” he told the president. “There’s not a lot there. A lot of child pornography — obviously we can’t put any of that out. There are some mentions of you, but nothing substantive.”

Most of Trump’s senior advisers had rejected the idea of releasing the FBI’s raw interview notes — the 302 forms, the unverified records of witness accounts, some involving victims. The primary concern was not protecting the victims. It was protecting the president.

The plan, developed over weeks, was to build a public-facing, searchable website and release everything in one overwhelming flood. The theory was that burying the MAGA base in millions of documents would satisfy the demand for transparency while making any specific damaging allegation difficult to find or amplify.

Then, in an August 13 Situation Room meeting, an official raised the subject of a disturbing, uncorroborated accusation from unsealed 2015 defamation case filings — an allegation by another Epstein victim, Sarah Ransome, involving a secondhand claim about Trump and a woman named Jen in Epstein’s network. The allegation was specific. It was graphic. An administration official had already run a test search on the private version of the planned website. The material was among the first items to appear.

The vice president thought they should release it anyway. “I think we should put it out,” Vance said. “It would cause people to say we’re going further than we need to.” Wiles quickly told the group the president would not be OK with it.

White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles and her deputy, James Blair, were key members of Trump’s team discussing the crisis. “The communications strategy of this group got us here,” Blair told the others. “I don’t know that it’s going to get us out.” Credit...Haiyun Jiang/The New York Times

One official would later describe the experience of discussing the allegations in the White House Situation Room as “surreal.”

The full public website — the one they had conceived as their escape hatch — was never released to the public.

V. Bongino’s Reckoning

Dan Bongino had built his entire public identity on the promise that the FBI under Trump would be different. Transparent. Honest. Willing to expose what the deep state had hidden.

He had given up millions of dollars in podcast revenue. He had left the audience he had spent years cultivating. He had joined an institution he had spent years attacking, because he believed this time would be different.

When the July 7 memo went out — the unsigned, one-and-a-half-page statement saying there was no client list, that Epstein’s death was a suicide, that the case was closed — Bongino showed up to the daily Justice Department meeting in a volcanic mood. The moment he entered the room, he turned on Bondi.

“You fucked this thing up from the start,” he told her. “The way you’ve been talking about this — that dumb fucking charade with the Epstein files, the ‘They’re on my desk’ nonsense, all the promises to the folks out there.”

In a Fox News interview, Bondi seemed to confirm the existence of an Epstein client list, which the MAGA base believed was hidden in the files. But no such list was found.Credit...Evan Vucci/Associated Press

Two days later, in a meeting with Wiles, Bongino was accused of leaking a sensitive story about Epstein and Trump to ABC News. He offered $100,000 in cash, on the spot, to anyone who could get the reporter to confirm it on speakerphone.

Wiles tried to rally the group. “We all got ourselves into this —”

Bongino cut her off. “No, no, no, no, no. We didn’t get ourselves into anything. I warned you guys about this the whole time, and you ignored me. And exactly what I said was going to happen happened.”

He told Wiles he was out. Then he walked out of the Situation Room, onto West Executive Avenue, climbed into Patel’s armored SUV, and directed the driver to FBI headquarters.

His friends hoped he would resign — a public act of protest that would have made him a martyr. White House advisers intervened. If Bongino quit over Epstein and went public, it would severely damage the president. He stayed.

In private, as Haberman and Swan reported, he told a confidant what he really thought: “This is going to be President Trump’s Iran-Contra.”

VI. What the Polling Said

Iran-Contra didn’t bring down Ronald Reagan. It damaged him. It defined his second term. It established, permanently, that the administration had lied — and that the American public had been sophisticated enough to notice.

In late March 2026, Tony Fabrizio — the president’s own chief pollster — circulated an internal memo to roughly a dozen Trump advisers summarizing two nights of focus groups. The memo listed the Epstein files as the sixth most important issue raised by participants, behind inflation, the economy, foreign policy, immigration and health care — but ahead of data centers, military issues, crime, safety, and being “pro-working class.”

In the section on key takeaways, Fabrizio’s memo stated: “There is also a consistent mention of the Epstein files, which came up in every group and is a real negative with some of these voters.”

Every group.

Not the fringe. Not the conspiracy theorists. Not the noisy online minority that Wiles and Blair had dismissed as irrelevant while insisting, in those early months, that Republican voters didn’t care. Every group.

The pages ultimately released would reference Trump, his family, and Mar-a-Lago more than 38,000 times. Flight records showed at least eight trips on Epstein’s private jet between 1993 and 1996. In January 2024, Trump had declared he had never been on the plane.

“There are a lot of questions about it,” he told reporters in February 2026. “But nothing on me.”

VII. The Scorecard

What they promised: The client list. Full transparency. The end of the deep state cover-up.

What they delivered: A one-and-a-half-page unsigned memo. Surveillance footage with a missing minute. A stripped-down document dump. A private Maxwell interview whose transcript was never released. Maxwell quietly moved to a minimum-security camp in Texas — with no explanation — which only deepened the suspicion.

What they said about each other: Bongino called Bondi’s handling a catastrophe. Patel and Bongino told Wiles that Bondi needed to resign. Bongino privately referred to the attorney general as “Blondie” — echoing Laura Loomer’s derisive nickname — and told a confidant she had “fucked the whole thing up.” Wiles dismissed Vance to colleagues as “a major conspiracy theorist.” Blair told the Situation Room that the communications strategy had created the problem.

What they knew: That the files mentioned Trump more than 38,000 times. That he had flown on Epstein’s jet at least eight times. That the birthday book existed. That the unsubstantiated allegations existed. That releasing everything would be damaging, and suppressing everything was impossible.

What they chose: Neither. Both. The appearance of one while engineering the other.

What Bongino gave up: Millions in podcast revenue, his audience, his credibility with the base he had built, and his sense of himself as someone who told the truth.

What he got in return: The chance to watch the story he had promised to expose become the story that consumed the administration he had joined to expose it.

VIII. The Asimov Frame

Isaac Asimov’s First Law of Robotics held that a robot may not injure a human being. The law assumed that harm would come from external threats — enemies, accidents, malice from outside the system.

It did not account for a system that injures itself.

The Epstein crisis was not visited upon the Trump administration by its enemies. It was built, over years, by the men and women who are now sitting in the Situation Room trying to contain it. Patel’s podcasts. Bongino’s promises. Bondi’s desk. Vance’s campaign speeches. Trump’s own insistence that the files would be released, right up until the moment he needed them buried.

They created the demand. They could not meet it. And now they are in the room where they killed bin Laden, arguing about how to make a dead pedophile disappear.

He won’t disappear.

Fabrizio’s focus groups told them so. Every group.

The president who broke every institution, redirected the federal government against his enemies, and brought the world’s richest men to the Oval Office bearing tribute has discovered the one thing his power cannot do.

He cannot make Jeffrey Epstein stop mattering.

A Canadian Observation

There is a phrase in Canadian political culture — “les affaires” — used to describe the inevitable moment when a government’s accumulated sins catch up with it. Not a single scandal but a pattern. Not one bad decision but the weight of many, piling up until the structure cracks.

The Epstein files are Trump’s les affaires.

What makes them different from every other crisis is that they are not, at their core, about politics. They are about children. They are about a network of powerful men who used money, access, and the mechanics of elite society to abuse girls, and about the decades of institutional failure — across administrations, across parties, across the full breadth of American power — that allowed it to continue.

The Situation Room meetings are extraordinary. The binder debacle is darkly comic. Bongino’s eruption is almost Shakespearean in its self-destructive clarity.

But none of it is funny. Not really.

Because somewhere beneath the crisis management and the communications strategy and the argument about nipples in the White House bunker, there are the women. There are the girls who were thirteen. There is Virginia Giuffre, who died by suicide in April 2025, three months after Trump returned to power, having spent years telling her story to a world that found it inconvenient.

The Situation Room was not repurposed to protect them.

It never was.
