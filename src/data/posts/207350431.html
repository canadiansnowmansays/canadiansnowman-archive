This part is shorter than the others in this series, deliberately. Some things are better counted than argued.

One note before the list: these deaths are not all the same kind of event, and treating them as if they were would be its own kind of dishonesty. I have marked each entry by category — a fatal shooting during an active enforcement encounter is not the same thing as a death from a medical episode in custody, and neither is the same as a death a medical examiner has ruled a homicide. The categories matter. What ties these entries together is not that they are identical. It is that in every category, across every entry below, the government’s first public account has either gone unverified for months or has been directly contradicted once independent evidence emerged.

January 2026 alone, nine deaths tied to immigration enforcement:

  • Renee Nicole Good, 37, a mother of three and a Minneapolis resident, shot and killed by an ICE agent on January 7. A U.S. citizen. [Fatal shooting, active encounter — government account contradicted by video.]

  • Alex Jeffrey Pretti, 37, a VA nurse, shot ten times in five seconds by CBP officers on January 24. A U.S. citizen. [Fatal shooting, active encounter — government account contradicted by video.]

  • Geraldo Lunas Campos, 55, a father from Cuba, died January 3 at Camp East Montana in El Paso, Texas. ICE said he became disruptive and was placed in segregation, then said he had tried to harm himself. A medical examiner ruled the death a homicide. [In-custody death, ruled a homicide — government account changed twice.]

  • Luis Gustavo Núñez Caceres, 42, a son and brother from Honduras, died January 5 at the Joe Corley Processing facility in Conroe, Texas. [In-custody death — cause reported as a medical complication; no independent review made public.]

  • Luis Beltran Yanez Cruz, 68, a father from Honduras who had lived in the United States for 26 years, died January 6 at a facility in Calexico, California. [In-custody death — cause reported as a medical complication; no independent review made public.]

  • Parady La, 46, a father from Cambodia who came to the United States at age two, died January 9 at the Federal Detention Center in Philadelphia. [In-custody death — cause reported as a medical complication; no independent review made public.]

  • Heber Sánchez Domínguez, 34, a husband and father from Mexico, died January 14 at a facility in Lovejoy, Georgia. [In-custody death — cause reported as a medical complication; no independent review made public.]

  • Two further detention deaths recorded in January, bringing the month’s total in ICE custody to at least six. [In-custody deaths — details limited in public reporting.]

Before January, going back to New Year’s Eve:

  • Keith Porter, 43, a father of two daughters, shot dead by an off-duty ICE agent in a Los Angeles apartment complex on December 31, for firing a gun into the air to celebrate the new year. DHS called him an active shooter. No charges have been filed. [Fatal shooting, off-duty encounter — no video, contested account, unresolved.]

Earlier still, kept out of public view for a year:

  • Ruben Ray Martinez, shot and killed by ICE agents during a traffic stop in Texas in March 2025. A U.S. citizen. His death did not become publicly known until February 2026 — eleven months later, and only after Minneapolis had already made these shootings a national story. [Fatal shooting, active encounter — concealed from public record for nearly a year; details of DHS’s account remain limited even now.]

  • Silverio Villegas-Gonzalez, a Mexican immigrant shot and killed in Chicago in October 2025 as he drove away from officers. [Fatal shooting, active encounter — details limited in public reporting.]

Since Minneapolis, and continuing:

  • At least thirteen ICE and CBP shootings recorded between September 2025 and February 2026, four of them fatal, spanning suspected criminals, immigrants without legal status, and American citizens. [Mixed — shootings during arrests, chases, and standoffs; circumstances vary by case and are not aggregated here beyond the count itself.]

  • Julio Sosa-Celis, shot and wounded by ICE agent Christian Castro after a chase — the case in which federal prosecutors’ own charges collapsed and two agents are now under investigation for lying under oath.   • Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, shot and killed in Houston on July 7 — the shooting that opened this series. Within days, three eyewitnesses and the Harris County Medical Examiner had already contradicted the government’s account, which is more scrutiny than the Martinez case received in eleven months. [Fatal shooting, active encounter — government account disputed by witnesses; death ruled a homicide.]

  • Joan Sebastian Guerrero, 26, a Colombian man shot and killed by an ICE Enforcement and Removal Operations officer in Biddeford, Maine, on July 13 — six days after Araujo. DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin told Sen. Angus King the man had “weaponized” his vehicle, the same word used in Good’s, Pretti’s, and Araujo’s cases, and initially told King the man was the target of the warrant being executed before correcting himself the same day. [Fatal shooting, active encounter — government account changed within hours; no body camera footage exists.]

By the New York Times’s running count, federal agents have now fired on at least 22 people since enforcement operations intensified last year. Six have died. Three were American citizens.

Across 2025, the backdrop against which all of this occurred: 32 deaths in ICE custody, a two-decade high, before 2026 had even begun. [In-custody deaths, prior year — cited here as context, not itemized individually.]

I am not going to editorialize much on this list, because the list does the work. But I will say what the list makes plain, and what seven parts of reporting have been circling since Part I: this was never a story about two people, and it was never really a story about three. Good and Pretti are the two names that broke through, the two deaths that produced protests, congressional hearings, a fired cabinet secretary, and the series you are reading. Martinez is the third American citizen killed by these agencies — and the one whose death the government simply did not disclose for nearly a year, until Minneapolis had already made the subject impossible to bury quietly. That gap between three months and eleven months, between Good's death making headlines within hours and Martinez's death surfacing eleven months after the fact, is itself a data point about which deaths this administration expects the public to notice and which ones it has reason to hope will pass unremarked.

Six people died in ICE detention in a single month and the country barely absorbed it before Minneapolis happened seventeen days later and swallowed all the attention whole. A man was shot for celebrating a new year with a gun fired into open sky, a thing tens of thousands of Americans do every December 31st, and no charges have followed. A medical examiner called a death a homicide and the agency’s own account of it changed twice. None of these people are less dead for being less well known.

This is what a body count looks like when an institution has decided, consistently, that its own account of events should be taken as final. Each name on this list arrived with a government explanation attached — disruptive behavior, active shooter, self-defense, weaponized vehicle — and in every case where independent evidence has actually surfaced to test that explanation, the explanation has not survived contact with it.

Notice, too, how the explanations repeat. Good was accused of ramming a vehicle at an officer. Salgado Araujo, six months and one continent of news cycles later, was accused of the same thing, in nearly the same words. Lunas Campos’s death was first attributed to disruptive behavior, then, when that did not hold, to an attempt at self-harm. An institution does not need to lie about every individual case to produce a body count like this one. It only needs a small number of templates, applied consistently, and a public attention span short enough that few people notice the templates being reused. That is not a conspiracy. It is simply what happens when the first draft of an event is also, functionally, the last draft anyone in power intends to revise.

This ledger counts the dead, because death is the harm that cannot be argued away or quietly settled later. But it is not the only harm, and a ledger of fatalities alone risks implying it is. At Blanche’s own confirmation hearing, Sen. Alex Padilla described a case that belongs nowhere in the count above precisely because the man survived: an elderly Minnesotan pulled from his home by federal agents in his underwear, in weather ten degrees below zero, with no apparent check beforehand that they had the right person. Nobody died. Nothing about that outcome was owed to caution on the agents’ part. The distance between that encounter and Renee Good’s was not principle. It was luck — whichever way a moment happened to break. A ledger built only from deaths cannot show how many other encounters sat that close to the same line and simply didn’t cross it.

I keep this ledger not to flatten these people into a statistic — each name above had a family, a job, a reason to still be alive — but because the individual essays in this series, mine included, risk letting Good and Pretti stand in for everyone, when the honest accounting requires all of the names, together, in one place, at least once.
