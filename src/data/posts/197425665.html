FBI Director Kash Patel testifying before the Senate Appropriations Subcommittee on Commerce, Justice, Science, and Related Agencies, May 12, 2026. He held up a laminated sheet of crime statistics each time his conduct was questioned. (C-SPAN)

On Tuesday afternoon, the Director of the Federal Bureau of Investigation sat before the Senate Appropriations Subcommittee on Commerce, Justice, Science, and Related Agencies to request $12.53 billion in taxpayer funding — a 17.8 percent increase over the prior year — and instead delivered a master class in the very behaviour that has made him the subject of this series.

Kash Patel came to the hearing armed. Not with a budget — the actual numbers were almost beside the point — but with a single laminated sheet of statistics. A 20-point drop in homicides. 44,000 violent offender arrests. 6,300 missing children located. Eight of the FBI’s top ten most wanted fugitives apprehended. He held the sheet up for the cameras more than once. He read from it when challenged. He brandished it like a talisman, which is precisely what it was: not evidence of competence but a shield against accountability. It is a document designed to end conversations, not start them.

It did not work.

The Opening Prosecution

Senator Chris Van Hollen of Maryland, the subcommittee’s ranking Democrat, had clearly come prepared to use this hearing as something other than a budget review. His opening statement was five minutes of controlled demolition.

“Director Patel, I do not care one bit about your private life,” Van Hollen began, establishing a framework that would prove important. “I don’t care about what you do on your own time and own dime — until it interferes with your public responsibilities.”

Then the blade: “These reports about your conduct, including reports of being so drunk and hung over that your staff had to force entry into your home, are extremely alarming.”

Van Hollen was referring to reporting by The Atlantic — reporting that Patel has sued over and denied, and that The Atlantic has said it stands by. The senator was careful in his framing. He said “if true.” He gave Patel every opportunity to deny, explain, or rebut. What he got instead was something far more revealing than any denial.

(Van Hollen / Patel): The hearing's defining image: Senator Van Hollen asks for the third time whether the FBI Director knows it is a crime to lie to Congress. Patel's expression tells the story the stats sheet cannot. (C-SPAN)

But first, Van Hollen laid out the full indictment. Not just the drinking allegations. The political firing of career agents, including personnel from a counterintelligence unit monitoring threats from Iran — fired, according to multiple reports, for their role in investigations related to Donald Trump. The weaponization of FBI investigative resources against journalists. The account of decorated Special Agent Ryan Driscoll, who has publicly stated that Patel made clear his future at the Bureau was tied to his political allegiance to the President.

“You are asking for more resources,” Van Hollen said, “at a time when you are misspending valuable resources on political revenge instead of focusing on defending our national security.”

It was a comprehensive brief. It was also, as we shall see, merely the overture.

The Stats Sheet Defence

Patel’s response to all of this was the laminated sheet.

Readers of this series will recognise the move. It is textbook TACO — specifically the Obfuscate quadrant, in which the subject responds to a question about behaviour by answering a question about outcomes. You asked about the drinking? Here are arrest statistics. You asked about the purge of Iran experts? Here are fentanyl seizures. You asked about journalists being investigated? Here are missing children found.

The sheet is not irrelevant. The crime statistics are real and, if accurately attributed, genuinely impressive. The FBI’s workforce — the 38,000 men and women Patel invoked in his opening — are clearly doing extraordinary work. But that is precisely the point Van Hollen was making and that Patel kept dodging: the question is not whether the FBI’s career professionals are competent. The question is whether their director is fit to lead them.

A hospital can have excellent surgeons and still have an impaired chief of staff. The surgeries do not disprove the impairment.

Senator Patty Murray (D-WA), Vice Chair of the Appropriations Committee, delivering the hearing's sharpest line: "If you really want to pop bottles in a locker room, stick to podcasting. Leave law and order to people who really care about justice." (C-SPAN)

Senator Patty Murray of Washington made this distinction with characteristic bluntness. After raising the branded bourbon Patel has been reported to distribute, the Olympic hockey locker room celebration in Milan, and the broader pattern of conduct, Murray delivered the line of the hearing:

“If you really want to pop bottles in a locker room, stick to podcasting. Leave law and order to people who really care about justice.”

Patel held up the stats sheet again. Murray was unimpressed. “I appreciate the work of our FBI agents,” she said, “but serious leadership is a concern.”

The Iran Question

The firing of FBI personnel with Iran-related expertise — carried out shortly before the United States launched a war with Iran — has been one of the most consequential threads in this series. Tuesday’s hearing added a remarkable wrinkle.

Senator Chris Coons of Delaware asked directly: “Do you disagree that there were 10 Iran specialists dismissed right before the war began?”

“Yes,” Patel replied.

When Van Hollen returned to the question later, he pressed harder. Were the fired individuals Iran experts? “No,” Patel said flatly. Did the group include people involved in counter-espionage activities with respect to Iran?

“I don’t have the list in front of me.”

Read that again. The Director of the FBI fired a group of agents. When asked whether any of them had counter-espionage experience with Iran — a nation with which the United States is currently at war — he does not know. He did not check. He cannot say. He has the laminated stats sheet but not the names and specialties of the people he terminated from the nation’s premier law enforcement agency during wartime.

Van Hollen caught the dodge: “So you don’t know whether or not you fired people with counter-espionage experience with respect to Iran.”

Patel’s answer was revealing in a different way: “I terminated anyone and everyone that weaponized law enforcement.”

This is an admission dressed as a principle. He is saying the criterion for termination was not competence, not performance, not fitness — it was involvement in investigations that displeased the President. Whether those individuals also happened to be the Bureau’s Iran experts was, to Patel, immaterial. The mission didn’t matter. The loyalty test did.

The Polygraph Denial

Senator Murray raised the polygraphing of FBI employees — a practice documented by multiple news outlets, in which dozens of Bureau personnel have reportedly been subjected to polygraph examinations to determine whether they shared unflattering information about the director. Patel’s response was categorical: “We have not done so.”

He did not say the polygraphs didn’t happen. He said the FBI — meaning, by implication, himself personally — did not direct them. “I don’t order any polygraph tests,” he insisted, attributing any such examinations to “career internal investigators.”

This is a distinction so fine it could shave with it. Career investigators polygraphing employees about leaks concerning the director, without the director’s knowledge or instruction? The alternative explanation — that Patel’s own internal affairs apparatus independently decided to hunt for people embarrassing the boss — is not the exculpatory narrative he seems to think it is. It is arguably worse.

The Softball Innings

Senator Lindsey Graham (R-SC), whose questioning focused on fentanyl seizures, the Maduro extradition, and whether the DEA should be abolished — a representative sample of the Republican side of a hearing that functioned as two entirely different proceedings in the same room. (C-SPAN)

It would be unfair not to note what the hearing looked like from the other side of the aisle. Republican senators treated Patel less like a witness and more like a visiting dignitary.

Senator Lindsey Graham asked whether the DEA should be abolished — a question designed to elicit a triumphant no. Senator Katie Britt of Alabama praised the murder rate decline effusively and asked Patel to expand on his budget request, which is a bit like asking a restaurant to describe its own food. Senator Bill Hagerty of Tennessee spent his entire allotment celebrating the Memphis Safe Task Force, a joint federal-local crime initiative that does, by the numbers, appear to have been remarkably effective.

The contrast was not subtle. Democrats asked about drinking, firings, polygraphs, and the targeting of journalists. Republicans asked about fentanyl seizures and World Cup security. There are two hearings happening in the same room, two FBI directors being described, and two entirely different relationships with the concept of oversight.

This is not a commentary on which party is right. It is an observation about what oversight looks like when it becomes optional — when a committee divides not on questions of emphasis but on whether questions should be asked at all.

The Meltdown

The hearing’s final minutes were unlike anything I have seen in years of watching congressional testimony.

Van Hollen returned for a closing round. He began by noting that Patel, in his earlier response, had made “a couple of provably false statements” — specifically the claim that Van Hollen had been “slinging margaritas” with Kilmar Abrego Garcia during the senator’s visit to El Salvador, where the immigrant had been sent to a high-security prison by the Trump administration in violation of a court order.

“Coming from the mouth of the FBI director, to make provably false statements in a hearing like this is extremely troubling,” Van Hollen said.

What followed was an exchange that should be studied in every political science course in the country.

Senator Chris Van Hollen (D-MD), ranking member of the subcommittee, pressing Patel on whether he knows it is a crime to lie to Congress. The director refused to answer the question three times. “You are a disgrace, Mr. Director,” Van Hollen concluded. (C-SPAN)

“Do you know that it is a crime to lie to Congress?” Van Hollen asked.

Patel did not answer the question. He answered a different question: “I have not lied to Congress.”

Van Hollen tried again. “Do you know, Mr. Director, that it is a crime to lie to Congress? Do you know that?”

“I do not lie to Congress,” Patel replied.

Van Hollen pressed a third time. “I didn’t ask you that.”

And here Patel abandoned any pretence of composure: “You want to correct your time in this session where you got steamrolled by the facts, so you can have a Twitter narrative so you can raise more money and spend more money on $7,000 bar tabs and I’m not going to give it to you.”

The $7,000 bar tab. Patel returned to this three separate times during the hearing — an apparent reference to expenses associated with a Van Hollen staff function. Each time he deployed it, it was in response to a question he did not want to answer. It is the Patel equivalent of a smoke grenade: it does not resolve the firefight, but it briefly obscures the field.

“You are a disgrace, Mr. Director,” Van Hollen said.

“I find it troubling that you lie to the American people every day,” Patel replied.

The Unanswered Question

What Patel never did — not once, across three attempts — was answer the actual question. He was not asked whether he had lied. He was asked whether he knew that lying to Congress is a crime. It is a yes-or-no question with exactly one correct answer, and that answer is yes. Every first-year law student knows it. Every FBI agent knows it. The director of the FBI refused to say it.

Why? One possibility is rage — that Patel was too angry to parse the distinction between the question asked and the question he wanted to answer. Another is strategy — that any acknowledgement of the statute might later be cited if his testimony is referred for investigation. A third is instinct — that for Patel, every question is an attack, and every attack requires a counter-attack, and the content of the counter-attack matters less than its velocity.

All three explanations lead to the same conclusion: this is not a man who should be running the Federal Bureau of Investigation.

The TACO Cycle, Live on C-SPAN

For those following the TACO framework through this series — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — Tuesday’s hearing was the entire cycle compressed into two hours.

Threaten: The margaritas accusation. The $7,000 bar tab, deployed three times. “I find it troubling that you lie to the American people every day.” Each one a warning shot at a senator exercising oversight — the unmistakable message being that scrutiny will be met with personal attacks from the director of America’s most powerful law enforcement agency.

Accommodate: The agreement to take an alcohol use disorders test. Pressed by Van Hollen, Patel said, “I’ll take any test you’re willing to take.” This is classic conditional accommodation — the appearance of cooperation wrapped in a dare, designed to shift the burden from the accused to the accuser.

Capitulate: Not yet. Not publicly. But the test agreement is a concession that would have been unthinkable in Parts I through VI. The wall is developing cracks.

Obfuscate: The laminated stats sheet. The pivot to crime statistics every time conduct was raised. The assertion that fired agents were not Iran experts without being able to name them or describe their specialties. The claim that polygraphs were ordered by career investigators, not by the director — a distinction without a difference, deployed as though it were a vindication.

What the Hearing Tells Us

From Toronto, 550 kilometres north of the room where this happened, the picture is both clearer and sadder than it probably appears from inside the Beltway.

The FBI’s rank and file are doing historic work. The statistics are real. The arrests are real. The children found and the fugitives captured and the fentanyl seized — all real, all important, all worthy of the resources being requested.

And the man sitting in the director’s chair responded to routine oversight by accusing a United States senator of drinking on the taxpayer dime, by three times refusing to acknowledge that it is a crime to lie to Congress, by claiming not to know whether he fired people with Iran expertise during a war with Iran, and by holding up a laminated sheet of other people’s accomplishments as though it were a character reference.

Senator Van Hollen asked whether Kash Patel knows it is a crime to lie to Congress.

The question remains unanswered.
