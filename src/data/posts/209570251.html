INTERNAL MEMORANDUM — NOT FOR PUBLIC RELEASE National Gallery of the Republic, Department of Conservation and Collections RE: Condition Report and Acquisition Recommendation — “The Republic, Gilded” (mixed media, ongoing, 2017–present) Prepared by: Office of the Chief Conservator, Dominion Division Date: August 9, 2026

To the Board of Trustees,

You asked me, three weeks ago, to file a condition report on the object informally known around this office as “the whole thing.” I want to be precise about scope, because precision is the only dignity left to us here. I am not reporting on a single artifact. I am reporting on a nation-sized accretion of gold leaf that has, over roughly a decade, metastasized across nearly every surface within the President’s reach, and which this Department is now being asked, with a straight face, to catalogue as though it were art rather than symptom.

I have done my best. What follows is the report, filed under protest, as all of them now are.

Provenance

The gilding did not begin in 2025; it merely graduated. Cataloguing the earlier strata, we find gold leaf troweled onto the walls of a Palm Beach estate in the 1980s at a documented cost of roughly seven million dollars, gold-plated sinks installed near a ballroom on the same property, and — the crown jewel of the ledger, so to speak — an entire yacht, purchased from the Sultan of Brunei in 1988 and rechristened the Trump Princess, whose couches, sinks, and ceilings were, per contemporaneous accounts, bathed in it. A Las Vegas hotel followed in 2008, its 406,000 square feet of facade coated in gold-infused glass, apparently on the theory that a building, too, can wear a tie clip. The collector has been remarkably, almost admirably consistent. What has changed is not the taste. It’s the custodian, who is now, on paper, the American taxpayer, and who did not sign up to underwrite anyone’s midlife glow-up at national scale.

A note on methodology before I proceed: conservators are trained to describe condition, not motive. I have tried to hold that line throughout this report. I have not always succeeded, and where I have failed, I have at least tried to fail in the passive voice, which is the closest thing to professionalism available to a person in my position.

Provenance, Continued

The early record credits much of the collector’s formative gilding instinct to his first wife, who served for a time as the family firm’s vice president of interior design and who, per later biographical accounts, is understood to have encouraged rather than tempered it. She was, notably, buried in a gold casket, a detail the Department includes not for prurience but because it establishes, beyond doubt, that the commitment was never really about décor. Décor ends at the property line. This did not. The collector went on to retain a designer best known for casino interiors to handle a Manhattan penthouse and an Atlantic City property, and, some years later, a group of private citizens — acting, it should be stressed, entirely without federal encouragement — erected an unauthorized twenty-two-foot statue of the collector at one of his Florida golf properties, finished, naturally, in gold, because subtlety has never once been permitted to survive contact with this file. I raise all of this only to establish that the current collection did not spring from nothing. It is the terminal stage of a private hobby that has, over four decades, quietly annexed the public balance sheet.

Description of Current State

The Oval Office presents as follows: thick gold frames stacked along the walls like ransom notes; gold vases and gold ice buckets arranged on the fireplace mantle, as though the room might at any moment be called upon to chill champagne; a ring of gold stars applied to the ceiling, presumably so the occupant need never look up and see an actual one; gold appliqués filling whatever gaps remained, on the evident theory that a blank wall is simply an insult that hasn’t been addressed yet. A personally designed golden challenge coin has been affixed, permanently, to the center of the office door, at approximately eye level, which the Department notes only because door-mounted coinage is not a recognized archival practice, and because it means visitors are now greeted, before the man himself, by his own face in metal. Curators visiting the space have independently described it as resembling, in turn, a Middle Eastern bazaar and an opulent roadside diner. The Department declines to adjudicate between these comparisons. Both were offered in good faith. Both are, in our professional opinion, insufficiently unkind.

Outdoors, the collection has expanded to include two nineteen-foot bronze statues near the Lincoln Memorial — Valor and Sacrifice, dedicated in 1951 as a postwar gift from Italy, previously regilded in a more restrained finish in 1971 — now recoated in 23.75-karat gold leaf at a cost of 5.1 million dollars, of which roughly 3.25 million went toward fifty-five pounds of gold. As reported in The Atlantic, the contract was awarded without competitive bidding to a single Maryland gilding firm, on the strength of an internal National Park Service assessment that the job could not credibly go to anyone else on the required timeline. Market comparables were, per the same reporting, not seriously pursued. Two companion statues nearby, collectively titled The Arts of Peace, remain in scaffolding, presumably awaiting their turn.

I want to flag a conservation concern here that is somewhat delicate. The statues were originally fire-gilded — a technique that yields a duller, deeper finish, the kind that ages gracefully rather than announcing itself. The current regilding does not appear to have used that method. The result reads, to the trained eye and the untrained eye alike, as considerably brighter than either the 1951 original or the 1971 restoration. Whether this was an aesthetic choice or a scheduling one, the Department is not equipped to say. We can say that from certain angles, at certain hours, the statues are visible from several blocks away, which was not previously true, and which some visitors have found disorienting given the statues’ proximity to Arlington National Cemetery.

Documented Acquisitions to the Permanent Collection (Gifted, External)

For completeness, the following items have entered the collection as gifts and merit brief notation: a golden trophy, presented by FIFA on the occasion of a new peace prize; a 24-karat gold base for a glass sculpture, presented by the Chief Executive of Apple; and a golden pager, presented by the Prime Minister of Israel to commemorate a military operation. Each item has been accessioned. None, to our knowledge, has been formally appraised, though our informal sense is that appraisal was never really the point.

Statement from the Collector

Asked to characterize his relationship to the material, the collector has been unusually forthcoming across several public occasions, at one point informing his own Cabinet that he considers himself, in his words, a gold person. On a separate occasion, discussing an upcoming visit to a French palace, he drew a distinction between gold leaf and what he called the real deal, apparently in reference to Versailles — a distinction the Department finds interesting mainly because Versailles is, itself, extensively gilded, and the collector’s palace, by his own account, is not meant to be an imitation of it so much as a rebuttal.

Prior Incident on File (Case No. 700 BCE, Phrygia)

The Department maintains a single precedent file for cases of this general shape, and Board members deserve to know it exists. The subject, one Midas, King of Phrygia, petitioned a visiting deity for the power to render anything he touched into gold, received it in full, and was divested — in fairly short order, and in this sequence — of his dinner, his wine, his own hands’ usefulness for any task requiring texture, and finally his daughter, whom he embraced in a moment of forgetting and converted, permanently, into statuary. He did not, notably, ask for any of this to be reversed until the statuary stage. Prior to that point, by every surviving account, he considered the arrangement a considerable professional success.

The Department flags the file not because history repeats itself with any reliability — it doesn’t, that’s a folk saying, not a conservation standard — but because the file’s closing note has aged with unusual precision: the subject got exactly what he asked for, at full fidelity, and it did not once occur to him, until his own child had gone rigid and yellow in his arms, that the wish had been a description of loneliness rather than wealth. We are not equipped to say whether the current collector has read this file. We are equipped to say that nothing in the present collection suggests he has, and that the Department, for what it’s worth, keeps a spare copy on hand at all times, indexed under “warnings, disregarded.”

One distinction is worth noting for the file, if only in fairness to the original claimant. Midas held an actual throne, granted by actual gods, in an actual kingdom that predated him and would, in due course, bury him. Whatever his failures of judgment, the title itself was not in dispute. The present collector holds no throne, no kingdom, and no god’s favor that we are aware of; he has simply built the trappings — the crown motifs, the gilded thrones-in-all-but-name, the fireplace mantle arranged like a coronation set — around an office that was designed, on purpose, four blocks from here, not to have a king in it. Midas got the wish and lost the daughter. The current subject appears to have skipped the wish and gone straight for the furniture, which the Department notes is, if nothing else, a more survivable arrangement, and a considerably less impressive one.

(A clerical note, appended so the Board is not embarrassed at the next hearing: this is the King of Phrygia, spelled M-I-D-A-S, and not the video commentary outlet spelled M-E-I-D-A-S, which is a wholly separate operation devoted to cataloguing this administration’s failures in a considerably more contemporary medium. The Department cannot speak to whether the latter organization chose its name with the former in mind, but concedes that if it did, it was the single most efficient act of institutional shade in recent memory, and we say that with professional envy.)

Comparable Institutions

Board members occasionally ask whether this sort of thing has precedent elsewhere. It does, though rarely at a comparable scale of self-authorship. The Kremlin’s gilded interiors, for what it’s worth, were largely installed by prior tenants; the current occupant inherited his gold rather than commissioning it fresh, which some visiting scholars consider a meaningfully different curatorial act. Versailles, the collector’s own preferred point of comparison, is worth a brief historical footnote: eighteenth-century visitors to the palace described conditions rather less resplendent than the gilt suggests — overflowing chamber pots, a king reputed to have bathed only a handful of times in his life, courtiers sweating through velvet in unventilated halls. The gold, in other words, was never really about comfort, or even taste. It was about ensuring that the only thing anyone remembered afterward was the gold. On that narrow metric, the comparison holds up better than the collector probably intends.

“A cyclist stopped, photographed the statues for approximately ninety seconds, said nothing, and left.”

“Overheard comparison to a casino lobby. Overheard, separately, comparison to a very large trophy. Declined to adjudicate between the two, per department policy.”

“One visitor asked whether the National Guard presence nearby was for security or for the gold specifically. I did not have an answer for this and I am noting it here so that someone eventually does.”

Funding Source

Board members have asked, reasonably, where the money comes from. Our understanding, consistent with public reporting, is that the statue regilding was funded through the National Park Service’s existing budget — the same budget currently absorbing significant workforce reductions and a maintenance backlog at parks nationwide. We note this not to render judgment, which is above the Conservation Department’s pay grade, but because provenance-of-funds is a standard field on our intake form, and the form does not have a box for “reallocated,” so we have simply written it in by hand.

Comparanda

For context, the Board may find it useful to know that gold leaf itself is applied in a layer roughly four to five millionths of an inch thick, and that in most restoration projects of this kind, the labor cost of application exceeds the market value of the gold itself. This is, in other words, an unusually expensive way to convey the impression of wealth without actually transferring very much of it. Whether that is the point, or beside the point, or the entire point, the Department leaves to the Board’s discretion.

Risk Assessment

Three risks merit ongoing monitoring. First, weather: gold leaf of the thickness used here is not a structural material, and the Department has no reason to believe this batch will weather differently than any other outdoor gilding exposed to a Washington summer and its accompanying humidity, pollen, and pigeon traffic. Second, theft: fifty-five pounds of gold, however thinly distributed across two nineteen-foot bronzes, is a meaningful sum, and the Department notes without further comment that National Guard personnel have been stationed nearby since the unveiling. Third, and this is the risk the Department is least equipped to assess but feels obligated to flag anyway: precedent. There is, as of this writing, no announced plan for the two remaining Arts of Peace statues still under scaffolding, nor for the triumphal arch reportedly under consideration for the bridge nearby, nor for any of the other statuary within eyeshot of the current work. The collection, in the Department’s professional judgment, is not finished. It is mid-accretion. We would ask the Board to budget the next report accordingly.

Recommendation

I am not going to recommend deaccessioning. I don’t think that’s realistic, and I don’t think the collector would take the call. What I will say, as Chief Conservator, is that gold does not fail quietly. It flakes. It dulls unevenly. The Reflecting Pool renovation elsewhere on the grounds has already demonstrated what happens to a Trump-era infrastructure project left exposed to weather, oversight, and time, and I would gently suggest the Board budget accordingly for this collection’s eventual and, I think, inevitable difference of opinion with the elements.

I will also say, once, and then put it back in the file where it belongs: every version of this story ends the same way, and it has ended the same way since Phrygia. Not with the gold failing. With the man who asked for it discovering, too late and in front of an audience, that he’d converted the one thing that could have loved him back into an ornament. Nobody in Case No. 700 BCE got a warning either. They got a myth, written afterward, by people who’d watched it happen and wanted the rest of us to see it coming.

In the meantime, I recommend we continue to document. Future conservators will want the record. They will want to know that in the year 2026, while a country’s grocery bills climbed and its safety-net rolls shrank, a great deal of institutional energy went into making sure two bronze soldiers near a cemetery could be seen gleaming from a moving car. They will want the receipts. We have kept the receipts. We have, God help us, laminated them.

Respectfully submitted,

Office of the Chief Conservator Dominion Division

A note appended by the Board Secretary, filed under separate cover: several trustees have asked whether the gold can, at minimum, be melted down and returned to the Treasury upon a change of administration. The Conservation Department’s position is that this is a policy question, not a conservation question, and that our department does not do policy. We do, however, keep a very good inventory.
