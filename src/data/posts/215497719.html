For two months this series has treated the collapse of the June 17 ceasefire as a fact without an author — an event that happened, rather than one anybody in particular did. That gap has just been filled, and not in the direction anyone assumed. The attack on three commercial ships in the Strait of Hormuz on July 7, the one that set a Qatari LNG tanker ablaze and dragged the United States back into open war within a day, was not ordered by Iran’s government. According to Farnaz Fassihi’s reporting for The New York Times, drawn from interviews with eight Iranian officials, three Revolutionary Guards members and four former officials, the strike was a rogue operation — and the man who is supposed to have final authority to arbitrate exactly this kind of internal chaos has not been seen or heard from in six months.

The Strike Nobody Ordered

President Masoud Pezeshkian learned of the July 7 attack while in Iraq for the elder Ayatollah Khamenei’s funeral, and reportedly phoned Gen. Ahmad Vahidi, the Revolutionary Guards’ commander in chief, in a rage. Vahidi’s answer was the more remarkable fact: he told the president he had neither ordered nor known about the strike in advance, and that Iran’s Supreme National Security Council — the body meant to be running both the war and the peace talks — had been kept in the dark as well. Officials traced the operation to Hossein Taeb, a spymaster ousted from his old Guards intelligence post in 2022 after a string of security failures, who resurfaced after the assassination of the elder Khamenei and reportedly helped steer the succession toward his son. Taeb had spent weeks telling the new supreme leader that Iran’s peace deal was a mistake. One procedural detail stands out: the Guards’ Khatam al-Anbiya command, which routinely claims responsibility for strikes before and since, never claimed this one. That silence, officials said, is itself close to a confession.

Iran’s government has since built a public case that Washington broke the ceasefire first — over Israel’s continued campaign against Hezbollah, over frozen assets, over American ships allegedly slipping through unblockaded corners of the strait. Almost nobody inside Iran’s own political class believes this, including, by several accounts, Mr. Pezeshkian himself. When generals told him the supreme leader had personally approved the July 7 strike, he asked for proof. He is still, by the account of people close to him, waiting.

The Phantom in the Ambulance

This series has tracked Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei’s total public absence since taking his father’s title in March as a live thread for months, and the new reporting deepens it into something closer to a governance emergency than a security precaution. His mother-in-law gave an interview this month saying the family has had no contact with him at all. His father-in-law offers only that he is, God willing, healthy. Mr. Pezeshkian says he has met the supreme leader twice — once for seven hours last month, on unspecified terms he will not describe — but the first encounter, by the account of two officials and a former official, involved being driven around Tehran in an ambulance with blacked-out windows while a secure video link connected him to a man on a monitor who claimed to be relaying the leader’s words. Mr. Pezeshkian later confided to an ally that he was not certain he had been speaking with Ayatollah Khamenei at all. A senior cleric asked publicly, on state television, how long the country would be expected to endure a leader nobody can see — six months, a year, ten years — without sliding into what he called perpetual weakness. The phrase attached to Khamenei’s own approval of the June deal, that he backed it because the president and the Security Council had endorsed it but disagreed “on principle,” has since become the hard-liners’ scripture: proof, in their reading, that the supreme leader’s real preference was for war all along.

Escalation Wins the Argument

The internal debate this uncertainty has produced is, per Ms. Fassihi’s reporting, a genuinely binary one: return to negotiations, or escalate toward strikes on American vessels, American casualties, and higher global oil prices, in the hope of forcing an end to the naval blockade that has driven Iran’s oil exports to zero. Brig. Gen. Seyyed Majid Mousavi, who commands the Guards’ Aerospace Force, pushed a detailed war plan built around intensifying Houthi and Iraqi-militia attacks on regional oil infrastructure. Mr. Pezeshkian and Gen. Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf — the parliamentary speaker and Iran’s own lead negotiator, whose late-summer break with “proportionate responses” this series has already tracked — argued the plan risked the abyss. As of last Saturday, they had lost the argument. Ghalibaf’s own top adviser, Mahdi Mohammadi, put it plainly: the war has entered “the phase of escalation.” The National Security Council shake-up that followed the July crisis, and the supreme leader’s installation of the veteran Gen. Mohsen Rezaei to run it, was meant to paper over exactly this kind of factional rupture. It has not.

Two Straits, One War

What that internal victory for the hard-liners produced, within days, is the starkest version yet of this series’ toll-booth thesis. As Robert F. Worth wrote for The Atlantic, Trump’s adversaries now control both of the Middle East’s critical maritime chokepoints, through which roughly a third of the world’s oil shipments pass daily — not one strait tightening, but two falling at once. The Houthis took the Red Sea port of Mokha on September 10 and Perim Island, inside Bab al-Mandab itself, the following day. The strait is barely twelve miles wide at some points, and shipping lanes run within three miles of Perim — well inside range of Houthi missiles and drones. April Longley Alley, a Yemen specialist, put the operational shift bluntly: control of the coastline now lets the Houthis simply sit and fire at whatever passes, cheaper and easier than the standoff attacks this series has tracked all summer.

The government forces that lost Mokha collapsed largely because Saudi Arabia’s own alliance management failed first. Riyadh forced an Emirati withdrawal from Yemen in December over a separate dispute, leaving a patchwork of militias with no coordination between commanders, intelligence, and coast guard, per Farea Al-Muslimi of Chatham House. Contrast that with 2022, when joint Saudi-UAE support blunted an earlier Houthi breakthrough at Marib — coordination that analyst Michael Knights notes existed then and doesn’t now. Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman reportedly called Trump twice in a single day urging strikes on the Houthis. The Mecca Alliance, Saudi Arabia’s mutual-defense pact with Pakistan and Turkey, has produced no help from either partner.

Fawaz Gerges of the London School of Economics called Houthi control of Mokha a game changer, for the militia and for Iran alike, and the shipping data bears him out: only two Saudi-flagged cargoes passed through Bab al-Mandab in the week before Perim fell, against roughly thirty-four ships a day transiting Hormuz at June’s brief high point, according to Lisa Friedman, Jenny Gross and Rebecca F. Elliott’s reporting for The New York Times. Both routes Saudi Arabia built as workarounds for Iran’s closure of Hormuz are now compromised at once, which is precisely the toll-booth logic this series has tracked since the summer: control of a chokepoint is worth more than the ability to fight a war outright, because it lets you tax or throttle everyone who still needs to move oil through it, indefinitely, without needing to win anything on the ground.

The Third Front

A new actor entered the picture Friday: a drone attack launched from Iraqi territory forced Saudi Arabia to shut down its East-West pipeline, the route it had been using to bypass both the closed Strait of Hormuz and the increasingly hostile Red Sea. Satellite imagery reported by Sam Meredith for CNBC, from Vantor via Getty Images, shows the toll in stark before-and-after detail: a pumping station at the pipeline’s midpoint reduced to blackened, fire-scarred ground.

The pipeline itself is no minor detour. Running roughly 750 miles from the Abqaiq fields on the Gulf coast to the Red Sea port of Yanbu, with a design capacity of 7 million barrels a day, it has, according to Aramco chief executive Amin Nasser, done more to stabilise oil markets during this war than Washington’s own strategic-reserve releases. Baghdad asked Riyadh to hold off retaliating while it investigates; Iraq has long hosted Iran-aligned militias capable of exactly this kind of strike, whether or not Tehran ordered it — a structural echo of the July 7 pattern, in which capability has repeatedly outrun central command’s control over it.

The Venezuela Precedent

Amid all this, CNBC’s Anniek Bao reported the clearest statement yet of what President Trump actually wants out of the war, and it was not the quick capitulation he promised in February. Speaking to reporters at the Irish Open on Sunday, he said the U.S. would “ultimately get out” of the conflict — “unless we decide to stay and keep the oil like Venezuela.” The comparison was pointed: under the August arrangement he was invoking, Venezuela ceded majority American control of more than 65 billion barrels of reserves, over double America’s own, for $209 billion into Caracas’s treasury, with Secretary of State Rubio claiming close to $100 billion in added private investment on top. Trump said the Venezuela revenue has already “paid for the war many times,” predicted the Iran war would end this year, likely after the midterms, and claimed Tehran has been “calling constantly” for a deal — which Iranian officials have specifically denied. Separately, Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said Monday they had destroyed an advanced American MQ-1 drone over the strait, unconfirmed independently, the latest in a string of strikes on U.S. unmanned systems.

The Numbers That Prove It

Brent crude has moved in a single week from $100 to $105 to a brief spike above $110, eased to about $104 by Sunday, then jumped again Monday on the pipeline closure — trading near $107 to $108, with WTI above $102. Brent is now up more than 20 percent in the past month alone, WTI nearly 25 percent — the volatility itself now the story, not any single figure. Diesel has crossed $6 a gallon in the U.S., up roughly 60 percent since the war began; gasoline sits above $4.30, up 44 percent. August inflation came in at 3.4 percent annualized. The ten-year Treasury yield has held near 4.98 percent, its highest since 2023; the thirty-year mortgage rate has followed it to 6.76 percent, the highest in more than a year, with existing home sales already falling. Hormuz traffic tells its own before-and-after story: an average of thirty-four ships a day passed through during June’s brief memorandum-of-understanding window; September has averaged nineteen. The ceasefire’s one measurable achievement was nearly doubling strait traffic. It evaporated the moment the deal did.

The International Energy Agency cut its global oil demand forecast for the year by 2.5 million barrels a day on Friday, its steepest revision yet, projecting only a partial rebound in 2027. Heating oil is up 52 percent year over year, just as northern households start thinking about winter, and fuel now accounts for as much as 30 percent of American food costs, according to Kate Gordon, a former U.S. Department of Energy adviser — the Gulf-to-grocery-bill bridge this series has been building toward for months, no longer abstract. The S&P 500 briefly snapped a four-day losing streak Friday but still closed the week lower overall.

None of this squares with what the administration is telling the public. Nancy A. Youssef and Jonathan Lemire reported for The Atlantic that Vice President Vance said last week the United States is not, strictly, at war. President Trump has called the conflict “small potatoes” even as he has separately termed it a war outright — and as this week’s companion Ministry of Truth entry explores in more detail, the Navy is quietly planning to keep two aircraft carrier strike groups in the Gulf through the end of the year, a commitment level rarely matched outside the Afghanistan and Iraq wars. The USS Abraham Lincoln’s own deployment ran 264 consecutive days at sea and left a crew coping with food shortages and declining morale before it was finally relieved. Adm. Daryl Caudle, addressing sailors bound for a similarly open-ended tour, offered the honest version the public messaging won’t: the enemy, he said, gets a vote.

The Meeting That Wasn’t

Monday’s diplomacy never happened. As Aaron Boxerman reported for The New York Times, Oman’s foreign minister, Badr Albusaidi, announced late Sunday night that the Iran–Gulf Arab meeting was postponed “in the interests of consensus” — hours after the East-West pipeline attack, a sequencing the reporting leaves readers to draw their own conclusions from. Officials had expected to sign an Iran-Oman arrangement establishing a shipping route through Hormuz, likely involving some kind of service fee for passage. That, too, is now on hold.

The weekend supplied its own grim postscript to Sunday’s still-open Hormuz incident: the governor of Iran’s Qeshm island said the vessel struck by a projectile was Iranian, and that the attack killed one person and injured four. A second, separate strike landed the same day inside Saudi Arabia itself — Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree claimed an attack on a military site near Sharurah, while Saudi officials reported two people wounded and a mosque damaged by a projectile in a neighbouring province.

Bahrain, already refusing to attend even before the postponement, called any dialogue with Iran a matter of “disregarding facts.” The UAE took the opposite path, its crown prince meeting President Pezeshkian on the sidelines of the BRICS summit in India Saturday — the first public Iran-UAE leadership contact since the war began. Bader al-Saif of Kuwait University captured the bind smaller Gulf states are in: they’ve been collateral damage between Washington and Tehran for months, and still have to find a way to keep dealing with Iran regardless. Some observers have taken to calling the current standoff “no war, no peace” — and Trump himself, for the first time, admitted the description may be closer to true than his public line allows: he said he came close to launching a massive new attack on Iran but pulled back, in part on pleas from Gulf states worried about the cost of a full-scale war resuming.

By the Numbers

Two Middle Eastern chokepoints, one adversary alignment. Brent up roughly 50 percent since February 28. Diesel up 60 percent. At least 22 sailors killed in the Gulf since the war began; at least 23 ships struck near Oman in July and August alone. Ninety-day span, one ceasefire, one collapse, one rogue command nobody in Tehran has fully explained. Senator Kennedy of Louisiana remains the lone Senate Republican still willing to say so publicly. Narges Mohammadi remains in Evin Prison, whatever the men arguing over escalation and negotiation decide about the country she is being held in.
