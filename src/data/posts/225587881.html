In the summer of 1990, on the eve of the World Cup final in Rome, three tenors walked onto a stage at the Baths of Caracalla and sang together for the first time. The concert made history and very little new music. Each man sang his own arias, the duets were arranged so that nobody had to give way, and the encore was “Nessun dorma” three times over. It sold millions of records. Nobody mistook it for an ensemble.

On Wednesday evening in Shenzhen, three other men rehearsed a similar act. Before the banquet that opened the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit, Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin shook hands and smiled for the cameras, as The New York Times reported. Xi stood at a podium banked with flowers and raised a glass of red wine. Trump sat at the centre of the table with his back to the press, Xi on his left, the two of them talking through interpreters when the reporters were ushered out. About an hour later the president went back to his hotel. Across central Shenzhen, roads had been closed for the visitors.

The real performance came on Thursday: the first meeting ever of the leaders of the United States, China and Russia. Between them they hold more than 90 percent of the world’s nuclear weapons. They were scheduled to spend about an hour together.

The tenor from Moscow

Putin made sure he arrived first. He landed in the early hours of Wednesday morning and was met on the tarmac by Cai Qi, Xi’s longtime deputy, rather than by Xi, who saved that honour for Trump later in the day. But the Russian president had his own entrance music. As his plane touched down, Russian strategic nuclear bombers landed in Vietnam, the first such deployment there since the Soviet era. The Times read it as a reminder, before the trilateral talks, that Moscow is still the world’s largest nuclear power.

Then he spent more than two hours with Xi, at least their fourth meeting this year, first in full delegations and then over a smaller “working breakfast.” Xi called the relationship “steadfast as a rock through wind and rain.” Putin praised a 43 percent rise in tourist traffic since visa-free travel began, and spoke of both countries taking “constructive approaches to shaping a more equitable, multipolar world order,” his usual phrase for a world in which America no longer runs things.

China has kept Russia’s war economy going: it buys Russian energy and sells Russia the electronics and consumer goods that Western companies stopped selling after the invasion. Putin owes Xi more than Xi owes Putin, and both men know it. The Chinese leader’s choreography on Wednesday was careful. The warm words went to Putin. The red carpet went to Trump.

Trump would see Putin separately as well, the White House said, their first meeting since the Alaska summit failed to produce a deal on Ukraine. The Kremlin was less eager. Its spokesman, Dmitri Peskov, told state media there would probably not be time for full talks, though the two would find a few minutes for Ukraine and perhaps Iran. Last week, Trump’s envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff asked Putin to let European officials join the Ukraine talks. The Kremlin turned them down in public.

Not Yalta

Everyone has a comparison ready, and it is the same one. Analysts have been calling Shenzhen “Yalta 2.0,” after the February 1945 conference in Crimea where Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin settled the shape of the postwar world.

David E. Sanger and Peter Baker of The Times took that comparison apart. Yalta ran for eight days, with diplomats and generals working the back rooms to turn the leaders’ talk into plans. Shenzhen offered an hour or so, which by comparison was, in their words, “a glorified photo op.” Yalta was a meeting of allies about to defeat a common enemy. These three men have never been partners in anything and are unlikely to become partners now. They disagree about China’s fast-growing nuclear arsenal, the regulation of artificial intelligence, weapons in space, the war in Ukraine and the war in Iran, a conflict that has, as Sanger and Baker put it, haunted Trump, enriched Putin and angered Xi.

The historians add a second, subtler objection: Yalta was never quite Yalta either. Speaking to Leo Sands of The Times, David Reynolds of Cambridge said the fate of postwar Europe “was decided on the battlefield, not at the conference table.” By February 1945 the Red Army was nearly in Berlin, and Roosevelt and Churchill had privately accepted Soviet control of Eastern Europe. The historian Catherine Grace Katz pointed out that the real carve-up came a year earlier in Moscow, when Churchill and Stalin scribbled their secret “percentages” agreement on a sheet of paper. Yalta also did things the legend forgets: Churchill fought to spare Germany a second Versailles, and Roosevelt used the conference to push the blueprint for the United Nations.

So the myth of Yalta is itself a kind of opera, a story about three great men dividing the world at a table, when most of the dividing had been done elsewhere by armies and by earlier, quieter bargains. Shenzhen fitted that pattern better than its promoters would like. Whatever was settled on Thursday had already been settled on the battlefields of Ukraine, in the export rules for American chips, and in the American midterms. The table was where the results were performed.

Three arias

Each man came to sing his own part, and the parts did not harmonise.

Trump had said for days that his first priority was “denuclearization,” which Sanger and Baker call a sloppy way of saying arms control. After the midterms, there was something else he wanted too: the picture. A president who has just lost Congress, and who has never hidden his admiration for leaders without pesky legislatures or a nosy press, got to sit with two of them as an equal, a world decider for an afternoon.

Putin wanted Ukraine discussed, perhaps reckoning that Trump wanted a peace deal badly enough not to be fussy about its terms. Above all he wanted the room itself. “He’s at the big boys table with the other great superpowers,” Alina Polyakova of the Center for European Policy Analysis told the Times. For a man who has lost half a million soldiers in a war of nearly five years, being seated with Washington and Beijing was a kind of readmission.

The numbers do not support the seating plan. America and China have the world’s two largest economies. Russia ranks eighth, roughly one-eighth the size of China’s economy and one-eleventh the size of America’s, and beyond weapons and energy it makes little the rest of the world wants to buy. What Putin was buying in Shenzhen was what Sanger and Baker call geopolitical swagger unjustified by reality.

Xi got the role he likes best, convener of the great powers, with the guest list in his hand. He was reportedly wary of any meeting without a settled outcome, and he meant to use this one in the service of his longer project: making China the world’s largest military, economic and technological power by 2049, the hundredth anniversary of Mao’s revolution. For Xi, the meeting was not the point. Hosting it was.

The empty chairs

The most interesting thing about a trilateral summit is who is not in the room. Sanger and Baker listed them: Japan, India, South Korea, Australia, Britain, France, Germany, Italy, Brazil, Canada, Mexico, South Africa and, most notably, Ukraine and Iran, the two countries whose wars sat at the top of the agenda.

Some of the absentees were at least in the building on Wednesday. Xi barely managed a smile for Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi of Japan, with whom he has been in a bitter diplomatic quarrel for a year. Prime Minister Mark Carney of Canada had his first in-person meeting of the year with Trump, an encounter Baker described as tense. Days earlier Carney had angered the administration by repeating that Canada would look elsewhere, China included, if Washington did not lift its tariffs on Canadian cars. His office’s account of the meeting said only that the two men “discussed the work underway to reach a mutually beneficial trade agreement,” as Ian Austen of The Times reported.

Europe was not in Shenzhen at all, and that, for Putin, was part of the attraction. “Demonstrating Europe’s irrelevance is very important to Putin at this point,” Michael Kimmage of the Kennan Institute told the Times. He expected the meeting to be forgotten quickly. But he traces a line from the Treaty of Versailles through Yalta to Shenzhen that shows, he said, that “Europe is being erased.”

That may overstate it. But the seating chart tells you what kind of world the three tenors were performing: one of spheres of influence, the arrangement of the late 1800s, when a handful of empires drew lines across everyone else’s maps. Trump’s national security strategy speaks of an updated Monroe Doctrine to keep outsiders out of the Western Hemisphere. Putin talks of restoring the empire of Peter the Great, starting with Ukraine. Xi builds bases on artificial islands in the South China Sea and runs exercises around Taiwan. Asked in January whether he believed in spheres of influence, Trump said it was not necessarily where he wanted the world to go, but that it was where it was going.

The song nobody would sing

There was one subject the American tenor had promised not to raise.

A week ago Jensen Huang resigned as chief executive of Nvidia, America’s most valuable chip company, after the revelation of an affair with a businesswoman whom American intelligence believes was spying for China. Trump had said he would not discuss the case with Xi. Baker called the visit’s quality, in The Times, “see-no-evil, hear-no-evil.” The Times has reported that Trump was told about the matter at least twice, once on his way to Beijing in May. He has denied it, said he does not know whether the woman was a spy, and voiced support for Huang’s reinstatement. On Wednesday the Times added another layer. Paul Mozur and Adam Satariano of The Times reported that Megaspeed, the company Alice Huang built, bought more than $4 billion of Nvidia chips with Alibaba as its customer and backer, and that Alibaba co-founded Zhejiang Lab, a research centre with strong ties to China’s military whose researchers have worked on automating cyberattacks and simulating war games. Whether any of the chips reached the lab is unclear, and Alibaba calls any suggestion that it serves the military groundless. It is the sort of thing a president might raise with his host. This one had said he would not.

It is a familiar silence. In a prime-time address in July, Trump accused China of hacking or buying American voter rolls. When he hosted Xi for a lavish state visit in September, he said nothing about it in public. “We have a very good relationship with China, and you don’t want to insult them,” he said before leaving for Asia.

Meanwhile the quarrel his silence was meant to smother travelled with him. Scott Bessent, the Treasury secretary, has blamed Howard Lutnick, the commerce secretary, for resisting limits on chip sales to China. Both men made the trip. At the welcome in Shenzhen they were seen standing apart.

Curtain up

What would the hour produce? The people closest to the Ukraine talks have mostly concluded that Putin has no interest in a deal; his latest proposal repeats the maximalist demands he made before the 2022 invasion. Michael McFaul, a former ambassador to Moscow, pointed out that a leader seeking peace would not be bombing the bridges of Kyiv on the eve of the summit. “He gets the photo op,” McFaul told the Times, “and he gets to call it Yalta 2.0.”

Not everyone is so bleak. Charles Kupchan of Georgetown, a former national security aide to Bill Clinton and Barack Obama, made the case for the meeting in Foreign Affairs before it was even announced. He shares the worries: Trump has a record of cosying up to autocrats, Kupchan wrote, and must not strike a closed-door bargain on Ukraine and then force it on Kyiv, or give ground on Taiwan in return for help elsewhere. He wants Trump to aim low, at the few problems where three-way talk might help: North Korea, Ukraine, Iran and nuclear arms. The last is the most urgent. New START, the final nuclear treaty between Washington and Moscow, expired in February, and the Pentagon expects China to have more than 1,000 operational warheads by 2030. Even with no results, Kupchan argues, the meeting is worth having, because the three men are paving the way for their successors to cooperate across ideological lines. He also wrote that it was far too soon to let Putin out of the penalty box. Shenzhen would test which half of that argument held.

In the end the hour ran to about 80 minutes, around a round wooden table at Kylin Villa, a lakeside guesthouse that once hosted Kim Jong-il, with Xi in the centre, Trump on his left and Putin on his right, as The New York Times reported. The opening remarks were three arias. Xi called nuclear weapons “a sword of Damocles hanging over humanity” that should be “completely prohibited and thoroughly destroyed,” without mentioning that China has the fastest-growing arsenal on earth. Putin said the three were united by “the status of being the victorious countries in World War II.” Trump said they were “uniquely positioned” to reduce the world’s nuclear weapons, then conceded that once you have them, it is very hard not to have them. Then the doors closed.

Earlier, Trump had spent about 40 minutes with Putin, greeting him with “Who is he? He’s my friend,” and an hour with Xi, who told him the United States should “insist on doing the right thing” on Taiwan. A Bloomberg reporter who shouted a question about Jensen Huang at the Trump–Xi meeting was barred from the trilateral. When it was over, Trump went straight to the airport without a word to reporters, and the first account of what had been agreed came from Moscow and Beijing.

The Three Tenors sold millions of records and never once sang as a trio in the true sense. Each kept his own key and his own aria, and the audience applauded the spectacle of three famous voices on one stage. Shenzhen will be remembered the same way: not for what was sung, but for who was standing where. What the three men actually said to one another, and what each says afterwards that they said, is a different performance. That is Part V.
