The case for reunification is not simply the case for the People’s Republic of China having what it wants. At its strongest and most honest — which is not always how it is presented — it rests on historical continuity, on the argument about what political stability in the Asia-Pacific actually requires, and on a critique of the liberal democratic model that is more serious than its Western critics usually acknowledge. Let us examine each in turn.

The Historical Argument

Taiwan highlighted under Qing Dynasty control. The historical argument begins with the 1683 annexation and two centuries of integration with mainland China.

Begin where Beijing begins: with the unbroken thread of Chinese civilisational connection to Taiwan that predates the Dutch, predates Koxinga, and was formalised in the Qing annexation of 1683. For two hundred years before Japan took the island in 1895, Taiwan was part of the Chinese imperial system. Han Chinese settlers from Fujian and Guangdong transformed the island’s demographics, culture, language, and agricultural practices. The familial and cultural connections that formed over those two centuries did not evaporate when Japan arrived. They persist, in the Taiwanese society of today, in the Minnan language spoken by the majority of Taiwanese families, in culinary traditions, in religious practice, in the temple architecture that dots the island’s cities and towns, in the family histories that cross the strait.

Cairo Conference, 1943: Chiang Kai-shek, Franklin D. Roosevelt, and Winston Churchill. The Allied commitment to return Taiwan to China forms a core part of the reunification historical case

The wartime Allied powers — the United States, Britain, and the Republic of China — agreed at Cairo in 1943 that territories Japan had stolen from China, including Taiwan, would be returned to China. This was not an afterthought. It was a considered political commitment made by the leaders who were fighting the war and who intended to design the post-war order. Japan accepted these terms in its surrender. The argument that this commitment was subsequently undone by the legal technicalities of the San Francisco treaty — that the failure to name a recipient in Article 2(b) somehow created a new legal status for Taiwan — reads like the work of lawyers looking for exits rather than an honest account of what the victorious powers intended.

China’s government changed in 1949. That is undeniable. But states, in international law, are not their governments. The Soviet Union remained the Soviet Union through multiple changes of government. Germany remained Germany through catastrophic defeat and partition. The principle of state continuity holds that when a government changes — even through revolution, even through the kind of total political transformation that 1949 represented — the state’s international rights and obligations do not vanish with the old regime. The People’s Republic of China is the successor state to the Republic of China on the mainland. It inherited the ROC’s seat on the UN Security Council in 1971 when the General Assembly voted to recognise this reality. It inherited the ROC’s treaties. It inherited the ROC’s territorial claims.

On this reading, the question is not whether Taiwan is part of China — the historical record, the wartime agreements, and the principle of state continuity establish that it is — but how and when the political separation of 1949 is brought to an end in a manner that is stable, peaceful, and acceptable to all parties.

The Stability Argument

The second pillar of the reunification case is strategic, and it deserves more serious engagement from the independence camp than it usually receives.

Taiwan’s current status — functional independence, formal ambiguity, American security guarantee of uncertain reliability — is not a stable equilibrium. It is a situation maintained by a specific configuration of power that is changing. The People’s Liberation Army Navy has become the world’s largest fleet by hull count. China’s military budget has grown for thirty consecutive years. The military balance across the Taiwan Strait has shifted measurably toward Beijing with every passing decade, and the trajectory of that shift is not reversing.

The American security guarantee, always contingent and ambiguous, is becoming more explicitly transactional under the current administration. Taiwan has been required to pay more, invest more in the American defence industrial base, and reduce its dependence on American protection — all while the American president has signalled, in a Fox News interview following his May 2026 summit with Xi Jinping, that he is “not looking to have somebody go independent” and that the prospect of travelling “9,500 miles to fight a war” holds no appeal. The Silicon Shield — Taiwan’s control of the world’s most advanced semiconductor manufacturing — provides some protection, but it also means Taiwan’s strategic value to the United States is economic rather than primarily democratic. Economic calculations change.

Shifting military balance: Chinese military exercises encircling Taiwan. The stability argument rests on the changing power dynamics across the strait.

The reunification argument holds that a stable, negotiated resolution of Taiwan’s status — one modelled, perhaps, on a genuinely federalist framework that preserved Taiwan’s democratic institutions and legal system within a broader Chinese constitutional structure — would be preferable to the current trajectory, which leads toward either an indefinitely maintained and increasingly precarious status quo or a military conflict whose consequences for the entire Asia-Pacific would be catastrophic. The stability that reunification might provide, on this argument, is not just in China’s interest. It is in Taiwan’s interest and in the region’s interest as well.

The “one country, two systems” model, under which Hong Kong was administered after 1997, is frequently cited as evidence that Beijing cannot be trusted to honour such an arrangement. The critique is legitimate. Beijing’s progressive erosion of Hong Kong’s autonomy, culminating in the National Security Law of 2020, demonstrated that the commitments made to Hong Kong’s population were not durable. But the stability argument for reunification does not necessarily depend on “one country, two systems” as Beijing currently practices it. It depends on whether some form of genuine autonomy arrangement could be negotiated and maintained — a different and more difficult question.

The honest proponent of reunification acknowledges that Hong Kong is a genuine problem for their argument. They tend to respond that Taiwan’s situation is categorically different from Hong Kong’s in ways that would give it more leverage in any negotiation — its democratic institutions are more deeply rooted, its military capacity is more substantial, its economic significance to global technology supply chains is more critical — and that a Taiwan incorporated into China on terms that stripped it of those advantages would be economically and strategically damaging to China itself, creating incentives for Beijing to maintain real autonomy rather than merely nominal autonomy. This is a more hopeful argument than Hong Kong’s experience supports. But it is not an incoherent one.

1997 Hong Kong handover ceremony. “One country, two systems” in practice — the precedent that reunification advocates must address.

The Critique of Liberal Universalism

The third pillar of the reunification argument is the most uncomfortable for Western readers, which is precisely why it deserves the most careful examination.

The liberal democratic framework that underlies the independence case — the argument that Taiwan’s people have a right to self-determination, and that their democratic preferences should determine their political future — rests on a universalist claim: that these principles apply everywhere, to everyone, regardless of history or context. China’s reunification advocates challenge that universalism, not always in good faith but sometimes with genuine force.

The challenge runs roughly as follows. The principle of self-determination has been applied selectively by Western powers throughout the post-colonial period. It was applied generously to territories that Western powers found strategically useful to separate from their former empires, and denied to territories — Kurdistan, Tibet, Catalonia, the Donbas — whose separation was inconvenient to Western allies or interests. The principle was not applied when the United States found it inconvenient. It is being applied now, in Taiwan’s case, primarily because Taiwan’s independence serves American strategic interests in containing Chinese power, not because the United States has a consistent principled commitment to self-determination anywhere.

This is a critique of Western hypocrisy, not of the principle itself. But it lands with some force, because the hypocrisy is real. And it leads to the broader point: that the current international order, which Taiwan’s independence camp appeals to for legal and moral support, is an order designed and maintained primarily by and for Western powers, and that China’s challenge to that order — including its insistence on resolving the Taiwan question on its own terms — is not simply authoritarian aggression but also a challenge to a system that has served Chinese interests poorly for most of the past two centuries.

The reunification argument, at its most sophisticated, does not merely assert that Taiwan belongs to China because Beijing says so. It asserts that the post-war international order’s treatment of Taiwan — the deliberate ambiguity, the strategic manipulation, the instrumentalisation of Taiwan’s status in American-Chinese relations — has itself been a form of injustice, one that has kept the wound of 1895 open not out of principle but out of great-power convenience. Reunification, on this reading, is not conquest. It is the completion of a decolonisation that the post-war settlement deliberately left unfinished.

The Limits of the Argument

Having made the strongest honest case for the reunification position, intellectual honesty requires identifying where it is weakest.

The historical argument, however valid on its own terms, cannot answer the question of what the people of Taiwan want now. History does not determine consent, and consent is what democratic legitimacy requires. The fact that Taiwan was part of China before 1895 does not establish that its people, in 2026, consent to be part of China now — particularly a China governed by a party that they have never elected and cannot remove.

The stability argument is real but paternalistic in its structure: it argues that a particular outcome would be good for Taiwan regardless of whether Taiwan’s people have chosen it. Stability achieved by overriding the expressed preferences of twenty-three million people is not the kind of stability that liberal principles recognise as legitimate.

The critique of liberal universalism is accurate as far as it goes, but it proves too much. The fact that the United States has applied self-determination inconsistently does not establish that Taiwan’s people have no right to self-determination. It establishes that the United States is hypocritical, which is not a revelation.

And the Hong Kong precedent, dismissed by reunification advocates as inapplicable, is the most powerful single piece of evidence available about how Beijing actually behaves when it has control over a population it promised autonomy to. The promises made to Hong Kong’s people were specific, public, and codified in an international treaty. They were broken. Systematically. Within twenty-five years. Jimmy Lai, the publisher who exercised the press freedom that “one country, two systems” was supposed to guarantee, is serving a twenty-year sentence — the longest handed down under the National Security Law — while Donald Trump, who raised Lai’s case in Beijing this month, described it as a discussion item rather than a demand.

Jimmy Lai and Hong Kong pro-democracy voices under the National Security Law. The most difficult challenge to the reunification stability argument.

The people of Taiwan know this. They watched it happen in real time. And their polling numbers on reunification moved accordingly.

The case for reunification is not without substance. But its strongest version requires trusting Beijing to honour commitments it has already demonstrated it will not honour, in service of a historical claim that cannot substitute for the living consent of the people whose future it seeks to determine.
