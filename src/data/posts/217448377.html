By Stephen Gordon

On the morning of Tuesday, September 22, 2026, Donald Trump arrived at the United Nations to address the 81st session of the General Assembly. Among the reporters gathered outside was Kaitlan Collins, CNN’s chief White House correspondent. She asked a simple question built on a new CNN poll: two-thirds of Americans do not believe the United States is winning the war with Iran, so when will it end?

He did not answer it. As CNBC reported, he gestured at her and said he was surprised CNN was there covering him. Then, in the version everyone has now heard: “You shouldn’t be here. You should not be here covering me. You said you weren’t going to cover me. You shouldn’t be covering me.”

Collins replied that the United Nations had let them in. He walked away.

This series exists to answer a question Lawrence O’Donnell put on the table: if Donald Trump develops dementia, how would anyone know? We have answered by counting rather than diagnosing. We note patterns, date them, file them, and watch whether they thicken. Most weeks the material is abundant and the difficulty is selection. This week the difficulty is the opposite. The press-ban story is so loud, and so thoroughly a story about institutions, lawsuits and First Amendment doctrine, that the behavioural signal risks getting lost in the legal noise.

So let us set the legal story aside. It has been told in full elsewhere on this Substack, over four instalments of Ministry of Truth. What stays here is the man.

The Map Error

The rudeness is not the data point. Collins has been on the receiving end before. At the White House Correspondents’ Dinner in July he mocked her award and told her to smile. Contempt for a particular reporter is baseline Trump, as constant as the weather in Winnipeg, and a series that counted every insult would soon be counting nothing else.

The data point is the geography.

On Friday, September 18, he banned CNN, MS NOW and Politico from the White House. Four days later he stood outside a building in Manhattan that does not belong to him, run by an organisation that does not answer to him, and seemed genuinely surprised to find the banned network there. His word was “surprised”. He did not say “annoyed” or “defiant”. He said surprised.

A ban on entering one building had become, somewhere between Washington and Turtle Bay, a ban on existing within his sightline. Collins had to explain the point on the spot, and CNN explained it again later on air: the UN had credentialed them to cover the UN.

In the categories this series has used since the beginning, that is not disinhibition, and it is not quite confabulation either. It is closer to what clinicians call a failure of context-tracking: the rules of one setting carried into another as though the world had been reorganised to match. It sits in the same family as the Part XXI “57”, a number he was never meant to say aloud, spoken in a room where it did not belong. Something that should have stayed in one compartment leaked into another.

One exchange proves nothing. But this series never claims that one exchange proves anything. It claims that the file gets thicker.

The Premise He Invented

Then there is the sentence that has drawn less attention: “You said you weren’t going to cover me.”

CNN never said that. Collins said so on air within hours, adding that the network would keep covering the president despite efforts to keep it off the White House grounds. The Hill reported that late that Tuesday Trump returned to the point in a long Truth Social post. He called Collins a “Third Rate ‘Reporter’”, repeated that she and the others “said they weren’t going to be covering me”, and asked why she had been there at all.

It is worth being fair about where the invention might have come from, because that is precisely how this category works. On Monday, September 21, the five networks in the White House television pool (ABC, CBS, CNN, Fox and NBC) suspended pool coverage in protest at the ban. So there was a real event in which networks declined to point a camera at him. Refusing to operate a shared pool camera on his terms is a different thing from refusing to cover him. Only the first happened, but the second is the one he remembered.

Part XXII introduced “self-mythologising confabulation”: the firefighter story, in which the invention made him the hero. Part XXI’s “57” was data confabulation, a precise-sounding figure delivered with total confidence. This is a third subtype worth naming: premise confabulation. A fragment of something real (the pool walkout) is reshaped into a claim about what someone else said, and that claim then justifies the grievance. The invented premise does work. It turns Collins from a credentialed reporter doing her job into a hypocrite breaking a promise she never made.

He did not say it once and let it go. He said it at the UN, then wrote it again that night. That is the more interesting fact. A slip of the tongue gets corrected, or at least goes quiet. A confabulated premise gets repeated, because by then it has become the memory.

The Trigger

Look again at what Collins actually asked. She did not ask about the ban. She asked about the war, and specifically about a poll showing that most Americans think it is being lost.

On September 9, 2026, Tom Nichols wrote in The Atlantic Daily that Trump tends to lash out hardest at whoever is nearest when the news goes badly, whether friend or foe. His examples were the Epstein files turned against his own party, the Iran humiliation turned against South Korea, and trade frustration turned against Canada. We banked that piece at the time as a candidate for a new category in this series, one distinct from perseveration, confabulation and disinhibition. We called it displacement.

On September 25, 2026, in The Atlantic, Quinta Jurecic independently arrived at the same mechanism from the other direction. She observed that Trump reaches for the press as a target when the news is bad, and that the news is currently very bad: fuel prices driven up by the Iran war, sinking congressional Republican numbers, his own falling approval. The press ban, on her reading, is not a policy. It is a way of not hearing.

Two writers at the same magazine, sixteen days apart, have described the same reflex from two different sets of evidence. The Collins exchange is the reflex in miniature: bad news arrives, and the response is not to answer it but to object to the person who delivered it.

That is enough to take displacement off the bench. From this instalment onward it is a named category in the series ledger, alongside the other four.

The Move He Has Already Lost

Jurecic makes a point the legal coverage mostly treated as trivia. The judge assigned to the case, Timothy Kelly, is the same judge who ruled in 2018 that the White House could not strip Jim Acosta of his credentials. In 2020 the D.C. Circuit held, in the Brian Karem case, that press access once granted cannot be withdrawn without due process. Trump chose to fight a battle he had already lost twice, before a judge who had already ruled against him on the same question.

Asked before the ruling whether the ban would survive, he was, by Jurecic’s account, indifferent: it was good to make the point, he said, whether the ban survived or not.

There are two ways to read that. One is strategic: the ban was always theatre, the point was the gesture, and losing in court costs nothing with an audience that does not read court rulings. That reading deserves to be taken seriously, and this series has always said so when it applies.

The other reading is the one this series exists to test. Perseveration, as we have used the term since the start, is not just repetition. It is repeating a move after it has stopped working, with no apparent registration that it has stopped working. The Acosta ban in 2018 was a targeted punishment of one reporter, in a building Trump controlled, over one confrontation. The 2026 ban is broader, rests on legal ground already known to be lost, and was defended in court at one point by a Justice Department filing made up mainly of a new Truth Social post. It is the same move made again, with less preparation, against worse odds, and with the outcome treated as irrelevant in advance.

The strategic reading explains the indifference. It explains the rest less well.

The Man Without the Microphone

The best image of the week needs no commentary, though it will get some.

On Monday, September 21, at the unveiling of a new White House helipad, Trump spoke with no network boom microphones present, because the pool had walked out the same day. The video, streamed by administration staff, showed him gesturing, his mouth moving and no sound coming out. Jurecic calls it vaguely comic, which is generous.

Then, on Thursday, September 24, Xi Jinping arrived for a state visit, and the ruling restoring press access had come down only hours earlier. Because of confusion over carrying it out, the barred reporters were still stopped at the gates that morning, and the pool held off until the next day. The result was that the most carefully staged diplomatic occasion of Trump’s autumn went out with no American live television coverage at all.

Here is a man who, by every account over forty years, organises his day around being watched. In the space of one week, a decision that was his alone set off the chain of events that left him unheard at his own ribbon-cutting and unseen at his own state visit. Jurecic’s summary is that his fit of pique deprived him of the attention he covets. That is the political reading. The behavioural reading is simpler and more troubling: the connection between the action and its obvious consequence was not made, or was made and did not matter.

This series has logged similar gaps before: the July 26 tablet-throwing binge, the Labor Day image flood, the Pentagon speech freeze on September 11. What they share is not rage but slippage, a growing distance between what he intends and what he ends up doing.

The Objection, Answered Again

Jurecic ends with the observation every sceptic of this series eventually reaches for. Every president struggles with irrelevance as his power wanes, she writes, and Trump has never been one to decline gracefully.

That is the baseline problem, stated in a single line. He has always been petulant. He has always attacked the press. He has always banned the reporters he dislikes. So how can anyone tell a lifelong temperament from a decline?

The answer this series has given for twenty-two instalments is that you do not look at whether the behaviour happens. You look at its shape. Temperament is steady. What changes in decline is the precision: how well aimed the behaviour is, how well it fits the setting, how well it serves its own purpose.

The 2018 Trump banned one reporter from one building after one confrontation, and knew exactly which building he controlled. The 2026 Trump banned three outlets from his house, then expressed surprise at meeting one of them in someone else’s. He invented a promise the network never made and repeated it that night in writing. He picked a legal fight he had already lost twice. And he silenced his own microphones and cameras in the week he most wanted them.

The temper is the same. The aim is not. That difference is the whole of this series’ argument, and this week added four entries to it.

A Prediction, Dated

Jurecic’s title is a forecast: a two-year tantrum. Her reasoning is that if Republicans lose unified control of Congress in the midterms on November 3, 2026, the area Trump directly controls will shrink further, his allies will be less inclined to fall in line, and the fury will grow in proportion to the shrinkage.

This series can make that forecast more specific, and it is worth recording now so it can be checked later.

If the framework is right, a shrinking sphere of control will produce more than more of the same. It will produce more map errors: more occasions on which the rules of the space he still controls are asserted in spaces he does not. Expect bans that assume they travel, orders issued to people who do not report to him, and grievances aimed at people who were never party to the thing he is aggrieved about. Expect confabulated premises to multiply, because every loss of control needs a story in which someone else broke a promise first.

If the next two years bring the tantrum without those errors, loud but well aimed, then the strategic reading was right and this series should say so. That is the point of counting rather than diagnosing. The count can prove you wrong.

The UN let Kaitlan Collins in. Wherever he goes from here, the question is whether he still knows where he is.
