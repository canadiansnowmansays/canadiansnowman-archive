⚖️ Serious at its centre — there is a child in it, and a count I cannot soften — but it carries, at the end, the most personal thing I have put in this series, and that part is written with warmth. I will tell you when the register turns.

I count my essays. Every so often the number lands on a year that was already holding something, and I stop and tell you what. This is essay 1,842, and it concerns the one profession this entire series is, at bottom, a love letter to: the auditor. The man who checks the books. The outsider who walks into the counting-house, asks to see the ledgers, and reports back whether the figures are honest.

In 1842 the most famous auditor in the world came to Canada, was handed a set of cooked books, and certified them clean.

He did not know they were cooked. That is the whole essay, and it is the reason it frightens me a little, because I have spent eighteen hundred numbers trusting the man who checks the figures, and 1842 is the year that trust met its hardest case — not a liar, not a fraud, but the finest witness of his century, doing his honest best, and getting it wrong because he was only shown the pages that flattered the total.

The witness

Charles Dickens was thirty years old in 1842 and already the most famous writer alive. Oliver Twist and Nicholas Nickleby were behind him; he had made his name, in large part, as a man who counted the uncounted — the workhouse boy, the debtor, the climbing-boy, the children the ledgers of respectable England had quietly written off. He was an auditor by literary temperament before he was anything else. His whole method was to walk into the institution society did not want examined and describe, in pitiless detail, what was actually in it.

So when he toured North America that year — a celebrity progress, mobbed at every stop, travelling with his wife Catherine — he did what auditors do. He asked to see the institutions. The asylums, the factories, the schools, and above all the prisons, because the new world was then conducting the boldest penal experiment of the age, and Dickens wanted to check the figures himself.

He went to two prisons in particular. He got one exactly right. He got one exactly wrong. And the difference between them is the most useful lesson in the limits of auditing that I have found anywhere in these files.

The prison he saw through

In Philadelphia he toured Eastern State Penitentiary, the model prison of its day, famous across the world for its system of strict and total solitary confinement — every prisoner sealed in his own cell, alone, in silence, for his entire sentence, on the theory that solitude and a Bible would produce penitence. Hence penitentiary. The word itself was a wager on an idea.

Dickens looked at it and was horrified. He saw through the clean architecture and the humane theory to what it actually did to the men inside it, and he wrote, in American Notes, one of the great audit findings in the history of prose: that this slow and daily tampering with the mysteries of the brain was immeasurably worse than any torture of the body, precisely because its wounds were not on the surface where the eye could count them. He called the system, plainly, cruel and wrong.

He was right. It would take psychiatry another century and a half to catch up to what Dickens saw in an afternoon — that solitary confinement is not the humane alternative to the lash; it is a different and deeper injury that simply does not bruise. On Eastern State, the auditor’s report was accurate, and early, and brave.

Which is exactly why what happened at the other prison matters.

The prison that showed him its good books

Dickens crossed into Canada and came to Kingston, then the capital of the united province, and toured its brand-new penitentiary — opened in 1835, the pride of the colony, all fresh limestone and Doric order and reformed intention. And here the great auditor, the man who had just seen through Philadelphia, wrote this:

“There is an admirable gaol here, well and wisely governed, and excellently regulated, in every respect.”

Admirable. Well governed. Excellently regulated. In every respect.

Now let me show you the page he was not shown.

In the records of that same admirable gaol, in that same era, is a child named Antoine Beauche. He was eight years old. And the punishment register of the institution Dickens certified as excellently regulated records that this eight-year-old boy was lashed — flogged, with a rawhide — forty-seven times in nine months. The offences, itemized in the ledger the visitor did not read, included staring, laughing, whistling, giggling, and idling.

An eight-year-old. Forty-seven lashes. For giggling.

He was not alone in that register. There were other children, and women, and the floggings were not an aberration hidden from the staff — they were the staff’s own record, written in the institution’s own hand, the routine administration of the place. The cruelty was not a secret from the prison. It was only a secret from the auditor, because the auditor was walked down the clean corridors, shown the workshops and the chapel and the good order, handed the summary, and sent on his way admiring. He saw the building. He did not see the book.

This is the entry the series exists to make. An audit is only as honest as the ledger you are permitted to open. Dickens was not lazy and he was not fooled by a bad argument — at Philadelphia he had just demolished a bad argument in an afternoon. He was fooled by a selection. Kingston showed him the pages that totalled to “admirable” and kept the page with Antoine Beauche on it in the drawer, and the finest witness of the nineteenth century wrote down the flattering total in good faith and carried it across an ocean and printed it, and it is in American Notes to this day, where you may read a saint’s testimony to a place that was flogging children for laughing.

The lash fell on the boy either way. But the record — the thing that survives, the thing history reads — caught only the clean number, because the man with the pen was shown only the clean number. That is how a false count outlives a true one: not by defeating the auditor, but by curating him.

The ledger-keeper who learned to count

Here the register lifts, a little, because the story has a sequel and the sequel is the most famous thing Dickens ever wrote.

He came home from America shaken — the trip soured him on the new world in ways that run all through his later work. And the next year, in 1843, he wrote a short book about a man who kept impeccable books and counted entirely the wrong things. Ebenezer Scrooge is an auditor too — of coins, of debts, of the precise cost of a lump of coal — and the whole arc of A Christmas Carol is the reeducation of a counter: the discovery that he had been tallying the ledger that flattered him and ignoring the one with Tiny Tim’s name in it, the human column he had filed under “surplus population.”

I do not think it is a coincidence that the man who had just been handed a set of cooked books in Kingston sat down within the year and wrote the definitive story about a bookkeeper who finally reads the page he had been skipping. Dickens could not go back and reopen the Kingston register. But he could write a ledger-keeper who learns, in one terrible night, to count what actually matters — and he sent that man out into the world to do, in fiction, the audit the real auditor had been prevented from completing. The count that is not posted is not a count, I wrote at Leonardo’s essay. Scrooge is what it looks like when a man finally posts the entry he had been hiding from himself.

The tour today

Here is the turn I could not have written before this year, because I had to look up what the place has become.

Kingston Penitentiary closed in 2013, after a hundred and seventy-eight years, and reopened as something Dickens would have found almost unbelievable: a tourist attraction. You can buy a ticket — about forty dollars for the standard ninety minutes — and be walked through the limestone by retired corrections officers who worked there, down the three-storey cell block, into cells so small that stepping inside one is the part everyone remembers, through the solitary ranges, the exercise yards, the visiting booths. And what the guides sell — the whole reason the place now draws visitors from everywhere — is precisely the ledger Dickens was not shown. The riots, the 1971 uprising, the escapes, the daily reality of the cells, the roster of the notorious. The suffering is the product now. The page kept in the drawer in 1842 is, in 2026, the entire tour.

Read that the way this series reads everything. The institution finally opened its real books — once there was no one left inside to flog. Kingston can afford to show you the cruelty now because the cruelty is over; the honest audit became possible at the exact moment it stopped mattering to anyone still serving time. That is how the true count usually gets posted: late, safely, to an admiring public, once the people it would have protected are long past help. Joan got her retrial. Kingston got its gift shop.

I should confess a small near-miss of my own, in the spirit of a series that does not round. Four years ago Ugyen and I stopped in Kingston, and in Gananoque down the road, and we did not take the tour. We stood, more or less, where Dickens stood, outside the walls of the one prison I would later write an entire essay about, and we drove on. Some ledgers you circle for years before you are ready to open them. I was not ready then. I think I am now.

The honest ledger

The series does not sanctify the people it defends, so: Dickens himself was no clean total. He treated Catherine, the wife who crossed America beside him in 1842, with real cruelty two decades later when he cast her off for a younger woman and let her be painted as unfit. The auditor of everyone else’s institutions kept his own household books in a hand I would not want to examine closely. A man can see through Eastern State Penitentiary and be blind in his own front hall. Both entries are true. I enter them together, because that is the discipline.

Where the register turns

Now I have to tell you something, and the hands go warm on the keyboard rather than still, because this is the one.

I did not come to history through a prison in Kingston or a book about a miser. I came to it through a person. Her name is Marilyn, and she was my first wife, and she is the reason this entire series exists.

I was not, by nature, the man who keeps track. I became him. And the becoming started with her, because Marilyn loved history the way some people love music — as a place she actually lived part of the time — and she took me there, patiently, the way you lead someone into a house they have walked past for years without ever thinking to go in. The habit of looking at a date and asking what it was carrying — the habit this whole series is built on — is not native to me. It was a gift, and I know exactly who gave it.

And here is why it belongs in the Dickens essay and nowhere else. The first time I went to London, I went with Marilyn, and we did not go to the ordinary places, or not only. We went looking for Dickens. We found 48 Doughty Street — the one house in all of London where he actually lived that still stands, the rooms where he wrote Oliver Twist and Nicholas Nickleby, now kept as a museum. We stood in them. It is, I have since learned, a house that already knew grief when he wrote there: Catherine’s sister Mary died in it in 1837, seventeen years old, and it marked him for life. A house of young death, and two young people from Toronto walking through it, decades ago, not yet knowing their own count.

And we went to find the Old Curiosity Shop, the crooked little building off Portsmouth Street that still trades on the name — though I will apply the standing tongs and tell you the claim is disputed, the building older than the novel, the sign very likely a bookseller’s clever retrofit. A shop that named itself after the book, rather than the book after the shop. Marilyn would have wanted me to say so. She was the one who taught me that loving the past and checking the past are the same devotion, not opposite ones.

The marriage did not last. The love of history did. And I have come to understand, somewhere around essay 1,500, that every number I have stopped for — every year I have audited, every cooked book I have tried to reopen, including Kingston’s — traces back to a woman walking a reluctant young husband through the rooms where Dickens wrote, teaching him to ask what the date was carrying. The counting outlived the marriage. In a sense I only fully see now, the counting is still hers.

I audit history because she showed me it was worth auditing. That is the entry. It has been sitting in the drawer for eighteen hundred essays, and 1,842 is the page I was always going to have to open, because it is the one with both of them in it — the auditor who was shown the good books, and the person who taught me to ask for the other ones.

The auditor at my feet

Dior cannot be curated, and I have come to think it is her finest quality. You cannot walk Dior down a clean corridor and hand her a flattering summary; she goes straight for the drawer you did not want opened, the pocket with the receipt in it, the spot on the floor the mop missed. She audits by nose and she does not accept the guided tour. Kingston could have shown Dickens anything it liked; it could have shown Dior nothing, because she would have been under the floggings-register desk inside a minute, reading the only page that mattered, and declining to sign.

The closer

So we leave Dickens in 1842 with two prison reports in his bag — one of the truest and one of the falsest things he ever wrote — and no way of knowing which was which, because the difference was never in his eyesight. It was in which books he was allowed to open.

The lesson travels. An auditor shown only the good pages will certify a place that flogs children, in perfect good faith, and his good faith will make the false total more durable, not less, because who will doubt a saint? The defence against it is not a better auditor. It is an open ledger — the right to see the page they did not hand you.

The next landmark is essay 1,844, and it is about the year that right got a new weapon. A man tapped out a short message on a machine that turned words into clicks and sent them down a wire faster than any horse, any ship, any courier in the history of the species — and distance, which had protected every cooked book and curated every visitor since the beginning, suddenly stopped counting. What a prison could hide from a travelling auditor in 1842, it would have a harder time hiding once the wire reached the gate.

What hath God wrought, the first message asked. We shall see. We usually do, eventually.

See you at 1,844.
