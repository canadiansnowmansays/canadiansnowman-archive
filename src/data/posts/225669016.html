At about four in the morning Toronto time on Thursday, 19 November 2026, Judge Yuji Iwasawa, president of the International Court of Justice, began reading a judgment in the Great Hall of Justice at the Peace Palace in The Hague. He read for more than two and a half hours. Most of that time went on cataloguing what Myanmar’s security forces did to the Rohingya of northern Rakhine State in 2016 and 2017: the killings, the sexual violence, the targeting of children and babies, the methodical burning of villages, the attacks on people trying to flee. Only near the end did he reach the sentence the room had been waiting seven years to hear.

By nine votes to six, the court found that Myanmar “has committed genocide against the Rohingya group in northern Rakhine State.”

As Jeanna Smialek reported from The Hague and Hannah Beech from the refugee camps of Cox’s Bazar, Bangladesh, for The New York Times, it was the first time a state, as opposed to an individual, had been held responsible for violating the 1948 Genocide Convention. The court that the United Nations Charter created in 1945 to settle disputes between countries had, after eighty-one years, finally used the word about one of them.

This series has been asking what the United Nations is for and whether it works. The four dispatches from New York in September covered the General Assembly at full volume. This one comes from the UN’s other headquarters, the quiet one in the Netherlands that most people forget belongs to the system at all. What happened there on Thursday was the best and the worst of the institution in a single morning. The law finally said the word. The machinery for making the word mean anything is the same machinery this series has been describing as broken since its essay on the veto.

What the court found

Genocide is the hardest charge in international law to prove, by design. The judge spent part of his reading on the legal definition, and it bears repeating because it is narrower than most people assume. Mass killing is not enough. Ethnic cleansing is not enough. Destroying a people’s culture, their mosques and schools and language, is not enough. What the Convention requires is the specific intent to destroy a group, in whole or in part, in a physical or biological sense. The perpetrators have to have meant it.

States do not usually write that intent down. So the court did what courts do with circumstantial cases: it looked at the pattern. Myanmar’s defence, as it had been since 2019, was that its forces were conducting counterinsurgency operations after Rohingya militants attacked police posts and an army base in August 2017. The court rejected that. It found that widespread sexual violence, the targeting of children and babies, the systematic burning of villages and attacks on fleeing civilians were not consistent with an intent to fight insurgents. Taking all the evidence together, the judge said, “the only reasonable inference” was an intention to destroy a substantial part of the group.

The numbers behind that inference are United Nations estimates: at least 10,000 people killed and some 400 villages burned in the “clearance operations” that began on 25 August 2017. Within weeks, around 750,000 Rohingya had crossed into Bangladesh, the fastest refugee exodus in recent history. Al Jazeera, reporting from The Hague, noted that the court identified four proven genocidal acts: killing, serious harm, forced displacement and measures to prevent births.

The court went further than the headline finding. It ruled that Myanmar had also violated its obligations to prevent genocide and to punish those responsible, that it had obstructed investigation, and that it had never passed the legislation the Convention requires. On one count it held back. Despite years of hate speech against the Rohingya spread by the military, the court did not find that Myanmar had directly and publicly incited genocide.

Then came the remedies. By fourteen votes to one, the court ordered Myanmar to stop any continuing violation of the Convention, to investigate anyone suspected of breaking it, and to give assurances that it would not happen again. Myanmar owes the Rohingya both restitution and compensation, with the amount to be set in future proceedings. It must make possible the voluntary and dignified return of those it drove out. And it must report back to the court on its progress within two years.

The word nobody would say

To understand why Thursday mattered, it helps to remember the last time the court faced this question about a state and flinched.

In February 2007, in the case Bosnia and Herzegovina brought against Serbia, the court accepted that genocide had taken place at Srebrenica in July 1995, where more than 7,000 Bosnian Muslim men and boys were murdered. It found that Serbia had failed to prevent it, and had failed to cooperate fully with the war crimes tribunal for the former Yugoslavia. But, as the court’s own press release set out, it found that Serbia had not itself committed genocide and had not been complicit in it. The killers were Bosnian Serb forces, and the court would not attribute their intent to Belgrade. In 2015 it dismissed both Croatia’s genocide claim against Serbia and Serbia’s counterclaim against Croatia.

That left an uncomfortable record. The Genocide Convention binds states. The only court that can rule on states had never found one guilty of the crime. Individuals had been convicted at the Rwanda and Yugoslavia tribunals and in the Cambodian courts. States had walked out of The Hague with findings of negligence. Al Jazeera’s correspondent described Thursday’s ruling as only the fourth time since the Holocaust that an international court has found genocide was committed. JusticeInfo.net, which covers international courts closely, called it what it was: the first time the UN’s court has condemned a state for the crime.

The vote was close. Six of the fifteen judges did not join the genocide finding, and their reasons will matter to lawyers for years. But nine is a majority, and the judgment is final.

A small country with standing

The case exists because a small West African nation decided that a crime half a world away was its business.

Gambia, a Muslim-majority country of fewer than three million people, brought the case in November 2019 on behalf of the Organization of Islamic Cooperation. It had no border with Myanmar, no Rohingya population and no obvious stake. What it had was the Convention itself, which treats the prevention of genocide as the common concern of every state that signed it. Myanmar challenged Gambia’s right to sue, and in 2022 the court ruled that Gambia had standing. Since then, eleven other countries have formally lent their support, among them the Maldives, Germany and the Democratic Republic of Congo.

The case also produced one of the stranger scenes in the court’s history. At the first hearings in December 2019, Myanmar was defended in person by Aung San Suu Kyi, then the country’s civilian leader and a Nobel Peace Prize winner for her years of nonviolent resistance to the generals. She stood in the Peace Palace and denied that genocide had taken place. Her supporters have always said she had little real power over the military, and the generals proved the point in 2021 when they overthrew her. She is now 81 and still in detention. The junta that ousted her inherited her defence.

Outside the court on Thursday, Dawda Jallow, Gambia’s attorney general, welcomed the finding of what he called the “crime of crimes” and promised that his country would stand with the Rohingya until justice is served.

Half a world away

In the camps of Cox’s Bazar, where some 1.2 million Rohingya now live in the largest refugee settlement on earth, hundreds of men in prayer caps and their best clothes gathered around an outdoor screen to watch a judge few of them could understand. When the verdict came, some cheered and some wept. Boys and men formed a circle and chanted the name of the country that had brought the case. One boy told Beech he thought Gambia was in Europe, though he wasn’t sure. It was, he said, a wonderful country.

The celebration was real. So was everything around it. Beech, who covered the 2017 exodus, described camps where life is getting worse, not better. The monsoons bring floods and landslides; the dry season brings fires that burn through bamboo shelters. Because of international funding shortfalls, monthly food aid has fallen from $12 a person to $10. That is per month. Rohingya who had been about to be resettled in the United States were told, after President Trump returned to office, that they were no longer welcome. Rival armed groups fight gun battles in the alleys, and families keep their teenagers indoors for fear of kidnapping and forced recruitment.

The violence did not end in 2017, either. Mohammed Yeasin told Beech that his wife and sister were killed in a drone attack in August 2024, carried out not by the military but by an armed group from the ethnic Rakhine community that shares the Rohingya homeland. He was glad of the ruling, he said, but did not know when his sorrows would end.

Most people Beech asked had not known the trial was happening at all. Shomina Ara, whose husband was killed by a mortar strike and who still carries the shrapnel in her body, said she did not really understand the word “genocide.” What she understood was that people in Myanmar wanted the Rohingya gone. It took the court nine years to reach the conclusion she had reached on her own.

Article 94 and the veto

Here is where this dispatch rejoins the rest of the series.

The International Court of Justice has no police, no army and no bailiffs. Under Article 94 of the UN Charter, every member state undertakes to comply with the court’s judgments in cases to which it is a party. If a state refuses, the other party may take the matter to the Security Council, which “may” decide on measures to give effect to the judgment. Note the word. The Charter does not require the Council to act, and the Council’s decisions on substantive matters are subject to the veto.

Readers of this series’ essay on the veto will know where this goes. As The New York Times noted, two permanent members, China and Russia, would most likely protect Myanmar. Beijing has spent years cultivating the generals, and Moscow has been one of the junta’s main arms suppliers. A draft resolution to enforce the judgment would very probably die in the chamber in New York with a single raised hand.

It would not be the first time. In 1986 the court ruled that the United States had broken international law by mining Nicaragua’s harbours and arming the Contras, and ordered it to pay reparations. Washington rejected the court’s jurisdiction, and when Nicaragua took the judgment to the Security Council, the United States vetoed the resolution calling for compliance. The veto exists to protect the great powers and their friends from exactly this. It was in the design from San Francisco onward, and it has been used to shield the powerful and their clients ever since.

There is a loophole, and this series’ essay on Korea described it. The Uniting for Peace procedure, invented during the Korean War to get around a Soviet veto, lets the General Assembly take up a question when the Council is deadlocked. The Assembly cannot impose binding sanctions, but it can recommend them, and it can put every member state on record. Whether anyone will try is another matter.

Even full enforcement might not reach the people it is meant to help. Myanmar has been at civil war since the 2021 coup. The junta does not control large parts of the country, including much of Rakhine State, so it could not organise a dignified return of the Rohingya even if it wanted to. A government spokeswoman in Naypyidaw did not answer a phone call seeking comment after the verdict. Maria Elena Vignoli of Human Rights Watch urged governments to “press Myanmar’s junta to comply with the judgment.” The National Unity Government, the exiled administration formed by lawmakers ousted in the coup, welcomed the ruling as an important moment for the Rohingya and called for an end to the flow of arms and money to the regime.

What a verdict is for

If the court cannot enforce its judgment and the Council will not, it is fair to ask what Thursday achieved.

Three things, at least. First, a legal fact now exists that did not exist on Wednesday. The court’s judgment is final and binding, and it cannot be appealed. Every future argument about the Rohingya, in parliaments, in sanctions committees and in asylum hearings, starts from a ruling that genocide happened and that a state did it. Second, the remedies outlive the generals. Compensation will be set in future proceedings, and an obligation to pay does not expire when a junta falls. If Myanmar ever has a government that wants to rejoin the world, this bill will be waiting for it. Third, the reasoning travels. The court has now shown how it reads intent from a pattern of conduct, and that will be studied closely in the other genocide case on its docket, South Africa’s claim against Israel over Gaza.

None of that feeds anyone in Cox’s Bazar on $10 a month. The distance between the Peace Palace and Jamtoli camp, between a nine-to-six vote and a teenage girl kept indoors so she will not be taken, is the distance this whole series has been measuring. The United Nations was built to say what is right and then ask the strongest powers for permission to act on it. On Thursday the first half worked, at last. The second half is now up to a chamber in New York where the answer is already fairly predictable.

At The Hague afterwards, Gambian and European lawyers posed for photographs in the lobby wearing sparkly sashes. In the camps, the crowds walked home through muddy alleys in the dark. Mohammed Yeasin Zuhar, 23, carried his 47-day-old son and told Beech the ruling filled him with joy, because it meant the boy would not have to grow up there.

He may be right. The law has done its part. The rest is up to the governments.
