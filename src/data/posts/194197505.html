Twenty-one families. $783 million. In the 2022 midterms, this tiny group of donors outspent the combined small-dollar contributions of millions of ordinary Americans. A vast crowd gathers on the National Mall — a visual reminder of how many voices are now drowned out by how few wallets. This is the post-Citizens United order: democracy in form, oligarchy in practice. A new nobility, not crowned by blood, but by money.

In the 2022 midterm elections, twenty-one donor families contributed $783 million to political causes. The millions of ordinary Americans who gave small amounts to candidates they believed in were outspent, collectively, by those twenty-one families.

Read that sentence again. Not because it is difficult to understand, but because it is easy to read past — one of those statistics that lands and then dissipates before its full weight can register. Twenty-one families. Seven hundred and eighty-three million dollars. An amount that exceeded the combined small-dollar donations of millions of Americans who each gave what they could afford.

This is the post-Citizens United landscape at its most concrete. Not a legal abstraction. Not a theoretical concern about the appearance of corruption. Twenty-one families, a number you could seat at a long dinner table, exercising more influence over the composition of the United States Congress than every ordinary donor in the country combined.

This is the new nobility. It does not wear titles. It does not hold hereditary offices. It does not need to. It holds something more useful: the ability to determine, with a level of influence that no democratic theory adequately accounts for, who runs for office, who wins, and what they owe when they get there.

The names change somewhat from cycle to cycle, but the roster of mega-donors in the post-Citizens United era has a consistent cast of principals whose motivations, strategies, and records of political investment are worth examining in some detail. Not to demonize individuals — though some of what follows is not flattering — but because the abstraction of “big money in politics” becomes considerably more useful when it has faces and interests attached to it.

Sheldon Adelson was a Las Vegas casino magnate who spent, by various estimates, between $150 million and $500 million on Republican political causes over the final decade of his life. His political priorities included opposition to online gambling — which competed with his casino business — support for Israeli right-wing governments, and the advancement of Republican candidates who would pursue those priorities. He gave $20 million to a super PAC supporting Newt Gingrich in the 2012 Republican primary, effectively keeping Gingrich’s campaign alive long past the point at which it would otherwise have collapsed. When Gingrich withdrew, Adelson transferred his support to Mitt Romney. Romney’s subsequent positions on Israeli policy were notably aligned with Adelson’s preferences.

This is not corruption in the legal sense. It is also not, by any candid assessment, the conduct of a democracy in which political power flows from the consent of the governed.

Charles and David Koch — the Kansas-based industrial and petrochemical billionaires — built the most sophisticated political money infrastructure in the post-Citizens United era. The Koch network, as it came to be known, was not merely a collection of donations to candidates. It was a system: a web of nonprofit organizations, think tanks, donor networks, voter data operations, and political advocacy groups that functioned as a parallel party infrastructure operating alongside — and often more effectively than — the Republican National Committee. Americans for Prosperity, the Koch brothers’ flagship political organization, spent hundreds of millions of dollars in multiple election cycles, primarily on anti-regulatory, anti-tax, and anti-climate-action messaging and candidate support.

The network was notable not only for its scale but for its sophistication. It conducted its own polling, developed its own candidate recruitment pipeline, ran its own voter mobilization operations. It was, in the language of political science, a shadow party — an organization with the resources and capabilities of a major political party, accountable to no one except the people who funded it.

George Soros, the Hungarian-American financier, is the left’s most prominent mega-donor and the right’s most reliable bogeyman. His political giving has focused on criminal justice reform, drug policy, immigration, and the election of progressive prosecutors at the local level — a strategy that is either a principled effort to reform a broken system or a billionaire attempting to purchase local government, depending on your politics. His 2022 giving was in the hundreds of millions. He funds organizations whose missions he supports. He expects those organizations to pursue those missions. This is, in formal terms, exactly what the First Amendment as interpreted by Citizens United permits. It is also, in functional terms, a wealthy individual shaping public policy through the deployment of private capital.

Peter Thiel is a different case, worth examining more carefully because his politics are more explicit about what the political money system is actually for. Thiel wrote in 2009 that he had given up on the reconciliation of freedom and democracy — that democratic politics had become incompatible with the economic liberty he valued. He has funded candidates, most notably J.D. Vance in Ohio and Blake Masters in Arizona, who represent a politics of concentrated executive authority, reduced democratic accountability, and systematic skepticism of the institutions that constrain private power. He gave $15 million to a super PAC supporting Vance. Vance won. Vance is now vice president of the United States.

Elon Musk’s political spending in the 2024 election cycle exceeded $250 million, making him the largest individual political donor in American history by a substantial margin. He has since been appointed to a position — leading the Department of Government Efficiency — from which he has exercised extraordinary influence over the federal government’s operations. Whether this constitutes a return on his political investment or a coincidence of shared vision is a question worth sitting with.

The academic literature on the relationship between political donations and policy outcomes is extensive, contested, and ultimately more honest than the donor class would prefer.

The honest finding is this: money does not buy votes in the crude transactional sense that most corruption statutes contemplate. Politicians do not generally change their positions in direct response to individual donations, in ways that can be demonstrated in court. What money buys is more diffuse and more durable: access, agenda-setting, the recruitment of candidates who share the donor’s priorities before they are elected, and the creation of policy environments in which the donor’s interests are well-represented without anyone needing to be explicit about it. This dynamic is heaviest on economic issues — taxation, financial regulation, trade, healthcare — where donor preferences diverge most sharply from those of ordinary voters. On social or foreign policy questions where the wealthy have less concentrated financial stake, the influence is lighter and less systematic.

Political scientists call this “investment theory of party competition” — the idea that political parties and candidates are, in effect, investment vehicles for economic interests, and that the returns on those investments come not through explicit transactions but through the systematic alignment of policy outcomes with donor preferences over time.

The evidence for this theory is substantial. Studies of roll call voting in Congress have consistently found that on issues where donor preferences diverge from constituent preferences — financial regulation, tax policy, trade policy, healthcare — legislators’ votes tend to align more closely with donor preferences than with constituent preferences. This is not a left-wing talking point. It is a finding from peer-reviewed political science research conducted by scholars across the ideological spectrum.

None of this is illegal. All of it is, in the Roberts Court’s framework, constitutionally protected. The Court has said that ingratiation and access do not constitute corruption. The political science literature has found that ingratiation and access produce systematic policy outcomes that favor the ingratiating over the unrepresented. The Court has said this is not its problem.

The particular geography of the new nobility’s political investments reveals something important about the mechanism.

The new nobility does not distribute its money across the full range of contested political questions. It concentrates heavily on a specific set of issues where its financial interests are directly at stake: taxation of wealth and capital, regulation of financial markets and industries, antitrust enforcement, environmental regulation, labor law. On these issues — which are also, not coincidentally, the issues where the preferences of the wealthy diverge most sharply from the preferences of ordinary Americans — the political investment is heaviest, the candidate selection most deliberate, and the alignment of policy outcomes most consistent.

On issues where the wealthy do not have a concentrated financial stake — social policy, foreign policy, identity politics — the investment is lighter and the influence less systematic. The new nobility is not trying to purchase all of American politics. It is trying to purchase the specific parts that affect its wealth.

This is rational. It is also, from the perspective of democratic theory, precisely the problem. The democratic promise is that political outcomes reflect the preferences of citizens. The campaign finance reality is that political outcomes reflect the preferences of citizens weighted by their capacity to finance political activity — which means, in the post-Citizens United world, that the preferences of twenty-one families carry more weight than the preferences of everyone else combined on the issues those families care most about.

There is a Gilded Age quality to all of this that is not accidental.

The original Gilded Age — the period of unconstrained corporate political power that produced the Tillman Act of 1907 — was an era in which the railroad magnates and oil barons and steel kings exercised political influence in ways that were not always subtle. Mark Hanna raised $3.5 million from corporations to elect William McKinley in 1896 and was not embarrassed about it. The influence was direct, visible, and, eventually, politically unsustainable — which is why the Tillman Act passed, and why the Progressive Era reforms that followed it had broad popular support.

The new nobility is not embarrassed either. Elon Musk spent $250 million electing Donald Trump and was rewarded with a government position. Peter Thiel’s investment in J.D. Vance is now yielding a vice presidency. The transactions are visible to anyone who looks. What has changed since 1896 is not the behavior but the legal framework: what Mark Hanna did was eventually prohibited, prosecuted, and reformed. What Musk and Thiel are doing is constitutionally protected.

The Gilded Age had a Progressive Era. What the new Gilded Age has is a Supreme Court that has decided the Progressive Era’s reforms were unconstitutional.
