When this series closed in August, Part X ended with a promise kept deliberately modest. If any of the open threads ever resolved, this newsletter would tell those stories separately, as they happened, rather than pretend the file was finished. One of the threads it named was the confirmation itself. Todd Blanche became Attorney General by a 50-to-49 vote, and one of the fifty was Senator Dan S. Sullivan of Alaska.

On October 22, 2026, twelve days before Election Day, Mr. Blanche’s Justice Department went to federal court to protect Senator Sullivan’s seat.

That is not quite how the department describes it. The department says it is protecting the integrity of a federal election. But the lawsuit has an unusual property for an integrity case: it only switches on if one particular candidate loses. A blank cheque, it turns out, can be written in either direction.

I. The Filing

The suit was filed in the U.S. District Court for the District of Alaska in Anchorage, against the state and its election officials. As Devlin Barrett reported for The New York Times on October 23, it was signed by a senior department official, Stanley Woodward, and it asks a federal judge to step into a state-run election before a single general-election ballot has been counted.

The theory runs like this. Senator Sullivan, a two-term Republican, is in a close race with Democrat Mary Peltola, a former congresswoman. Also on the ballot is a retired Petersburg schoolteacher named Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. The department says confusion between the two names could cost the senator enough votes to lose. The filing asks that Alaska not certify any result that may be impacted by voter confusion, and it defines the trigger with two conditions. First, Senator Sullivan does not win. Second, if every vote cast for the other Sullivan had instead gone to the senator, and Alaska’s ranked-choice count played out from there, the senator would have won.

If both conditions are met, the department wants certification held until there is an investigation into whether votes for Daniel J. Sullivan were really meant for the senator, plus whatever further relief the court sees fit.

Note what is missing. There is no mechanism for this investigation, because there cannot be one. Alaska, like every American state, uses a secret ballot. Nobody can knock on a door in Bethel or Fairbanks and ask which Sullivan a voter had in mind, and no ballot carries a name that would let anyone try. The remedy is not an inquiry. It is a pause, with no defined end, applied to one outcome and not the other.

II. A Question Alaska Already Answered

The awkward part for the department is that none of this is new. Alaska has already litigated the two-Sullivan problem, quickly and thoroughly, through its own courts.

Daniel J. Sullivan, a former U.S. Forest Service worker and elementary teacher, filed to run on May 29, 2026, with a campaign site that advertised one Dan Sullivan challenging another, as Reuters reported. The state’s Division of Elections, which answers to Republican Lieutenant Governor Nancy Dahlstrom, removed him from the ballot on the theory that he was a decoy. He sued. On June 26, as the Juneau Independent reported, Superior Court Judge Thomas Matthews ruled the removal illegal, holding that the Division had no legal authority to judge a candidate’s motives when deciding whether his candidacy is legitimate.

The state appealed. Three days later, on June 29, the Alaska Supreme Court affirmed, ordering him onto the primary ballot and sending the question of how he should be listed back to the Division, as Alaska Public Media reported. The full weight of the Republican establishment had pressed the other way. Attorneys general from fourteen Republican-led states filed a brief asking the court to keep him off, according to the Associated Press. They lost.

What the courts did instead was the ordinary, sensible thing: distinguish the candidates on the ballot itself. The Petersburg Sullivan appears without a party designation, while the senator is listed as the Republican incumbent. The voters then had their say in the August 18 primary. According to the Alaska Beacon, Senator Sullivan took 41.4 per cent and Daniel J. Sullivan took 2.5 per cent. Under Alaska’s system, the top four advance, so both are on the November ballot alongside Ms. Peltola.

The federal government’s interest arrived on primary day. Grand jury subpoenas were issued in an investigation into whether the challenger entered the race to confuse voters, a source told Reuters in an August 18 report. Republicans had an argument from the start, and it deserves stating fairly. As the Times has reported, the challenger registered as a Republican only shortly before the filing deadline, appeared to be working with a Democratic strategist, and seems to have a history of donating to Democratic candidates, Ms. Peltola among them. Daniel J. Sullivan has always said he entered the race of his own accord, and the Peltola campaign and the Alaska Democratic Party have repeatedly denied any part in his candidacy. All of this was put to Alaska’s courts, which ruled that the state had no power to judge a candidate’s motives. In early September, President Trump twice brought the race up in public remarks, telling Republican lawmakers that the Democrats had found a man named Dan Sullivan to run against his candidate.

On October 28, those suspicions acquired a paper trail. In a long investigation for The New York Times, Kellen Browning reported that Democrats had done more or less what Republicans accused them of. In the summer of 2025, a Peltola staffer was told to search voter records for every Dan Sullivan in Alaska. What followed was an operation known internally as the ballot composition project. According to the Times, it involved senior aides at the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee and an Ohio operative named Jerid Kurtz, who travelled the state under an assumed name and pitched would-be spoiler candidates over beers. Mr. Kurtz reportedly told the team he had found a Sullivan in Petersburg who disliked Mr. Trump, liked Ms. Peltola, and would run if it helped her. The project did not stop at one name. Mr. Kurtz also met David Leslie, a progressive Democrat who had advanced from the primary, and Mr. Leslie withdrew on August 31. That moved a second Republican long shot, Gerald Heikes, onto the ballot in his place. Mr. Heikes says he was recruited in the spring by a man from the Midwest. When the Alaska courts kept the other Sullivan on the ballot in June, a committee staffer told an internal group chat, “Other Dan won, will be on ballot,” and three colleagues answered with heart emojis.

The account has soft spots, and they should be stated plainly. Its most detailed source, Elisa Rios, is Ms. Peltola’s former campaign manager. She was fired in August, had been preparing a wrongful-dismissal suit, and tried last week to retract everything she had told the paper. The Times published anyway, saying her account matched the rest of its reporting, which drew on twelve people and dozens of text messages. Ms. Rios says Ms. Peltola was not directly involved but was aware. The Peltola campaign and the committee both deny recruiting anyone, and neither answered the paper’s detailed questions. Daniel J. Sullivan, who now describes himself as a moderate Republican, did not respond.

So the sequence is: the state tried to remove him, the state’s courts said no, the voters weighed in, and federal prosecutors opened a grand jury probe. Then, with no indictment announced, the department went to federal court to ask for the one remedy the state courts would never have contemplated. It wants to keep the result of a real election in a drawer. Six days after the filing, the Times suggested the Republican suspicion had been right all along.

Then the story moved again. On October 29, according to two officials who spoke to The New York Times, the Justice Department issued dozens of subpoenas to people believed to be connected to the ballot-stacking plan. Whether any charges will follow is unclear. The Republican chairman of a House oversight committee has separately sought interviews with two of those involved. That evening, at a rally in Juneau, Ms. Peltola addressed the matter directly for the first time. She said she had known nothing about any candidates on the ballot other than herself and the incumbent, and added, “I am not interested in tricking voters.” It is a flat denial, and it sits squarely against Ms. Rios’s account that the candidate was “definitely aware.” Earlier in the week Ms. Peltola had dismissed the revelations as just another distraction. One of those accounts is wrong, and nobody outside the campaign can yet say which. That question belongs to testimony under oath, not to a certification freeze. The Times reporting changes the politics of the case. As the sections below show, it does not change the law.

III. The Arithmetic of a One-Way Ratchet

The filing’s two-part trigger repays close reading, because it is cleverer than it looks.

Alaska counts its general election by ranked choice. If no candidate wins a majority of first choices, the last-place candidate is eliminated and those ballots move to each voter’s next ranked choice. That continues until someone crosses fifty per cent. In a four-candidate race, Daniel J. Sullivan will almost certainly be eliminated early, and his ballots will flow to whoever his voters ranked second.

Think about what that means for the confused voter the department is worried about. A supporter of the senator who mistakenly marked the wrong Sullivan first would very likely have ranked the right Sullivan second. When the challenger is eliminated, that ballot travels to the senator. Ranked choice is, by design, the system best equipped to repair exactly this kind of mistake.

The department’s test does not allow for that repair. It does not ask where the challenger’s ballots actually went. It hands every one of them to the senator, including the votes of Alaskans who chose the Petersburg Sullivan on purpose, perhaps as a protest against the incumbent, and who then ranked Ms. Peltola second or ranked no one at all. Under the department’s counterfactual, those deliberate choices are reassigned to the candidate those voters chose not to support.

Now set that test against the polling. In a four-way InsiderAdvantage survey taken October 9 and 10, Ms. Peltola led in the first round, and a simulated final ranked-choice round put her at 50.1 per cent to the senator’s 48.0, according to Newsweek. A Data for Progress poll released October 20 showed her ahead 51 to 49 after the ranked-choice rounds, as Political Wire reported. In the August primary, as Kellen Browning noted in the Times, Ms. Peltola finished nearly 15,000 votes ahead of the senator, while the other Sullivan drew about 4,000. Even if every one of those 4,000 had gone to the incumbent, the order would not have changed. The trigger only matters in a close race, and a close race is exactly what the polls describe. If the challenger’s November share resembles his August one, it is roughly the size of the gap. The department has designed a trigger that fires in precisely the outcome the polls say is most likely: a narrow Peltola win.

The asymmetry is the point. Under this test, a narrow Sullivan win is certified on schedule. A narrow Peltola win is frozen pending an investigation that cannot be conducted. Heads we win, tails don’t count.

IV. The Lawyers in the Building

Readers of the earlier parts of this series will recognise the shape of the department now bringing this case.

Mr. Blanche was Donald Trump’s lead criminal defence lawyer before he ran the Justice Department. Stanley Woodward, who signed the Alaska filing, became Associate Attorney General, the department’s third-ranking post, in November 2025. Before government, he represented Walt Nauta, the president’s valet and co-defendant in the classified-documents case, as well as Kash Patel, now the F.B.I. director, and several January 6 defendants. When he was nominated in April 2025, Andrew Goudsward of Reuters noted that confirmation would mean the department’s top three officials had all personally defended the president or his close associates.

Until the afternoon of October 22, a defender of the department could at least argue that the suit was the department’s own idea. The president removed that argument himself. Hours after the filing, as Devlin Barrett reported in the Times’ live coverage, Mr. Trump told reporters: “I’m the one that said do it.” The sequence is now on the record in his own words. In August, Senator Sullivan was one of the votes that put Mr. Blanche in the attorney general’s chair by a single-vote margin. In October, the president directed Mr. Blanche’s department to sue to keep the senator’s seat out of the Democratic column if he loses it, and then said so out loud. The department’s spokeswoman calls the other Sullivan’s candidacy frivolous. The senator’s own campaign sounds notably cooler, saying only that it will let the complaint run its course and that Alaskans can tell the difference between the candidates. This series spent ten parts documenting what this Justice Department declines to investigate: the shootings in Minneapolis, Houston and Biddeford, and the medical unit at Delaney Hall. It is worth noting which matters do get its urgent attention, how quickly, and on whose instruction.

V. What the Courts Are Likely to Say

The legal community’s verdict arrived almost as fast as the filing. Notre Dame law professor Derek Muller, one of the country’s more careful election-law scholars and not a reflexive partisan, wrote that the department’s argument was “one of the worst ones I’ve ever seen,” as Reuters reported. His broader point, as he told the AP in its October 23 report, is that the federal statutes the department relies on simply do not give it the power to stop a state from certifying.

David Becker of the Center for Election Innovation and Research, who advises election officials of both parties, told the Times he could not recall the federal government ever trying to block certification, let alone eleven days before an election. The federal government, he said, has no authority at all to tell Alaska when or how to certify, and the state’s officials are simply following state law, as the Constitution requires.

Alaska has some history here, and it does not favour the department. In 2010, Republican Joe Miller challenged the count of write-in ballots that gave Senator Lisa Murkowski her famous write-in victory. A federal judge briefly paused certification, but only so that Alaska’s own courts could decide the state-law questions first, as NPR reported at the time. The Alaska Supreme Court then refused to overturn the result, and Mr. Miller stepped aside rather than block certification, as NBC News reported. Even when the challenge came from a losing candidate with an actual dispute over actual ballots, the federal court deferred to the state. Here, the challenge comes from the federal executive, about ballots not yet cast, on a question the state’s highest court has already settled.

Does the Times investigation rescue the suit? In court, probably not. Recruiting spoiler candidates is ugly, but it is generally legal, and both parties have done it, in Alaska and well beyond it. Two years ago, the state Republican Party backed an effort to keep a Democrat, a man running his campaign from a New York prison, on the ballot against Ms. Peltola in her House race. A short history of the practice published by The New York Times on October 29 runs from two Joseph Russos on John F. Kennedy’s first primary ballot in 1946 to two Jerry Garcias in a Texas constable race in 2020. The line that matters is money. In Florida in 2020, a former Republican state senator, Frank Artiles, recruited a friend named Alexis Rodriguez to run against an incumbent Democrat named José Javier Rodríguez, who lost by 32 votes. The decoy later pleaded guilty, and Mr. Artiles was convicted in 2024 of campaign finance violations for paying him to run, a verdict he has appealed. The Alaska Supreme Court has already held that motive is no test for getting onto the ballot, and nothing in the Times story repairs the remedy. If the other Sullivan was planted, his voters still cast secret ballots, some of them deliberately, and no investigation can establish which Sullivan each of them meant. If anyone broke a law, Florida shows where to look: whether money changed hands, and whether campaigns and super PACs coordinated illegally. The proper response would be what Florida did, a prosecution of the people who ran the operation, not an asterisk on the votes of Alaskans who had nothing to do with it.

The most likely outcome is that a judge in Anchorage dismisses the case or denies relief, and the department appeals, or doesn’t. Either way, the courts will probably stop it.

VI. Why It Matters Even If It Loses

So why file it at all? Mr. Muller’s answer, as Reuters reported it, is that the department is trying to win the press release rather than the lawsuit, and that answer deserves to be taken seriously.

A lawsuit like this does not need to succeed to work. It places a sentence in the public record that reads, in effect, “the federal government warned that this result might be tainted.” If Ms. Peltola wins by two points on November 3, that sentence will be waiting. It will be quoted on cable news, cited in fundraising emails, and repeated by anyone who would prefer that the result be seen as illegitimate. The suit pre-loads the grievance before the votes exist. And thanks to the Democrats’ own operatives, the grievance now has a true story attached to it. That makes it much harder to wave away, and the party has no one to blame for that but itself.

It also fits a pattern. At a rally in Alaska the week before the filing, as Zolan Kanno-Youngs reported for The New York Times on October 15, Mr. Trump called ranked-choice voting a way to cheat and said he had instructed the U.S. attorney in Alaska to monitor the vote tallies closely. The same month, the administration sued California over what it calls 20,000 likely noncitizens on its voter rolls. In the final week it went further, issuing federal grand jury subpoenas to California’s local election officials, and the courts twice refused its demand that the state set those 20,000 ballots aside. The Washington Post framed the Alaska suit against broader concerns about whether the administration will accept the midterm results at all. The public has noticed. In a New York Times/Siena poll of likely voters conducted October 12 to 19, half said they were worried Republicans might try to overturn the results through illegal means, against 31 per cent who said the same of Democrats. The share who do not trust the count to be accurate has risen to 23 per cent, up from 17 per cent before the 2024 presidential election.

The point was made bluntly on PBS’s Washington Week with The Atlantic in its last broadcast before the vote. One panellist noted that Mr. Trump had ordered the certification suit before the Times reported how the second Sullivan reached the ballot, and asked how the Justice Department proposed to establish what any individual voter intended. Would agents knock on doors and ask which Sullivan someone had meant? Another pointed out that Alaska was always going to be slow and confusing, since it counts absentee ballots postmarked by Election Day for up to 15 days and then runs a ranked-choice tabulation. The scandal does not create that silence. It fills it with suspicion. And in the August primary, the panel recalled, Ms. Peltola beat both Dan Sullivans combined. She did not need a decoy to win then. If she wins narrowly now, by fewer votes than the other Sullivan collects, the margin will become the argument, whatever the law says.

The stakes are not small. Control of the Senate could turn on Alaska, a state President Trump carried by about fourteen points in 2024 and where his party now trails in the polls. A department that wanted to signal it would stand behind whatever the voters decide had an easy option. It could have said nothing. Instead, it chose to tell Alaskans, before they vote, that one possible outcome comes with an asterisk.

In the language this newsletter uses to track the administration, this is the “Threaten” stage of the familiar cycle. What comes next depends less on the courts, which seem likely to hold, than on whether Alaska’s officials and Alaska’s voters treat the threat as what it is: noise.

A Note From the Canadian Side of the Border

Stephen Leacock wrote about elections with real affection for their absurdities. In Sunshine Sketches of a Little Town, Mariposa’s great election turns on rumour, patronage and the whisky question, and the town’s hotel keeper ends up in Parliament. What Leacock never imagined, even in satire, was the national government suing in advance to stop the count from standing.

Canadian elections have their own quirks, but this particular move would be hard to stage here. Federal ridings are run by Elections Canada, an independent agency that reports to Parliament rather than to the government of the day. A disputed close count goes to a judicial recount before a judge, and a contested result goes to the courts on application by a voter or a candidate. The Attorney General of Canada has no role in deciding when a riding’s result becomes final, and no minister would expect to be given one.

There are two Dan Sullivans on the Alaska ballot, and it now appears that one of them was put there on purpose. Alaskans are entitled to weigh that when they vote, and to hold it against whoever arranged it. But only one of the two is the incumbent senator, and the ballot says so in plain type. The voters of Alaska, as their own Supreme Court has observed, are perfectly capable of reading it. The question this filing actually raises is not whether Alaskans can tell two candidates apart, or even whether one party played dirty to confuse them. It is whether the people now running the Justice Department can tell the difference between protecting an election and protecting a result.

Update, November 1, 2026

Section V predicted that a judge in Anchorage would dismiss the case. On Sunday, two days before the vote, Chief Judge Sharon Gleason of the U.S. District Court in Alaska did exactly that, as reported by The New York Times. Her reasons are the ones this essay set out. On the secret ballot, she found that the government had offered no answer at all to how ballots could be segregated, and a law-enforcement inquiry into certain voters’ intent conducted, without breaking the secrecy that the whole system rests on. No rebuttal, no explanation, no mechanism.

On the Constitution, she took the government at its word and then read its own requests back to it. Federal lawyers had said in their filings that they were not trying to interfere in the time, place and manner of Alaska’s election, matters the Constitution leaves to the states. The judge listed what they had actually asked for: an order stopping certification if certain results came in, an order requiring the state to set aside certain ballots, and an order letting the United States investigate what the voters who chose Daniel J. Sullivan really meant. Those requests, she wrote, “certainly involve the time, place, and manner” of the election. She added that federal intervention in the middle of an election would itself produce the very voter confusion the government claimed it wanted to prevent. Last month she had already noted that the suit arrived after Alaskans had begun voting, and nearly four months after the Alaska Supreme Court ordered the second Sullivan onto the ballot.

It was the administration’s third election defeat in three days. On Friday, a federal court in California barred it from threatening to prosecute state and local officials who do not follow its ballot-handling directives, and a federal appeals panel refused to order California to set aside the roughly 20,000 mail-in ballots the administration says went to possible noncitizens. In Alaska, the Justice Department filed a notice of appeal the same day, saying the judge had ruled on procedure and had not disputed its claim that the second Sullivan was a frivolous candidate.

None of this settles how Daniel J. Sullivan came to be on the ballot. That question remains open, and it belongs where it always belonged: with Governor Mike Dunleavy’s criminal investigation of the Peltola campaign, and with any prosecutor who finds that money or coordination crossed a legal line. What the ruling settles is that the answer cannot be extracted from the ballots of Alaskans who had no part in it.

The coin has been taken away. The call has not. On CNN on Sunday, Senator Sullivan promised to support whatever result the voters deliver, and in the same interview suggested, without saying how or by whom, that the election could be stolen. The lawsuit was dismissed. The doubt it was built to plant is still on the ballot.

Update, November 8, 2026

The count has landed where the lawsuit’s test was designed to bite. On the state’s unofficial tally as of Wednesday, November 4, Peltola led by 637 votes, and Daniel J. Sullivan had about 3,600, according to Newsweek. The ranked-choice tabulation is on November 18, and certification on November 25. If the senator overtakes her when the other Sullivan’s ballots are redistributed, as Section III suggested many would be, the question disappears. If he does not, the appeal is what comes next.
