In July 1991, India was three weeks from defaulting on its sovereign debt. The government’s foreign exchange reserves had fallen to approximately $1.2 billion — enough to cover roughly two weeks of imports. The country was, in the blunt language of finance, broke. Gold reserves were being airlifted to London as collateral for emergency loans from the International Monetary Fund. The economy that Nehru had spent forty years building — the planned economy, the licence raj, the temples of modern India — was collapsing under its own weight.

The Prime Minister was P.V. Narasimha Rao, a quiet, polyglot Congressman from Andhra Pradesh who had not been anyone’s first choice for the job. He had been preparing to retire from politics when Rajiv Gandhi was assassinated in May 1991, and the Congress Party, desperate and leaderless, turned to him as a safe pair of hands. Rao chose as his Finance Minister a man who had never held elected office and who was not, in any conventional sense, a politician.

Manmohan Singh was an Oxford-trained economist who had spent his career in the Indian bureaucracy — the Planning Commission, the Reserve Bank of India, the Finance Ministry. He was soft-spoken, turbaned, Sikh, and possessed of an analytical clarity that was the antithesis of Indian political rhetoric. He stood before Parliament on July 24, 1991, and delivered a budget speech that dismantled the economic model India had followed since independence.

The licence raj was over. India was open for business.

I. What Was Dismantled

To understand what Singh did, you have to understand what he was dismantling.

The Nehruvian economic model was built on the premise that the state should control the commanding heights of the economy. Heavy industry — steel, coal, power, telecommunications, defence — was reserved for the public sector. Private enterprise required government licences for nearly every significant economic activity: to start a business, to expand production, to import raw materials, to change a product line. The number of licences required to manufacture a single product could reach double digits. A famous anecdote — possibly apocryphal, certainly representative — holds that an Indian entrepreneur needed 80 separate approvals to produce a new design of toothbrush.

The system protected Indian industry from foreign competition through tariffs that were among the highest in the world — averaging over 100 percent on many categories. It redistributed economic activity geographically through regulations that determined where factories could be built. It controlled prices, dictated wages, and managed foreign exchange through a regime that made the rupee effectively non-convertible.

The system also produced an economy that, by 1991, had delivered four decades of growth averaging 3.5 percent — barely ahead of population growth, insufficient to make a meaningful dent in poverty, and falling further behind every year as the Asian Tigers — South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong — demonstrated what export-led growth could achieve. China, which had begun its own liberalization under Deng Xiaoping in 1978, was already growing at rates that made India’s performance look like standing still.

The licence raj had created a culture of bureaucratic power that was self-perpetuating. Every licence was an opportunity for a government official to extract a bribe. Every regulation created a constituency of beneficiaries who would resist its removal. The system was not merely inefficient. It was corrupt by design — not because the designers intended corruption, but because a system that requires government permission for every economic decision inevitably creates a market for that permission.

II. What Was Built

Singh’s reforms were sweeping in their scope and cautious in their rhetoric. He quoted Victor Hugo — “No power on earth can stop an idea whose time has come” — and proceeded to demolish the apparatus that had governed Indian economic life for four decades.

Industrial licensing was abolished for all but a handful of strategic sectors. Import tariffs were slashed from an average of over 100 percent to 30 percent over the following years. The rupee was devalued and eventually made convertible on the current account. Foreign direct investment was welcomed in sectors that had been closed to it for decades. Public sector monopolies were broken. The stock market was reformed. Tax rates were reduced.

The effects were not instantaneous, but they were transformative. India’s GDP growth rate, which had averaged 3.5 percent from independence to 1991, rose to an average of 6 to 7 percent in the following decades, occasionally touching 9 percent. The IT sector — effectively nonexistent before liberalization — exploded. Bangalore, Hyderabad, Pune, and Gurgaon became global technology hubs. Indian software companies — Infosys, Wipro, TCS — grew from startups to multinational corporations. The Indian middle class, variously estimated at 300 to 600 million people depending on the definition, emerged as a global consumer market and a political force.

This is the India my roommate Rikesh comes from. He works in IT. He is part of a generation of Indian professionals who were produced by the liberalization — educated in India’s technical institutions, trained in English (Macaulay’s colonial language repurposed as a global competitive advantage), and equipped with skills that the global economy was hungry for. His presence in my Toronto townhouse is, in a very literal sense, a product of 1991. Before liberalization, the Indian IT professional class that now staffs Silicon Valley, powers outsourcing, and runs tech departments in every major city in the Western world did not exist in anything like its current form.

III. What Was Not Changed

The triumphalist narrative of Indian liberalization — the “India Shining” story that the BJP would later attempt to ride to electoral victory — obscures as much as it reveals.

Liberalization created wealth. It did not distribute it. India in the post-reform era has produced more billionaires per unit of GDP than almost any comparable economy, while simultaneously maintaining one of the largest populations living in absolute poverty. The Gini coefficient — the standard measure of income inequality — has risen steadily since 1991. The gap between urban India and rural India, between the IT professional in Bangalore and the farmer in Bihar, between the English-speaking elite and the Hindi-speaking masses, has widened in ways that the reformers did not anticipate and that the political system has struggled to address.

Agriculture, which still employs roughly 40 percent of India’s workforce, was largely untouched by liberalization. Indian farmers — small landholders operating on marginal plots, dependent on monsoon rains, indebted to local moneylenders, unable to access the credit and insurance systems that protect farmers in developed economies — saw little benefit from the IT boom. Farmer suicides became a national crisis in the 2000s and 2010s, with hundreds of thousands of indebted farmers taking their own lives. The farm laws that Modi attempted to pass in 2020 — ostensibly a further liberalization of agricultural markets — triggered the largest sustained protest movement in Indian history, led by Punjabi farmers who saw the laws as a corporate takeover of the food system.

Gurneet’s Punjab is at the centre of this story. Punjab is the breadbasket of India, the state that powered the Green Revolution, the state whose farmers feed the nation. It is also a state in crisis — agricultural debt, water depletion, a drug epidemic that has devastated a generation, and the sense that the liberalization that enriched Bangalore and Mumbai passed Punjab by. The farmers’ protest of 2020–2021, which blockaded Delhi for over a year and forced Modi into one of his rare retreats, was a Punjabi revolt. It was also a reminder that India’s economic transformation is incomplete, contested, and felt very differently depending on which India you inhabit.

IV. The Two Indias

The most consequential outcome of liberalization is the emergence of what economists and journalists have called “the two Indias” — a formulation that is a simplification but a useful one.

One India is urban, English-speaking, globally connected, technologically fluent, and upwardly mobile. It shops at malls, watches Netflix, works in air-conditioned offices, and aspires to the consumer lifestyle of the Western middle class. Its children attend private schools. Its professionals hold foreign passports or green cards. Its cultural reference points are as much American and British as they are Indian. This India has benefited enormously from liberalization. It is the India that the world sees in tech conferences, in Bollywood exports, in the Indian diaspora’s success stories.

The other India is rural, vernacular, locally rooted, and economically precarious. It depends on agriculture, manual labour, and the informal economy. It speaks Hindi or regional languages, not English. Its children attend government schools of wildly variable quality. Its relationship to the global economy is as a supplier of cheap labour, not as a participant in knowledge-economy wealth creation. This India has 800 million people enrolled in the National Food Security Act — a program that provides subsidized grain to roughly 60 percent of the population. Eight hundred million people in a country that has a space program and nuclear weapons.

Both Indias vote. Both Indias matter politically. And the tension between them — between the aspirational urban middle class and the struggling rural majority — is the fissure that every Indian political movement since 1991 has tried to exploit.

Nehru’s Congress promised socialism and delivered bureaucracy. The BJP promises development and delivers Hindu nationalism. Neither party has found a formula that genuinely bridges the two Indias. The closest anyone has come is the promise of “development” — a word that in Indian politics functions the way “freedom” functions in American politics, meaning everything and nothing, invoked by all sides and defined by none.

V. The Global Indian

Liberalization did not merely change India. It changed the world’s relationship with India.

Before 1991, India was, in the Western imagination, a land of poverty, spirituality, and backwardness — a place where hippies went to find themselves and aid agencies went to find clients. After 1991, India became a market, a partner, a competitor, and — increasingly — a power. The Indian diaspora, which had been emigrating for decades but in relatively modest numbers, became a global phenomenon. Indians are now the CEOs of Google, Microsoft, and IBM. Indian-Americans are the highest-income ethnic group in the United States. The Indian diaspora in Canada — roughly 1.8 million people of Indian origin, the largest visible minority group in the country — has transformed Canadian politics, cuisine, culture, and the real estate market of every major city.

I live in this transformation. My roommates are it. Rikesh came to Canada because liberalization created the educational and professional infrastructure that equipped him for a global career. Gurneet came because the Punjabi diaspora — one of the oldest and most established migration networks in the world — provided the community connections that make relocation possible. Ugyen, from Bhutan, is part of a related but distinct migration pattern — South Asians moving within and beyond the subcontinent through networks of language, kinship, and opportunity that the West barely sees.

The global Indian is one of the great success stories of the post-Cold War era. It is also a story with complications. Brain drain — the loss of India’s best-educated citizens to Western economies — remains a contentious issue. Remittances flow back, but talent does not. The India that produces world-class engineers and doctors exports them to countries that did not bear the cost of their education. And the diaspora itself is politically complex — Indian-Americans and Indian-Canadians do not form a monolithic voting bloc. They are divided by the same caste, regional, and religious lines that divide India itself, and the politics of the homeland — particularly the BJP’s brand of Hindu nationalism — has extended into diaspora communities in ways that are reshaping the politics of their adopted countries.

VI. The Stage Is Set

Liberalization gave India an economic engine. It did not give India an answer to the question that has defined this series: How does a country this diverse hold together?

Nehru’s answer was secularism, planning, and the moral authority of the independence movement. That answer exhausted itself by the 1980s. The Congress Party’s monopoly broke. The licence raj collapsed. The intellectual framework that had governed India since 1947 — socialist economics, non-alignment, the Nehruvian consensus — was dismantled, partly by its own failures and partly by the reforms of 1991.

What replaced it? For a decade after liberalization, the answer was unclear. Coalition governments came and went. Regional parties gained power. The BJP — the political arm of Hindu nationalism — entered government for the first time, briefly, in 1996, and then returned with greater strength in 1998 under Atal Bihari Vajpayee, who governed as a moderate within a coalition. India tested nuclear weapons. India opened further to the world. India grew.

But something else was happening beneath the surface. A movement that had been building since the 1920s — patient, organized, ideologically coherent, and utterly committed to redefining what India meant — was approaching its moment. The economic transformation of 1991 had created a new middle class that was aspirational, nationalistic, and receptive to a message that combined economic ambition with civilizational pride.

The saffron turn was coming.
