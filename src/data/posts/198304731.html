Ninety Minutes

The nine-person jury — six women, three men — began deliberating on Monday morning, May 18, 2026. They had spent three weeks listening to testimony from billionaires, board members, forensic accountants, law professors, and the chief executives of two of the most valuable companies on earth. They had seen hundreds of exhibits. They had heard a bridge metaphor, a golden jackass story, and the phrase “are you completely trustworthy?” They had been asked to decide a $150 billion question about the future of artificial intelligence.

They returned in ninety minutes.

The verdict was unanimous. Every claim — breach of charitable trust, unjust enrichment, aiding and abetting against Microsoft — was rejected. Not on the merits. On the clock.

The jury found that Elon Musk had waited too long to sue. His claims were barred by the statute of limitations. The jury determined that Musk was aware of the conduct described in his complaint as far back as 2021 — three years before he filed suit in the summer of 2024. The three-year window for breach of charitable trust had closed. The two-year window for unjust enrichment had closed. The gate was shut before the jury ever reached the room behind it.

Drew Angerer/Agence France-Presse — Getty Images

Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers accepted the verdict immediately. “The court now confirms the prior indication that it would accept the jury’s findings as its own,” she said. “I think that there’s a substantial amount of evidence to support the jury’s finding, which is why I was prepared to dismiss on the spot.”

On the spot. The judge had been ready to do it herself. The jury saved her the trouble, and did it unanimously, in less time than it takes to watch a feature film.

The Room After

None of the three billionaires were in the courtroom when the verdict was read.

Musk had not been seen at the courthouse since his testimony in the trial’s first week. He had spent the closing arguments in Beijing, at a state dinner, between courses, while his lawyer apologised to the jury for his absence. Altman and Brockman, who had attended much of the trial, were also not present for the final moment.

The verdict was heard by lawyers.

In the hallway outside the courtroom, OpenAI’s legal team hugged and congratulated one another with what the New York Times described as “big slaps on the back.” William Savitt, OpenAI’s lead counsel, wore a wide grin. Jason Kwon, OpenAI’s chief strategy officer, celebrated with the trial team. Down the hall, the team gathered in a room. Applause erupted from inside.

Musk’s lawyers looked, in the words of reporters present, “sunken in their chairs.” Marc Toberoff, one of Musk’s attorneys, gave a one-word comment on the decision: “Appeal.” Steven Molo, Musk’s lead counsel, told the judge that Musk intended to appeal but said the legal team had “not yet decided how to proceed.”

Outside the courthouse, Savitt spoke to reporters. He called the verdict confirmation of “what this lawsuit was — a hypocritical attempt to sabotage a competitor.” He said OpenAI “is a not-for-profit, mission-driven organization that has been and will continue to be faithful to that mission.” He gave Musk some credit: “He is a formidable adversary, of course. But we feel we have a strong case.”

A labour group chanted outside: “Nothing about us, without us.” A man in a pink beanie and a glitter-covered belt asked if Musk’s company Neuralink was “the mark of the beast.” Some in the crowd chuckled nervously.

Microsoft issued a statement: “The facts and the timeline in this case have long been clear, and we welcome the jury’s decision to dismiss these claims as untimely.”

The judge gave the jury its final instruction. She told them they could now speak to “anyone about anything” — a laugh went across the gallery — but only at a reasonable time and place and only with their consent. She prepared them for the attention they would receive. Then she left them with a quotation, paraphrasing another judge:

“A jury reflects the attitudes and mores of the community from which it is drawn. It lives only for the day and does justice according to its limits. The group of jurors who are drawn to hear a case make a decision and then melt away. It is not present the next day to be criticized. It is the one governmental agency that has no ambition. It is as human as the people who make it up.”

She excused the jury. They melted away.

What the Jury Did Not Decide

The verdict deserves one more paragraph before this series turns to what it means, because what the jury decided is inseparable from what the jury did not decide.

The jury did not decide whether OpenAI breached its founding promise. It did not decide whether Sam Altman enriched himself through self-dealing between companies in which he holds personal stakes. It did not decide whether the for-profit conversion looted the nonprofit. It did not decide whether the nonprofit is a shell. It did not decide whether OpenAI abandoned its commitment to open sourcing. It did not decide whether Microsoft’s $13 billion investment changed the mission. It did not decide whether AI safety was compromised.

It decided that Musk knew about these things — or should have known — before August 2021, and that his lawsuit, filed in 2024, arrived too late.

The question the trial asked — whether a public promise made to build technology for humanity’s benefit can be broken without consequence — was not answered. It was not reached. Three weeks of testimony, fourteen major witnesses, hundreds of exhibits, and the most comprehensive public record of how the most consequential AI company was built — and the jury answered a question about a calendar.

This is not a criticism of the jury. The statute of limitations exists for sound legal reasons, and the evidence that Musk knew — the 2018 term sheet, the 2020 blog post he tweeted about, the years of communication with Altman — is substantial. Savitt’s closing argument on this point was the strongest argument either side made in the entire trial. The jury was right to take it seriously. The judge was ready to rule the same way.

But the effect is that the trial’s central question remains unanswered by any court. The record exists. The verdict does not address it. The founding promise of OpenAI — nonprofit, safe, open-source, for the benefit of humanity — has been neither vindicated nor repudiated by a federal judge. It has been placed, by a procedural ruling, beyond the reach of this particular plaintiff.

Musk’s one-word response — “Appeal” — suggests he will try again. An appeal on statute of limitations grounds faces long odds, because the question is factual (when did Musk know?) rather than legal (what does the law require?), and appellate courts defer to juries on factual questions. The antitrust claims against Microsoft and OpenAI remain technically alive but the judge has previously indicated they are unlikely to proceed. The trial, for practical purposes, is over.

What This Confirms

Cross-references to The Asimov Gap

The ninety-minute verdict confirms the argument this series has been making since Part 1: the trial was never going to answer the governance question. No trial could. The question of whether a public promise to build AI for humanity’s benefit can be enforced is not a question any existing law is equipped to answer — because no existing law was written for this purpose.

Musk’s lawsuit was built on charitable trust doctrine — a legal framework designed for museums, universities, and foundations. It was never designed to govern the conversion of a nonprofit AI research lab into an $850 billion commercial enterprise backed by the largest technology company on earth. The doctrine asks whether donated funds were used for their intended charitable purpose. It does not ask — because it was never built to ask — whether the most powerful technology in human history should be governed by the promises of its founders or by enforceable public law.

The jury answered the question the doctrine could accommodate: did Musk file on time? The answer was no. The question the doctrine could not accommodate — should OpenAI’s founding promise be enforceable? — was never asked, because no legal framework exists in which to ask it.

That is the Asimov Gap stated as a verdict.

Savitt’s post-verdict statement is worth reading closely. He said OpenAI “is a not-for-profit, mission-driven organization that has been and will continue to be faithful to that mission.” This is the company’s position, stated by its lawyer, on the courthouse steps, after a jury declined to evaluate it. The statement is now the official record — not because it was tested and confirmed, but because the challenge was dismissed before testing could occur.

The trial produced a record. Twelve days of testimony. Hundreds of exhibits. Text messages and emails that nobody intended to read in a federal courthouse. The record includes Musk’s plan to pass control of AI to his children. The record includes Altman’s answer — “I believe so” — to the question of whether he is trustworthy. The record includes a golden jackass inscribed “Never stop being a jackass for safety.” The record includes Shivon Zilis, mother of four of Musk’s children, unable to support his account. The record includes Musk in Beijing while his lawyer apologised for his absence. The record includes a forensic accountant testifying that Musk’s $37 million was fully spent by November 2017. The record includes a $200 billion nonprofit equity stake. The record includes “we did not feel it was appropriate for one person to control our mission.”

The record is the resistance. It was always going to be. The verdict was always going to be a footnote to the record, regardless of which way it went. The laws that should govern AI were not on trial. They do not exist. Until they do, the record is what we have.

The Aftermath

Altman is free. OpenAI’s IPO — expected to be among the largest in history — will proceed without legal encumbrance. The for-profit conversion is unchallenged. The $850 billion valuation is intact. The data centre expansion plan — hundreds of billions of dollars of infrastructure — will continue. The Workshop, OpenAI’s lobbying office blocks from the White House, opened the same day the closing arguments were delivered. The room where it happens is open for business.

Musk is free too, in a different sense. The SpaceX IPO prospectus is expected any day. xAI, his AI company, merged with SpaceX in February at a combined valuation of $1.25 trillion. The man who sued to prevent AI from being controlled by a commercial enterprise will take his own commercial AI enterprise public within months of losing the lawsuit.

The trial’s central irony — stated by Bret Taylor, restated by every major witness, confirmed by the documentary record — survives the verdict intact. The man who sued because no one person should control AI submitted a $97.4 billion bid to become the one person controlling AI. The board said no. The jury said too late. The irony does not require a verdict to be true.

The labour group outside the courthouse had the last word that matters. “Nothing about us, without us.” Nine jurors in Oakland spent ninety minutes deciding a procedural question about a billionaire’s lawsuit. The question of who governs AI — for the seven billion people who did not file a lawsuit, did not testify, did not sit on the jury, and did not attend the state dinner in Beijing — remains exactly where it was before the trial began.

Unanswered.

The Judge’s Words

Gonzalez Rogers told the jury, in the final moments, that a jury “is the one governmental agency that has no ambition. It is as human as the people who make it up.”

The AI industry could use a governmental agency with no ambition. One that lives only for the day, does justice according to its limits, makes a decision, and melts away. One that is as human as the people who make it up.

That agency does not yet exist. The trial confirmed it. The verdict confirmed it. The gap remains.
