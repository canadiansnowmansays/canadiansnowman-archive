There is a particular kind of vanity that afflicts declining empires, and it is not the vanity of believing themselves strong. It is the vanity of believing themselves watched, and of mistaking the quality of the audience’s attention for the quality of the performance. Beijing has spent the better part of this year cultivating exactly that vanity in the rest of us, and on 31 August 2026, a Pew Research Center survey handed the Party its reward: across three dozen countries, China is now viewed more favourably than the United States, and more respondents said they trust Xi Jinping to “do the right thing” in world affairs than said the same of Donald Trump.

I have spent sixteen parts and a fiscal reckoning charting how China arrived at this moment — the humiliation, the long climb back, the imperial restoration under Xi that gave this series its title. I did not expect the coda to be a popularity contest, and I did not expect China to be winning it. But here we are, and it is worth pausing on before this series closes, because the gap between how China is now perceived abroad and how it is actually functioning at home is, in its own way, the whole story of the Long Return in miniature: a civilisation that has always understood the performance of stability to be inseparable from the fact of power, now performing so well that even a wary world has started applauding.

The Atlantic‘s Michael Schuman, writing from Beijing, put the contradiction plainly this week: China’s “stability” looks, from the inside, a great deal like policy paralysis, economic stagnation, and intensified political repression. The Global Times and Xinhua have spent 2026 telling the world that Chinese predictability is a rare and valuable anchor in an uncertain age. The world, per Pew, appears to believe them. The trouble is that the anchor is dragging.

It is worth being precise about what the Pew number actually measures, because precision is the thing propaganda depends on its audience not bothering with. It is not a measure of Chinese governance. It is a measure of contrast — thirty-six countries looking at two options and choosing the one that has not, this particular year, threatened their tariffs, insulted their leaders, or announced a new war on a Tuesday. China’s rise in the poll owes at least as much to Washington’s collapse as to anything Beijing has actually delivered. That is not a small distinction. A reputation built on being the less alarming of two options is not the same thing as a reputation earned, and it is considerably more fragile, because it depends entirely on the other option staying alarming. Xi does not need to solve his economy to keep winning that contest. He only needs Trump to keep losing his.

Start with the man Xi has just thrown overboard. Fang Xinghai, until recently one of China’s most respected securities regulators and a figure well regarded by international business leaders, is now under Party investigation for unspecified disciplinary violations. Readers of Part XI will recognise the shape of this immediately: a technocrat with market-oriented instincts, sidelined not because he failed but because his philosophy no longer suits a regime that prefers state direction to reform. Fang joins a list this series has already catalogued at length — the purges are not a phase Xi grew out of on his way to consolidating the throne. They are the mechanism by which the throne stays consolidated. The Emperor Returns, and keeps returning, precisely by ensuring no one capable of succeeding him is left standing near the door.

The economic picture Fang might once have helped repair has not improved since Part XVII’s account of Hui Ka Yan’s sentencing on 20 August 2026. Home prices continue falling four and a half years after Evergrande’s collapse, still eating into the savings of the middle class this series has followed since Deng’s reforms first created one. Youth unemployment, which became a genuine social crisis in 2023, is now simply chronic: 18 percent among workers aged sixteen to twenty-four as of last month. Retail sales grew a mere 0.6 percent in July — not stagnation exactly, but its close cousin, the kind of number that suggests a population too anxious about its housing wealth and its children’s prospects to spend freely even when permitted to. And the official growth figures themselves are increasingly treated with polite scepticism by outside economists: the research firm Rhodium Group puts China’s actual 2023 growth closer to 2 percent against an official 5 percent, and closer to 3 percent in the two years since, a gap wide enough to swallow an entire policy debate. China’s debt, meanwhile, has climbed to roughly three times the size of its economy under Xi’s watch — a heavier burden, proportionally, than even the American figures this series’ companion project has spent an entire book auditing.

Consider, too, what is sitting empty while all of this unfolds. China’s stock of vacant and unfinished apartments dwarfs anything in comparable Western housing markets, a monument to the same speculative logic this series watched build across the 1990s and 2000s, when property became the primary vehicle for middle-class savings precisely because Deng’s reforms had opened few other places to put them. Xi inherited that arrangement rather than designed it, but four and a half years into the Evergrande unwind, he has chosen to let the correction run rather than paper over it with the kind of monetary sugar rush a Western central bank would reach for on reflex. There is a reading of that restraint as prudence — a refusal to re-inflate a bubble that needed to deflate. There is also a simpler reading, which is that a middle class sitting on falling home equity and a youth cohort facing one-in-five unemployment odds is a middle class with less energy left over for demanding the reforms Fang Xinghai might once have championed. Paralysis, in this light, is not merely a failure to act. It is, for a regime built on Xi’s preferences, a tolerable outcome.

None of this has altered the strategy. Xi remains fixed on state-directed advances in technology and AI as the mechanism that will eventually lift the whole economy, an approach this series has traced back to the same instinct for top-down direction that shaped the Belt and Road and the tightening chronicled in Parts XIII and XIV. The instinct has not produced the reform the property crisis and the youth unemployment crisis both plainly call for, and there is no evidence, fourteen years into Xi’s tenure, that it will.

What the strategy has produced, reliably, is control over the narrative around it. A sweeping law that took effect in July 2026 makes Mandarin the primary language of instruction nationwide and instructs parents to guide children to love the Communist Party — the latest instalment in the assimilation pressure on Uyghurs, Tibetans, and Mongolians that Part XVI documented as an unfinished return in more than the economic sense. And control now reaches into registers of Chinese life that would once have seemed too trivial to bother with: local authorities in Wuhan are reportedly investigating the comedian Guo Degang for altering the lyrics of a patriotic song beloved by Party cadres during a performance last month. A regime confident in its own stability does not typically need to police a stand-up act. One that needs the performance of unity to substitute for the substance of it does.

That performance has an export market, and this is where the addendum earns its place alongside “The Bill Comes Due” rather than simply repeating it. China is not dropping bombs, and Schuman is careful to credit that restraint. But Beijing’s economic support for Russia and Iran functions, in effect, as a proxy underwriting of two wars it need not fight directly — a cheaper and more deniable form of influence than the military entanglements this series’ American-focused companion pieces have spent so much time cataloguing on the other side of the ledger. Closer to home, China’s oversupplied and subsidised manufacturers are shipping twice as much production overseas as they were a year ago, in the absence of adequate demand at home, and the European Union now runs a trade deficit with China exceeding one billion dollars a day — enough that Brussels is actively seeking new powers to protect its industries from the flood. Weak internal demand, in other words, is being exported as an external threat, and the countries absorbing it are the ones now telling Pew’s pollsters they trust Xi more than Trump.

There is a comparison worth drawing here that this series has not yet made, and it is not to Mao or Deng but to Tokyo. Readers who lived through the 1980s will remember a different Asian economy once treated as the inevitable successor to American primacy — praised abroad for its discipline, its long horizons, its capacity to out-plan a distracted West, right up until the property bubble it had built its middle-class wealth upon collapsed and the “lost decades” began. Japan’s advantage over China’s present position was a functioning democracy and a rule of law robust enough to survive the reckoning. China’s property correction is arriving without either cushion, at a moment when the man overseeing it has spent fourteen years systematically removing the technocrats — Fang Xinghai chief among them, this month — who might otherwise argue for a softer landing. The Long Return has always been about the weight of historical memory in Chinese governance; the irony worth sitting with here is that the memory Xi seems least willing to consult is the most recent and most relevant one, sitting in plain view a few hundred miles across the East China Sea.

It would be tidy to end there, with the observation that flattery is cheaper than reform and China has simply chosen the cheaper option. But sixteen parts of this series have argued for something more specific than that, and the specificity is worth holding onto here. China’s capacity to make paralysis look like patience is not a trick invented for 2026. It is the same capacity that got the country through the Century of Humiliation, through Mao’s convulsions, through the anxious calculus of Deng’s opening — a civilisational patience that has, at various points in this series, been genuinely admirable and genuinely ruinous, sometimes in the same decade. What is different now is the audience. For most of the periods this series has covered, that patience was aimed inward, at a population being asked to wait for a promised future. What Pew’s survey captures is a regime that has become skilled enough at the performance to aim it outward as well, at a world exhausted by the alternative on offer from Washington and grateful, for the moment, not to have to look too closely at the ledger.

The ledger does not balance itself because the audience stops asking to see it. Fang Xinghai’s fall, the Mandarin law, the comedian in Wuhan, the vacant towers still standing empty above a falling property market — these are not separate stories from the Pew survey. They are the fine print the survey’s respondents have not read, and the Party is counting, not unreasonably, on most of them never reading it. Schuman’s closing line is the right one to leave this series on: the competition between China and the United States may be less a contest for supremacy than a race to see which country hits bottom first. This addendum’s only amendment to that verdict is a smaller, colder one — that the country ahead in the popularity contest is not necessarily the country ahead in the race, and that a mandate built on perception is still, in the end, a mandate someone has to keep performing for, indefinitely, with no reviews permitted.
