Part XX showed a Pacific kingdom preserving its sovereignty through deliberate, proactive constitutional reform. Thailand, known as Siam until 1939, preserved its own sovereignty through an almost entirely different set of tools, a combination of shrewd geographic positioning, genuinely skillful royal diplomacy, and a domestic legal architecture around the crown that remains, to this day, considerably more absolute in its protection than any other monarchy this series has examined in its final chapters.

Siam’s survival as the only Southeast Asian state never formally colonized by a European power owes an enormous amount to simple geography combined with two successive monarchs’ genuine diplomatic skill in exploiting it. By the mid-nineteenth century, Siam sat wedged directly between British-controlled Burma and Malaya to the west and south and French Indochina to the east, and both colonial powers had strong practical incentives to preserve Siam as a buffer state separating their own rival territorial holdings rather than risk a direct confrontation with each other over which empire should absorb it. King Mongkut, known to Western audiences largely through a considerably fictionalized and, by modern Thai standards, deeply resented portrayal in the musical and film The King and I, reigned from 1851 to 1868 and pursued a deliberate strategy of engaging with Western science, language, and diplomacy on Siam’s own terms, corresponding directly with European heads of state and scientists, studying English and Latin, and signing a series of trade treaties, most consequentially the Bowring Treaty with Britain in 1855, that granted Western powers favourable commercial access without ceding formal sovereignty, a considerably more successful negotiating outcome than the unequal treaties this series traced forcing China into humiliating concessions during the same general period in Part III.

His son, King Chulalongkorn, reigning from 1868 to 1910, continued and dramatically accelerated this strategy of modernization as a legitimacy and survival tool, abolishing slavery, reforming Siam’s administrative and legal systems along Western lines, and undertaking two extensive personal tours of Europe, in 1897 and 1907, that gave him direct relationships with reigning European monarchs and made Siam diplomatically legible to colonial powers in a manner this series recognizes immediately from Tonga’s own King Tupou I’s deliberate constitutional modernization examined in Part XX. Chulalongkorn did cede some peripheral Siamese territorial claims to both Britain and France during his reign, most significantly portions of Laos and Cambodia to French pressure in 1893 and 1907, and northern Malay territories to Britain in 1909, pragmatic territorial concessions that, much like the strategy this series traced through Britain’s own gradual accommodation in Part XVIII, preserved the kingdom’s core sovereignty by voluntarily surrendering less essential peripheral claims rather than risking the whole through confrontation.

Siam’s absolute monarchy survived until 1932, when a bloodless coup led by a coalition of military officers and Western-educated civil servants calling themselves the People’s Party forced King Prajadhipok to accept Siam’s first constitution, transforming the country from an absolute to a constitutional monarchy in a single decisive stroke this series recognizes as broadly similar to Tonga’s own later voluntary reform in Part XX, though considerably more compressed and considerably less voluntary on the reigning king’s own part. The 1932 revolution’s own stated grievances are worth including here, because they echo, in a distinctly Southeast Asian idiom, complaints this series has now traced against absolute monarchy across nearly every civilization it has examined. The People’s Party’s manifesto accused the absolute monarchy of governing through favouritism toward royal relatives rather than merit, of maintaining a bloated and unaccountable court bureaucracy at public expense during a period of genuine economic hardship following the global depression of the early 1930s, and of denying ordinary educated Siamese citizens, many of whom, like the coup’s own civilian leader Pridi Banomyong, had studied law and political science in France and returned home only to find the kingdom’s actual governance closed to any but the highest royal circles, any meaningful path to political participation. The coup itself was executed with minimal violence, seizing key government buildings in Bangkok in the early hours of the twenty-fourth of June, 1932, and presenting King Prajadhipok with a fait accompli he had little practical capacity to resist, a genuinely bloodless transition this series notes as a rare and welcome exception to the violence that has accompanied so many of the other monarchical transitions examined across its previous twenty-one parts.

Prajadhipok, finding himself increasingly marginalized by the new constitutional government and the country’s rapidly shifting political factions through the 1930s, abdicated entirely in 1935 and spent his remaining years in exile in England, and Siam’s subsequent decades were dominated less by any reigning monarch’s own authority than by a shifting and often violent competition for power between military strongmen, culminating in a formal renaming of the country from Siam to Thailand in 1939 under the nationalist military government of Field Marshal Plaek Phibunsongkhram.

It was the long reign of King Bhumibol Adulyadej that rebuilt the crown’s practical relevance from this diminished starting point, though the circumstances of his own accession deserve a moment’s attention first, since they remain, to this day, one of modern Thai history’s most sensitive and least openly discussed episodes. Bhumibol ascended the throne in 1946 following the sudden death of his elder brother, King Ananda Mahidol, found dead of a gunshot wound in his palace bedroom under circumstances that official investigations at the time and in the decades since have never conclusively resolved as accident, suicide, or assassination, a genuine unresolved mystery this series notes here in the same spirit it has approached comparably unresolved royal deaths elsewhere, including Haile Selassie’s own disputed final hours examined in Part XI, without claiming to settle a question Thai courts, royal investigators, and independent historians have themselves been unable to close. Discussion of the case within Thailand itself remains heavily constrained by the same lèse-majesté protections this part will examine directly, which is itself a telling illustration of how completely those protections extend even to inconvenient historical questions about the reigning dynasty’s own more distant past. Bhumibol went on to reign for an extraordinary seventy years until his own death in 2016, rebuilding the Thai crown’s practical political relevance almost entirely from the ceremonial diminishment the 1932 revolution had imposed on it, without ever formally reversing that revolution’s constitutional settlement. He accomplished this through decades of patient, deliberate cultivation of personal moral authority rather than any restoration of formal constitutional power, touring Thailand’s poorest rural provinces extensively throughout his reign to sponsor thousands of small-scale development projects addressing irrigation, agriculture, and rural poverty directly, a strategy of visible, hands-on royal engagement this series recognizes from the Ashanti and Zulu kings’ own emphasis on demonstrated presence examined in Part XII, and from Jigme Khesar Namgyel Wangchuck’s own similar rural engagement in Bhutan examined in Part XXI.

This accumulated moral authority proved decisive during Thailand’s chronic instability under a long succession of military coups, of which the country has experienced more than a dozen successful attempts since 1932, alongside several further failed ones, making it one of the most coup-prone states examined anywhere in this entire series, a genuinely striking statistic for a country that, throughout this same period, maintained regular if frequently interrupted elections and a formally functioning parliamentary system existing in an uneasy and repeatedly disrupted parallel with recurring military intervention. Bhumibol intervened directly and publicly on at least two occasions widely regarded as genuinely pivotal to Thailand’s modern political history: in 1973, his public support for student protesters against a military government contributed directly to that government’s collapse, and in 1992, his televised summoning of both a coup-installed prime minister and the leader of pro-democracy protests to kneel before him in a widely broadcast audience, urging reconciliation after a violent military crackdown on demonstrators, is credited by most Thai political historians with ending that particular crisis and forcing the military government’s resignation within days. Neither intervention rested on any formal constitutional power the 1932 settlement had granted him; both rested entirely on an accumulated personal moral authority that made his public word, even without any accompanying legal force behind it, sufficient to end crises the country’s own formal constitutional machinery repeatedly proved unable to resolve on its own.

This moral authority has been protected, throughout Bhumibol’s reign and into his son’s succeeding one, by one of the most stringent lèse-majesté laws examined anywhere in this series, a provision of the Thai criminal code making it a serious criminal offence, carrying potential sentences of up to fifteen years’ imprisonment per individual violation, to defame, insult, or threaten the King, Queen, heir apparent, or regent. This law has been enforced with particular vigour during periods of political tension, and Thailand’s courts have interpreted its scope broadly enough to encompass social media posts, academic commentary, satirical theatre productions, and even, in some prosecuted cases, sarcastic remarks about the royal family’s pet dogs, a breadth of enforcement that human rights organizations have repeatedly criticized as functioning less to protect royal dignity in any narrow sense and more as a general-purpose tool for suppressing political dissent under a formally apolitical legal pretext. Unlike most defamation statutes examined in comparable legal systems elsewhere, Thailand’s lèse-majesté law permits any citizen, not merely an aggrieved party or state prosecutor, to file a formal complaint against another citizen, a provision that has made the law a frequently used instrument in ordinary personal and political disputes entirely unrelated to any genuine royal grievance, since a complaint alone, whatever its ultimate merit, can trigger a lengthy and personally ruinous police investigation and prosecution against the accused party.

The current monarch, King Maha Vajiralongkorn, who succeeded his widely revered father in 2016, has presided over a reign that international observers and Thai citizens themselves have generally characterized as considerably more personally controversial than his father’s, marked by an unusual and closely scrutinized personal life, including several marriages, a formally elevated royal consort in addition to his queen, and periods of extended residence in Germany rather than Thailand itself, a pattern that has drawn periodic diplomatic friction with German authorities uncomfortable hosting a foreign head of state who appears, by some accounts, to conduct meaningful state business from German soil. His reign has also been marked by a series of significant legal and administrative changes strengthening the crown’s own formal authority: in 2017, shortly after his accession, the Crown Property Bureau, which manages an enormous portfolio of Thai land and corporate holdings previously classified as state rather than personal royal property, was reorganized under new legislation placing its assets under Vajiralongkorn’s direct personal control rather than the more institutionally separated arrangement that had prevailed under his father, and in 2019 two elite army units historically responsible for Bangkok’s security were formally transferred from the Ministry of Defence to come under the king’s own direct personal command, consolidating a degree of formal royal authority over palace finances and military assets that Bhumibol himself, for all his accumulated moral influence, had never held in such an explicit and legally codified personal form. Thailand experienced a significant wave of youth-led pro-democracy protests beginning in 2020, initially sparked by the dissolution of a popular opposition political party but rapidly expanding into a broader set of demands that included, remarkably and for the first time in decades of Thai political history, open and public calls for reform of the monarchy’s own constitutional role, greater transparency over the vast crown property portfolio, and repeal of the lèse-majesté law itself, protesters at several major rallies displaying the three-finger salute borrowed from the Hunger Games films as a symbol of resistance and reciting an explicit ten-point manifesto for monarchical reform that would have been almost unthinkable to state publicly under Bhumibol’s own reign. The current government has responded to this movement with a substantial wave of new lèse-majesté prosecutions against protest organizers and participants, including, in several widely reported cases, sentences reaching decades in length for cumulative charges stemming from multiple social media posts, a scale of enforcement that has drawn sustained criticism from international human rights organizations and has done little to resolve, one way or the other, the underlying generational divide in Thai public opinion the 2020 protests first brought into open view.

Bhumibol’s two most celebrated interventions deserve slightly more texture before this part turns to that broader argument, since the specific mechanics of each reveal exactly how his moral authority actually functioned in practice, separate from any formal constitutional lever. In October of 1973, following a bloody military crackdown on student protesters demanding a permanent constitution and an end to military rule, Bhumibol opened the gates of the Chitralada Palace to protesters fleeing the violence, granted asylum to student leaders, and shortly afterward accepted the resignation of the ruling military dictator, actions that carried no formal constitutional authority behind them whatsoever but which, given the reverence already accumulated around the crown, proved sufficient to end the immediate crisis and force the military government from office within days. The 1992 intervention, sometimes remembered as Black May given the substantial civilian death toll in the preceding crackdown, saw Bhumibol summon coup leader General Suchinda Kraprayoon and opposition figure Chamlong Srimuang to a televised audience broadcast live across the country, during which both men, by long-standing custom, knelt on the floor before the seated king as he urged reconciliation and warned that continued conflict served no one’s interest, a single broadcast image so powerful in a culture steeped in deference to the crown that Suchinda resigned from the premiership within days of its airing.

What Thailand’s case adds to this series’ broader argument, set against Tonga’s own voluntary reform in Part XX and Bhutan’s own in Part XXI, is a genuinely important complication: moral authority accumulated through decades of demonstrated personal engagement and restraint, as Bhumibol so successfully built it, does not automatically transfer intact to a successor, and a crown’s continued legal protection through mechanisms like lèse-majesté can preserve the institution’s formal untouchability even as the specific personal legitimacy that once justified deference to it visibly weakens, a genuinely unstable combination this series has not quite seen in this precise form anywhere else in its survey, and one whose eventual resolution remains, as of this writing, still very much unfolding rather than settled.

Part XXIII turns from Southeast Asia’s sole surviving monarchy to the empire whose own six-century decline gave the world its most enduring shorthand for a fading great power, the sick man of Europe, and whose final abolition came not through foreign conquest or popular revolution against the sultan personally, but through the same secularizing military officer who built an entirely new republic on the empire’s ashes.
