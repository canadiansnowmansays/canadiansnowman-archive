GOVERNMENT OF CANADA / GOUVERNEMENT DU CANADA Canada Revenue Agency — International Water & Air Accounts Division NOTICE OF ASSESSMENT

Account Holder: United States of America Account Number: 1-800-WASTED-CAUSE Assessment Period: Ongoing, retroactive to 1964 Re: Tariffs proposed on Canadian wildfire smoke, per Presidential communication dated July 17, 2026

Dear Valued Trading Partner,

Thank you for your recent correspondence proposing tariffs on Canada to compensate the United States for smoke drifting south from wildfires in Ontario. The Agency has reviewed your submission carefully, in the unhurried and thorough manner for which we are known, and would like to draw your attention to a related account that appears, on our records, to have gone unaddressed for some sixty-two years.

SECTION 1: THE PRINCIPLE ESTABLISHED

We understand your position to be as follows: air that crosses a border, uninvited, carrying material one party did not ask to receive, may reasonably be billed to the party of origin. The Agency finds this principle sound, consistent, and — this is the important part — already in operation, in the opposite direction, for a much larger volume of a much wetter substance.

SECTION 2: THE ASSET IN QUESTION

Every year, the Hugh Keenleyside Dam near Castlegar, British Columbia — one of three dams Canada built and maintains under the 1964 Columbia River Treaty — releases water southward into the Columbia River system. This water irrigates approximately $8 billion of American agriculture. It turns the turbines that generate a significant share of the Pacific Northwest’s hydropower. It moves 42 million tons of American commercial cargo a year. None of this water asked to be sent. It goes because Canada, unlike certain other parties to certain other agreements, honors its treaties.

SECTION 3: EXISTING TERMS, FOR THE RECORD

The Agency notes, purely in the interest of completeness, that the Treaty already entitles Canada to a share of the downstream power benefits generated by this arrangement — the so-called “Canadian Entitlement,” negotiated in good faith, paid out for six decades without incident. We mention this only because your recent proposal to charge Canada for airborne particulate matter appears to have been drafted without reference to the much larger cross-border resource transfer already running, quietly, in Canada’s favor.

SECTION 4: A NOTE ON RECENT NEGOTIATING CONDUCT

The Agency further notes that it was the United States, not Canada, that paused modernization talks on this very Treaty in March of this year, amid unrelated trade tensions, leaving sixty-plus years of cooperative water management in a state of administrative limbo. Canada did not retaliate at the time. Canada does not retaliate quickly, as a rule — see Section 5.

SECTION 5: PRIOR ENFORCEMENT ACTION (FOR PRECEDENT PURPOSES)

The Agency wishes to remind the Account Holder that Ontario previously demonstrated its capacity to act on exactly this kind of grievance, announcing a 25 percent surcharge on electricity exports to the United States earlier this year, in direct response to tariff threats. That surcharge was suspended within approximately 24 hours, following a single phone call. The Agency mentions this not as a threat but as a data point: Canada is capable of moving fast when required, and has, on at least one occasion this year, chosen not to.

SECTION 6: A GOOD-FAITH ALTERNATIVE PROPOSAL

Rather than pursue tariffs on Canadian wildfire smoke — a substance which, per Canada’s own emergency management minister, is substantially a product of a warmer, drier climate affecting both nations, and which Ontario’s premier notes his province has assisted the United States with directly, dispatching water bombers to California in recent years and personnel to Minnesota this very week, without invoice — the Agency proposes the following instead: a mutual, reciprocal accounting of all cross-border natural resources, rendered without complaint, extended without compensation, for the duration of the relationship. Canada is prepared to begin this accounting immediately. Canada suspects the resulting balance will not favor the party requesting it.

SECTION 7: ON THE SUBJECT OF “GOOD FAITH,” A DEFINITION FOR THE RECORD

The Agency notes that the Account Holder has, on occasion, expressed concern that Canada does not negotiate in good faith. The Agency would like to submit, for the file, the Account Holder’s own remarks upon signing the currently operative trade agreement between our two nations, on July 1, 2020: that it was “the largest, fairest, and most balanced trade agreement ever negotiated,” and that “there’s never been anything like it.” The Account Holder further specified, that same year, that this was “the best agreement we’ve ever made.”

The Agency has cross-referenced this against more recent remarks by the same Account Holder, describing this identical agreement — the one he negotiated, signed, and toured the country praising — as containing terms he could not explain, having apparently read them “at night” and asked himself, in his own words, “Who would ever sign a thing like this?” The Agency does not know. The Agency has checked the signature page. It appears to be his.

For clarity: the agreement under review by the current administration, and the agreement celebrated by the previous administration as the finest trade deal in the nation’s history, are the same document, signed by the same president, four and a half years apart. Canada considers this relevant to any future discussion of who, precisely, negotiates in good faith.

SECTION 8: THE LIQUOR CABINET

The Agency would be remiss not to mention a related account already settled, in part, without need of this Notice. Following earlier tariff actions, Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia, and several other provinces removed American wine, beer, and spirits from government shelves entirely — indefinitely, and without exception, per direction of the Premier of Ontario, who noted at the time that “there’s never been a better time to choose an amazing Ontario-made or Canadian-made product.” Ontario-grown wine sales subsequently rose 52 percent. Canadian deluxe whisky rose 94 percent. One Ontario distillery reported its own whisky sales up 300 percent, its vodka up 100 percent. Nova Scotia’s local spirits rose nearly 15 percent the same year.

The Agency notes, in the interest of full disclosure, that Ontario is currently sitting on approximately $79 million in unsold American bourbon, paid for and warehoused before the ban took effect, which the province is not currently permitted to sell and does not appear in a hurry to solve. This is offered not as a grievance but as an illustration: even when Canada’s countermeasures leave money sitting quietly on a shelf it cannot legally stock, the shelf space has, on balance, been filled rather well by someone else.

SECTION 9: BALANCE DUE

Amount currently owed by Canada to the United States for wildfire smoke, per your proposal: To be determined by an authority Canada does not recognize.

Amount the United States has, by treaty, already agreed it owes Canada for water sent south via the Keenleyside Dam, annually, since 1964: Also to be determined, though considerably larger, and already being paid.

Net balance, all accounts considered: In Canada’s favor. It generally is.

We trust this clarifies matters. Should you wish to dispute this assessment, please note that Canada’s Minister of Emergency Management is available Tuesdays, and the Premier of Ontario is available essentially all the time, whether you call him or not.

Yours in continued cooperative water and air management,

The Agency
