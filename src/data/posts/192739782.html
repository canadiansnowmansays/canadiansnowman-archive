U.S. Marines retreat from the Chosin Reservoir, North Korea, December 1950. The Korean War was the first test of the post-war collective security architecture — and the first demonstration that the United States would bear real costs to defend it. Thirty-six thousand Americans died in a war fought not to defend American territory, not to secure American resources, but to uphold the principle that armed aggression would be collectively resisted. The principle was worth defending. The cost was real.

At 4:00 in the morning on June 25, 1950, ninety thousand soldiers of the Korean People’s Army crossed the 38th parallel and began their advance south. They were equipped with Soviet tanks, Soviet artillery, and Soviet organizational doctrine. They were supported by the explicit approval of Joseph Stalin, who had calculated — correctly, it turned out — that American Secretary of State Dean Acheson’s January 1950 speech defining the American “defensive perimeter” in Asia had excluded Korea and therefore signalled that the United States would not intervene.

Stalin was wrong about the last part. And in being wrong, he helped establish one of the most important precedents in the history of the American-led international order: the precedent that the United States would fight, at significant cost, for the principle of collective security rather than for direct national interest.

Korea was the first test of the post-war architecture. It was not a test anyone had designed or wanted. It was the test history imposed — the moment when the gap between rhetorical commitment to collective security and actual willingness to bear the costs of collective security had to be resolved. The resolution — imperfect, costly, and incomplete — produced consequences that echo to this day.

Why Korea Mattered

Korea had no oil. It had no significant strategic resources. It was not a democracy. It was not a long-standing American ally. It sat at the edge of the American defensive perimeter that Acheson had defined, and Acheson himself initially doubted whether defending it was worth the cost.

This is precisely what made the decision to defend it so significant.

When Truman decided — within twenty-four hours of the North Korean invasion — that the United States would intervene, he was not responding to a direct threat to American security. South Korea’s fall would not have endangered American territory, American trade routes, or American citizens in any immediate sense. What it would have endangered was the credibility of the entire post-war architecture of collective security.

The UN Charter committed its members to collective resistance against aggression. The principle was clear. The question was whether it meant anything when put to the test. If the most powerful nation in the world — the nation that had done the most to build the Charter and the most to promote the principle of collective security — allowed naked military aggression to succeed without response, the principle was exposed as meaningless. Every small nation that had aligned with the Western order on the basis of a belief in collective security would have reason to recalculate. Every adversary would have reason to test the commitments further.

Truman understood this. “If we let Korea down,” he said, “the Soviet[s] will keep right on going and swallow up one piece of Asia after another... If we were to let Asia go, the Near East would collapse and no telling what would happen in Europe.”

This reasoning — the domino logic that would later be applied disastrously to Vietnam — was not wrong in the Korean context. It was right. The defense of South Korea was a demonstration that the post-war collective security architecture had teeth. That demonstration had real strategic value, independent of the specific value of Korean territory.

The United Nations Security Council in session, June 1950. The Council’s authorization of collective military action in Korea — obtained within two days of the North Korean invasion — was possible only because the Soviet Union was boycotting the Council at the time and could not exercise its veto. The authorization gave the intervention a legitimacy that shaped American foreign policy for decades

The United States secured UN authorization for the intervention within two days — a speed that was possible only because the Soviet Union was boycotting the Security Council at the time (protesting the Council’s refusal to seat Communist China) and therefore could not exercise its veto. It assembled a multinational coalition of sixteen nations under UN command. It fought under the UN flag.

This was not merely procedural. It was the collective security principle made operational — the demonstration that the architecture worked, that the commitment to respond to aggression was real. Fifteen other nations sent troops to Korea. None of them had direct national interests at stake. They were acting in support of the principle.

That principle — the willingness of member states to contribute to collective security even at national cost — is the foundation on which every subsequent collective action has rested. It is what makes NATO meaningful and what Trump’s questioning of it so dangerous. An alliance whose members will only contribute when they have direct national interests at stake is not an alliance. It is a collection of bilateral relationships waiting to be renegotiated.

The War Itself

The Korean War lasted three years and killed approximately 36,000 Americans, perhaps 200,000 South Korean soldiers, roughly 400,000 Chinese soldiers, and somewhere between one and two million Korean civilians. It ended in an armistice, not a peace treaty, that restored approximately the pre-war border at the 38th parallel.

By the standards of war aims, this was an extraordinary result. The United States had gone to war to repel an act of aggression and restore the territorial status quo ante. That is exactly what it achieved. The aggressors did not gain. The principle was upheld.

But the war’s conduct taught lessons that would not be fully absorbed for two decades — lessons about the limits of military power, about the gap between stated war aims and operational reality, about the tendency of wars to acquire logic of their own that overwhelms the original strategic purpose.

The most important of these lessons was the MacArthur problem.

General Douglas MacArthur was perhaps the most celebrated American military commander of the twentieth century — the hero of the Pacific War, the proconsul who had governed Japan after its surrender and overseen its transformation into a stable democracy. He was also, by 1950, a man whose confidence in his own strategic judgment had exceeded the bounds of civilian control.

When Chinese forces entered the war in October 1950 — after MacArthur had driven north beyond the 38th parallel toward the Chinese border, ignoring explicit intelligence that China would intervene if American forces approached the Yalu River — MacArthur’s public response was to advocate for widening the war: bombing Chinese bases in Manchuria, using Nationalist Chinese forces from Taiwan, and if necessary deploying nuclear weapons.

Truman fired him in April 1951.

The firing — which produced a storm of public outrage and a congressional inquiry that came close to censuring the President — was one of the most significant acts of the Truman presidency. It was the assertion, under enormous political pressure, of the principle that civilian authority governs military command in a democracy, that generals do not make strategy, and that the war’s limited aims could not be expanded simply because the military commander believed expansion was necessary.

General Douglas MacArthur addresses a joint session of Congress, April 19, 1951, days after President Truman relieved him of command

MacArthur’s farewell address to Congress — “Old soldiers never die, they just fade away” — was received with wild enthusiasm by a public that did not fully understand what was at stake. What was at stake was the principle that democratic nations fight wars for defined political purposes under civilian control, and end them when those purposes are achieved or judged unachievable. The alternative — wars prosecuted to whatever conclusion the generals deem militarily necessary, regardless of the political and human costs — is not a feature of democratic governance. It is a path to something else.

The MacArthur precedent runs directly to the present. When Hegseth says in March 2026 that the choice to end the war in Iran is “Trump’s alone,” he is invoking — probably without knowing it — the principle Truman established in 1951. Civilian control of military operations, for all its messiness, is one of the post-war order’s most important institutional achievements. It is the difference between wars fought for political purposes and wars fought for military ones.

The distinction matters. Political wars can be ended. Military wars tend to continue until one side is destroyed.

The Price of Principle

Korea established a precedent that was simultaneously necessary and dangerous.

Necessary, because the post-war collective security architecture required the demonstration that the United States would bear real costs to defend it. Without that demonstration, the architecture was worthless — a collection of rhetorical commitments that no adversary needed to take seriously.

Dangerous, because the logic of precedent — if we don’t defend here, our commitments elsewhere become incredible — can justify almost any intervention anywhere. It is a logic without natural limits. Every piece of territory, every government under pressure, every act of aggression anywhere in the world becomes a potential test of American credibility. And if every test must be met, the result is not collective security. It is global empire — the commitment to police the entire world at whatever cost.

The twenty years between Korea and the end of the Vietnam War were largely a working-out of this tension. Korea established that the United States would fight for principle. Vietnam demonstrated that this principle, applied without strategic discrimination, could produce catastrophe.

The architects of the post-war order understood the tension but did not resolve it. They could not. The resolution required judgments about when American credibility was genuinely at stake and when it was not — judgments that proved, in practice, to be extraordinarily difficult to make correctly under political pressure.

What they did establish — through Korea, through the institutional frameworks they built, through the norms they developed — was a set of criteria for when collective action was justified: when aggression was clear, when authorization was genuine, when aims were limited and achievable, when the response was proportionate. These criteria did not guarantee good decisions. They provided a framework for making them, and a standard against which decisions could be evaluated.

The Iran war of 2026 fails most of these criteria. The aggression was not clear — Iran’s nuclear program, while a genuine concern, did not pose an imminent threat. The authorization was not sought — Congress was not consulted, no UN resolution was obtained. The aims were not limited — they shifted from nuclear disarmament to regime change to simple coercion within weeks. The proportionality is not apparent — 11,000 strikes in thirty days, 1,574 civilian deaths including 236 children, against a country that had no nuclear weapon and no capacity to strike the American homeland.

Korea established that America would fight for principle. What it did not establish — what no institutional architecture fully can establish — is that every fight claimed to be for principle actually is.

What Korea Built

The Korean War’s most lasting legacy was not territorial or military. It was institutional.

The war demonstrated that the UN collective security system could function — imperfectly, asymmetrically, dependent on Soviet miscalculation and Cold War contingency, but functionally. It established the precedent that American military action could be undertaken under international authorization and in coalition with allies, a precedent that shaped American foreign policy for forty years.

It also established the pattern of American extended deterrence in Asia — the commitment to the defence of South Korea, Japan, and eventually Taiwan that would define American strategic posture in the Pacific for the rest of the century. Japan, forbidden by its American-written constitution from remilitarizing, became the logistical and economic anchor of American Pacific strategy. South Korea, transformed over the following decades from a devastated war zone into one of the world’s most dynamic economies, became one of America’s most important trading partners.

These outcomes were not inevitable. They required sustained American commitment, economic engagement, and institutional investment over decades. They were the product of the same logic that had produced the Marshall Plan: that American security was best served by the prosperity and stability of its allies, and that investing in that prosperity was not charity but strategy.

The contrast with the present is painful. Japan and South Korea — the nations whose security was established through the Korean War and sustained through seven decades of American commitment — were, in the first weeks of the Iran war, reportedly “not receiving any communication from the Trump administration.” They were deeply dependent on Persian Gulf oil, acutely vulnerable to the economic consequences of the Hormuz closure, and entirely excluded from the decisions that produced those consequences. The commitment built through Korea — the commitment that had sustained American primacy in Asia for seventy years — was being treated as irrelevant.

Old soldiers never die, MacArthur said. What he did not say is that old commitments do. Not with a battle or a treaty. With a pattern of behaviour that makes them, slowly and then suddenly, incredible.

Canada and the Collective Security Moment

Canadian soldiers of the Princess Patricia’s Canadian Light Infantry in Korea, 1951. Canada sent 26,000 soldiers to Korea under the UN flag — a significant commitment for a country of 14 million. The decision was made as a deliberate expression of the Canadian commitment to collective security: the principle that a rules-based international order is worth defending even when the direct national interest is not immediately at stake. Five hundred and sixteen Canadians died in Korea

Canada sent troops to Korea under the UN flag — 26,000 Canadians served, 516 died. It was a significant commitment for a country of 14 million, and it was made deliberately, as an expression of the Canadian commitment to the collective security principle that Keenleyside’s generation had worked to embed in the post-war architecture.

The Canadian decision was not without controversy. There were arguments — serious ones — about whether Korea was worth the cost, whether the UN authorization was genuine or procedurally accidental, whether the expanding war aims that followed the Chinese intervention made the original commitment untenable. These arguments prefigured the debates about Vietnam that would consume the next generation.

But the underlying principle — that Canada’s security was bound up with the maintenance of a rules-based international order, and that maintaining that order sometimes required bearing costs that exceeded direct national interest — was not seriously contested. Canada fought in Korea not because Korean territory mattered to Canadian interests but because the principle of collective resistance to aggression mattered. A world in which aggression was rewarded was a world in which Canada — a medium power with a large and powerful neighbour — was significantly less safe.

This remains the Canadian strategic reality. Canada’s security is not guaranteed by Canadian military power, which is modest. It is guaranteed by the rules-based international order within which great power competition is constrained, and by the American commitment to that order that anchors the entire system.

When that commitment frays — when the United States begins to behave as a power that respects no rules except its own interests, that threatens allies as readily as adversaries, that conducts wars of choice without consultation or authorization — Canada faces a strategic environment for which its post-war assumptions provide no guidance.

The Canada of Hugh Keenleyside’s generation had a strategy for living next to a superpower: embed it in institutions, support its leadership while insisting on multilateral constraints, contribute to collective security to demonstrate the value of the principle. That strategy worked for seventy years because the superpower being managed was willing, on balance, to be managed.

That willingness is no longer evident. And Canada, like every other nation in the post-war order’s orbit, is left to figure out what comes next.
