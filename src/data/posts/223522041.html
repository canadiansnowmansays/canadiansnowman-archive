This series set out to audit the Democratic Party. Was it building coalitions or running purity tests? Was it taking back the vocabulary of freedom, patriotism and family from the other side, or ceding it? Borrowing the Westminster idea of a loyal opposition, the series asked whether the Democrats were an alternative government in waiting or just a collection of people who disliked the current one.

Fifteen parts later, the voters have filed their own report. On 3 November 2026 Democrats won the national House vote by about 13 points, the largest margin since 1976, and flipped seven Senate seats in Maine, Iowa, Kansas, Ohio, North Carolina, South Carolina and Texas. By any measure the opposition opposed successfully.

The more useful question for this epilogue is which parts of the audit held up, and which did not.

Part I: the purity test that wasn’t

The series began in August with “Wake Up, Dems.” Abdul El-Sayed had just won Michigan’s Senate primary by about a point over Haley Stevens, and the piece worried that Democrats were mistaking primary purity for electability. It drew the parallel with the Tea Party, whose primary wins in 2010 cost Republicans winnable Senate seats.

El-Sayed won. He will be the first Muslim in the Senate, after one of the ugliest campaigns in the country, in which the president called him a “jihadist” and Stevens, in the final days, endorsed his Republican opponent. Part X, “The Apology,” had argued that his apology to Michigan’s Jewish Democrats was the first live example of the coalition discipline the series was asking for. That discipline seems to have mattered. A state senator who had run against both of them in the primary summed up the Republican message to The New York Times: health care costs are too high, “and that guy’s a Muslim.” Voters, she said, were disgusted.

The warning in Part I was not wrong in general. It was wrong about this candidate in this year. The audit should record both.

Part IV: nobody asked Chuck, but Chuck recruited

“Nobody Asked Chuck” catalogued Chuck Schumer’s authority problems in Michigan, Maine and Florida, where the base kept defeating his chosen candidates. The results were mixed in a revealing way.

In Maine, Troy Jackson, the logger who replaced the party’s first nominee and rejects Schumer’s leadership, beat Susan Collins. Russell Berman, reporting from her party in Bangor for The Atlantic, watched her make a long, beaming pass through the ballroom to “Don’t Stop Believin’” and never come back. Shortly after midnight she called Jackson to concede, without a speech. In Florida, Angie Nixon, who beat the DSCC’s recruit in the primary despite being outspent sixteen to one, lost the Senate race to Ashley Moody. It was one of the Republicans’ few bright spots.

“I was caught up in the Trump tsunami,” Collins told Carl Hulse of the Times the next day. “I want results and they want angry rhetoric.” Her career had been inspired by Margaret Chase Smith, the Maine senator who denounced Joseph McCarthy. “I have now had my Margaret Chase Smith moment,” she said. They had each lost their last race for the Senate.

But the part of the record that Part IV underweighted was recruitment. Michael Scherer and Ashley Parker reported in The Atlantic that Schumer persuaded Mary Peltola, Roy Cooper and Sherrod Brown to run, and backed Josh Turek and James Talarico early. Cooper proved so strong in North Carolina that Democrats could move money out of the state to other races. When he began talking about a Senate majority in 2025, Schumer said, people rolled their eyes.

Schumer told Franklin Foer of The Atlantic that flipping Mississippi had been an old fixation, his “white whale.” Cindy Hyde-Smith had blocked Scott Colom’s nomination to the federal bench in 2022; Schumer later pitched him on the race over lunch at his Brooklyn apartment. With the count still under way, Colom led her by more than 1,800 votes. Schumer’s opening plan as majority leader is about 15 modest bills on costs and corruption, from insulin prices to a ban on stock trading by members of Congress, designed to make Republicans either vote against them or make Trump veto them.

Part V: what governors knew

“What Governors Know That Washington Doesn’t” held up best of all. Its argument was that the governors and governor-candidates were speaking to voters as people rather than as a party base, with Rob Sand’s Iowa singalongs and Andy Beshear’s advice to “talk like a normal human being.”

Democrats won a majority of the country’s governorships for the first time in two decades, including Iowa, Georgia, Ohio, Texas and Wisconsin. Most of the winning Senate candidates ran like governors: on gas, groceries and health care, with as little party branding as they could manage. Annie Andrews in South Carolina declined to name a single Democrat she wanted to campaign with her. Beshear, who chairs the Democratic Governors Association, gave the formula in an interview on election night: campaigns “laser focused on the lives of our people, and not just fealty to Donald Trump.” Democrats will hold 29 governorships.

Part VII: the big tent, pitched

“The Big Tent” drew on Daron Acemoglu’s argument that the most successful left-of-centre coalitions in history were built on broad economic populism rather than an oppressor-and-oppressed framework. Look at the Democratic Senate class of 2027: a pediatrician in South Carolina, a Methodist minister in Kansas, a seminarian in Texas, a Paralympic gold medallist in Iowa, a logger in Maine, a Muslim public-health doctor in Michigan and a gay congressman from a New Hampshire diner family. It is hard to think of a broader tent. Ezra Klein put the lesson in eight words on his podcast for The New York Times: “The big tent is real, and the map is fake.” In Kansas, as Tom Nichols noted in The Atlantic, the new Democratic senator was born in 1964. The last Democratic senator from Kansas was born in 1879.

Part IX: the slogan, found

“The Money and the Slogan” lamented that Democrats had no elevator pitch. By November they had one, and it was almost aggressively dull. Scherer and Parker reported that Hakeem Jeffries spent two years forcing his caucus to talk about the cost of living and very little else, even when members wanted to fight about immigration raids, attacks on universities or Trump’s authoritarian impulses. When the Obamacare subsidies expired, he insisted the shutdown fight be about health-care costs alone. His victory backdrop on election night read “Fighting for an Affordable America.” His strategists learned to fold corruption into the same argument: things cost too much because the system is rigged.

Part IX also worried about the money gap. Republicans outspent Democrats across the Senate battlegrounds, and Musk alone spent close to half a billion dollars. It did not matter. The pitch beat the cash.

Parts XI and XII: the converts and the hedgers

“The Converts” profiled former Republicans running as Democrats. Rebecca Bennett, a Navy helicopter pilot who grew up in a Republican household in Texas and left the party in 2016, unseated Tom Kean Jr. in New Jersey. In Florida the convert strategy met its limit. David Jolly, the former Republican congressman who became the Democratic nominee for governor, lost to Byron Donalds, who becomes Florida’s first Black governor. Even so, Donalds won by about one point, roughly 108,000 votes out of more than 8.2 million, after outspending Jolly by about $150 million, in a state Republicans had carried by nearly twenty points four years earlier. “It has been the honor of a lifetime to move this state 14 points in a new direction,” Jolly said in his concession speech, as The New York Times reported. The convert came closer than anyone expected, and still lost.

The convert in the other direction fared worse. Jeff Van Drew was elected to the House as a Democrat in 2018, switched parties a year later and pledged his “undying support” to Trump in the Oval Office. He lost his South Jersey seat to Zack Mullock, the Democratic mayor of Cape May, in a district he had won by 17 points two years earlier. “It kind of is a Watergate,” he said in his concession speech.

“The Party That Cannot Decide What It’s Fighting For” questioned Josh Turek, a former Republican in Iowa who had voted with Republicans hundreds of times, and asked whether accommodation was a form of surrender. Turek won a Senate seat in a state Trump carried by 13 points. On that question, at least in Iowa, the hedgers have the better argument. Elaine Godfrey reported in The Atlantic that every county in both Iowa and Kansas moved left. Turek and Rob Sand, the new Democratic governor, both broke with their party to say that transgender girls should not play in girls’ sports. “Iowa is the testing ground,” said Adam Jentleson of the Searchlight Institute, “and Sand and Turek are the models.” They will also have more company. The centrist Blue Dog Coalition announced on 4 November that it would have at least 14 new members, more than doubling its size. Asked about the divide, Schumer brushed it aside: “Whether they were progressives or moderates, they were saying the same things!”

Parts XIII and XIV: fight and ownership

Sarah Longwell’s focus groups, in “The Wrecking Ball,” found that Democratic voters wanted fight more than ideological adjustment. The candidates who won seem to have offered both: a fight against Trump’s prices, war and corruption, conducted in the language of moderation.

“Rent or Own” examined the party’s choice to invest in its own long-term infrastructure in Pennsylvania’s First District, against Brian Fitzpatrick, the one House Republican who had survived three times in a district Trump lost three times. Fitzpatrick lost. Democrats took four Pennsylvania seats in all.

The one that got away

If the audit needs a cautionary tale, Montana supplies it. Seth Bodnar, a well-funded independent, and Alani Bankhead, the little-known Democratic nominee, spent months pressuring each other to drop out. Neither did. They split the anti-Republican vote and handed the open Senate seat to Kurt Alme. On a night when Democrats won Senate seats in Kansas and South Carolina, Montana was the price of the very coalition failure Part I warned about.

Klein pointed to a second warning, in Maine. Jared Golden, the most conservative House Democrat, held the reddest district any Democrat represented, and was hounded by the left until he retired. His progressive successor as nominee, Matt Dunlap, lost the seat to Paul LePage, the former Republican governor, despite the wave. “Jared Golden was like a most valuable player for the Democrats,” Klein said. A party that wants a durable majority has to protect the people who can win where it otherwise cannot.

What the audit missed

The honest finding of this epilogue is that the opposition won mostly because the government lost. Scherer and Parker quoted a Democratic pollster who noted that the party’s favourability had barely moved. “I think Trump lost it,” the pollster said. Idrees Kahloon wrote in The Atlantic that Democrats did learn the lessons of 2024, including that they had drifted too far left on immigration and on transgender issues. He also argued that it was Republican leadership, above all the war and the tariffs, that lost the election.

Peter Beinart, writing in The New York Times, pointed to the strongest argument Democrats found, the one Jon Ossoff rode to an easy re-election in Georgia: “Yes, the system really is rigged. But he’s not unrigging it. He is re-rigging it for himself.” He added the catch that this series should keep at the top of its file. Voters see Democrats as corrupt too.

The next audit

A loyal opposition, in the Westminster sense, is an alternative government. In January the Democrats stop being an opposition in Congress and become, at least there, a government. They will have subpoenas, committee gavels and a veto over Trump’s nominees. They will not have the votes to override his vetoes.

The test from here is different. It is no longer whether Democrats can oppose. It is whether they can govern on affordability with a president who has promised to be “Mr. Veto,” without spending two years on revenge, and without forgetting that a one-point primary in Michigan and a split vote in Montana were as much a part of this story as the landslide.

Sandy Dockendorff, a longtime Democratic activist in Iowa’s Des Moines County, gave Godfrey the warning this series would put at the top of the next audit. “This is not a coronation of the Democratic Party,” she said. Voters wanted something different. “Our problem will be creating that something different.” And now, she added, “the internecine war begins.”

The base had been saying something similar for months. In June, Godfrey reported in The Atlantic on a grassroots surge larger than 2018’s, with eight million at the third No Kings protest, and on activists as angry at their own leaders as at Trump. “Are we building something durable,” asked Yasmin Radjy of Swing Left, “or is it all a house of sand?” On 3 November the sand held.

Van Z. Hampton, a retired Kansas judge and registered Republican who voted a straight Democratic ticket for the first time since 1976, had advice for the winners, which he gave to Sabrina Tavernise of The New York Times: they had won votes “not because we love you, but because we need you to do the right thing. Be wise about support you got.”

Jeffries, who is expected to become the first Black Speaker of the House, set the terms on 4 November. “The days of the House of Representatives functioning like a wholly-owned subsidiary of the Trump administration are over,” he said. He called the result “an American tsunami.” The audit’s next question is what a party does with a tsunami once the water goes back down.

The audit continues.
