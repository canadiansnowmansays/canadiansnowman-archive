I was premature. This series cannot conclude. The pattern it documents — accountability evasion perfected across 58 years — is not historical. It is present tense. And it is accelerating.

The Setup

Part 40 of this series documented Trump’s $10 billion lawsuit against the IRS — a case so transparently corrupt that the presiding judge, Kathleen M. Williams, had ordered both sides to explain by May 20 whether an actual legal dispute existed, or whether the president was effectively suing himself.

What Judge Williams did not know — what no one outside the government knew until the New York Times reported it on May 19 — was that her instinct was supported by the government’s own lawyers. Career civil servants in the IRS Office of Chief Counsel had prepared a 25-page memorandum recommending that the Justice Department move to dismiss Trump’s suit. The memo outlined multiple defenses: that Trump had likely filed too late, since his personal lawyer Alina Habba was present in court when IRS contractor Charles Littlejohn pleaded guilty in October 2023 — more than two years before the January 2025 filing; and that the IRS could not be held liable for the actions of a contractor employed by Booz Allen Hamilton. In other cases involving the same leak, the Justice Department itself had argued exactly that position.

The memo was delivered to Treasury officials in April. Whether they forwarded it to the Justice Department remains unclear. What is clear is that no Justice Department lawyer ever appeared in court to respond to the suit. No government attorney disputed a single one of Trump’s claims. The career professionals did their jobs. The political appointees buried the work.

The answer to Judge Williams’s question arrived two days early.

On the morning of May 18, Trump’s lawyers filed a motion to dismiss the case “with prejudice” — meaning he cannot refile. Judge Williams, in a terse order, acknowledged she had been “stripped of jurisdiction” and closed the case by end of day. The judge who was about to expose the collusion was cut out of the process entirely.

Minutes later, the Justice Department announced the creation of the $1.776 billion “Anti-Weaponization Fund.”

The amount is symbolic — 1776, the year of American independence. The timing is not. The president sued himself for $10 billion, his own Justice Department prepared to settle with him, and when the judge asked uncomfortable questions about whether this constituted an actual case, Trump simply withdrew the suit and pocketed a slush fund worth more than the GDP of several small nations.

No court approval required. No congressional authorization needed. No oversight mechanism in place.

The Structure

The fund will be overseen by a five-member commission appointed by Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche — who until recently was Trump’s personal defense lawyer, representing him in the classified documents case and the January 6 prosecution. Trump can fire any commission member at will. The commission can issue both monetary awards and formal apologies to anyone who claims they were victims of “weaponization” by the Biden administration.

The Justice Department's announcement provided no definition of "weaponization." No eligibility criteria. No evidentiary standards. No cap on individual payments. The only constraint is temporal: the fund stops processing claims on December 15, 2028 — weeks before Trump leaves office. If the fund were genuinely designed to compensate victims of government abuse, its most obvious beneficiaries would be James Comey, prosecuted on fabricated charges, or Mark Kelly, subjected to a vindictive investigation, or Adam Schiff, targeted for lawful oversight. None of them will apply, because none of them would survive review by a Trump-appointed commission. The termination date ensures the fund expires before anyone else can repurpose it.

The money comes from the Justice Department’s Judgment Fund — a perpetual congressional appropriation normally used to pay court judgments and settlements. This fund requires no additional congressional approval. Trump’s Justice Department can simply write checks.

The original nine-page settlement agreement was signed by Frank Bisignano, who holds the newly created title of IRS chief executive. Bisignano was never confirmed by the Senate for this role and is simultaneously serving as commissioner of the Social Security Administration — splitting his time between two agencies while signing settlements worth billions.

The Trump administration maintains the fund addresses legitimate grievances from individuals who faced politically motivated prosecutions under Biden. Critics argue this framing itself rewrites history by treating lawful prosecutions as persecution.

To whom will the money flow? The announcement was coy, but the implications are clear. Anyone who believes they were unfairly prosecuted during the Biden years can apply. This explicitly includes the nearly 1,600 people charged in connection with the January 6 attack on the Capitol — the same people Trump pardoned on his first day back in office.

The president who incited an insurrection, pardoned the insurrectionists, and is now preparing to pay them with taxpayer money.

The Personal Benefit

Throughout Tuesday, May 19, Vice President Vance and administration officials repeated a single defense: Trump would not personally benefit from the fund.

“Is a dollar of this money going to Donald Trump personally? No,” Vance told reporters. “Is a dollar of this money going to Donald Trump’s family? No.”

That defense lasted approximately four hours.

On Tuesday afternoon, the Justice Department quietly posted a one-page addendum to the settlement agreement on its website. Signed by Todd Blanche, the document stated that the government would be “FOREVER BARRED and PRECLUDED from prosecuting or pursuing” any pending tax claims against Trump, his family members, and his businesses.

No court ordered this. No negotiation with an adversary produced it. Trump’s former personal lawyer, now acting as attorney general, simply signed away the IRS’s authority to audit the president — the same president whose $10 billion lawsuit against the IRS had been the pretext for the entire arrangement.

The financial implications are enormous. In 2024, the New York Times reported that a loss in a pending IRS audit could cost Trump more than $100 million. IRS procedures call for mandatory annual audits of the president’s tax returns. Those audits are now dead. Not suspended. Not deferred. “FOREVER BARRED.”

Federal law prohibits the president and other executive officers from instructing the IRS to start or stop specific audits — but the statute appears to include a carve-out for the attorney general. Trump found the loophole. His lawyer signed the paper.

Danny Werfel, who led the IRS under Biden, told The Atlantic he could not envision any scenario in which granting that kind of audit immunity “would be an appropriate settlement term or remedy.”

One person who apparently agreed: Brian Morrissey, the general counsel of the Treasury Department — a Trump appointee — who resigned on Monday, hours after the fund was announced. When your own appointee walks away from the arrangement, the self-dealing is no longer deniable. It is documented in a resignation letter.

So the defense collapses. No dollar from the fund goes to Trump personally. But permanent immunity from IRS audits — worth potentially hundreds of millions — goes directly to Trump, his sons, and his businesses. The fund was the headline. The audit immunity was the prize.

The Legitimacy Operation

Acting U.S. Attorney General Todd Blanche

The False Precedent

The Justice Department’s announcement cited legal precedent: the Keepseagle v. Vilsack settlement, in which the Obama administration created a $760 million fund to compensate Native American farmers who had faced decades of systemic discrimination by the Department of Agriculture in farm loan programs from 1981 to 1999.

The comparison exposes how fundamentally different the two arrangements are.

Keepseagle was a court-approved class action settlement with a defined plaintiff class, clear evidence of harm documented over 18 years, court oversight of the settlement process, specific eligibility criteria and compensation caps ($50,000 per claimant, up to $250,000 with additional documentation), and independent claims administration. When $300 million remained after all claims were paid, it went through a court-supervised cy pres process: $38 million to nonprofits supporting Native American farmers, $266 million to a trust funding programs for Native farmers for 20 years.

The Trump fund has no defined plaintiff class, no documented harm requiring compensation, no court oversight, no eligibility criteria beyond claiming “weaponization,” no compensation caps, a five-member commission appointed by Trump’s former personal lawyer with members subject to firing by Trump, and any remaining money reverting to the federal government.

The Obama administration settled a legitimate discrimination lawsuit with court oversight and distributed remaining funds to benefit the affected community. The Trump administration created a slush fund to pay political allies with no judicial review.

Calling these comparable is not a legal argument. It is gaslighting.

The Constitutional Violations

Rep. Jamie Raskin, who identified multiple constitutional concerns with the fund.

Representative Jamie Raskin, ranking member of the House Judiciary Committee, identified at least four ways the fund violates the Constitution.

No Case or Controversy (Article III). No one can be both plaintiff and defendant in the same case, and no president can concoct a lawsuit against the government so he can “settle” his own bogus case.

Usurpation of Congressional Power (Article I). Only Congress has the power to appropriate money, and Congress never voted on a $1.7 billion slush fund at the Department of Justice.

Violation of the 14th Amendment (Section 4). The Fourteenth Amendment prohibits the government from assuming any obligation incurred in aid of insurrection or rebellion. If this fund pays January 6 rioters, Trump will be using federal taxpayer dollars to compensate participants in insurrection.

Separation of Powers. Ninety-three House Democrats filed an amicus brief minutes after Trump dismissed his lawsuit, arguing the arrangement violates separation of powers. “Never in the history of the United States has a sitting president sought a monetary settlement from the government he leads,” the brief stated.

Even conservative legal scholars expressed concern. Ed Whelan, a former Scalia clerk, called the fund “highly questionable.”

Two emerged in the same week.

The suit alleges the administration exceeded its statutory authority by creating the fund without congressional authorization, calling it “a slush fund to finance the insurrectionists and paramilitary groups that commit violence in his name.” More than 150 officers were injured during the January 6 attack. Why This Proves the Series Thesis

I began this project on Thanksgiving 2025 asking a simple question: Why has every mechanism of accountability failed when it comes to Donald Trump?

Forty-seven essays later, I thought I had answered it. The legal system failed because prosecutors were cautious and the defendant became president. The electoral system failed because voters didn’t know, didn’t believe, or didn’t care. Congress failed because partisanship overwhelmed institutional duty. The media failed because both-sides journalism cannot process a subject who operates only in bad faith.

But I missed something. I documented the failures retrospectively — fraud unpunished, assaults unavenged, insurrections pardoned, convictions discharged. What I did not fully grasp until May 18 is that the failure is not just historical. It is structural and ongoing.

The $1.776 billion slush fund could not exist if any single accountability mechanism were functioning.

If the courts worked, Judge Williams would have blocked it. If Congress worked, the House would refuse funding or the Senate would investigate. If the Justice Department worked, the acting attorney general would not be Trump’s former personal defense lawyer administering a fund to pay Trump’s allies. If the media worked, the Keepseagle comparison would be exposed as fraudulent in every outlet, not just partisan ones. If the electoral system worked, the political cost of paying January 6 rioters with taxpayer money would be prohibitive.

None of these mechanisms are working. And Trump knows it.

The fund did not even operate alone. On the same day the Justice Department announced the settlement, federal prosecutors asked a judge to drop all charges against Gautam Adani, the Indian shipping magnate accused of running a massive bribery scheme. Adani had hired one of Trump's personal lawyers, who reportedly told the Justice Department that his client would invest $10 billion in the American economy. The price of justice: a promise of investment. On May 18 alone, the DOJ settled the president's manufactured lawsuit, created a fund for his allies, and dismissed charges against a foreign billionaire who hired the right lawyer. By Tuesday, they added IRS audit immunity for the president's family. Four transactions. Two days. One department. Zero accountability.

The fund is the logical endpoint of 58 years of accountability evasion. When you face no consequences for fraud, no consequences for sexual assault, no consequences for inciting insurrection, no consequences for 34 felony convictions, the next step is obvious: convert the presidency into a payment system for your allies, structured as a legal settlement, administered by your lawyer, funded by taxpayers, and concluded before oversight returns.

He is not hiding it. The amount — $1.776 billion — is a taunt. The timing — the day after I finished documenting his impunity — is almost poetic. The mechanism — suing yourself, settling with yourself, paying your allies, immunizing yourself from audits — is so brazen that describing it accurately sounds like hyperbole.

This is why I cannot conclude this series. Not because there will always be one more scandal to document — though there will be. But because the thing I am documenting is not a series of discrete failures. It is a system that has collapsed.

Editor’s Note — May 30, 2026

The court did not stay closed.

On Friday, May 29, federal judge Kathleen M. Williams — the same judge who had been “stripped of jurisdiction” when Trump withdrew his suit eleven days earlier — reopened the case. Two additional details from Judge Williams’s order deserve attention. Both men may now face questions from a federal judge.

When this addendum was first published, the argument was that Trump had perfected the art of accountability evasion — that the pattern documented across 46 parts of this series had reached its logical endpoint: a president suing himself, pocketing a slush fund, and walking away before anyone could ask questions.
