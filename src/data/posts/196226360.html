The Verdict

Cassidy finished third. He was eliminated. The full accounting is below.

February 13, 2021

The second impeachment trial of Donald Trump lasted five days. When the Senate voted on conviction, the result was 57-43 for guilty — a majority, but short of the two-thirds required to convict. Seven Republicans crossed the aisle.

One of them was Bill Cassidy of Louisiana.

Cassidy is a physician. He is methodical, careful, and not given to dramatic gestures. His vote was not a declaration of war. It was a medical man’s assessment of the evidence: the president had incited an attack on the Capitol; the evidence supported that conclusion; the vote should reflect the evidence. He said so plainly. His state Republican Party censured him within hours. He accepted the censure with the equanimity of a man who had expected it.

Five years later, that single vote is the entirety of the charge against him. Not his record — and Cassidy has a record. As the top Republican on the Senate health committee, he has delivered infrastructure money, healthcare funding, and disaster relief to Louisiana for a decade. His seniority in a Senate where seniority matters has been worth billions to his state. He is, by any conventional measure, an effective senator.

None of that appears in the prosecution’s case. The prosecution has one exhibit, and it is five years old, and it is a piece of paper with Cassidy’s name on it.

Welcome to the Louisiana Loyalty Trial.

The Charge and the Prosecutor

Trump moved with unusual precision in this case. In January 2026 — a full sixteen months after the trial and five years after the vote — he posted on Truth Social: “RUN, JULIA, RUN!!!” He called her a great star, a total winner, a true America First patriot.

Julia Letlow official portrait

Julia Letlow is a congresswoman from Louisiana’s 5th district. She took the seat in a 2021 special election after her husband Luke, who had just been elected, died of COVID-19 before taking office. She is a former university administrator with a doctorate from the University of South Florida. She has no particular record of legislative achievement, no distinctive policy positions, and no history of challenging her party’s leadership. What she has is loyalty — abundant, visible, performative loyalty — and a willingness to run.

“In a state as conservative as ours,” she said at her launch event, “we shouldn’t have to wonder how our senator will vote when the pressure’s on.”

This is the prosecution’s entire argument. Not here is what I will do for Louisiana. Not here is where my opponent has failed you. But: you should not have to wonder. The senator’s job, in this formulation, is not to represent Louisiana’s interests but to relieve the president’s uncertainty. Predictable obedience is the qualification. Everything else is decoration.

There is one detail about the prosecutor that deserves noting, because of what it reveals about the trial’s internal logic. After her husband died of COVID in late 2020, Julia Letlow urged Americans to get vaccinated, calling herself “a huge proponent of the vaccine.” Her children are fully vaccinated. She is, in other words, running to replace the pro-vaccine senator on the grounds that he is insufficiently loyal — while holding the same position on vaccines that she must now keep quiet about to win. The accusation is not that Cassidy believes the wrong things. It is that he has, on occasion, said them out loud.

The Complication: Fleming

If this were a straightforward Trump-backed challenger versus disloyal incumbent story, it would be simpler. It is not.

John Fleming portrait

John Fleming is Louisiana’s state treasurer, a former congressman, a physician, and Trump’s own deputy chief of staff during the first term. He entered this race as a third candidate and refused to leave. As of the latest polling, he is actually leading — at 28% to Letlow’s 27% and Cassidy’s 21%, with 22% undecided.

Fleming positioned himself as the authentic Trump loyalist, more experienced than Letlow and more reliable than Cassidy. He helped build the House Freedom Caucus. He had Trump's ear. He just didn't have Trump's endorsement in this race, which put him in the peculiar position of running as a proxy for Trump's values while competing against Trump's chosen proxy.

The result was that the "loyal" vote was split — and it didn't matter. Cassidy, the one man Trump actually wanted gone, consolidated only the small segment of Louisiana Republicans who believed their senator's job involved occasionally exercising independent judgment. It was not enough.

The anti-Cassidy vote did not need to consolidate. There was enough of it for two.

The Parallel Trial: The Supreme Court Rewrites the Rules

Something else happened in Louisiana this week that nearly overshadowed the Senate race — and shouldn’t have.

On April 29, 2026, the Supreme Court handed down its ruling in Louisiana v. Callais. By a 6-3 vote along partisan lines, the court struck down Louisiana’s congressional map as an unconstitutional racial gerrymander — the same map that had been drawn to create a second majority-Black district, which had been created in the first place because courts found the previous map likely violated the Voting Rights Act.

Justice Samuel Alito wrote for the majority. Justice Elena Kagan’s dissent, joined by the court’s other two liberal-leaning justices, called the decision’s effect on Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act essentially fatal: the provision, she wrote, had been rendered “all but a dead letter.”

The practical consequences arrived within 24 hours. Louisiana suspended its House primaries — which had been scheduled alongside the Senate race on May 16 — to allow the legislature to draw new maps. Florida lawmakers immediately passed a new congressional map creating four more GOP-leaning districts, citing the Louisiana ruling. Tennessee signalled it might follow. An NPR analysis estimated the ruling puts at least 15 House seats currently held by Black members at risk.

Senator Raphael Warnock of Georgia said plainly: “They are literally changing the diverse face of our Congress in one fell swoop.”

This is the structural context of the Louisiana Loyalty Trial. While Trump is using the primary system to punish a senator who voted his conscience five years ago, his Supreme Court appointees have been quietly dismantling the legal architecture that protected minority representation in Congress. The retribution tour and the judicial renovation are running in parallel, toward the same destination.

Bill Cassidy voted against Trump on February 13, 2021. The Voting Rights Act, in its meaningful form, effectively died on April 29, 2026. The Loyalty Trials have many courtrooms.

The Irony Cassidy Won’t Escape

Bill Cassidy — this is the serious look of a medical man’s assessment

There is a particular cruelty in Cassidy’s position that deserves naming.

He has not become a Democrat. He has not become a critic of Trump’s broader agenda. He is, by his own description, “a conservative who’s pro-life, pro-Second Amendment, pro-oil and gas, who’s delivered for Louisiana.” He voted for Kennedy’s HHS nomination — despite his own expertise as a physician and his role as chair of the Senate health committee making him more qualified than almost anyone in that chamber to evaluate Kennedy’s fitness. He extracted promises, accepted them as genuine, and voted yes.

The promises were broken. Kennedy’s agency has flouted them. Cassidy has grown more critical of the agency’s decisions — carefully, always keeping those criticisms separate from Trump himself, as though the president and his appointee are entirely unrelated.

The specific promises that were broken deserve naming, because they are not abstract policy disputes — they are the work of Bill Cassidy’s medical life.

Cassidy spent twenty-five years as a liver doctor at a charity hospital. He started a programme that vaccinated 36,000 Louisiana children against hepatitis B. He has spoken often about loading an 18-year-old patient into a medevac helicopter, knowing a $50 vaccine could have saved her. When Kennedy came before his committee, Cassidy was more qualified than almost anyone in the Senate to evaluate what Kennedy’s tenure at HHS would mean for public health. He said so directly: “If there is any false note, any undermining of a mama’s trust in vaccines, another person will die from a vaccine-preventable disease.”

Kennedy’s advisory panel subsequently voted to remove the recommendation for the hepatitis B vaccine — the vaccine that defined Cassidy’s medical career — at birth.

The CDC website still carries a page headlined “Vaccines Do Not Cause Autism.” It survives only because Cassidy blocked its removal. But the rest of the page is now filled with what the American Medical Association has called misleading claims linking vaccines to autism. Cassidy won the battle on the headline. He lost everything beneath it.

And then came the whooping cough. In late 2024 and early 2025, Louisiana was hit with its worst outbreak in three decades. Two infants died. Cassidy personally asked Kennedy to call on parents in the state to get their children immunized — a physician-senator asking the man he had confirmed to help save babies in his own state from a vaccine-preventable disease. Kennedy gave no public response.

This is not political miscalculation. This is a physician watching the work of his life be dismantled by the man he voted to confirm, in exchange for a political accommodation that did not work and will not save his Senate seat.

The return on that accommodation has now been precisely quantified: MAHA Action, the political arm of Kennedy’s Make America Healthy Again movement, has pledged one million dollars to unseat Cassidy. And after Cassidy helped block the confirmation of Casey Means — Kennedy’s wellness-guru ally nominated as surgeon general — Kennedy took to X to deliver the verdict on the olive branch: Cassidy, he wrote, “once again did the dirty work for entrenched interests seeking to stall the MAHA movement and protect the very status quo that has made America the sickest nation on earth.”

He confirmed the man. The man spent a million dollars to end him. The accommodation cycle is complete.

James Carville, who knows Louisiana politics as well as anyone alive, offered the summary judgment: “Bill Cassidy sold his soul to the devil, and he didn’t get anything for it.”

He bent. He accommodated. He found ways to stay in the fold while retaining a sliver of professional integrity. And none of it mattered. The trial is not about his record. It’s about that piece of paper from February 2021.

This is the message the Loyalty Trials are designed to send: there is no accommodation sufficient. There is no subsequent loyalty that erases a prior deviation. The only defence is to never have deviated in the first place. And since no one can un-cast a vote, the only defendants who escape are the ones who were never charged.

The Verdict

Saturday, May 16, 2026, 11:15 p.m. ET

The five-year grudge paid off.

With 93 percent of the vote counted, Julia Letlow led with 44.8 percent. John Fleming finished second at 28.4 percent. Bill Cassidy — the incumbent, the physician, the chairman of the Senate health committee, the man who had delivered billions to his state — finished third, at 24.7 percent. He will not appear on the June 27 runoff ballot. Decision Desk HQ, NBC News, and the Associated Press all projected the result before 10 p.m. Central. The trial was over in under four hours.

Cassidy did not merely lose. He was eliminated. In a three-way race, more than 73 percent of Republican primary voters chose someone other than their sitting senator. The anti-Cassidy vote did not need to consolidate around a single challenger; there was enough of it for two. Fleming, who did not even have Trump’s endorsement, still beat the incumbent by nearly four points. The loyalty question was not close.

The geography told the rest. Letlow dominated the north — her congressional district, the rural parishes, the evangelical corridor. Fleming carried pockets of the northwest, his old House turf. Cassidy held only what was left: the New Orleans–Baton Rouge corridor, the population centres, the places where a physician-senator’s record on healthcare funding and disaster relief still counted for something. His Louisiana had shrunk to two cities and a highway between them.

On Fox News, as the numbers came in, Cassidy offered his epitaph in the same clinical language he has used for five years: “You may go back and flagellate yourself over decisions in the past. I do not. I move on.” It was the voice of a man who had spent a career treating the evidence as sufficient. The evidence was now sufficient about him.

But the concession speech was different. The clinical mask dropped, and the physician finally named the disease: “Our country is not about one individual. If someone doesn’t understand that and attempts to control others through using the levers of power, they’re about serving themselves.” It was the thing he had not said for five years — the accusation he had kept separate from the man, the diagnosis he had declined to make. He made it on the way out the door.

Derek Babcock, the chair of the Louisiana Republican Party, provided the accommodation’s obituary. Cassidy, he said, had worked hard to earn the party’s forgiveness for the impeachment vote. “I just can’t say that I’m seeing it.” Five years of loyalty, a Kennedy confirmation, billions in funding for the state. The forgiveness was not forthcoming. It was never going to be.

The June 27 runoff will be Letlow versus Fleming — Trump’s endorsed candidate against Trump’s unofficial understudy. It is no longer a trial of loyalty versus independence. It is an argument within MAGA about which form of loyalty deserves the seat. Cassidy is not a party to it. The question of whether a senator may exercise independent judgment and survive has been answered, in Louisiana, definitively.

One last number. Of the seven Republican senators who voted to convict Donald Trump on February 13, 2021, four had already retired or resigned rather than face the primary electorate. Cassidy was one of three who remained in office. He is now the first of the seven to be defeated outright. The precedent is set: there is no statute of limitations, no subsequent accommodation, no record of service sufficient to offset a single act of conscience. The vote is the verdict, and the verdict is permanent.

Trump’s memory is the longest in American politics. Louisiana Republicans have ratified it.

Closing

Bill Cassidy looked at the evidence on February 13, 2021, and voted accordingly. He spent five years explaining that vote, accommodating its consequences, and trying to stay useful inside a party that would prefer he had never cast it. On Saturday night, the party told him it was done listening.

Thomas Massie has a debt clock. Bill Cassidy has a vote — and a career’s worth of work being undone by the man he accommodated to try to protect it.

James Carville’s fourteen words contain the whole story: “Bill Cassidy sold his soul to the devil, and he didn’t get anything for it.”

From up here in Toronto, watching a physician-senator lose his seat for a five-year-old act of conscience — eliminated by a primary challenger whose sole qualification is the promise of obedience — I keep thinking about the Hippocratic oath. First, do no harm. It is a commitment to let the evidence guide the judgment, even when the judgment is uncomfortable. Cassidy cast that kind of vote in February 2021. He spent five years trying to walk it back. The hepatitis B vaccine his career was built on is now under threat from the man he walked it back for. Two babies died of whooping cough in his state, and the man he confirmed would not even ask parents to vaccinate their children. And on Saturday night, 73 percent of his own party’s voters told him none of the rest of it mattered.

Both men on trial in this series — Massie and Cassidy — are guilty of the same offence: doing the job they were elected to do. The next defendant did it even earlier, and more publicly, and the whole country was watching.
