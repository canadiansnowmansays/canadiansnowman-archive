Ronald V. Dellums Federal Courthouse, Oakland, California

There has never been a witness quite like Shivon Zilis in a corporate governance trial.

She joined OpenAI as an adviser in 2016. She served on its nonprofit board of directors from 2020 to 2023. She worked for Elon Musk at Tesla and Neuralink. She is the mother of four of his children. She was present at the Seattle party house in August 2017 when Musk threatened to withhold funding and Greg Brockman feared a physical attack. She was the person Musk texted in February 2018 — two weeks after leaving OpenAI’s board — to ask whether she should “stay close and friendly” with OpenAI to keep information flowing, or begin to disengage. She resigned from OpenAI’s board in March 2023, the month Musk launched xAI, his competing AI company.

She took the witness stand on Wednesday in a gold dress and sunglasses, having arrived at the courthouse with a companion. She was questioned for most of the day.

If the trial’s first week established the founding dispute, Zilis’s testimony established its human architecture — the network of relationships through which Musk remained connected to a company he had nominally left, the personal entanglements that made that network possible, and the question of where loyalty to a mission ends and loyalty to a person begins.

What the Text Messages Showed

The evidentiary core of Zilis’s testimony was a series of text messages entered into evidence — a private channel through which information, personnel strategy, and competitive intelligence flowed between Musk and the company he had left.

On February 16, 2018 — two weeks after Musk’s departure from OpenAI’s board — Zilis texted him: “Do you prefer I stay close and friendly with OpenAI to keep info flowing or begin to disassociate? Trust game is about to get tricky so any guidance for how to do right by you is appreciated.”

Musk’s reply: “Close and friendly, but we are going to actively try to move three or four people from OpenAI to Tesla.”

In the same text thread, Musk added: “There is little chance of OpenAI being a serious force if I focus on Tesla AI.” Zilis responded that there was “very low probability of a good future if someone doesn’t slow Demis down” — referring to Demis Hassabis, the head of Google DeepMind.

A text from August 2017 — before Musk’s departure — showed Zilis updating him on a meeting with Brockman and Ilya Sutskever about equity distribution in the proposed for-profit company. She summarized their view that no single person should have unilateral control over AGI if they developed it. Musk’s reply: “This is very annoying. P”

An April 2018 email showed Zilis offering Musk updates on OpenAI’s fundraising and technical progress — specifically a project to develop an AI that could play video games — while noting she had reallocated most of her time to Neuralink and Tesla, and offering to pull more hours back to OpenAI oversight if he wanted.

OpenAI’s lawyers used these documents to argue that Zilis served as a covert liaison between Musk and the company he was suing — that Musk was never as uninformed about OpenAI’s direction as his testimony implied, and that his decision not to object until ChatGPT became a global phenomenon reflected strategic calculation rather than belated discovery.

What Zilis Said in Her Own Words

Zilis pushed back on the framing throughout her testimony, with a care for language that was itself revealing.

On whether she was funnelling information to Musk: “Funnel? Absolutely not.”

On the “trust game” text — OpenAI’s lawyers used it to imply she was being duplicitous with Altman and Brockman: “I would have preferred it if I’d written ‘trust framework.’” She described the period as the co-founders going through “a weird half-breakup” and said she was trying to figure out “how to navigate, if I was still going to be a facilitation or a bridge.”

On her loyalty: “I had an allegiance to the best outcome, AI for humanity.”

On her role with Musk: she denied being his de facto chief of staff — contradicting Musk’s own testimony last week — and said she had worked for three of his ventures: OpenAI, Tesla, and Neuralink, but that her roles were distinct.

On Microsoft’s $10 billion investment: she said she was comfortable with the deal when she approved it as a board member, but grew more concerned after leaving the board and after a private conversation with Musk. “You are naïve,” he told her. Her worries escalated after the board fired Altman in late 2023 and Microsoft helped restore him five days later. “I remember being terrified,” she said. “After that ouster happened, everything we put together over so many years with the nonprofit lost its teeth and it was not able to fulfill its role.”

The Personal Testimony

Zilis described the origin of her relationship with Musk with a clinical precision that the courtroom appeared to receive in silence.

It began, she said, with “a one-off” at a corporate off-site event. Later, after health issues altered her original plans for single motherhood, Musk “offered to make a donation” as a platonic sperm donor. She accepted. Their relationship later evolved into something romantic.

She said she had a romantic relationship with Musk going back at least a decade. But she did not disclose to Brockman — or to the OpenAI board — that Musk was the father of her children until Business Insider contacted her to say it was about to publish an article naming him.

Brockman had testified Tuesday that Zilis told him in 2021 that she was pregnant with twins via IVF and that her relationship with Musk was “entirely platonic.” He learned the truth from news reports. He then confronted her. She confirmed Musk was the father. Brockman testified that many board members wanted to remove her at that point. The board voted and decided to let her stay. “We trusted her to keep the Elon conflict under control,” he said.

Zilis resigned from the board in March 2023 — the month Musk launched xAI.

Mira Murati — The Deposition

Mira Murati, the former chief technology officer of OpenAI, said she did not completely trust Sam Altman, its chief executive.Credit...Jim Wilson/The New York Times

Before Zilis took the stand, the court heard a video deposition from Mira Murati, who joined OpenAI in 2018 and served as its chief technology officer from 2022 until late 2024. Her testimony added a voice that has been notably absent from the trial’s first week — someone who worked closely with Altman and formed a view of him that is neither Musk’s nor OpenAI’s legal team’s.

Murati said she did not completely trust Altman. He was not always candid with her, she said, and sometimes undercut her role as a key executive. “It was completely management related,” she said. “I was asking Sam to lead with clarity and not undermine my job.”

She also criticized the board over the firing. The board had not been transparent about its reasons for removing Altman and had not understood the consequences of its actions. “They were not prepared for the transition and stabilizing the company,” she said.

Murati’s testimony is analytically significant because it occupies a position neither side fully controls. She is not Musk’s witness — she is not arguing that OpenAI betrayed its nonprofit mission. She is not OpenAI’s witness — she is not arguing that Altman’s leadership was exemplary. She is a former senior executive saying, under oath, that she did not completely trust the CEO and that the board that tried to remove him was also unprepared for what came next. The Farrow-Marantz investigation documented this pattern in prose. Murati put it into the trial record.

What This Confirms

Cross-references to The Asimov Gap

The Zilis text chain is the trial’s most significant governance document. Not because it proves Musk’s case or OpenAI’s — it does neither cleanly. It proves that the governance of the world’s most influential AI company, in the years immediately following its founding, was conducted through a private text channel between the world’s richest man and a woman who simultaneously worked for his companies and served on the nonprofit’s board. The board voted to let her stay when her conflict of interest became undeniable. They “trusted her to keep the Elon conflict under control.” That sentence is the governance of OpenAI in one line.

Murati’s testimony confirms the Farrow-Marantz pattern under oath. The investigation documented that Altman was not always candid with senior executives and sometimes undercut their roles. Murati said the same thing in a video deposition. She is not a Musk ally. She has no apparent stake in the outcome of the lawsuit. She simply worked there and formed a view. The view matches the investigation’s findings.

The “trust game” text is the trial’s most revealing phrase. Zilis said she would have preferred “trust framework.” The distinction she is drawing — between gaming a trust relationship and managing a trust framework — is precisely the distinction at the centre of the entire case. Was OpenAI’s governance a framework designed to produce trustworthy outcomes, or was it a game being played by people with competing loyalties? Zilis’s instinct to reach for the word “game” and her retrospective wish that she had chosen differently is the most honest moment of testimony so far.

The personal entanglements are not a distraction from the governance story. They are the governance story. The board knew about Zilis’s relationship with Musk. They voted to let her stay. They trusted her to manage a conflict of interest that was, by any reasonable standard, unmanageable. The AI company that was supposed to govern transformative technology for the benefit of humanity was, in its critical early years, partly governed through a text channel between its largest donor and his romantic partner, who sat on its board without disclosing that relationship until a reporter forced the issue. This is not a scandal adjacent to the governance failure. It is the governance failure.

What Comes Next

Sam Altman is expected to take the stand. The trial has been building toward his testimony since it opened. Everything Savitt established in Musk’s cross-examination, everything Brockman and Zilis and Murati have added to the record — all of it is preparation for the moment when Altman sits in the chair that Musk sat in and answers questions under oath about what he knew, what he said, and what he did.

The liability phase is expected to conclude by May 21. The judge decides.
