Stephen Gordon · Reported through October 15, 2026

Somewhere in the Texas TikTok feed there is a short clip of a young man approaching James Talarico at an event. He introduces himself, mispronounces the candidate’s name, and asks him to name his favourite drag queen in Texas. Talarico, without much hesitation, names Alyssa Edwards. The young man is delighted.

The clip exists in at least two versions. On the official Republican TikTok account it is evidence for the prosecution. On a Talarico fan account it carries the caption “Talarico recognizes talent!” and has been watched about thirty-two thousand times. Same fifteen seconds, same candidate, same drag queen, opposite verdicts. If you want to understand what the Texas Senate race looks like to the millions of people who now get their news from a phone held vertically, that clip is as good a place to start as any.

Regular readers will notice that the drag theme keeps returning to this series. In The Drag, it was the president. Here it is the feed. Texas has a gift for supplying its own punchlines.

One Day, 1,189 Videos

Stuart A. Thompson of the New York Times did something simple and revealing. With social listening data from the National Conference on Citizenship, a nonpartisan nonprofit that catalogues social media posts for research, the Times collected every TikTok post about the Texas Senate race over a single 24-hour period in September. There were 1,189 of them.

A tour of the sample is a tour of the whole campaign in miniature. There is CNN’s footage of Talarico’s primary victory speech, past 400,000 views. There is a NowThis clip of Talarico explaining what he thinks truly disrespects Jesus, at 2.4 million views, comfortably the largest audience in the set. There is Talarico’s own post of the Jimmy Kimmel interview, billed as the one Trump’s Federal Communications Commission didn’t want anyone to see, at a million views. There is a Talarico answer about “welfare queens” from Jubilee’s Surrounded, recycled by an unofficial fan page. There is a clip about Paxton spending the Fourth of July, America’s 250th, in London and Iceland, while Talarico spent it in Austin with Willie Nelson.

On the other side there is Congressman Brandon Gill of Texas, whose video accusing Talarico of daydreaming about trans children reached 950,000 views. There are commentators with nicknames for Talarico that this series will not repeat. There is an influencer calling him a radical anti-capitalist in campaign-moderate clothing. And there is Team Paxton itself, posting a clip of the candidate asking viewers for help at his website. The automatic captions render it as “Kent Faxon dot com.”

That last one is not a metaphor. It only feels like one.

The Seventy-Thirty Referendum

The headline finding is lopsided. About 70 percent of the videos about the race were about Talarico, positive and negative together. About 30 percent were about Paxton.

That is partly by design. Republicans have tried from the start to make November a referendum on Talarico, and above all on his past remarks about transgender issues. Vinny Minchillo, a Texas Republican consultant whose agency, Glass House Strategy, makes political advertising, explained the logic to the Times without any pretence. Making a race about your opponent, he said, is always a good place to be. He described the contest as a back-alley knife fight with rusty knives.

The strategy has worked, in the narrow sense that the conversation is about Talarico. But look at what that means for the man who is actually on the ballot. Even the pro-Paxton videos in the sample, such as one from the Texas podcaster Solomon Calvin Green, were about Talarico’s supposed shortcomings rather than Paxton’s strengths. Paxton’s own account posted sparingly, mostly clips from public appearances in which he criticised Talarico. In the 24-hour snapshot, one of his posts amounted to five words: “We fight for Texas future.”

This is the same pattern the series noted in San Antonio on October 7, when the president flew in to rescue Paxton’s campaign and spent most of the evening talking about the border, the Democrats and himself. Roger Clemens introduced Trump. Paxton got a brief walk-on. It is now consistent enough to call it a strategy: Republicans in Texas are running against Talarico rather than for Paxton. The trouble with a referendum on your opponent is that it can be won by the opponent.

Two Million and Three Thousand

Some numbers need no commentary. Talarico’s personal TikTok account has more than two million followers, and his videos routinely draw hundreds of thousands of views. In October 2025, when Axios first looked at his social-media strategy, the figure was 1.4 million. He has added the population of a mid-sized Texas city since then.

Paxton’s official account has just over 3,000 followers. That is roughly a high school football crowd in a small town on a Friday night. His most-viewed post has a little over 120,000 views, and it is a video featuring Vice-President JD Vance, not Paxton. A single attack video by Congressman Gill outdrew the official Paxton campaign’s entire presence on the platform.

The official Republican TikTok account, meanwhile, has not mentioned Paxton since early July. It has found time to post about Talarico, including the drag queen clip.

Some of this is generational. Republicans shunned TikTok for years, older voters never warmed to it, and party strategists are still working out what to do with it. Minchillo said it more plainly: being on TikTok is now acceptable, but nobody knows what to do there. That could stand as the motto of the Paxton digital operation.

Still, an absence is an absence. In The Machine, this series counted the debates Paxton has agreed to: none. Then came the San Antonio rally, where his own president barely mentioned him. Now the feed, where his own party’s account hasn’t said his name in three months. A candidate who is missing from his debate stage, from his rally and from his feed is running an unusual kind of campaign. It is a campaign that asks voters to elect the man who isn’t the other one. There is an irony here the Paxton campaign seems not to have noticed. In the second week of October, while its own candidate was missing from the stage, the rally and the feed, it was paying for missing-person posters of James Talarico, who was at home with the flu.

The Clippers

Here the story needs some honesty from Talarico’s side as well.

Hundreds of the videos in the Times sample came from “clippers,” accounts that cut short clips from interviews, rallies and media appearances and post them on what look like fan pages. In the 24-hour window, clippers produced nearly a quarter of all posts about the race. They have flooded the Texas algorithm for anyone looking for information about the election.

Not all of that enthusiasm is spontaneous. The Times found dozens of TikTok accounts that had been paid $4 for every 1,000 views to post positive Talarico clips, according to a job posting and online records of creators who took the work. A company called Frontier Media claimed responsibility for the payments and could not be reached for comment. The Talarico campaign said it was not involved in paying anyone.

There is no reason to doubt the campaign’s denial, and nothing in the reporting suggests it is false. But readers of The Machine will notice a mirror here. That essay documented a Republican candidate whose advertising, until his own $20 million buy in October, was paid for almost entirely by someone other than his campaign. Ninety-nine cents of every pro-Paxton advertising dollar came from super PACs. It now appears that some share of Talarico’s online fervour is also being paid for by someone other than Talarico. Super PACs buy airtime. Whoever Frontier Media is working for buys views.

The difference is that a super PAC must disclose its donors to the Federal Election Commission. Nobody yet knows who is paying the clippers.

That doesn’t make the fan accounts fake, and it doesn’t make Talarico’s two million followers fictional. It does mean the feed should be read the way any sensible person reads a campaign advertisement: with a raised eyebrow and an eye on the cheque.

Not from Around Here

The Times found another oddity: relatively few of the videos were posted by people who live in Texas.

The Texas feed is dominated by outsiders with clear political agendas. One of the more pointed anti-Talarico videos came from Sandy Pomales, a right-wing influencer who lives in Florida. Talarico’s supporters include Texans and Americans from across the country who have adopted the race as their own. A trans creator’s post urging fellow Texans to vote for Talarico and for Gina Hinojosa, the Democratic nominee for governor, even if they have no hope left, reads as authentically local. Much of the rest does not.

The national interest is understandable. This is one of the most competitive races of the midterm season, Talarico is the best-funded Democrat in Texas history, and both parties treat the seat as a test of something larger. Still, the effect is a little strange. On the platform, Texas looks less like a state than a stage set, where performers from elsewhere act out their politics for an audience that is mostly from elsewhere too. The people who will actually decide the race, the ones who need a Texas driver’s licence to cast a ballot, are a minority in their own feed. Katrin Bennhold, host of the Times’ newsletter The World, put the problem in a sentence on October 15, 2026, though she was writing about algorithms in general rather than Texas: “A.I. makes you think that that thing on top of your feed is the most important thing in the world — but you’re the only one reading your feed.” On TikTok, every voter in Texas is watching a slightly different Senate race.

Views Are Not Votes

Dallas Jones, a Democratic strategist and president of the Texas consultancy Elite Change, gave the Times the most important sentence in the piece. “It’s a turnout race,” he said. “It’s an enthusiasm thing.” In his view, positive clips about Talarico could help drive voters to the polls.

Put that beside a voice from the other side of the race. In San Antonio on October 7, Julie Funk, a retired first-grade teacher from Bulverde, told the Times she would vote for Paxton: “Look, we’re voting for him, let’s just put it that way.” Her friends winced when his name came up. That is the Republican coalition at the moment: committed, unenthusiastic, and largely absent from the platform where Talarico’s support is loudest.

On paper, this is the Democrats’ advantage. A party that cannot match Greg Abbott’s machine on the ground, as The Machine argued, has built something else instead: a vast, cheap, self-replicating broadcasting network that reaches voters who never watch local news. But the Axios piece in October 2025 carried a warning from political consultants that posting on TikTok does not necessarily win over persuadable voters, and that warning still stands.

There are three problems with assuming that views will become votes.

The first is demographic. The platform skews young, and midterm electorates skew old. The voters TikTok reaches best are the ones least likely to turn out in a November without a presidential race at the top of the ballot.

The second is geographic. If many of the viewers aren’t Texans, a million views may represent a few hundred thousand Texans, or far fewer. Nobody outside the platform knows the real figure.

The third is the one this series keeps returning to. A feed cannot drive anybody to a polling place that has been closed. It cannot take a voter off the 1.2 million-name suspense list. It cannot restore the straight-ticket option Texas abolished. Enthusiasm is necessary in Texas, but it has never been sufficient. Beto O’Rourke had enthusiasm to spare in 2018.

Early voting opens on October 19 and closes on October 30. Election Day is November 3. By November 3, the feed will finally meet the ballot box, and we will learn how much of the enthusiasm on screen was real.

The Parody Stage

In the last part of the Times sample there is a comedian with a mock attack ad. He announces, with great solemnity, that Talarico wants gay abortion taught in elementary schools. A large red X appears on screen. “Not on my watch.”

That is the stage a political attack reaches just before it stops working. When the charges against a candidate become familiar enough for comedians to parody, they are no longer persuading anyone. They are entertaining people. The rusty knives are still out, but the audience has started laughing at the knife fight.

Ken Paxton set out to make this race about James Talarico. On TikTok, at least, the internet obliged him completely, so completely that it seems to have forgotten Paxton is running. Seventy percent of the conversation is about his opponent. His own account is followed by about as many people as a small-town football game. His party’s account hasn’t mentioned him since July. At his own rally, a retired pitcher introduced the president, and Paxton got a walk-on. And when Paxton finally asked the platform for help, its software couldn’t spell his name.

The referendum Paxton wanted is under way. He may yet win it. But a referendum on your opponent only works if voters remember there is someone else on the ballot, and on TikTok they seem to have forgotten.
