There is an old fable about a tortoise and a hare, and every child who has heard it knows the moral. Every adult who has worked in a large organization knows the moral is routinely ignored. Speed is exciting. Speed photographs well. Speed gets the press conference. Patience gets a footnote.

On Sunday, October 11, 2026, a six-wheeled tortoise roughly the size of a small car crossed a line no other vehicle has ever crossed off this planet. As Kenneth Chang reported in the New York Times on October 12, 2026, NASA’s Perseverance rover pushed its odometer past 28.06 miles, overtaking the total distance driven by Opportunity, the smaller rover that trundled across Mars from 2004 until it fell silent in 2018. NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory confirmed the rover’s position on Monday, October 12, and announced the new total: 28.1 miles, or about 45 kilometres. No machine anywhere in the solar system, excepting the ones on Earth, has driven farther.

Nobody at Starbase, Texas, appears to have noticed.

Last week’s instalment of this series, published October 6, 2026, asked who gets to own the Moon and who gets to put a nuclear reactor on it. This week’s question is quieter and, I think, more revealing. While SpaceX races to catch a spaceship with a pair of mechanical chopsticks, a robot on Mars has been quietly demonstrating a different philosophy of exploration altogether. It set its record by stopping.

The Record That Waited

Mission managers expected Perseverance to pass Opportunity’s mark in August 2026. Then the rover rolled up to something unusual at the northern end of a valley in a region called Lac de Charmes, just outside Jezero Crater: a scatter of light-coloured rocks sitting in a landscape that is overwhelmingly dark.

Most of the Martian crust is basalt, a fine-grained volcanic rock of dull grey. The planet’s famous red comes from rusted dust, not from the stone underneath. So when something pale turns up, geologists pay attention, and the rover’s team did what scientists do. They stopped and looked. For two months.

The pale rocks turned out to be gabbros, coarse-grained cousins of basalt that form deeper underground, where magma cools slowly enough to grow large crystals. The rocks in Lac de Charmes are some four billion years old. Bedrock that ancient no longer exists on Earth, where plate tectonics has spent the intervening aeons dragging the old crust down into the planet’s interior and melting it. The science team now suspects these particular blocks bear the marks of three separate meteor impacts: one that melted the crust and produced the gabbro, a second that shocked and remelted it, and a third that flung the blocks to where they now sit. The rover tried to drill one for a sample. The rock was too hard.

The record, in other words, was postponed for a geological detective story. Katie Stack Morgan, the mission’s project scientist, put the order of priorities plainly when Chang interviewed her in early October: “it’s the rocks that really drive the mission.”

Hold that sentence in mind. We will come back to it.

From 341 Feet to Driving Itself

It is worth remembering how far this capability has come, because the distance is the point.

NASA’s first Mars rover, Sojourner, landed in 1997. Over twelve weeks it covered 341 feet, less than one-fifteenth of a mile. It could execute only the exact commands it was sent, and on one occasion it wedged itself against a rock with a wheel in the air because a human driver on Earth had misjudged a distance. Every centimetre of Sojourner’s journey had to be planned by a team of engineers working across a communications delay of many minutes.

Perseverance plans its own route. Its cameras build three-dimensional maps of the terrain ahead, its software picks a safe path, and it drives without turn-by-turn instructions from Pasadena. An additional, faster computer chip makes it markedly better at this than its near-twin Curiosity, which landed more than eight years earlier and has covered less ground. Mark Maimone, one of the rover’s drivers at JPL, told IFLScience that more than 90 per cent of Perseverance’s driving is now done autonomously.

The result shows up in the arithmetic. According to NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory, Perseverance completed a full Martian marathon, 26.2 miles, on June 14, 2026, the 1,890th Martian day of its mission, after five years and four months of driving. Opportunity needed eleven years and two months to cover the same distance.

Readers of my companion series, The World’s Driverless Race, will appreciate the irony. Here on Earth, the self-driving industry has spent a decade and many billions of dollars promising autonomy that keeps arriving next year. Meanwhile, a government robot on another planet, operating with a twenty-minute radio delay and no roadside assistance, has quietly been driving itself for most of the past five years. It does not hold investor days. It does not post on X. It simply drives, and when it finds something interesting, it stops.

The Margin Is the Message

Here is the detail in Chang’s reporting that I find most quietly devastating, at least for anyone who has followed this series.

Before launch, NASA certified Perseverance to drive 20 kilometres, or 12.4 miles. It has now driven more than twice that distance. Steven Lee, the mission’s project manager, noted that the rover is well past its certification, and NASA is now working to extend that certification to 100 kilometres, just over 62 miles. Matthew Wallace, now JPL’s deputy director for flight systems, told the Times he could see the rover going beyond 100 kilometres if things continue as they have, unless the team stumbles onto an extraordinary site and decides to stay put for years.

That is what engineering margin looks like. Perseverance was built so conservatively that it keeps outliving its own paperwork. Every mile beyond the twelve it was rated for is a mile of headroom its designers deliberately bought, and the dividend is a rover that may still be working when some of the people who built it have retired.

Now set that beside the summer this series has chronicled. In Part IX, I wrote about SpaceX’s public offering as the thinnest float in history. In Part X, I followed Starship Flight 13 through three scrubs to a July 24 launch in which only 10 of the booster’s 13 engines relit for landing. The booster was lost; the ship survived. Flight 14 finally reached orbit on September 28, 2026, deploying 26 Starlink satellites, and I covered it in Part XII. That was a genuine achievement, and I said so.

But it is a programme that lives on the edge of its tolerances by design. SpaceX’s philosophy is to fly, break, learn, and fly again, and there are real arguments for it. Hardware-rich iteration has produced the most capable rocket family in history. The problem is not the method. The problem is the claim, made loudly and often, that this method is the only serious way to explore space, and that the slower, margin-heavy, science-first approach of NASA’s robotic programme is a relic.

Perseverance is the rebuttal. It is, by any measure, the most productive vehicle ever to drive on another world. It got there by being overbuilt, underhyped, and willing to wait.

The Samples Nobody Is Coming For

There is a sadder thread in Chang’s report, and it leads straight back to Elon Musk.

Perseverance was sent to Jezero Crater because the crater was once a lake and its ancient river delta was a promising place to look for traces of past life. The rover has been sealing rock cores in tubes ever since, some carried aboard, some deposited in a cache on the Martian surface. Among them are samples from a rock nicknamed Cheyava Falls, drilled in 2024, which contains minerals that could be signs of ancient microbial life. These are, potentially, the most important geological specimens humanity has ever collected.

They were supposed to come home. Mars Sample Return, a joint effort between NASA and the European Space Agency, was designed to retrieve them. It has been cancelled because it was judged too expensive. Chang notes that NASA could revive it as a commercial mission but has made no announcement.

Consider the shape of that sentence. The United States government, which on August 20, 2026 announced a national space transportation policy aiming for more than 1,000 launches and re-entries a year on American soil by 2030, has concluded it cannot afford to go back to Mars for the groceries. The samples sit in their tubes. The cheapest path to retrieving them, if there is one, may run through a commercial bidder.

And who is the most obvious commercial bidder for a Mars mission? The man who has spent two decades promising to build a city there. I make no prediction about whether SpaceX will bid, or whether NASA will ask. I simply observe that the most concrete scientific reason to go to Mars right now is sitting in a few dozen sealed tubes, and the loudest advocate for going to Mars has so far preferred to talk about colonies.

A Planet Barely Fit for Purpose

Which brings me to the second article that crossed my desk this week, and to the question underneath all of Musk’s Martian rhetoric.

Musk has long argued that humanity must become multiplanetary as insurance against catastrophe on Earth. Mars, in this telling, is the backup drive. As Natalie Wolchover reported in the New York Times on October 13, 2026, launching a new series called “Other Worlds,” a growing number of astronomers think the more interesting question is not whether we can survive somewhere worse than Earth, but whether there are places better.

Their starting point is humbling. Earth, it turns out, is only marginally habitable. Had our planet formed just 2 per cent closer to the Sun, its water would have boiled away before life could take hold. The Sun is slowly brightening, and in roughly half a billion years, Earth will be too hot for complex life. We are not living on the ideal planet. We are living on one that got lucky and is running out of luck.

The astronomers René Heller and John Armstrong proposed in 2014 that the best homes for life might be “superhabitable” worlds: rocky planets 20 to 30 per cent wider than Earth and about twice as massive, with thicker atmospheres, stronger magnetic fields, and shallow, island-dotted seas. The ideal host star would be a K-dwarf, an orange star cooler than the Sun that burns steadily for tens of billions of years, giving evolution far longer to work. Heller put the challenge to anthropocentric thinking in a phrase: “why should Earth be a reference?”

If Earth itself is only marginally habitable, then Mars is not a backup. It is a fixer-upper with no roof, no insulation, a thin, unbreathable atmosphere, and a radiation problem in every room. That does not make exploring it worthless. It makes the “insurance policy” framing a sales pitch rather than a plan.

And notice how the serious search for a better home is actually being conducted. It is being done by looking. According to the European Space Agency, its Plato telescope, which carries 26 cameras designed to find rocky planets around Sun-like stars, is scheduled to launch in March 2027 from French Guiana, aboard a European Ariane 6. Heller, one of Plato’s leaders, expects it may turn up perhaps a dozen promising super-Earths around K-dwarfs. The Extremely Large Telescope under construction in Chile and NASA’s planned Habitable Worlds Observatory in the 2040s are meant to study their atmospheres for signs of life.

None of these instruments will ferry a single colonist anywhere. All of them will tell us more about where life might actually thrive than any number of tower catches in Texas.

To the Moon, Alice

This series takes its title from Ralph Kramden’s famous threat, the bluster of a man promising grand action he has no intention of carrying out. It has always been a series about the gap between what is promised in space and what is delivered.

This week, the gap runs in an unexpected direction. The thing that delivered was the thing that promised least. Perseverance did not announce a date for its record. It did not tell anyone it would be the first to anything. It set out with a certification for 12.4 miles, drove 28.1 and counting, and when it found four-billion-year-old rocks it thought were worth studying, it let the record wait.

Flight 15 is next. As New Space Economy reported on October 11, 2026, SpaceX is now aiming for November, with planning information circulated on October 8 pointing to a tentative date of November 8, after earlier material had suggested October 19. The headline objective is the first attempt to catch a Starship upper stage with the launch tower’s arms. If it works, it will be a remarkable piece of engineering, and Part XV will say so.

But I will be thinking about the tortoise. On Mars, the rocks drive the mission. At Starbase, the mission drives the calendar, and the calendar keeps losing.
