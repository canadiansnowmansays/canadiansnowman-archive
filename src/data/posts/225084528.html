Painted on the side of the spacecraft were two words: DON’T PANIC.

It was a joke, of course. The upper stage that flew on Starship Flight 15 was the 42nd off the production line, and in Douglas Adams’s The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy, 42 is the answer to the ultimate question of life, the universe and everything. “Don’t Panic” is the reassuring inscription on the cover of the fictional Guide itself. SpaceX engineers have a sense of humour, and somebody with a paint roller decided this was the ship to show it on.

At about 4:20 p.m. Eastern time on Saturday, November 14, 2026, nobody needed to panic. As Kenneth Chang reported from Starbase for The New York Times, the spacecraft dropped out of a grey Texas sky, pivoted from its belly-flop to vertical, hovered beside the launch tower and slid between the two giant mechanical arms known as the chopsticks, which closed around it and held it in midair. “That was an amazing view,” Chang wrote.

It was. For the first time in history, the upper stage of an orbital rocket came back from space to the very pad it launched from and was caught.

In Part XIV, I wrote that if this worked, it would be a remarkable piece of engineering, and that Part XV would say so. So let me say it plainly, before the Leacock in me gets loose: it worked, and it was remarkable.

Now let us talk about the other half.

The Double Catch That Was a Single

Flight 15 was sold as a “double catch.” Both stages, the 33-engine Super Heavy booster and the ship on top of it, were to be plucked from the air by the same tower, the first full recovery of an orbital rocket in history. On November 6, 2026, Elon Musk called the manoeuvre “the critical breakthrough needed to make life multiplanetary,” as Teslarati reported.

The rocket lifted off at 5:47 p.m. Eastern time on Friday, November 13, 2026, into what Chang described as a clear azure sky. The ascent was nearly flawless. Then Booster 22 separated, turned around and headed home, and somewhere over the Gulf of Mexico it changed its mind.

According to Space.com, SpaceX called off the catch about six and a half minutes into the flight. The booster had detected a problem with its guidance, navigation and control system and, rather than steer a misbehaving stainless-steel skyscraper at the tower, it performed a simulated landing over open water. The Times noted that this ended “with a loud explosion.”

The explanations arrived in the usual order. First, the webcast commentators said they did not know why the catch had been called off. Then, as Drive Tesla Canada reported, Musk posted that the “booster was slightly low on fuel.” By the next day, as Teslarati reported in a separate piece, the account had firmed up: the engines had slightly underperformed, the booster ran short of propellant, and the fix would include better engine consistency and a software change to shut the engines down “about one second earlier.”

And then the sentence that has become something of a company refrain: “This booster loss does not change our timeline to reach high volume, fully reusable Starship flights.”

To be fair, and fairness is the price of the sardonic licence, the booster did exactly what a well-designed system should do. It detected a fault and declined to risk the tower. That is good engineering. It is simply not the double catch that was promised, and “the critical breakthrough needed to make life multiplanetary” is still, by Musk’s own definition, waiting for Flight 16.

Twenty-Two Hours in Orbit

The ship had a much better weekend.

It reached an orbit about 170 miles (275 kilometres) up and, over the following 42 minutes, released 26 of SpaceX’s new Starlink V3 satellites. SpaceX says that single batch adds about one terabit per second of capacity to its network, roughly equal to ten launches of the older V2 satellites. Three of the satellites carried cameras pointed back at the ship, to photograph the heat-shield tiles on its underside before re-entry.

This is a clever piece of engineering self-examination. Heat-shield tiles have been a recurring worry through Starship’s test flights, with tiles shed and scorched on the way down. Now the rocket brings along its own dental mirror. Overnight, SpaceX reported that ground cameras and two of the Starlink satellites showed the tiles in good condition. Flight controllers waved off the early-return options near Hawaii and Chile and let the ship complete its full 14 orbits, some 22 hours in space.

Saturday’s weather at Starbase was cool, breezy and grey, and Chang noticed that fewer spectators had turned up than for Friday’s launch. A SpaceX employee handed out foam earplugs at the press site. At 3:26 p.m. Eastern, one engine fired briefly and the ship dropped out of orbit. At 4:11 p.m. it survived peak heating over Mexico. At 4:19 p.m. the sonic booms reached the viewing site. At 4:23 p.m., Chang typed three words: “It was caught!”

SpaceX’s own post was less restrained: “Mechazilla catches Starship! Congratulations to the entire SpaceX team on this incredible achievement!”

Nor was anyone else. Gwynne Shotwell, SpaceX’s president, called the flip from belly-flop to upright “alien-level technology.” NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman posted that “it was almost magic.” Musk reported that he had hugged Ship 42 when it came home. And in The Atlantic, executive editor Adrienne LaFrance recalled that Tom Mueller, the engineer Musk hired as SpaceX’s first employee in 2002, had described the meeting years earlier when Musk first proposed catching a rocket “like catching a fly with chopsticks.” “We all just rolled our eyes,” Mueller said. “Some of us did not believe it could be done.”

LaFrance managed to hold two thoughts at once, which is more than most commentary on Musk manages: that he is “an omnidirectional boor,” and that he “builds awesome rockets.” I would add only that the rockets are built by several thousand people in black T-shirts, which brings us to the factory.

The Toy Factory

The day before the launch, SpaceX gave reporters a rare tour of Starfactory, the million-square-foot plant at Starbase where the rockets are built. No photographs were allowed, so Chang described it in words. It is gleaming white, staffed largely by people in black T-shirts and jeans, and laid out like an automobile assembly line, with booster production down one side of a long open aisle and “Shipland” down the other.

The numbers are startling. Each booster or ship takes seven to eight months to build, but so many are under construction at once that one booster rolls out every four weeks and one ship every two. Installing a single Raptor engine used to take five to six hours. It now takes 30 minutes.

Justin Styer, SpaceX’s senior director of Starship launch, explained the philosophy. Traditional rockets, he said, have been “extremely precious, artisanal, one-off creations.” SpaceX wants spaceflight to feel “extremely lame.” “We hope to unimpress you with the normalcy,” he told the reporters.

I confess I rather like that line. It is the most honest thing anyone at SpaceX has said in this series. The company’s real genius has never been the Mars rhetoric. It is the factory. Henry Ford did not sell the Model T by promising a colony on the Moon. He sold it by making cars boring, cheap and plentiful, and SpaceX is quietly trying to do the same thing with rockets.

Which makes it all the more striking what hangs from the ceiling of the Starfactory lobby: a giant globe of Mars.

About That Globe

The Times included a question in its live coverage that I suspect SpaceX would rather nobody asked: wasn’t Starship supposed to be heading to Mars already?

In March 2025, Musk said on X that Starship would leave for Mars by the end of 2026, carrying Tesla’s Optimus humanoid robots. Earth and Mars line up for the most efficient trip only once every 26 months, and the late-2026 window was the one that promise depended on. No Starship will be going through it. The test failures of 2025 put the programme behind schedule, and the Optimus robots, as the Times put it, “have appeared in demos but do not appear to be ready for official space missions, either.”

So, as I write, the closest thing humanity has to a working presence on Mars is still a car-sized NASA robot called Perseverance, which Part XIV left trundling through four-billion-year-old rocks at a speed best measured with a calendar. It is still there and still driving. It did not need to be caught. It was never coming back.

There is a pattern worth naming here, and it is the one this series was built to track. The engineering advances one real step at a time: an orbit in September, a caught ship in November. The rhetoric advances in leaps that never land: Mars in 2026, ten thousand launches a year by 2030, and every partial result reframed as “the critical breakthrough.” Ralph Kramden never actually sent Alice to the Moon. He just kept promising, louder.

What Comes Next

The real tests come in 2027, and none of them involve chopsticks.

The first is refuelling. Starship arrives in orbit with its tanks nearly empty, so before it can go anywhere useful, to the Moon or beyond, it must be refilled in space. SpaceX plans to launch two Starships in quick succession early in 2027, one from Texas and one from Florida, dock them, and pump supercold liquid methane and liquid oxygen from one to the other. Nobody has ever done this. The eventual plan calls for a whole fleet of tanker flights filling an orbital “gas station.”

The second is NASA. In the second half of 2027, the Artemis III astronauts, launched in an Orion capsule on NASA’s own Space Launch System, will practise docking in Earth orbit, first with Blue Origin’s Blue Moon lander and then with a Starship. That Starship will be an ordinary production model with a docking port bolted on top. It will have no crew cabin and no life support, so the astronauts will not be able to go inside. NASA wants to land astronauts on the Moon by the end of 2028, and it has not yet decided whose lander it will use for Artemis IV, the mission that is meant to do it.

Read that again, because it matters. Twenty months after Musk promised Mars, NASA is planning to test whether its astronauts can attach themselves to the outside of a Starship in Earth orbit. That is a sensible, careful, incremental step. It is Apollo 9, not Apollo 11. And it is about as far from a Martian colony as Toronto is from Mariposa.

Then there is the question every expert raised on Saturday: can this ship fly again? Alistair John, director of the aerospace engineering programme at the University of Sheffield, told the Times that the heat shield would “take a beating” on the way down and that SpaceX still has to prove it and the engines are “OK to go again.” Catching a rocket is not the same as reusing one. Reuse is the whole point, and nobody has demonstrated it yet for a Starship.

John also offered the line that best sums up the method. If something goes really wrong during a catch, he said, the falling ship “could take out the whole tower. It’s higher risk, higher reward.” On November 14, the reward came in.

Don’t Panic

In fairness to everyone, there is one more thing I owe SpaceX.

In Part XIV, I closed by saying that at Starbase the mission drives the calendar, and the calendar keeps losing. This time the calendar held, or near enough. In October, Musk said Flight 15 would “probably launch a little bit before Thanksgiving.” The planning slides said November 8. It flew on November 13, a five-day slip, with the FAA’s licence arriving, as Tesla Oracle reported, only hours before launch, on the same day a nationwide FAA ground stop had snarled American air travel for reasons that had nothing to do with rockets. For SpaceX, that counts as punctual, and I will not pretend otherwise.

So here is the scorecard for Flight 15. One orbit, on schedule. Twenty-six satellites, delivered. A heat shield that, for once, came home in one piece. A spaceship caught from orbit, a world first. And one booster that ran a second’s worth short of fuel and blew itself up in the Gulf, taking the “double” out of the double catch and leaving the “critical breakthrough needed to make life multiplanetary” on the to-do list for Flight 16.

That is a very good weekend for a rocket company. It is not a ticket to Mars.

Douglas Adams, who knew a thing or two about grand cosmic plans, also wrote that the Earth was demolished to make way for a hyperspace bypass, and that the plans had been on display at the local planning office in Alpha Centauri for fifty years. Nobody, the officials pointed out, had bothered to go and look.

I have been looking. Don’t panic. But do keep reading the fine print.
