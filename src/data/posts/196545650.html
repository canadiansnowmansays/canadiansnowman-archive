A Special Sunday Satirical Edition from Stephen

Author’s note: The following is a work of satire. All quotations attributed to named individuals are real. The situation they describe is also real. The author regrets that the distinction between satire and reality has become, in the sixty-fifth day of this war, increasingly technical.

INTRODUCTION: HOW TO WIN

Winning a war is simpler than it looks. The essential steps are as follows:

  1. Start a war.

  2. Win it.

  3. Announce that you have won it.

  4. Continue winning it.

  5. Announce again that you have won it.

  6. Describe the ongoing war as the cleanup phase of the winning.

  7. Launch a new operation to address a consequence of the winning.

  8. Call the new operation a gift.

  9. Repeat from step 3.

The United States of America, the greatest fighting force in human history, unmatched always, has been executing this strategy with precision and clarity and purpose for sixty-five consecutive days. The results speak for themselves, which is fortunate, because the results do not especially resemble winning.

But let us not get ahead of ourselves. This is a User’s Guide. We will proceed in order.

CHAPTER ONE: STARTING THE WAR

Before you can win a war, you must start one. This is easier than it sounds.

The key is to start the war during active diplomatic negotiations, at a moment when your adversary has not yet done the thing you are going to war to prevent them from doing. This creates a useful ambiguity about who started the war, which will come in handy later when you need to describe your adversary as “the clear aggressor.”

Some observers — the kind who read newspapers and remember things — will note that the Strait of Hormuz was open before the war started. This is technically accurate but strategically irrelevant. The important thing is that the Strait of Hormuz is open now, or will be shortly, thanks to a new operation you have just launched to reopen it.

The fact that you are the reason it closed is the kind of detail that belongs in footnotes, and footnotes are for people who have lost.

CHAPTER TWO: WINNING THE WAR

Having started the war, you must now win it. This too is simpler than it looks.

On Day 1, strike more than 13,000 targets. This is roughly twice the firepower of the initial “shock and awe” campaign in Iraq, which took a month. You have done it faster. This is winning.

On Day 2, announce that the enemy’s military has been destroyed. This is also winning, even if the enemy’s military continues to fire missiles at your ships, shoot down your fighter jets, close the world’s most important waterway, attack Gulf states that host your military bases, seize commercial vessels, deploy fiber-optic cable drones that your forces cannot jam, and maintain a negotiating position entirely unchanged from before the war began.

These are details. The important thing is the announcement.

CHAPTER THREE: THE NUCLEAR DUST

A word about the nuclear dust.

Iran had a nuclear program. You bombed it. It has been “obliterated.” The nuclear dust — which is not actually dust, but a gas stored in canisters roughly the size of large scuba tanks, volatile and highly toxic if exposed to moisture — is buried under rubble at Isfahan.

You will get all the nuclear dust.

The fact that Iran now has 11 tons of uranium at various enrichment levels — enough, if further enriched, for 50 to 100 nuclear weapons — is a separate matter. This uranium accumulated primarily after you withdrew from the 2015 nuclear deal in 2018 while Iran was complying. That deal was a horrible, one-sided deal. Under it, Iran had shipped 97% of its uranium to Russia and had too little for a single bomb. You pulled out. Iran enriched. There are now 11 tons.

This is not a contradiction. This is a reason you had to start the war.

The fact that you had to start the war because of a problem you created by ending the deal that solved the problem is the kind of circular logic that lesser minds find troubling. You find it clarifying.

You will get all the nuclear dust. The other 11 tons are a detail for Phase Two.

CHAPTER FOUR: THE CEASEFIRE (FIRST ONE)

After approximately five weeks of winning, you declared a ceasefire. This was also winning.

The ceasefire required Iran to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. Iran agreed to this, in the sense that it announced the strait was open, then reimposed restrictions when you refused to lift your blockade, then the strait became something in between open and closed that shipping companies described as “closed” and you described as “open.”

You posted on Truth Social: “IRAN HAS JUST ANNOUNCED THAT THE STRAIT OF IRAN IS FULLY OPEN AND READY FOR FULL PASSAGE.”

You meant to write “Strait of Hormuz.” You accidentally wrote “Strait of Iran.”

A former senior State Department official named Richard Haass later noted that you had, in this moment of inadvertent precision, named the central strategic consequence of the war.

The strait is, in practice, the Strait of Iran.

You have not mentioned this since.

CHAPTER FIVE: THE BLOCKADE

Having declared a ceasefire, you launched a blockade of Iranian ports. This was separate from the ceasefire. The ceasefire was about stopping the fighting. The blockade was about winning the peace.

Your Treasury Secretary announced that the IRGC leaders were “trapped like drowning rats in a sewage pipe” and that Iran’s oil industry would “soon collapse.” This was on April 27. It is now Day 65. Iran’s negotiating position is unchanged.

The blockade has successfully turned back 34 merchant vessels attempting to enter or exit Iranian ports. It has also, per Lloyd’s List, allowed at least seven Iranian-linked ships to transit through it using false transponder data. The ships disappeared in one place and reappeared in another, like a magic trick performed by a sanctions-evasion specialist.

Meanwhile, Russia — which you have been allowing to sell oil under a sanctions waiver because poor countries needed the supply during the crisis your war created — has been earning an estimated $200 million per day. Russia has used some of this money to provide Iran with satellite imagery showing the locations of your warships.

Senator Coons of Delaware called this “helping your adversaries gain money while you’re at war.” He suggested one did not need to read The Art of War to identify this as a problematic strategy.

You found the blockade very effective. The blockade continues.

CHAPTER SIX: THE ISLAMABAD TALKS (ALL OF THEM)

Having launched the blockade, you sent negotiators to Pakistan. This was the highest-level US-Iran diplomatic contact since 1979. Vice President Vance led the American delegation. The talks lasted 21 hours. No agreement was reached. Iran refused to accept your terms. Vance flew home.

You said Iran can call you.

The following week, you announced your negotiators would return to Islamabad for a second round of talks. They packed. The hotel was cleared. Pakistan distributed a statement to journalists. Iran’s foreign minister arrived with a written proposal. You cancelled the flight. “You’re not making an 18-hour flight to go there. We have all the cards.”

Iran’s foreign minister went to Oman, then came back to Pakistan, then went to Moscow, then went to Beijing.

You reviewed the concept of a deal.

CHAPTER SEVEN: THE UNCONDITIONAL SURRENDER

On Day 7, you posted: “There will be NO DEAL with Iran EXCEPT UNCONDITIONAL SURRENDER.”

This was a clear statement of your negotiating position.

On Day 65, you told reporters you were reviewing a proposal from Iran that would reopen the Strait of Hormuz and set aside the nuclear program for a later phase. You said you “can’t imagine it would be acceptable.” You then clarified you had not read it. They were going to give you the exact wording shortly.

At Tuesday’s Pentagon briefing, a reporter asked your Defense Secretary what happened to the demand for unconditional surrender.

Your Defense Secretary said: “The president hasn’t capitulated on anything.”

He did not explain where the unconditional surrender was.

CHAPTER EIGHT: PROJECT FREEDOM

After sixty-three days of winning, you launched Project Freedom.

Project Freedom is an operation to help guide stranded ships out of the Strait of Hormuz — the strait that was open before the war, that closed because of the war, that you declared open during the ceasefire, that closed again when the blockade continued, that you are now reopening with 15,000 service members, two aircraft carriers, more than 100 aircraft, guided-missile destroyers, the 82nd Airborne Division, and Navy MH-60 helicopters.

Your Defense Secretary called Project Freedom “a direct gift from the United States to the world.”

Your Defense Secretary’s own Pentagon correspondent noted, in real time, that the Strait of Hormuz was open before your Defense Secretary urged you to start the war.

The gift, in other words, addresses a problem created by the gift-giver.

This is a new form of generosity. Students of foreign policy have not previously identified it in the literature.

CHAPTER NINE: THE FIRST DAY OF PROJECT FREEDOM

On the first day of Project Freedom, the following occurred:

Iran fired cruise missiles and drones at US ships guiding commercial vessels through the strait.

Six Iranian speedboats were destroyed by Apache helicopter gunships.

Iran struck the Fujairah Oil Industry Zone — the largest oil storage area in the United Arab Emirates — with a drone. Fire broke out. Three Indian citizens were injured.

Iran fired four cruise missiles at the UAE. Three were intercepted. One landed in the sea.

UAE residents received four shelter-in-place alerts on their mobile phones.

The South Korean cargo ship HMM Namu caught fire after an explosion in the strait.

Four ships transited the strait. Before the war, 130 ships transited daily.

Hapag-Lloyd, one of the world’s largest container shipping companies, announced the strait remained closed for its vessels until further notice.

Iran’s foreign minister posted on social media: “Project Freedom is Project Deadlock.”

Your Defense Secretary said Iran “doesn’t control the strait.”

Both things are true in the sense that neither side controls the strait, and both things are true in the sense that four ships went through and 130 used to.

CHAPTER TEN: THE THRESHOLD

At Tuesday’s Pentagon briefing, a reporter asked General Caine — chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, the highest-ranking military officer in the United States — what would constitute a threshold for restarting major combat operations.

General Caine replied: “The threshold of restarting is a political decision above my pay grade.”

Let us sit with this for a moment.

The chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff has informed the American public, the United States Congress, the world’s shipping companies, 22,500 trapped mariners, the government of Germany, the government of Jordan, the glass factories of Firozabad, the camels of Petra, the gravediggers of Majdal Zoun, and the cooking oil smugglers of Kapikoy that the definition of war — when it begins, when it ends, when it resumes — is whatever the president decides it is.

This is the most important thing said at the briefing.

It is also, in the context of this User’s Guide, the most useful information we have encountered. Because it means the User’s Guide has no fixed endpoint. The winning continues until the president decides it has ended. The ceasefire holds until the president decides it has ended. The gift continues to be given until the president decides he is done giving.

The threshold is a political decision above the pay grade of the man responsible for executing it.

CHAPTER ELEVEN: THE KAMIKAZE DOLPHINS

At Tuesday’s briefing, a reporter asked whether reports of trained kamikaze dolphins in the strait could be confirmed.

General Caine: “I can’t confirm or deny whether we have kamikaze dolphins.”

Your Defense Secretary: “Something some bar units can undertake.”

He then confirmed there is a lane of safe passage through the strait.

The kamikaze dolphins, if they exist, have not been briefed on the threshold question. This may be for the best.

EPILOGUE: HOW TO WIN (REVISED)

We return now to the essential steps, updated for current conditions:

  1. Start a war.

  2. Win it.

  3. Announce that you have won it.

  4. Continue winning it.

  5. The strait closes.

  6. Launch a blockade.

  7. Send negotiators to Pakistan.

  8. Cancel the flight.

  9. Review the concept of a deal.

  10. Launch Project Freedom.

  11. Call it a gift.

  12. Four ships go through.

  13. Iran fires missiles.

  14. Six speedboats are destroyed.

  15. Fujairah is on fire.

  16. The ceasefire holds, technically.

  17. The threshold is a political decision.

  18. The nuclear dust remains at Isfahan.

  19. The camels of Petra are sleeping in the sand.

  20. The glass factories of Firozabad are going cold.

  21. A glass cutter named Saddam Hussein has found four days of work in a month.

  22. “The war is over there, but we’re getting killed here.”

  23. The gift continues.

  24. Repeat from step 3.

Project Freedom is underway. Commerce will be flowing. America is once again leading with strength and clarity and purpose for the benefit of the entire world. Our will is unshakable.

God bless our troops. God bless the mighty United States Navy. God bless the kamikaze dolphins, confirmed or denied.

The ceasefire holds, technically. The threshold remains a political decision. The dolphins are neither confirmed nor denied.
