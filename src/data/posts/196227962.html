The Verdict

Raffensperger finished third. Jones and Jackson advance to a June 16 runoff. The full accounting is below.

The Phone Call

January 2, 2021. Georgia Secretary of State Brad Raffensperger is on the phone with the President of the United States.

“I just want to find 11,780 votes,” Trump says. “Which is one more than we have.”

Raffensperger says no.

He has been saying no, in various formulations, for two months. He said no after the election. He said no after the first recount. He said no after the second recount. He said no after the hand audit. Georgia went for Joe Biden by 11,779 votes. The number was accurate. The process was clean. The result stood.

Raffensperger, a Republican who had supported Trump, told the President of the United States that the votes he was looking for did not exist. The call was recorded. The recording was released. The transcript was published.

And now, five and a half years later, Brad Raffensperger is running for governor of Georgia, polling at eleven percent, being outspent by approximately a hundred million dollars to one, in a primary dominated by two candidates competing to prove who loves Donald Trump more — while a third candidate, Rick Jackson, has built a substantial campaign largely around the promise that he, unlike Raffensperger, would have found the votes.

The Georgia Loyalty Trial is the most operatic of the series. It has a cast of thousands, a $100 million budget, a plot that stretches back to 2020, and a defendant who keeps insisting, with quiet stubbornness, that he did nothing wrong.

The Defendants and the Prosecution

The Georgia governor’s race features four Republicans of note:

Brad Raffensperger — the defendant. The secretary of state who certified the 2020 results, withstood months of pressure, and is now paying the institutional price. He is drawing support from self-described moderates and independents, from libertarian-leaning Republicans uneasy with the MAGA wing, and from the small but real slice of Georgia Republicans who believe that a state official's job is to administer elections accurately rather than to produce politically convenient results. He polls at eleven percent. He loaned his campaign $6.3 million and raised just $1.1 million from donors. He is running on integrity in a primary electorate where integrity, by some definitions, is a disqualifying trait.

Trump’s endorsed candidate Burt Jones at podium

Lt. Gov. Burt Jones — Trump's endorsed candidate. Jones has been the "Trump guy since 2015, not '16," as he puts it, with the competitive pride of an early adopter. He supported Trump's 2020 legal challenges. He has taken "battle scars," as he describes them. Trump endorsed him in August 2025, calling him a man with "the Courage and Wisdom to deliver strong results." He has loaned his campaign $19 million and raised $4.5 million from donors. He polls at approximately 25-27%.

The billionaire wildcard Rick Jackson at rally

Rick Jackson — the wildcard who has changed everything. A billionaire healthcare executive who grew up in foster care, could not afford college, and built a business empire — and who entered the race in February and immediately upended it. As reported in the New York Times, Jackson has contributed more than $83 million of his own money to his campaign — including more than $217,000 in non-monetary contributions for things like legal services, meals, and helicopter rides. He has raised less than $300,000 from donors other than himself. He is 72 years old, a former business partner of Jeb Bush, and has no previous political experience. His ads position him as more Trump than Trump, criticising Raffensperger's 2020 actions and promising the president a governor who will never require convincing. His slogan is essentially: I will never be a problem. He polls at 27-32%, leading the field, sustained by women voters and seniors in what political scientists have described as a counter-intuitive coalition.

Attorney General Chris Carr — a more traditional Republican who did not back Trump’s 2020 lawsuits, has aligned his style with Brian Kemp’s institutionalism, and has been bleeding support since Jackson entered. He polls at roughly six percent and appears to be largely a donor base at this point.

The race has one notable absence. Governor Brian Kemp — term-limited and still the most popular Republican in the state — endorsed none of these four. He saved his political capital for the Senate primary, where he backed Derek Dooley, the former University of Tennessee football coach and son of a famous Georgia football patriarch, against two Republican congressmen. Trump, meanwhile, made the opposite choice: he endorsed in the governor’s race but has stayed out of the Senate primary entirely. The president and the governor have divided the Georgia map between them, each picking the fight that suits his interests.

The Charge Against Raffensperger

The formal charge, never stated explicitly because the Loyalty Trials prefer their indictments implied, is this: in November and December 2020, when it mattered, Brad Raffensperger chose accuracy over allegiance.

The informal charge — the one whispered in ads, in rallies, in the framing of every attack — is that he is a coward, a traitor, a servant of the other side masquerading as a Republican official.

Neither charge acknowledges what Raffensperger actually did. He ran Georgia’s election with fidelity to the process. He ordered multiple recounts. He certified what the recounts showed. He told the President that the votes he wanted did not exist. When he was pressured to say otherwise, he declined. When the call was recorded and released, he stood by the transcript.

This is, in the older usage of the word, what a conservative public official does. He administers the office he holds. He follows the process. He certifies what the count shows. He doesn’t manufacture outcomes.

In the new usage, it is heresy.

The Eighty-Three Million Dollar Question

The most expensive Republican primary for governor in Georgia history has now consumed well over $100 million in advertising spending — most of it in the Jackson-Jones war, with Raffensperger and Carr as largely spectators to their own irrelevance.

Jackson and Jones have fought each other in court, in debate, in ads, and in the kind of personal antagonism that occasionally makes Georgia primaries feel like medieval succession disputes. Jackson sued Jones over campaign finance law claiming an unfair advantage. Jones used his position as lieutenant governor to push legislation targeting his opponent. A group called Georgians for Integrity spent $14 million in ads opposing Jones’s campaign.

The scale of Jackson’s investment deserves its own reckoning. Eighty-three million dollars of personal money — in a governor’s race, not a presidential campaign. He was largely unknown to Georgia voters three months ago. He has no government experience. He has built his entire candidacy on ad saturation during local newscasts, Atlanta Braves games, and streaming platforms. The message, as the Times described it, has been delivered in 30-second bursts: foster care kid, self-made billionaire, no entrenched politician, unfailing partner to Trump. It is the most expensive proof-of-concept in American gubernatorial history for the proposition that loyalty can simply be purchased.

And throughout all of this — through the nine figures and the court filings and the personal attacks — Raffensperger has been running at eleven percent, making his methodical case to a primary electorate that is, by its own description, 80% supportive of Trump, in a race where Trump endorsed someone else.

His path to the runoff was narrow. He needed to consolidate the moderates and independents — voters who were uncomfortable with both the Jackson and Jones versions of performative MAGA loyalty. He needed Carr’s collapse to accelerate. He needed the two frontrunners’ mutual destruction to leave a lane.

The lane did not open. It was, as predicted, a long shot. The long shot missed.

What Raffensperger Is Actually Arguing

He’s not arguing that Trump is wrong about everything. He’s not running as a critic of the MAGA agenda. He has been careful, in fact, to distinguish between his 2020 actions — which he defends as the proper discharge of his duties — and his broader political identity, which he insists is conservative.

“I passed the 2020 test,” he said in a December 2025 Atlanta Journal-Constitution interview. He means it as a statement of character. His primary opponents mean it as a confession.

What Raffensperger is arguing, stripped to its essence, is that there is a kind of Republican who believes in the institutions as well as the outcomes — who thinks elections should be administered accurately, courts should be independent, and state officials should not become instruments of any president’s electoral strategy. That such a Republican exists and is viable. That Georgia contains enough of them to get him to a runoff.

His eleven percent polling average suggested the answer was: not quite enough. His 14.5 percent on election night confirmed it — better than expected, still nowhere close.

The Structural Context

Two pieces of structural context connect this race to the broader series.

The first is the Supreme Court. On April 29, 2026, the court’s Callais ruling gutted Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act. The ruling originated in Louisiana, but its downstream effects arrived in Georgia almost immediately. Governor Kemp called a special session of the legislature for June 17 to redraw the state’s congressional and legislative maps — not for 2026, but for 2028, racing to change them before Republicans potentially lose the power to draw them at all. Teresa Bristol, a 53-year-old registered Democrat in Alpharetta, told the New York Times on Tuesday morning that the VRA ruling had motivated her to vote: “Our forefathers have fought for us to have the right to vote. For our opinions to matter. To have people who represent us.”

The second is the Georgia Supreme Court. Two Republican-appointed justices are up for re-election on Tuesday, with challengers backed by Barack Obama, Kamala Harris, and other leading Democrats hoping to tilt the balance of the formally nonpartisan court. Eight of the nine current justices were appointed by Republican governors. After Virginia’s Supreme Court threw out a voter-approved congressional map this month, the stakes of state court composition have never been more visible. Raffensperger’s 2020 stand was ultimately upheld by Georgia’s courts. Whether those courts remain independent enough to uphold the next such stand is now on the same ballot.

The Verdict

Tuesday, May 19, 2026, 9:45 p.m. ET

The 2020 verdict is confirmed.

With 44 percent of the vote counted and the Associated Press projecting a runoff, Burt Jones led with 37.9 percent. Rick Jackson followed at 34.1 percent. Brad Raffensperger — the man who told the President of the United States that the votes he wanted did not exist — finished third with 14.5 percent. He will not appear on the June 16 runoff ballot. Chris Carr trailed at roughly 10 percent.

Georgia Republicans were given the explicit choice between a man who certified their election accurately and two men who have made careers of criticising him for it. They chose the critics. They chose both of them. Raffensperger’s 14.5 percent outperformed his polling average of eleven percent — a modest vindication that changes nothing about the outcome. He needed a miracle. He got a few extra points and third place.

The runoff will be Jones versus Jackson — Trump’s endorsed lieutenant governor against the billionaire who spent $83 million to prove he didn’t need the endorsement. Jones held first place, which means the Trump endorsement technically “won.” But needing a runoff against a man with no political experience and a chequebook is not dominance. It is the Loyalty Trial’s most expensive proof-of-concept: loyalty can be purchased, the brand has partially detached from the man, and $83 million can buy a competitive position in a governor’s race even without the president’s blessing.

Raffensperger spent $6.3 million of his own money and raised $1.1 million from donors. Jackson spent $83 million. The ratio — roughly 75 to 1 — is the distance between integrity and viability in a Republican primary in 2026.

The same evening, across both the governor’s and Senate primaries, Georgia’s structural politics snapped into focus. On the Democratic side, Keisha Lance Bottoms won with 60 percent — a former Atlanta mayor and Biden administration official who avoided a runoff entirely. On the Republican Senate side, Mike Collins led at 41.4 percent, with Dooley and Carter trailing — Kemp’s endorsed candidate running second, not first. Trump stayed out of the Senate race. Kemp stayed out of the governor’s race. Both men picked the fight that suited them, and both results will shape a November in which Jon Ossoff, the most vulnerable Democratic senator in the country, is sitting on $60 million and waiting.

Closing

There is something almost classical about Brad Raffensperger’s position. The man who did his job correctly was on trial for doing his job correctly. The evidence of his wrongdoing was the accuracy of his work. The prosecution rested on the argument that he should have produced a different result from the same count.

He was the only defendant in the Loyalty Trials whose crime was committed in full public view, on a recorded phone call, while the world watched. He said no to a president who asked him to lie. Five and a half years later, he asked Georgia Republicans to reward that refusal. They gave him 14.5 percent.

From Toronto, watching the Georgia numbers come in on the same night Thomas Massie fell in Kentucky, I keep returning to the same thought. Not because Raffensperger’s defeat was surprising — the polling was clear. But because there was something in the act of running — of standing at eleven percent against $83 million and making the case anyway — that deserved acknowledgment.

He knew what the votes would say. He has always known what the votes say. That was, in fact, his entire crime.

Tomorrow we close the books on all four trials. The verdicts are in.
