“On the advice of counsel, I respectfully decline to answer based upon my rights under the Fifth Amendment to the Constitution.” Anthony Fauci said some version of that sentence, by the count kept across multiple newsrooms, more than one hundred times on Wednesday, July 29, 2026, over the course of a nearly three-hour hearing before the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee. He said it when asked about gain-of-function research funding. He said it when asked about the origins of the virus. He said it, most tellingly, when Senator Josh Hawley of Missouri asked him what day of the week it was, and again when Hawley asked him what color tie he was wearing — questions designed not to elicit information but to demonstrate, for the cameras, just how absurd a blanket invocation of constitutional privilege could be made to look when deployed against questions with no possible incriminating answer at all.

It is worth taking seriously, before assigning any judgment to that silence, exactly what legal position Fauci was actually in when he walked into that hearing room. Joe Biden had granted him a sweeping pre-emptive pardon on January 20, 2025, his last day in office, covering “any offenses against the United States which he may have committed or taken part in” over the preceding eleven years. That pardon is genuinely broad, and Republicans on the committee argued repeatedly, citing the 1896 Supreme Court decision in Brown v. Walker, that a fully pardoned witness forfeits Fifth Amendment protection for the conduct the pardon covers, since a pardoned person can no longer be prosecuted for it and therefore has nothing left to incriminate himself about. That argument has real legal force for anything Fauci did before January 20, 2025. It has none whatsoever for anything he might say inside that hearing room on July 29, 2026 — new statements, under oath, that could themselves form the basis of a fresh perjury prosecution regardless of what the pardon covers retroactively. Health Secretary Kennedy said as much himself this week, telling Fox News that if Fauci “lies again, then he could be subject to perjury prosecution” — a plain acknowledgment, from one of Fauci’s most committed adversaries, that new testimony carried real legal exposure the old pardon could not touch. Legal experts consulted by The Atlantic this week made the same point: whether Fauci could invoke the Fifth Amendment throughout depended on whether a “realistic threat of prosecution” existed, and given Paul’s years-long, repeatedly stated goal of seeing Fauci “behind bars,” that threat was not remotely theoretical.

Framed that way, Fauci’s silence looks considerably less like evasion and considerably more like ordinary legal self-preservation, of the kind any competent attorney would recommend to a client facing a committee whose chairman has spent years publicly promising to see him imprisoned. But the more interesting question this essay wants to sit with is not whether the silence was legally sound — it plainly was — but what it cost him, and whether that cost was worth it, given everything the rest of this series has tried to establish about who Fauci actually is. For a man who built his entire professional identity on the belief that expertise, patiently and clearly explained, would eventually win out over confusion and bad faith — the same belief that carried him through the AIDS-era confrontations with ACT UP, through the diplomatic tightropes of seven presidencies, through the daily briefings where he tried, however imperfectly, to correct a president’s misstatements in real time — sitting silent while accused, at length and on camera, of causing “the largest man-made plague in history” must have represented something close to a professional negation. Fauci built his career on the premise that if you explained the science clearly enough, people would eventually come around. On Wednesday, his own lawyers concluded that explaining anything at all was now more dangerous than saying nothing. That is not merely a legal strategy. It is the final, involuntary retirement of the exact professional instrument Fauci had relied on for fifty years.

The tone of the questioning throughout made clear that information was never really the goal. Hawley, at another point in the hearing, told Fauci directly that he had “become a narcissist and a megalomaniac and a liar” who had “done more to harm science than anybody in my lifetime” — an accusation delivered not as a question requiring an answer but as a statement for the record, since Hawley knew perfectly well what response he would receive. Paul, in his own closing remarks, called the hearing the “capstone” of Fauci’s career and accused him of displaying “hubris the likes of which the world has rarely seen,” language considerably more suited to a closing argument in a criminal trial than to a congressional oversight hearing whose stated purpose was fact-finding rather than sentencing. None of the senators making these statements appeared to expect, or particularly want, a substantive rebuttal. The Fifth Amendment invocation did not silence a debate that was otherwise going to happen; it simply removed the one voice in the room that might have complicated the predetermined narrative each side had arrived to deliver.

There is, as it turns out, a concrete financial answer to the question of why that narrative needed delivering so badly, and it surfaced only in the days after the hearing itself. As Sheryl Gay Stolberg reported for The New York Times, Kennedy, Paul, and Kennedy’s own son have each published a critical book about Fauci, and all three were released through Skyhorse Publishing or its subsidiary Regnery, a company run by Tony Lyons, a close Kennedy adviser who also heads a political action committee supporting Kennedy’s broader agenda. Kennedy’s 2021 book, “The Real Anthony Fauci,” has by his own account sold roughly 1.4 million copies and repeatedly reached the Times‘s own best-seller list; within hours of Fauci’s Fifth Amendment testimony, Kennedy was promoting that same book on social media and on Fox News, citing the hearing’s publicity as though it were simply good timing rather than a predictable consequence of an event he had helped stage. Paul, for his part, reported $275,000 in royalties from the publisher across 2023 and 2024 alone. Ethics experts consulted by the Times — including Donald Sherman of the watchdog group Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington, and Kathleen Clark, a law professor specializing in government ethics — stopped short of alleging any actual legal violation, but did not stop short of calling the arrangement what it plainly resembles: Clark described the hearing itself as functioning, in effect, as “a publicity event for Skyhorse Publishing,” and Sherman called the spectacle of officials accusing Fauci of profiting from public service while simultaneously profiting from a book accusing him of it “pretty rich.” None of this proves that Paul’s decade-long pursuit of Fauci was manufactured purely for royalties — a person can hold a sincere grievance and also benefit financially from prosecuting it in public, and the two motives are not mutually exclusive. But it does mean that when this essay describes Wednesday’s hearing as theater engineered to produce a clip rather than an answer, that theater had an identifiable box office, and several of the people running the production had a direct financial stake in ticket sales.

The hearing itself, considered simply as democratic theater rather than as a legal proceeding, deserves its own separate reckoning, because what happened in that room reflects at least as poorly on the institution conducting it as on the witness refusing to answer. Senator Ron Johnson of Wisconsin used his time to suggest, citing wildly unreliable self-reported data from the Vaccine Adverse Events Reporting System, that Covid vaccines had caused more than 39,000 deaths — a claim public health researchers have shown overstates the real total by roughly a thousand-fold, since VAERS accepts any submitted report without verifying causation. Senator Rick Scott of Florida told Fauci directly that he alone was responsible for every lockdown, every vaccine injury, and the collapse of public trust in American health institutions, a claim that erases the roles of fifty state governors, a sitting president, a Congress that authorized emergency spending, and the basic epidemiological reality of a virus that would have killed people regardless of who was standing at the podium. When Fauci’s own attorney, David Schertler, attempted to read a prepared statement on his client’s behalf explaining the reasoning behind the Fifth Amendment invocation, Paul cut him off, warned him that “another word” would get him removed, and then had security escort him from the room when he continued — an act that, whatever the technical rules of committee procedure permit a chairman to do, meant the one person in the room legally authorized to explain Fauci’s position on the record was physically prevented from doing so.

The contrast with Fauci’s past conduct before this same committee makes the silence feel even more pointed. In a 2021 hearing, when Paul accused him of being responsible, through gain-of-function funding, for millions of deaths, Fauci broke his usual composure entirely, pointed his finger across the dais, and told the senator directly that if anyone was lying in that room, it was Paul. That exchange — combative, unscripted, very much the voice of a man still confident that the facts were on his side and worth defending loudly — is the version of Fauci that existed before this week. The 2026 hearing offered no equivalent moment, not because Fauci had grown less confident in the underlying facts, but because his own lawyers had concluded that the 2021 version of Fauci was now a legal liability he could no longer afford to be. Watching the two hearings side by side is watching a man’s own attorneys strip away the one rhetorical weapon that had, for fifty years, been his most reliable — his willingness to argue back.

Democrats on the committee, for their part, largely declined to use their own time productively, opting instead to deliver speeches praising Fauci’s five decades of service and condemning the Trump administration’s broader record on public health, without asking Fauci a single substantive question he might actually have been able to answer. Only Senator Andy Kim of New Jersey, among the entire panel, used his allotted minutes to name what was actually happening in the room — not an investigation, but a demonstration of what he called the “hyper-partisanship growing in this country in dangerous ways,” a spectacle in which the underlying question of what actually happened during the pandemic’s chaotic early months had become entirely secondary to the performance of accusation and grievance for a television and social-media audience already sorted into its predetermined conclusions. Kim was, in my judgment, correct about nearly everything he said, and the fact that he was the only member of a twenty-some-person committee to say it is itself a fairly damning indictment of how little actual oversight occurred inside a room ostensibly convened to conduct it.

It is worth noting that Kim was not entirely alone in approaching the hearing differently, even if he was alone in naming the dynamic outright. Senator James Lankford of Oklahoma, a Republican, took a markedly different tack than his own party’s leadership on the committee, walking through a series of dispassionate, substantive questions rather than the theatrical provocations favored by Hawley, Scott, and Paul — questions Fauci was, in the end, no more able to answer than any other, given the blanket Fifth Amendment posture his lawyers had settled on, but questions asked, at least, in the spirit of actual inquiry rather than performance. That one senator from each party approached the hearing as something other than a stage is a small mercy in an otherwise fairly bleak three hours, and it is worth remembering, if only because it suggests the theatrical version was a choice most of the committee made, not an inevitability the format itself required.

One PBS panel discussion this week, moderated by Jeffrey Goldberg, put the two halves of this judgment about as cleanly as anyone managed to all week: that Senator Paul was right that Fauci had, at points, taken shortcuts and said things he ought not to have said, and that Fauci was right that Paul’s pursuit of him had become an obsession — both true at once, without either fact canceling the other out. The same panel reached for a specific historical comparison for the hearing itself that this series has approached more obliquely: not a bipartisan commission conducting an honest postmortem on a national tragedy, but something closer to a witch hunt, in the specific American tradition of McCarthyism, aimed at an 85-year-old public servant whose worst provable offense was a level of celebrity-struck vanity that would embarrass most people subjected to the same sudden fame. That comparison is a strong one to reach for, and I do not offer it lightly given how often the word gets misused. But a committee chairman using a Senate hearing to manufacture a viral clip about a witness’s necktie, while that witness’s actual scientific judgment about a genuinely uncertain early pandemic is retroactively recast as a capital offense rather than an honest scientist’s honest uncertainty, is not a wild stretch of the analogy. It is close to a textbook case of it.

None of this excuses Fauci from the parts of his record this series has already treated honestly — the vanity documented in his own diary, the school-closure discrepancy, the decades of accumulated institutional authority that made him genuinely difficult to challenge even when challenge was warranted. But there is a meaningful difference between holding a public official accountable for those specific, documented shortcomings and staging a three-hour spectacle built around a tie color and a carpet, engineered to produce viral video clips rather than actual answers, against an eighty-five-year-old man whose own lawyers had concluded, correctly, that any answer he gave risked a fresh prosecution regardless of its truth. The republic is not made safer by that kind of hearing. It is not made more informed by it either. What it produces, instead, is exactly what Senator Kim warned about: another data point in a long recent pattern of American institutions treating the appearance of accountability as an acceptable substitute for the substance of it, with the actual public health lessons of the pandemic — what worked, what didn’t, what should be different next time — no closer to being settled than they were before the gavel came down.

The hearing, dramatic as it was, was not actually the last word. Paul’s committee still had one further procedural move available to it, and one further constitutional argument still to litigate — whether a man protected by a presidential pardon can be compelled to answer questions about the very conduct that pardon was supposed to have already resolved. That vote, and the genuinely unsettled legal fight underneath it, is where this series turns next.
