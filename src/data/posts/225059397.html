Satire. The senators’ words and the former prosecutor’s arguments are real. The Family, its Consigliere and its bulletin are not.

FAMILY BULLETIN No. 47
CONFIDENTIAL. DO NOT FORWARD. DO NOT PRINT. DO NOT DISCUSS ON SIGNAL, AND FOR THE LOVE OF GOD DO NOT ADD A JOURNALIST TO THE CHAT.

From: The Office of the Consigliere
To: All Capos, Cabinet Secretaries, Associates, Contractors, Donors, and Anyone Who Has at Any Time Stood Within 200 Feet of the Oval Office
Date: Sunday, November 15, 2026
Re: Subpoena Season

1. Situation Report

Associates will by now be aware of the November results. The Office of the Consigliere has reviewed them closely and wishes to confirm that they are real, that no recount is planned, and that the Don’s remarks about “rigged” machines were for public use only and should not be repeated under oath.

From Sunday, January 3, 2027, the other side holds the committee gavels. With the gavels come the subpoenas.

The Office draws attention to the entire public statement of Senator Ron Wyden, issued on X shortly after the results came in, which read in full: “Subpoenas.” The Office has studied this statement for several days. It contains no threat, no adjective and no exclamation mark, and the Office finds it more alarming than anything the Family itself has posted in two years.

Senator Raphael Warnock has meanwhile described the Family’s administration as the most corrupt in American history. The Don has asked the Office to point out that this is a ranking, that he came first, and that he would like it on a plaque. The Office has noted the request.

2. The Other Side Has Published Its Playbook

On November 15, 2026, a former federal prosecutor named Randall D. Eliason wrote in The Atlantic that Congress should investigate the Family the way prosecutors take apart an organised crime family.

The Office objects to the comparison on two grounds. First, it is offensive. Second, it is accurate, which is worse.

Mr. Eliason describes a group held together by loyalty to an authoritarian leader, which prizes secrecy, enforces its demands through intimidation, and divides up responsibilities so that nothing can be traced back to the top. The Office has circulated this paragraph to the Capos as a compliment and been asked by two of them where they can get a framed copy.

The more serious problem is the method. Mr. Eliason advises the new majority not to bother impeaching the Don, who in his view would enjoy the attention and survive the Senate vote anyway. He suggests they leave the Don to his interior decorating and start at the bottom: the lower-level people, the contractors, the people who signed things. They build cases against them and persuade them to talk, then work up.

Associates should understand what this means. They are not coming for the Don. They are coming for you.

3. Frequently Asked Questions

Q: I have received a subpoena. Can I ignore it, the way we did before?

A: Before, the other side was in the minority and could only ask. Asking is a thing the Family has always found easy to decline. Now they can compel you to produce documents and testify under oath, and if you refuse they can go to court to enforce it. Mr. Eliason recommends they ask judges to fast-track these cases. The Office regrets to report that judges can move quickly when they want to.

The Office also warns that one committee staffer has been seen in the Library of Congress reading about “inherent contempt,” an old power under which Congress sends its own Sergeant at Arms to take a defiant witness into custody, without involving the Justice Department at all. It has not been used since the 1930s. The Office notes that the Family has spent two years reviving things that had not been used since the 1930s, and is in no position to complain if the other side tries one.

Q: If they hold me in contempt, who prosecutes me?

A: Under current law, a contempt citation is referred to the Department of Justice. The Department of Justice is run by the Consigliere. The Consigliere is writing this bulletin. Associates may draw their own conclusions.

Please see Section 5, the Fine Print, before drawing them too firmly.

Q: Can I take the Fifth?

A: Yes, until they offer you immunity.

Congress has the power to grant a witness immunity. Once that happens, nothing you say can be used to prosecute you, so you have no grounds to stay silent, and you must answer the question or face contempt. Mr. Eliason calls this an offer you can’t refuse. The Office would like associates to remember who used to make those offers and to reflect on how the situation has changed.

Committees have usually been reluctant to grant immunity in case it damages a later prosecution of the same witness. Mr. Eliason points out that this worry largely disappears if everyone assumes the Don will pardon the witness anyway. The Office has read that sentence many times. It is concerned that the Family’s own generosity has become the other side’s reason to make you talk.

Q: But the Don has promised to pardon everyone who has come within 200 feet of the Oval Office.

A: He has, and the Office stands behind the promise in the spirit in which it was given.

Measurement guidance is attached as Appendix A. In brief:

  • The 200 feet are measured from the Resolute Desk, not from the doorway.

  • The Rose Garden is in. The press briefing room is partly in, and the Office is reviewing which seats.

  • The ballroom is still a construction site, its walls have moved twice, and the Office can make no promises about anyone who stood in it.

  • Anyone who only stood near the Don at Mar-a-Lago, Bedminster or a rally should take note that the promise names an office, not a man.

Q: All right. But once I am pardoned, I am safe, and I don’t have to say anything. Correct?

A: No. This is the part of the bulletin associates should read twice.

A pardon does not excuse a witness from testifying before Congress. Worse, a pardon granted in advance removes the very thing the Fifth Amendment protects, which is a real fear of prosecution. A pardoned witness who says “I decline to answer on the grounds that it may incriminate me” may be asked, reasonably, incriminate you in what? The Don has already forgiven it.

To put it simply: the pardon that protects you from prosecution also takes away your right to stay silent. The Office did not design this system and would like it changed.

Q: Then I will testify and simply not remember anything.

A: The Office has no objection to forgetting in general. It does, however, draw associates’ attention to the difference between not remembering and lying.

A pardon covers crimes already committed. It cannot cover crimes you have not committed yet. If you lie to a committee in March 2027, that is a new federal offence, and it needs a new pardon. To get it, you would be relying on the Don, in March 2027 or later, to remember who you are, to still be pleased with you, and to sign the paperwork before something else catches his attention.

Mr. Eliason asks whether any witness would really want to stake their liberty on the promise of a pardon from an erratic and unfocused president. The Office does not accept that description of the Don. It does suggest that associates ask themselves the question privately.

Q: What does the pardon cover, exactly?

A: Federal crimes. Only federal crimes.

It does not cover state charges, and the Office reminds associates that there are fifty state attorneys general, several of whom are not fond of the Family. It does not cover civil lawsuits. It does not protect your law licence, your board seat, your consulting contracts, or the look your neighbours give you in the supermarket when your testimony has been on the evening news. Mr. Eliason calls this public shame. The Office has no remedy for it and has stopped looking for one.

Q: I am a Cabinet Secretary. Does any of this apply to me?

A: More than to anyone else. A pardon cannot stop Congress from impeaching and removing a Cabinet member or any other presidential appointee. The Office wishes it to be on the record that the new majority does not need sixty-seven votes to make your life very unpleasant. It needs a committee room, a camera and your calendar.

Q: I run a private company that did some work for the Family. I am not political. Why would they call me?

A: Because you are the bottom rung, and the bottom rung is where they are starting.

Mr. Eliason observes that private companies hold a great deal of relevant information and are likely to be more cooperative than the administration. The Office agrees. Your general counsel has a duty to your shareholders, not to the Don. Your auditors have a duty to the numbers. Your compliance department was never invited to any of the meetings and has been waiting years for someone to ask it a question.

The Office respectfully asks that, before you hand anything over, you consider the long and warm friendship between your company and the Family. It also recognises that it cannot actually stop you, and it has stopped pretending otherwise in writing.

Q: Who will be asking the questions?

A: This is the most painful item in the bulletin.

The committees are hiring lawyers and investigators. According to Mr. Eliason, many of them resigned from, or were forced out of, the Consigliere’s own Department of Justice. They know how to build a case from the bottom up because the Department trained them to. The Family then made it clear their services were no longer required. They have now been hired by the other side.

The Office notes that this is how it happens in every family. You push out the people who know where things are kept, and sooner or later they turn up on the other side, with a badge.

Q: Will they get the Don?

A: Criminally, probably not. The Supreme Court’s 2024 ruling on presidential immunity makes prosecuting a former president for official acts very hard. Mr. Eliason also expects the Don to pardon himself on the way out, a move no court has tested. The Office can neither confirm nor deny that the paperwork exists, except to say it is laminated.

The Office does, however, flag one sentence in Mr. Eliason’s essay. He writes that Congress can ensure the Don does not escape the judgment of history.

The Office has looked into this court. It has no judges the Family appointed. It does not take emergency applications. It cannot be pardoned, threatened, sued, or invited to dinner. It sits permanently, its records are public, and it has historically been unkind to men who called themselves the greatest of anything.

The Office recommends that associates not discuss this court with the Don. He has not been told about it, and the Office would like to keep it that way.

4. Approved Responses

Associates called to testify may use the following, in rotation:

  • “I don’t recall.”

  • “I’d have to check my notes.” (Do not have notes.)

  • “That conversation was privileged.” (Check with the Office first which privilege.)

  • “I was not in the room.” (Check with the Office first which room.)

  • “I have great respect for this committee.” (Use no more than once per hearing; it is starting to sound like the other thing.)

Associates are asked not to say:

  • “The Don told me he would take care of it.” This is the one sentence the other side is hoping to hear, and the Office would like it never to be said aloud, in any building, by anybody.

  • “Everybody does it.” The other side has the spreadsheets and has counted, and everybody does not do it to this extent.

  • Anything at all after the committee’s chief counsel smiles at you. In the Office’s experience, that smile means they already have the document.

5. The Fine Print

As set out above, a contempt citation sent to the Department of Justice will go nowhere, for as long as the Department is the Family’s.

The Office is obliged to add that federal contempt, perjury and obstruction charges carry a five-year statute of limitations. A witness who defies a subpoena in, say, February 2027 can be charged until February 2032. There is a presidential election in November 2028. The next president takes office on Saturday, January 20, 2029, leaving, by the Office’s count, three years and one month.

The Office is not predicting the result of the 2028 election. The Office did not predict the result of the 2026 election either, which is partly why this bulletin is necessary.

Associates who want a firmer assurance than this should note that the Office does not issue firmer assurances in writing. In fact, the Office would like to stop issuing anything in writing at all. It has been told that this bulletin, like every other communication from the Office, is covered by the Presidential Records Act. The Office is investigating who keeps putting that Act in its way.

In closing, associates are reminded of the Family’s first rule, which has never changed: we take care of our own.

They are also reminded of the second rule, which the other side has just started teaching in public hearings: the Family’s own takes care of itself first. That is why the bottom rung of the ladder is the first to talk.

Please memorise this bulletin and destroy it. Then, in light of the paragraph above, please do not destroy it, and consult counsel.

The Office of the Consigliere
Washington, D.C.
