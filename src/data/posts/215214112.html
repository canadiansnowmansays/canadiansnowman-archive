In 2012, a pound of raw steak cost about six dollars. By 2019, it had crept up to eight. Today it costs thirteen. Nobody had to look that up to feel it — you feel it every time you stand in front of the meat case doing the small, humiliating arithmetic of what you can still afford to put on the table.

As Ben Casselman and Edison Wu reported for the New York Times on September 10, 2026, that steak is not an anecdote. It is the shape of the entire American economy since 2019. Consumer prices overall have risen more than 30 percent since the start of that year — two and a half times the increase recorded over the equivalent stretch from 2012 to 2019. Beef and veal are up 98 percent since 2012. Bread is up 47 percent. Vehicle insurance has more than doubled. Day care and preschool are up 60 percent. Financial services, dining out, vehicle repair — all climbing at multiples of their pre-pandemic pace.

And yet the official story, the one delivered from podiums and printed on the chyrons, is that inflation is improving. It had fallen from a 2022 peak of 9 percent to 3.4 percent by July 2026 — a comfort that, as Part XI will show, did not survive September. By the technical definition — the rate at which prices are currently rising — the decline was real, and it was good news, and it was also almost entirely beside the point for the person standing at the meat case.

This is the gap the administration keeps failing to close, and the one this series has been documenting since Part I: the distance between the number that lives in a press release and the number that lives in a memory.

This is not a story about a single bad month or a single overpriced cut of meat. It is a story about what happens when a government asks a population to grade its performance on a curve the population never agreed to.

Two Different Arithmetics

Economists measure inflation as a rate — the percentage change from last month, or last year. Households measure inflation as an accumulation — the difference between what something cost when they last really noticed, and what it costs now. Those two measurements almost never agree, and they are not supposed to. But only one of them determines how a voter feels standing in a grocery aisle in October, weeks before an election.

Casselman and Wu note that if inflation truly settles at the traditional target of around 2 percent a year, the sticker shock of the past several years should eventually fade — the way nobody today is outraged that a Coke no longer costs a nickel. But that process takes years, not months, and it requires something the past eighteen months have refused to deliver: an actual return to normal.

Because just as inflation was approaching that target — 2.3 percent in April 2025, genuinely close to healthy — the Trump administration imposed a fresh round of sweeping tariffs on America’s trading partners, pushing the price of imported goods back up. And just as those effects were beginning to fade, the administration went to war with Iran, and energy prices followed the missiles upward. Gasoline hit four dollars a gallon across most of the country this spring and has largely stayed there. Diesel — the fuel that moves the truck that stocks the shelf that holds the steak — recently touched nearly six dollars a gallon, a record.

This is not an accident of timing. This is a pattern this series has named before, in Part III, when it examined the receipt Americans were handed at the end of the trade war: policy choices made in Washington arriving, with a lag of a few months, as a line item on a kitchen table. Tariffs became grocery prices. War became a gas bill. The Fed chairman can stand at Jackson Hole and declare affordability won; the pump does not read the transcript.

What Went Cheap, What Didn’t

Not every category tells the same story, and the honest version of this essay has to admit that. Furniture, which briefly spiked during the pandemic as homebound Americans built standing desks and patio sets, has largely come back down — though tariffs interrupted that decline too, and furniture today still sits well above where the pre-pandemic trend line would have placed it. Apparel is only modestly more expensive than the 2012–2019 trajectory would predict. Appliances, remarkably, are slightly cheaper than that trend line.

The Times’ analysis makes a useful distinction here between goods and services. Goods prices — the physical things Americans buy — were relatively well-behaved before the pandemic and only became genuinely disruptive afterward. Services prices — rent, child care, insurance, tuition, a restaurant meal — were already climbing faster than goods well before anyone had heard the word “coronavirus.” Long before “affordability” became the word of this election cycle, Americans were already uneasy about the cost of an apartment, a semester, a copay.

That distinction matters because it undercuts the administration’s preferred narrative twice over. The goods side of the ledger — the side tariffs directly hit — has genuinely worsened under this presidency in a way that is traceable, datable, and attributable. And the services side — the side tariffs don’t touch — was already broken, which means “the previous administration handed me a mess” and “my own trade policy made a subset of that mess worse” are both true at once, and the White House would very much prefer voters not notice they can hold both thoughts simultaneously.

The farmers market makes the same point with fewer middlemen in the way. As Emily Cochrane reported for The New York Times on October 15, 2026, Ed Vaught Jr. of Hidden Holler Farms now charges $9 for a dozen eggs at Nashville’s Richland Park Farmers Market, double his price six years ago, and he raises prices only when he is about to start losing money. His chicken feed is up 11 percent since 2025, and his weekly bill for egg cartons has more than doubled, from $230 to $500. Two sisters who grow flowers near Murfreesboro now drive to Kentucky for cheaper soil and pay a 7.5 percent tariff on their bulbs, and they have not dared raise their own prices. “Everyone is window shopping,” Tara McIntosh said. Nobody in that market is arguing about the inflation rate. They are arguing with a carton of eggs.

The Roof You Can’t Skip

The steak, at least, can be skipped. The roof cannot. As David W. Chen reported for the New York Times on October 13, 2026, in “The New Affordability Fight in the Midterms: Home Insurance Prices,” homeowners’ premiums have climbed 46 percent since 2021, nearly triple the rate of inflation. That is the same figure as the rise in overall prices since 2012, reached in less than half the time. Insurance also never shows up on a grocery receipt. It arrives folded into the mortgage payment, and nobody notices until the escrow statement comes.

Sarah Lopez-Lewis and Brent Lewis of Wichita found out that way. Two years ago their lender told them the monthly payment was going from $3,000 to $4,000, driven by taxes and insurance. Their twin daughters needed specialist medical care, so they sold the house and moved into a rented townhouse. In Kansas, the annual premium on a $300,000 house now runs $5,289, which is 84 percent above the national average. Claim payouts there nearly doubled in two years, to $879 million. Six of the ten most expensive states to insure a home are not on the hurricane coasts at all. They sit in Tornado Alley and Hail Alley.

An estimated one homeowner in seven has dropped insurance altogether to cover other bills. Gilbert Kirk, 71, of Kansas City, Kansas, was one of them when an August storm brought his house down around him. Weeks later it is still uninhabitable, and buying a policy is on his to-do list if he ever moves back.

Like the services in the chart above, this cost is not a tariff story. It was being driven by fires, floods and hailstorms long before the trade war. But it lands on the same kitchen table as the tariff-driven goods prices, and the voter paying both bills does not sort them by cause.

The voters have noticed. One survey found that 58 percent of Americans say rising insurance costs make them more likely to vote. In Kansas, the Republican Speaker of the House is running for insurance commissioner and promising insurers he will be “their worst nightmare.” When a Kansas Republican campaigns against insurance companies, the household ledger is no longer an abstraction.

The Wage Chase That Ended, Then Started Again

There is a version of this story that is almost sympathetic to the administration, and this series has never been in the business of withholding the sympathetic version. For a stretch of 2023 and 2024, wage growth did outpace inflation. By some measures, by the end of 2024 or into 2025, American workers had actually clawed back the ground they lost during the worst of the 2021–2022 surge. That was real, and it happened on this administration’s watch as much as the previous one’s.

But Casselman and Wu are careful to note the average conceals the wound. Older workers, and workers who weren’t positioned to job-hop their way to a raise, mostly did not catch up — their inflation-adjusted earnings simply fell and stayed fallen. And even the workers who did catch up ended up worse off than they would have been had the pre-pandemic trend simply continued uninterrupted. Catching up to a disaster is not the same as avoiding one.

And now, with tariff-driven goods prices climbing again and energy prices spiking from a war of choice, the wage chase has resumed — this time with workers behind again, not ahead. Part VI of this series, “The Cost of Borrowing to Get By,” documented what households do when wages don’t stretch: they lean on debt. This is the mechanism feeding that leverage. It isn’t that Americans forgot how to budget. It’s that the finish line keeps moving backward.

Running the Numbers Through TACO

This series has used the TACO framework — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — to trace how this administration handles the political liabilities it creates for itself, and the inflation data gives it a textbook run through all four stages.

Threaten came first, in the spring of 2025: sweeping tariffs announced as leverage, framed as a tool that would extract concessions from trading partners without costing Americans a cent. Accommodate followed almost immediately, as the White House began carving out exemptions for politically sensitive categories — semiconductors, certain agricultural inputs — while leaving the tariffs that hit ordinary household goods largely intact. Capitulate arrived in the data itself: by the time Casselman and Wu were compiling this analysis, the administration had quietly stopped promising tariffs would be painless and pivoted to arguing the pain was temporary, necessary, or someone else’s fault. And Obfuscate is where the Jackson Hole “I’ve won affordability” framing this series examined in Part I lives — a rhetorical claim built on the headline rate rather than the accumulated one, offered to an audience the administration is betting won’t check the steak prices against their own memory.

The pattern holds because it has held for every other liability this series has tracked, from tariff authority challenges to the child-care subsidy redirection in Part IX. Threaten big, accommodate the loudest complainers, capitulate quietly once the damage is undeniable, and obfuscate the record with a number that is technically true and functionally irrelevant. Inflation is simply the version of this pattern that shows up on a receipt instead of in a headline, which may be exactly why it is proving harder to talk away than the others.

Why the Rate Won’t Save Him

The dangerous political mistake — and it is one this administration keeps making, essay after essay in this series — is confusing the inflation rate with the inflation experience. A falling rate, when the country has one, is genuine good news for economists, for the Fed, for anyone reading a press release. It is close to meaningless for anyone doing the math against what they remember paying.

Voters do not compare this October to last October. They compare this October to the last time a price genuinely surprised them, however long ago that was — and for staples like steak, bread, and a tank of diesel, that surprise has been arriving on a loop since 2021, with each new one landing before the last one has faded. Every fresh tariff round and every fresh geopolitical shock resets the clock on the very thing that would eventually let voters stop noticing: time. Normalization requires stability, and this administration has not been able to sit still long enough to produce any.

There is also a structural problem baked into the administration’s timing, one this series has flagged before and that the data now confirms with unusual clarity: every time inflation has approached the point where normalization could plausibly begin — April 2025’s brief dip to 2.3 percent being the clearest example — a self-inflicted shock has arrived to interrupt it. The tariffs were a policy choice. The war with Iran was a policy choice. Both were sold, in their moment, as strength. Both showed up eight to twelve weeks later on a gas pump and a grocery receipt. A White House that keeps resetting its own inflation clock cannot simultaneously claim credit for a clock that never stops ticking.

None of this means the headline rate is meaningless, or that the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ 3.4 percent figure is somehow dishonest. It is an accurate description of the rate of change. What it is not — and what the administration keeps implicitly asking voters to pretend it is — is a description of how expensive life has become. Those are two different numbers, answering two different questions, and only one of them is on the ballot in November.

The Household Ledger keeps returning to a single, unglamorous truth: policy announced in Washington does not stay in Washington. It travels, with a predictable lag, into a grocery cart, a gas tank, a rent cheque, a day care invoice, an escrow statement — and it arrives there as something no press release can talk its way out of: a number the household already knows is wrong, because they remember what it used to be. Steak at six dollars a pound is not a data point anymore. It’s a grievance with a receipt attached, and receipts, unlike inflation rates, do not get revised downward.
