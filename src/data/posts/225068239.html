In 1943, the most important mathematical problem in the United States Army was not a secret weapon. It was a table.

Every artillery piece needed a firing table: a booklet telling the gunner how to aim for a given distance, wind, air temperature and shell. Each table required thousands of trajectory calculations, and each trajectory, worked out by a person with a desk calculator, took the better part of a working day. At the Ballistic Research Laboratory at Aberdeen Proving Ground in Maryland, rooms full of human “computers”, many of them young women with mathematics degrees, worked through them by hand. They could not keep up. Without accurate tables, as the National Museum of the United States Army puts it, soldiers could not target effectively and ammunition was wasted.

So the Army paid two engineers at the University of Pennsylvania to try something nobody had built before. This essay is about what that contract, and the government contracts that followed it, bought. You are probably reading it on the answer.

A Grotesque Monster

The contract began in June 1943 with $61,700 of Army Ordnance money and grew, through nine supplements, to $486,804.22, according to the US Army Research Laboratory‘s history of the project. J. Presper Eckert and John Mauchly of Penn’s Moore School of Electrical Engineering used it to build the Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer: ENIAC.

It was enormous. It had about 18,000 vacuum tubes, weighed some 30 tons, filled a room 30 feet by 50, and drew about 150 kilowatts of power, according to Penn Engineering. An Army report on early computers later called it a grotesque monster. It was also more than a thousand times faster than a person with a desk calculator. A trajectory that took a human computer most of a day took ENIAC about 30 seconds.

ENIAC was unveiled to the public on 14 February 1946, too late for the war it was built to fight. It was programmed, at first, by six women drawn from those same rooms of human computers, among them Frances Snyder Holberton, Jean Jennings and Frances Bilas, who worked out how to make it do anything at all by plugging cables and setting switches. For decades they were left out of the photographs’ captions and the history books; their names came back into the story only much later. The Army moved it to Aberdeen in 1947, ran it for more than 70,000 hours of calculations on everything from weather prediction to atomic energy, lent it to universities free of charge, and switched it off for the last time in October 1955.

The Idea Worth More Than the Machine

ENIAC’s most important legacy was not ENIAC. It was a memo.

On 30 June 1945, the mathematician John von Neumann, who had been consulting on the project, wrote a “First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC,” describing the design for ENIAC’s successor. Its central idea was that a computer’s instructions could be stored in its memory alongside its data, so that the machine could be reprogrammed by loading new instructions rather than rewiring it. The report was prepared under the same Army Ordnance contract with the Moore School. It circulated widely, and almost every computer built since, including the one in your pocket, follows its basic design.

There was a dispute over credit, as there always is. Eckert and Mauchly had done much of the engineering, and von Neumann’s name went on the memo. But because the Army treated the report as published, the ideas passed into the public domain. A government contract had produced a design that anyone could use. That is not a footnote. It is how an industry gets started.

Eckert and Mauchly went on to build UNIVAC I, the first successful civilian computer in the United States, according to the Census Bureau, which bought the first one and put it to work in 1951. The first paying customer of the American computer industry was the federal government counting its own people.

Whirlwind and the Cold War

The next leap came from the Navy and the Air Force.

In 1944, the Navy began funding a project at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology called Whirlwind, later carried on by its new Office of Naval Research, at first to build a flight simulator. It became something more important: one of the first computers that could respond in real time, fast enough to track an aircraft as it moved, according to the IEEE’s history milestone for the machine. In April 1951, Whirlwind plotted the interception of a real aircraft. In 1953, it became the first computer to use magnetic core memory, which would dominate computer memory for the next two decades. Whirlwind itself used about 5,000 vacuum tubes and cost more than $3 million in 1950 dollars, a sum no company of the day would have spent on a machine with no customers.

The Air Force then turned Whirlwind into a continental air-defence system called SAGE. Its IBM computers each used 49,000 vacuum tubes and weighed 250 tons; the first centre went live in 1958, and by 1963 there were 23 of them watching the skies for Soviet bombers. SAGE never had to stop an attack. What it did do was train a generation of programmers, prove that very large software systems could work, and give IBM the experience that made it the dominant computer company of the next quarter-century.

The Light Nobody Asked For

Not everything the military paid for was a computer. In 1954, Charles Townes and his students at Columbia University got the first maser working: a device that amplified microwaves by stimulating molecules of ammonia, the direct ancestor of the laser. Columbia credits the Joint Services Electronics Program, the armed forces’ fund for university electronics research, for supporting the work, according to the Columbia Nano Initiative, and the Air Force’s research office counts Townes among its early grantees. Townes shared the 1964 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Honesty requires one caveat. The first working laser, built in 1960 by Theodore Maiman at Hughes Research Laboratories, was paid for with company money, not a government contract. The federal claim is on the science that made it possible, not the device itself. That distinction matters, because it is exactly the point of basic research: government pays for the understanding, and industry builds the products.

The Chip Nobody Else Would Buy

By the late 1950s, engineers had a new idea: put an entire circuit on a single piece of silicon. Jack Kilby of Texas Instruments had a working integrated circuit by September 1958, and Robert Noyce of Fairchild Semiconductor soon produced a more practical version. There was one problem. The chips were expensive, unproven and, for most purposes, unnecessary. Almost nobody wanted to buy them.

The federal government did. In 1962, the Air Force announced that integrated circuits would guide the Minuteman II missile, and MIT began designing the Apollo Guidance Computer for NASA. The Apollo computer used about 200,000 Fairchild chips at $20 to $30 apiece, and Apollo was the largest single user of integrated circuits until 1965, when Minuteman overtook it, the Computer History Museum records.

The economist Richard Nelson later tallied the effect in his study Government and Technical Progress. In 1962, the federal government bought all of the integrated circuits sold in the United States. In 1965 it still bought nearly three-quarters. By 1968, as prices fell and companies found commercial uses, its share had dropped to just over a third. Government demand had carried a new technology through its expensive infancy until the market could take over. Silicon Valley’s founding customer was the Pentagon, with NASA a close second.

The Mother of All Demos

On 9 December 1968, a soft-spoken engineer named Douglas Engelbart sat on a stage in San Francisco’s Civic Auditorium in front of a hall full of computer scientists and, for about an hour and a half, showed them the future.

Using a terminal linked by microwave and telephone line to a computer about 30 miles away at the Stanford Research Institute in Menlo Park, and projecting his screen 22 feet high, Engelbart demonstrated a pointing device his team called a mouse, clickable links between documents, windows on a screen, live video conferencing and two people editing the same document at the same time. The research behind it was paid for by the government’s Advanced Research Projects Agency and the Air Force, and NASA lent the projector, according to DARPA. People who were there called it the mother of all demos.

The mouse itself had been built in 1964 by Engelbart’s engineer Bill English, out of a block of redwood. In 1971, English joined Xerox’s new research centre in Palo Alto, where he built a ball mouse for an experimental computer called the Alto. In December 1979, Steve Jobs and a group of Apple engineers visited Xerox and saw the Alto, the Computer History Museum recounts. In January 1984, Apple launched the Macintosh, with a mouse.

Every time you click on something, you are using a device the Pentagon paid for.

Aberdeen, Then and Now

The Ballistic Research Laboratory’s problem in 1943 was a shortage of people who could calculate fast enough. Eighty-three years later, Army research has a shortage of people again.

The Pentagon’s own review of its science and technology laboratories, ordered by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth in January 2026, found that their workforce had shrunk by about 14 per cent in a single year, roughly 19,700 people out of 140,900, DefenseScoop reported. The review blamed the hiring freeze, the funding lapse and the deferred-resignation buyouts, and found it was taking an average of 101 days to hire a replacement. The labs had also lost 108 scholarship students they had paid to train, an investment of $20.4 million walking out of the door. The Army’s own Aviation and Missile Center in Alabama shrank by nearly a quarter in 2025, from 3,338 civilians to 2,550, according to the Army’s year-end account, and is consolidating from six sites to four.

The Army has been reorganising at the same time. Hegseth’s Army Transformation Initiative merged the Army Futures Command, which oversaw much of its research, with its training command in October 2025. And in May 2025, Hegseth ordered a 15 per cent cap on the overhead the Pentagon pays universities for research, saying it would redirect up to $900 million a year, until a federal judge blocked it and later threw it out.

ENIAC was born because the Army paid a university to attempt something risky, with no guarantee it would work, in a hurry. Those are exactly the kinds of contracts this administration has been cancelling, and those university overheads are exactly what it tried to cut.

What the Customers Bought

Look back over this lineage and a pattern appears. At every step, the first customer was the government, and the government was buying something it needed for a mission: firing tables, air defence, a missile guidance system, a way to reach the Moon. It paid for things that did not exist yet, accepted that many would fail, and did not demand that the results be kept proprietary. The commercial industry that followed was built on what was left over.

The computer, the chip and the mouse were not inevitable. They were bought, by a government with a long horizon and a habit of funding risky ideas at universities and in its own laboratories.
