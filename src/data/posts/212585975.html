There is a particular authority that attaches to a man conceding a war he would once have argued for.

Robert Kagan spent the late nineties and early two-thousands as one of the intellectual architects of American armed primacy — a founding neoconservative, an early and vocal advocate for invading Iraq, a writer who built a career on the proposition that American power, applied confidently, produces order. In the Atlantic on August 23, he published an essay arguing that the United States has been defeated by the Islamic Republic of Iran, that the dominant power in the Middle East going forward will be Iran rather than America or Israel, and that Iran achieved this with American assistance.

For years, he writes, the world dealt with an Iran constrained. It is about to learn what an Iran unleashed looks like.

This diary has been tracking the same war weekly since February, from the outside, with no access and no sources. It is a useful moment to ask how much of Kagan’s verdict the record supports.

Most of it. Not all.

I. The Turning Point Was March

Kagan locates the decisive moment months earlier than this series did, and on reflection he is right.

When Israel struck Iran’s South Pars gas field in mid-March, Iran answered by hitting Ras Laffan in Qatar — the largest liquefied natural gas complex on earth. The message was immediately legible to everyone who needed to read it. Iran could not defeat the United States in an air war. Iran could inflict enough damage on Gulf energy infrastructure to tip the world into a prolonged economic crisis.

Trump halted attacks on Iranian energy targets, ordered a reluctant Israel to do the same, and wound the campaign down without extracting a single concession. He has not ordered a strike on Iranian energy infrastructure since.

Everything this diary has documented in the twenty-two weeks since — the collapsed memorandum, the pauses, the cancelled attacks, the threats against Oman, the strategy of nothing — follows from that fortnight. By the third or fourth unfulfilled threat, Kagan writes, Tehran had reason to conclude that Washington would not follow it up the escalation ladder. The subsequent record is difficult to read any other way.

II. Reading the Memorandum Again

The June memorandum has been treated in this series, and almost everywhere else, as a hastily drafted document whose vague language caused a dispute. Kagan reads it as something else: a surrender that was phrased to spare American feelings.

The text declares that Iran, and no other nation, will make arrangements for safe passage of commercial vessels. It provides that Iran will determine the strait’s administration in dialogue with Oman — without intermediaries or other participants. It does not restore the prewar status quo. It gives the United States no say in the waterway’s future administration. And it permits Iran to charge for passage after sixty days.

Put that way, the argument that has consumed the past two months — is a service fee a toll? — looks like a quarrel over the invoice on a house that has already changed hands.

Kagan adds a detail I had not seen elsewhere, and it lands hard. Mojtaba Khamenei reportedly had to be persuaded by President Pezeshkian to sign the thing. Iran’s supreme leader had to be talked into accepting terms Washington regarded as a concession.

He also offers the clearest available account of why the ceasefire collapsed, and it is uncomfortable. When the U.S. Navy began shepherding tankers up the Omani side of the strait, Tehran read it as an attempt to recover what had been signed away. The convoy operation that American officials describe as one of the war’s surprising successes was, from Iran’s chair, a treaty violation.

III. What Iran Is Actually Building

The most valuable passage in Kagan’s essay is not about power. It is about paperwork.

Iran has created the Persian Gulf Strait Authority and instructed shipping companies to register with it, obtain permits, follow Iranian-designated routes, and purchase Iranian-provided insurance and security. It is not merely claiming a right to collect money. It is claiming the right to decide who may pass — proposing to exclude vessels associated with governments it deems hostile, and demanding compensation from countries that participated in attacks against it.

India, Pakistan, Iraq, Turkey and China have already sought terms.

That is the part that should alarm anyone who cares about how the world’s waterways work. Passage through an international strait becomes a privilege that Tehran can grant, condition, delay or deny. And as Kagan observes, this is a more useful instrument than a nuclear weapon: a bomb deters an attack, while the strait yields leverage every day, over dozens of countries, without a shot being fired.

Nor was any of this improvised. In April, Mojtaba declared that Iran would implement new legal frameworks and management of the strait, and that foreigners arriving from thousands of kilometres away had no place in the Gulf except at the bottom of its waters. On the anniversary of Iran’s expulsion of the Portuguese from the strait in 1622, he said the bright future of the Persian Gulf would be a future without America.

The recurring complaint from Washington and much of the press — that the Iranians keep shifting their position and cannot make up their minds — does not survive contact with those statements. Iran has said the same thing since April. It is Washington that has changed its position weekly, as this diary has recorded in tiresome detail.

IV. Where the Record Complicates Him

Three places, and they matter.

The pipelines. Kagan dismisses the bypass routes on the grounds that new pipelines, ports and terminals will be as vulnerable to Iranian missiles as the old ones, and asks whether Iran will simply stand by while its neighbours build an escape from its control. Two days before his essay appeared, Iran's new security chief answered. As Erika Solomon reported for the New York Times on August 23, Mohsen Rezaei said Tehran would target those routes as well, and prevent even a single drop of oil from leaving the region.

So Kagan is right about the vulnerability. But he treats the bypasses as therefore futile, which does not follow. The Gulf states are building them anyway, because not building hands Iran a permanent veto, and because a route that must be defended is still better than no route at all. The alternative to expensive, exposed infrastructure is not safety. It is submission.

The economy. Kagan concedes the regime is corrupt, repressive, economically incompetent and widely hated, then argues it had all those weaknesses before the war. True. But some of what it has now is new. Inflation at ninety percent, a level with no precedent in Iran’s modern history. Energy production down by fifteen to thirty percent. A rial that has fallen a further forty percent. A central bank governor describing oil exports as close to zero. A government afraid to touch petrol subsidies because it knows what happened the last time. A regime can be victorious abroad and hollow at home, and Iran’s leadership is currently asking its citizens to treat their kitchens as missile bases.

The unanimity. This is the objection I would press hardest. Kagan’s Iran is a single actor with a settled strategy. The Iran in the reporting is two governments in one building. On the same weekend, Rezaei promised to choke off every drop of oil in the Gulf and President Pezeshkian described a full-scale economic, military and security war while calling for negotiations from a position of strength. Ghalibaf, the lead negotiator, has defended the June agreement against hardline attack. The security council was reorganised this month precisely because that argument has not been settled.

Kagan takes a swipe at Times headlines asking who will blink first, noting that Iran has never blinked while Washington has done nothing but. The line is satisfying and it is also too easy. A government does not overhaul its entire national security leadership in a fortnight if the question of what to do next has been resolved.

V. Operation Economic Outcast

Kagan’s essay appeared on a Sunday. On Monday afternoon the Treasury Secretary walked into a briefing room and provided the best available evidence for it.

As Alan Rappeport reported for the New York Times on August 24, Scott Bessent announced the creation of Operation Economic Outcast, intended to isolate Iran’s economy completely from the rest of the world. Treasury would tighten the noose and enforce what he called a zero-leakage approach. Those who tether themselves to the Iranian regime, he said, should expect to share in the isolation of a withering regime.

Note first the name. The Pentagon calls this war Operation Epic Fury. The Treasury Department has now given a sanctions package a military operation codename of its own, which is either an admission that the economic campaign has replaced the military one or an attempt to make a press conference feel like a war room. The president had promised an economic D-Day and the most crushing economic operation ever undertaken against any country.

What was actually announced: sanctions on more than sixty entities, individuals and vessels involved in procuring nuclear and missile technology; further measures touching digital assets, gold, aviation and shipping; waves of additional sanctions in the coming weeks; and one major financial institution to be named by the end of the week.

That is a substantial package by ordinary standards. It is not a D-Day. Its centrepiece is a single unnamed bank arriving in four days, with everything else scheduled for later.

Then came the question that decides whether any of it works. Asked about sanctioning Chinese banks — which are known to process sales of Iranian oil — Bessent said that no one is above the reach of American sanctions, did not mention China, and offered that the best way to engage with countries is through quiet diplomacy.

Quiet diplomacy is the opposite of zero leakage. China buys most of Iran’s oil; China is the leak. An isolation campaign that declines to name its largest evader has disclosed its own limit at the launch event.

Set that beside Section III. Iran has built a registry, issues permits, sells insurance, and has five governments seeking terms. Washington’s answer is to tell world leaders they must decide between America and Iran — while, as Bessent put it, declining to name names about which leaders the president has called. Two competing systems of compliance, and only one of them is telling anyone who has signed up.

The response from Tehran arrived before the announcement did. Minutes before Bessent began speaking, Ghalibaf posted that the United States is not in an economic position to further restrict its relations with other countries, dismissed the effort as bombast, and claimed that Iran’s trading partners had sent messages making clear they do not take such statements into account.

That last detail cuts against my own objection above, and I will not pretend otherwise. Ghalibaf is the negotiator, the man who defended the June agreement against hardline attack — and on this he sounds indistinguishable from Rezaei. The division inside the Iranian government is real, but it is narrower than a single weekend’s quotations suggest. On the question of whether America can still impose costs, Tehran’s moderates and its hardliners appear to have reached agreement.

One caution before anyone concludes that Economic Outcast is theatre. It may hurt a great deal. Analysts are divided on whether the Emirati embargo compounding with the naval blockade amounts to the most consequential economic action of the war or to a rupture Iran’s self-sufficiency model can absorb. But the historical frame is Kagan’s own: economic pressure has been applied to Iran for forty-seven years, and produced exactly one agreement, which Washington subsequently tore up. Presenting the fifth iteration of a failed instrument as a landing on a beach tells you more about the presenter than about the instrument.

VI. What He Gets Right That Nobody Wants to Say

The regional consequences, though, seem to me exactly as he describes them, and this diary’s own record supports him.

The Mecca pact — Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey, mutual defence language, a secretariat, Egypt expected to follow — is not a replacement Abraham Accords. It is the inverse. The Accords consolidated Israeli power and contained Iran. This alignment is less hostile to Iran, less friendly to Israel, and more independent of Washington. Kagan notes what its members have in common that nobody says aloud: Pakistan has nuclear weapons, and the Turks and Saudis want them.

And the effects reach far past the Gulf. A relatively brief war against a second-rank power has consumed much of America’s stock of missile-defence interceptors. The United States began a war it could not finish, exposed its partners to retaliation, and stopped when the costs became too great. Whatever Beijing concludes about Taiwan and Moscow about Europe, America’s allies are already asking whether Washington has the weapons, the stamina and the political will for another conflict.

They are asking it, one might add, in the same month that the president threatened to bomb Oman, cut military support to South Korea, and imposed fifty percent tariffs on Canadian cars and steel.

VII. The Author Is the Argument

I want to end on provenance, because it is doing more work in this essay than anything Kagan actually writes.

Plenty of people have argued that this war was lost. Most of them opposed it from the beginning, which allows their conclusions to be dismissed as ideology confirming itself. Kagan cannot be dismissed that way. He spent twenty-five years making the case that American power decisively applied is what holds the world together. He argued for Iraq. He is, in the precise sense, one of the people whose ideas produced the habit of mind that started this war in February.

And he has now written that the United States lost, that Iran will dominate the region, that the failed war gave Tehran something no nuclear weapon could have, and that America’s allies are drawing the obvious conclusions.

When the architects begin filing damage assessments, the argument about whether the building fell down is over. What remains is the argument about what it cost, and who pays.

VIII. By the Numbers

  • 1622 — the year Iran expelled the Portuguese from the strait, and the anniversary its supreme leader chose to promise a Persian Gulf without America.

  • 5 countries reported to have already sought terms with the Persian Gulf Strait Authority: India, Pakistan, Iraq, Turkey and China.

  • 60 days after which the June memorandum permitted Iran to charge for passage.

  • 90% Iranian inflation; 15–30% decline in energy production; 40% further fall in the rial.

  • 3 signatories to the Mecca pact; 1 of them nuclear-armed, 2 of them aspiring.

  • ~12 ships a day through the strait, against roughly 130 before the war.

  • 18 American service members killed; 653 wounded; at least 17 commercial sailors killed, three of them by American fire.

  • 0 of the war’s three original objectives achieved.

  • Narges Mohammadi remains in Evin Prison, in a country that a founding neoconservative now describes as the dominant power in the Middle East.

IX.

There is a version of this essay that simply endorses Kagan and stops. I have resisted it, because a diary that has spent six months insisting on precision should not abandon the habit the moment a prominent writer says something it agrees with.

Iran has not won everything. Its economy is in a condition its own central bank describes in language usually reserved for collapse. Its leadership is arguing with itself in public. Its neighbours are building around it at enormous expense, and will keep building even under threat, because the alternative is permanent dependence. Its supreme leader has not been seen in public in six months.

But Kagan is right about the thing that matters. The war was fought to prevent Iran from acquiring a weapon that would give it regional power, and it ended by handing Iran a form of regional power that no weapon could have provided. That is not a setback or a stalemate. It is the precise inversion of the objective, achieved by the party that set it.

Everything else is the sound of a government that has lost an argument continuing to talk. Epic Fury did not open the strait. Economic Outcast will not open it either. But a war can be given a name after it is lost, and so can the campaign that follows it, and the names will look tidy in the archives long after anyone remembers that the waterway used to belong to nobody and worked.
