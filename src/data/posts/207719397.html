By May 1940, Britain’s actual financial position was considerably more precarious than the term “special relationship” usually allows anyone to remember. The Neutrality Act, passed by Congress in November 1939, required belligerent nations to pay cash and arrange their own shipping for any American war matériel — the “cash and carry” provision that reflected America’s determination to avoid the entangling credit arrangements that had left European war debts from 1917 to 1919 unresolved and politically toxic for two decades afterward. Britain had cash and gold reserves in May 1940. It did not have enough of either to fund a war against Germany for very long, and everyone with access to the Treasury’s actual books, on both sides of the Atlantic, knew it.

What almost nobody remembers, because it survives mainly in the diary of a Canadian prime minister rather than in any British or American account of the period, is how close the crisis came to resolving itself in a way that would have made this entire series, and the special relationship it is examining, unnecessary. On May 24, 1940 — four days before Belgium surrendered, and the same week the British Expeditionary Force was being pushed back toward the beaches at Dunkirk — Secretary of State Cordell Hull telephoned Canadian Prime Minister Mackenzie King in Ottawa, expressing alarm at the gravity of the unfolding collapse in France and asking that Canada send someone to Washington to discuss it directly with Hull and, unmistakably, with Roosevelt. King sent Hugh Keenleyside, a career officer in Canada’s Department of External Affairs who had joined the department in 1928, alongside Lester Pearson, and who happened to be the man in Ottawa best positioned, by background and seniority, to carry a message of this weight on short notice.

Keenleyside reported back to King on May 26, and what he brought back was not reassurance. American officials, he told King, had concluded privately that France could not hold and that Britain, facing a German air force it could not match, might not either — and that in the event of collapse, Hitler could offer London a negotiated settlement whose price would be the surrender of the entire British Empire and, critically, the Royal Navy itself. A German fleet reinforced by Britain’s own ships, combined with whatever remained of the French navy, would leave the United States facing a hostile Atlantic naval power larger than anything the American fleet alone could counter. King recorded his own horror at this scenario in his diary the same week. This is worth sitting with for a moment, because it means that eighteen months before Pearl Harbor and Lend-Lease and Churchill’s own most famous wartime rhetoric, the working assumption inside the highest levels of the American government — relayed not through any British or American channel but through a Canadian civil servant sent on short notice from Ottawa — was that Britain’s survival was not a foregone conclusion, and that its fleet, the one asset that actually mattered to Washington’s own strategic calculations, might not remain British for very much longer.

I raise Keenleyside’s mission here for a reason that goes beyond family pride, though I will not pretend the family pride isn’t present. Hugh Keenleyside was my great-uncle, and the fact that he happened to be the man Mackenzie King sent to Washington during the single most dangerous fortnight of the entire war is, admittedly, a coincidence I am not going to pretend I discovered with total scholarly detachment. But the mission matters to this series regardless of who happened to carry it, because it is the clearest available illustration of something Scaramucci’s essay, and nearly every other account of the special relationship, structurally cannot show: that the relationship was never actually bilateral, however consistently it gets described that way. In the exact week the special relationship’s mythology usually locates as its founding crisis — Britain alone, Churchill newly installed as Prime Minister on May 10, the whole Allied cause apparently on the verge of collapse — the actual channel through which Washington’s fears and Britain’s peril passed ran through Ottawa, carried by a career Canadian diplomat, and recorded in a Canadian prime minister’s diary rather than in Churchill’s own copious memoirs of the period.

Keenleyside’s mission was not a one-off. Three months later, in August 1940, Roosevelt invited King to Ogdensburg, New York, to negotiate a formal defense arrangement between Canada and the United States — a recognition, on the American side, that Britain’s own capacity to guarantee Canadian security had become sufficiently uncertain that Washington needed its own direct defense understanding with Ottawa, independent of London. The result was the Permanent Joint Board on Defence, established that same month, and Keenleyside served as its Canadian secretary from 1940, becoming chairman of the Canadian section in 1944. The Board’s existence is, in its own quiet way, a rebuke to any account of 1940 that treats the special relationship as a closed conversation between two capitals: Washington’s actual crisis response included establishing a standing defense institution with Canada, running in parallel to whatever was being separately negotiated with Britain, precisely because Britain’s own ability to guarantee anything in North America could no longer be assumed. Canada was not a spectator to the special relationship’s founding moment. It was, at exactly this juncture, one of the load-bearing walls holding up the arrangement Scaramucci’s essay treats as a purely Anglo-American structure.

Britain’s own negotiation with Washington proceeded on a separate but overlapping track that same summer, and it is worth noting that the fear Keenleyside had carried back to Ottawa in May — that Britain’s fleet might end up in German hands — was still very much alive in Washington’s mind when the destroyer negotiations began. American negotiators made clear to their British counterparts that any transfer of destroyers would depend on an explicit assurance regarding the fleet’s disposition in the event of a British collapse, and Churchill, in his formal reply to Roosevelt that summer, committed that the Royal Navy would never be surrendered or sunk voluntarily — a promise that fell short of the ironclad guarantee Washington might have preferred, but one American officials judged sufficient to proceed. It is a direct, documented line from the anxiety Keenleyside reported to King in May to the specific assurance Churchill had to give in writing two months later, and it is a line that essentially never appears in the standard telling of the destroyer deal, which tends to present the fifty ships as a straightforward act of Anglo-American solidarity rather than as the second half of a transaction whose first half was a Canadian diplomat’s alarming report about what Washington actually believed was coming.

On September 2, 1940, Britain and the United States concluded the Destroyers for Bases Agreement, under which fifty aging American destroyers were transferred to the Royal Navy in exchange for ninety-nine-year leases on British military bases across Newfoundland, Bermuda, and the Caribbean — a genuinely consequential naval transfer at a moment when British destroyer losses to U-boats had become alarming, but one still constrained by the same cash-and-carry logic that made a larger, sustained American commitment impossible under existing law. Canada’s own parallel arrangement with Washington continued to deepen alongside it: in April 1941, Roosevelt and King concluded the Hyde Park Declaration, a further financial understanding that allowed Canada to supply war matériel to Britain while easing the strain on Canada’s own dollar reserves — a Canadian-American financial arrangement, in other words, explicitly designed to help keep the Anglo-American arrangement solvent, negotiated almost a full year before America itself entered the war. Churchill needed something considerably larger than fifty destroyers and a Canadian financing bridge, and by December 1940 Britain’s dollar reserves had fallen far enough that the Treasury’s own officials warned the country would be unable to pay for war orders already placed within a matter of months.

It is worth remembering, too, that Roosevelt was not operating from a position of free political choice when Churchill’s letter arrived. He had just won an unprecedented third term in the election of November 1940, campaigning on an explicit public promise that American boys would not be sent to fight in foreign wars, against a backdrop of substantial isolationist sentiment organized around groups like the America First Committee, which counted prominent public figures among its supporters and drew large, genuinely popular crowds at rallies through 1940 and into 1941. Any mechanism Roosevelt proposed for aiding Britain had to be defensible as something other than a step toward American entry into the war, which is precisely why the “lease or loan” framing mattered as more than a financial technicality: it let Roosevelt describe the arrangement to Congress and the public as the material aid of an arsenal supplying a fellow democracy, rather than as a financial or military commitment that implicated America directly in the war’s outcome. Churchill’s own gamble, in writing as bluntly as he did, was a bet that Roosevelt’s political room to maneuver was wider than the public rhetoric suggested. It was a correct bet, but it was a bet, made by a prime minister whose own dollar reserves gave him very little room to make a different one.

This is the context for the document Scaramucci’s essay calls “the greatest pitch letter in history” — the roughly four-thousand-word letter Churchill dispatched to Roosevelt, delivered to the president while he was cruising the Caribbean aboard the USS Tuscaloosa in early December 1940, laying out Britain’s position with a bluntness that reads, even eight decades later, as a document written by a man who understood precisely how much was riding on Roosevelt’s reaction to it. Churchill itemized Britain’s shipping losses, its need for merchant tonnage and escort vessels, and its dwindling ability to pay in dollars for any of it, and he explicitly asked Roosevelt to find a mechanism that did not require Britain to liquidate the last of its readily convertible overseas assets simply to keep the war effort funded through the following year. Roosevelt’s response, unveiled first in a December 29, 1940 fireside chat describing America as “the arsenal of democracy,” and then in a January 6, 1941 address to Congress, proposed exactly the mechanism Churchill needed: American war matériel supplied to Britain not through direct cash sale but through lease or loan, repayable in kind or in some other form after the war rather than in dollars up front. Congress passed the Lend-Lease Act on March 11, 1941, and in his own radio broadcast a month earlier, on February 9, Churchill had already previewed his end of the bargain: “Give us the tools, and we will finish the job.”

The numbers that followed are the ones Scaramucci’s essay cites, and they are accurate as far as they go: something on the order of thirty-one billion dollars in Lend-Lease aid flowed to Britain over the following four years, and the final installment of Britain’s associated war-debt repayment to the United States — a separate, smaller postwar loan rather than Lend-Lease itself, but part of the same broader financial reckoning between the two countries — was not paid off until 2006, sixty-one years after the war’s end. What the numbers alone cannot show, and what this part of the series exists to restore, is how contingent the entire arrangement actually was on a sequence of events that had nothing to do with any grand design on either side: a Canadian civil servant sent to Washington on four days’ notice in the worst week of the war, a Canadian defense board established because Britain’s own guarantees could no longer be trusted, and a letter from a prime minister whose country’s dollar reserves genuinely might have run out before Roosevelt or Congress found the political will to act. The “term sheet,” in other words, was not drafted in a boardroom by two confident principals negotiating from strength. It was drafted under conditions closer to a company genuinely facing insolvency within months, negotiating with its only realistic creditor, with a third party’s diplomat carrying the news of exactly how bad things had gotten before either principal had fully admitted it to the other.

There is a quieter thread running underneath all of this that I want to name before this part closes, because it will not get another chance to surface as directly as it can here. My own family’s connection to this story runs through Ottawa rather than through Blenheim or Wall Street, and it is a connection to the institutional machinery of the alliance rather than to the more romantic figures usually placed at its center. Keenleyside spent the rest of his career, well past 1940, working inside exactly the kind of unglamorous diplomatic infrastructure — joint boards, technical assistance administrations, ambassadorial postings — that keeps an alliance functioning in the decades between its dramatic founding crises. Churchill wrote the letters that get quoted eighty years later. Keenleyside, and people like him, kept the actual channels open that let those letters land somewhere useful. Scaramucci’s essay is not wrong that the special relationship compounded real returns across eight decades. It is simply telling the story from the vantage point of the two capitals that get to appear in the history books, rather than the third one that did a considerable amount of the load-bearing work in the exact week the relationship was being tested hardest.

Part VIII turns to Fulton, Missouri, and to the phrase Churchill coined there in 1946 — a phrase that, notably, still had no room in it for the country that had helped keep the alliance standing through its worst week.
