Postmark: Beijing. Studio One, CCTV. New Year’s Eve, Year of the Horse. One billion viewers. Sixteen machines, staggering on purpose.

On the eve of the Year of the Horse, in February of this year, more than a billion people sat down to watch television together — the annual Spring Festival Gala on CCTV, an event that makes the Super Bowl look like community-access programming — and were treated to the spectacle of humanoid robots performing Drunken Fist kung fu.

Let me repeat that, because it deserves repeating. Drunken Fist. The martial arts style built entirely on the appearance of losing one’s balance — the stagger, the wobble, the backward tilt arrested at the last possible instant. It is among the most difficult styles for a human to master precisely because it requires perfect control disguised as the total absence of control. And a troupe of Unitree H1 robots performed it on live television, alongside human children, while other robots vaulted tables, wielded nunchaku, and as Global Times reported, executed aerial flips three metres high and a grand spin of seven and a half rotations, repositioning themselves across the stage at four metres per second.

The year before — the 2025 Gala, Year of the Snake — sixteen of those same H1 robots had performed the Yangge, a folk dance from rural northern China, in floral red-and-white vests, twirling handkerchiefs. The handkerchief spin is the signature move of the Yangge, and it takes human dancers years to perfect. The robots managed it, wobbling slightly, in a performance directed by Zhang Yimou — yes, that Zhang Yimou, the man who staged the Beijing Olympics opening ceremony — and the world was charmed and mildly unsettled in roughly equal measure.

One year between those two performances. From careful triple-steps and synchronized arm-waving — what one European reviewer accurately called a programmed ballet — to autonomous machines correcting their own balance mid-flip using lidar and real-time force feedback. As CNBC noted, the robots that stumbled through a Beijing marathon in the spring of 2025, providing the internet with a season of comic material, returned within a year to make the comedy look like a rope-a-dope.

This postcard is about what happened on that stage. Not the engineering — though the engineering is remarkable — but the older and stranger question the engineering forces on us: what is the difference between moving and dancing? And why, of all the things a country determined to dominate the robotics century could do with its machines, did it choose to make them dance?

The dancer in the audience

In June, as The Atlantic reported, the magazine published a personal essay by a dancer named Valerie Trapp under the title “When Robots Come for Dance,” and it is the necessary companion piece to the Gala footage. Trapp watched the Unitree performances the way a portrait painter might have watched the first daguerreotypes: with professional attention, genuine admiration, and a cold spot forming somewhere in the chest.

Her essay introduced most readers to a field with the wonderful name of choreobotics — the study of robot movement through the lens of dance. Its researchers ask questions that sound whimsical until you realize they are foundational. Roboticist Amy LaViers and her collaborators have worked on cataloguing the basic units of movement — “movemes,” on the model of phonemes, the idea being that all human motion might decompose into a periodic table of elemental gestures. If you can catalogue the elements, you can recombine them. If you can recombine them, a machine can perform them.

And yet. The most arresting passage in Trapp’s essay is not about what robots can do but about what a human arm does without being asked. A dancer’s arm moving through a phrase carries — her word — a certain yumminess: the accumulated weight of every reach that arm has ever made, toward a mother, a lover, a subway pole, a falling glass. The philosopher’s argument that Trapp takes seriously is that dance is not the execution of movement but the expression of embodied experience — that a dance is everything a body has ever felt, played back through the body. A machine can replicate the trajectory of the arm to the millimetre. It has no reaches to remember.

I find I believe this argument and doubt it at the same time, which is usually the sign of an argument worth keeping. Because here is the uncomfortable counter-question: how would we know? When the Drunken Fist robot staggers and catches itself, the audience gasps in exactly the way it gasps for the human master. The gasp does not check credentials. If the performance produces the feeling, the philosopher’s distinction between moving and dancing begins to look like the distinction between a natural pearl and a cultured one — real, defensible, and invisible to nearly everyone at the jeweller’s counter.

We might call this the terpsichorean Turing test, and the honest answer is that the machines have not passed it — Trapp’s cold spot is itself the evidence; she could tell — but they are closer than any dancer expected them to be in her lifetime.

An old duet

It is worth saying that the machines have been auditioning for a very long time. In 1738, Jacques de Vaucanson exhibited a mechanical flute player in Paris — a life-sized automaton with mechanical lungs and articulated fingers that actually played the instrument, actually pushed air through it — and Parisian society paid handsomely to stand in a room and feel exactly what Trapp felt: admiration with a cold spot in it. Voltaire called Vaucanson a rival to Prometheus, which was either a compliment or a warning, and the eighteenth century, like ours, declined to decide which.

The twentieth century kept the duet going. The Bauhaus staged its mechanical ballets; Fritz Lang built his robot Maria and had her dance, because he understood that the dance, not the metal, was what would disturb the audience. And in our own era the modern tradition arguably begins with a video: Boston Dynamics’ robots doing the twist and the mashed potato to a sixties Motown number — released, with a timing nobody at the company can have fully intended, in the last days of the plague year 2020, when humanity was locked in its houses and the machines danced in an empty lab. That video has been viewed tens of millions of times, as IEEE Spectrum reported, and it split its audience down the middle between delight and dread in a way the company’s parkour videos never quite managed. Machines jumping is athletics. Machines dancing is a claim.

The Gala performances are the direct descendants of that claim, scaled from a lab demo to a national broadcast. The question hanging over Vaucanson’s flute player — is this a musician or a music box? — has simply been promoted, after three centuries of faithful service, to prime time.

Why they dance

Now to the harder-headed question. Unitree’s engineers did not teach the H1 to perform the Yangge because the company harbours a passion for the folk traditions of rural Shaanxi. The dancing serves purposes, and the purposes are worth stating plainly.

The first is technical honesty by other means. Dance is a brutally efficient demonstration of the hardest problems in robotics: dynamic balance, whole-body coordination, multi-agent synchronization, recovery from perturbation. A robot that can perform a seven-and-a-half-rotation spin and land it has solved problems that matter enormously on a factory floor, where the choreography is duller but the physics identical. As CNN observed in its analysis of the 2026 Gala, the dexterity on display — the tool handling, the force feedback, the self-recovery after a jump — signals directly to the industrial customer. The stage is a trade show with better lighting.

The second is softer and more interesting: dancing robots are likeable robots. Decades of research on human-robot interaction point the same direction — we fear the machine that stands still and watches, and we warm to the machine that moves with us. A robot doing kung fu alongside laughing children on the most-watched broadcast on Earth is an argument, aimed at a billion people at once, that these machines belong at the party. This is public-perception engineering on a scale no Western robotics company could dream of, and it is working. as CNN reported, citing state media,, robots from Unitree, MagicLab and Noetix sold out on JD.com during the broadcast itself. The Gala was, as one Weibo user put it, a product launch — and the product was not any particular robot. The product was comfort.

And the third purpose is the one this series keeps circling back to: industrial policy in costume. Four startups — Unitree, MagicLab, Galbot, Noetix — paid for their Gala slots in partnership deals the South China Morning Post reported were worth around 100 million yuan, roughly fourteen million US dollars, and considered it a bargain. Unitree’s founder, Wang Xingxing, met Xi Jinping at a high-profile symposium weeks after the 2025 performance went viral. He told state media before this year’s show that he had been under enormous pressure to exceed it. When the CEO of a robotics startup describes his New Year’s television appearance in the language of national obligation, you are no longer watching entertainment. You are watching a state rehearse its future in public — and inviting its citizens, and the watching world, to applaud the rehearsal.

The full bill of the 2026 Gala repays study, because the casting was as deliberate as the choreography. The evening’s very first robots were not athletes at all: Noetix sent its small Bumi robots into a comedy sketch about a grandmother and her grandson — the machine as family member, first item on the program. Later, an actress performed alongside a Noetix android built to look like her, a bit of doubling that Chinese television played for laughs and that a Part VIII reader will recognize as the uncanny valley being paved over with a punchline. MagicLab dressed its robot dogs in panda suits. Every genre of human affection — the grandchild, the twin, the pet — was assigned a machine understudy in a single evening of television. If the Unitree segment argued that robots are capable, the rest of the program argued that they are kin. The second argument is the more consequential one, and it was made entirely without a word of policy.

Nor is the stage strategy staying home. In late May, as RTÉ reported, humanoid robots — Unitree machines again, inevitably — shared a Seoul catwalk with human models at an event called the Mach33 Physical AI Fashion Show, each android in an outfit matched to its human partner’s, including a tasselled Texan number complete with a cowboy hat for the robot. The entertainment company behind it explained, with the disarming directness the industry has adopted, that robots too need to wear clothes. The stage, it turns out, is the humanoid industry’s preferred port of entry into every culture it visits — arrive dancing, and the customs inspection goes easier.

The MagicLab number made the subtext text. Human pop stars sang a song titled “Intelligent Manufacturing Future” while the humanoids danced behind them, and the lyric, in English, was: we are made in China. There are countries that would blush to be so literal. China is not one of them, and honestly, the candour is refreshing. The Americans wrap their industrial policy in the flag and call it freedom; the Chinese wrap theirs in a folk dance and call it the Year of the Horse. At least the folk dance has better choreography.

The view from the cheap seats

A Canadian watching all this occupies the seat we always occupy: the balcony. We are not going to win the humanoid race, and no minister will pretend otherwise. But we know something about the stage as an instrument of national feeling. This is the country of Cirque du Soleil, which conquered the world by understanding that the human body doing nearly impossible things is the one spectacle that never needs translation. The Gala engineers understood the same thing and simply changed the definition of body.

And Leacock, patron saint of this publication, would have recognized the whole affair instantly. He made a career of the small-town concert where the performance mattered less than what the town believed about itself while watching. The Spring Festival Gala is the largest small-town concert in human history. What China believed about itself, watching those machines catch their own falls, is the most important data point of the evening — more important than the rotation count.

What the stage rehearses

Here is where this postcard connects back to The Asimov Gap, the legislative chamber to this series’ field reporting.

Societies have always used performance to rehearse their relationship with the new. The Victorians put electricity on stage before they put it in houses. The word “robot” itself entered the language through a play — Čapek’s R.U.R., 1921 — before a single industrial robot existed, and the play was a warning. It matters, therefore, what we choreograph. The 2025 Gala choreographed harmony: robots and humans dancing the same folk dance, the machine folded into the oldest available tradition. The 2026 Gala choreographed something more ambiguous: robots doing martial arts, robots handling weapons — stage weapons, nunchaku and swords and poles, in the hands of machines, played for delight. The audience cheered. I watched the clips several times and found that my wonder, which this series is pledged to put first, kept arriving with a footnote attached.

Because the same force feedback that lets a robot catch a nunchaku lets it catch other things. The same cluster coordination that repositions sixteen dancers at four metres per second repositions sixteen of anything. The stage does not create these capabilities; it domesticates them — it teaches a billion people to associate the capability with applause. Whether that association is a gift or a groom depends entirely on what the machines are asked to do next, and that question is not answered on a stage. It is answered in ministries, and in the export-control offices this series’ parent publication spends its days examining.

The audience, to its credit, noticed. For every Weibo commenter predicting the performance would send shock waves across the Pacific, there was another posting a quieter and more piercing review, one that was echoed across the platform until it became the evening’s unofficial minority report: we are looking for humans amid all the robots. Eight words, and they contain the entire debate this series exists to cover. The producers gave the most-watched broadcast on Earth to the machines, and a measurable portion of a billion viewers spent the evening scanning the stage for their own species. That instinct — the eye that keeps hunting for the human in the frame — may turn out to be the most durable safety mechanism we possess. It is certainly the cheapest, and unlike the export controls, it ships pre-installed.

The dancer’s arm, Trapp reminds us, carries everything it has ever reached for. The robot’s arm carries everything it has ever been programmed to reach for — and the programming, unlike the memory, is transferable, copyable, and for sale starting at $13,500.

So: can they dance? Not yet, I think — the cold spot in the dancer’s chest is a more reliable instrument than any benchmark. Will it matter, once the performance is good enough that only dancers can feel the difference? That is the question the Year of the Horse left on the stage, under the lights, wobbling like a Drunken Fist master, perfectly in control.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one arrived with music.
