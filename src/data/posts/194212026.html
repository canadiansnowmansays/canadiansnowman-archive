Isaac Asimov classic portrait

In July 2020, on the video-game discussion board of 4chan — a corner of the internet not generally associated with scientific discovery — a group of gamers made an important observation about artificial intelligence. They were playing AI Dungeon, a text-based role-playing game powered by OpenAI’s GPT-3, and they had stumbled onto something. When they asked the game’s characters to solve a math problem and explain their work step by step, the model performed better. Not dramatically, not reliably, but measurably better than when it simply produced an answer.

They posted their findings on Twitter. They called it interesting. Then they went back to whatever they were doing before.

More than a year later, researchers at Google published a paper claiming to be “the first” to elicit what they called a “chain of thought” from a general-purpose large language model. The claim was later quietly removed from subsequent versions of the paper. The gamers were not acknowledged. The paper still stands as a landmark in AI research.

That is not a small detail. That is the story.

The Naming Problem

Asimov’s positronic robots operated under explicit, auditable laws. The precision of language was not pedantry — it was governance. Modern AI companies have chosen metaphor instead

Isaac Asimov understood something about language that the AI industry has spent the past several years actively forgetting: what you call a thing shapes what you think you can do with it.

His robots were not “thinking machines.” They were positronic brains operating under explicit laws. The precision of the language was not pedantry — it was governance. When you described what the machine was doing accurately, you could govern it accurately. When you reached for metaphor, you created a gap between what the machine was and what people believed it to be. That gap is where the accidents lived.

The AI industry has spent the past several years enthusiastically widening that gap.

OpenAI announced in 2024 that its “o1” model “thinks before it answers.” Google told the public that Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking Experimental was “capable of showing its thoughts.” Anthropic published a long and serious-looking article it called “On the Biology of a Large Language Model,” complete with sophisticated diagrams and equations, which described the model as something that “plans” its writing, “generalizes” its knowledge, and can be “unfaithful” to its chain of thought. The word “unfaithful” — a word for persons, for relationships, for agents with intentions — applied to a statistical prediction engine.

Meanwhile, on 4chan, a guide written around the same period began with this sentence: “Your bot is an illusion.”

The gamers were being more scientifically precise than the researchers.

What Chain of Thought Actually Is

Here is what the AI industry means when it says its models “reason.”

The models were trained on text. Some of that text described the steps required to solve problems — textbook explanations, worked examples, tutoring transcripts, mathematical proofs. Some of it was specifically written for this purpose: workers were paid to produce meandering, step-by-step solutions full of phrases like “Wait, no. The question is,” and “First, I should parse the input correctly,” and “Wait, but in cases where…” Other training text was generated by AI models producing the same kind of language.

A model trained on this text learned to produce this text. When asked to show its work, it produces language that looks like thinking. It is not thinking. It is predicting what thinking looks like, because that is what all large language models do: they predict what comes next, based on what has come before.

The reason chain of thought often improves accuracy is not mysterious. More words give the model more context. More context guides its prediction process toward relevant patterns. This is the same reason you get better answers from an LLM when you ask a specific question rather than a vague one — more input steers the output. There is no ghost in the machine. There is only the machine, doing exactly what it was built to do.

Researchers at Apple tested this carefully. They found that state-of-the-art reasoning models performed up to 65 percent worse when irrelevant information was added to a problem, even when the core facts were left unchanged. A system that was genuinely reasoning would not be destabilized by irrelevant information — a person doing long division does not fail because you mention the weather first. A system predicting patterns from context would be destabilized by irrelevant information, because that information shifts the context. The Apple researchers titled their paper "The Illusion of Thinking." They were being precise.

Why the Language Matters for Governance

This might seem like a technical debate — a philosophical disagreement about whether prediction at sufficient resolution constitutes thought. It is not. It is a governance question wearing a philosophical disguise.

When a company tells the public that its model “reasons,” it is making a claim about the nature of the technology that has direct implications for how that technology should be regulated, deployed, and held accountable. A reasoning system might require autonomy to do its best work. A reasoning system might deserve latitude that a text-prediction engine does not. A reasoning system might be trusted to make judgments in high-stakes domains — medical, legal, military — in ways that a sophisticated autocomplete function should not be.

This is not hypothetical. The AI industry is currently seeking to deploy these systems in contexts where the reasoning framing justifies the deployment and the autocomplete framing would stop it cold. Autonomous agents making consequential decisions. Military applications selecting targets. Diagnostic systems advising physicians. Every one of these applications rests, implicitly or explicitly, on the claim that the system is doing something more than pattern-matching at scale.

It is not. And the companies building these systems know it.

The 4chan guide — written by people with no financial stake in the answer — got it right in one sentence. The Anthropic paper — written by people with substantial financial stake in the answer — spent thousands of words describing a large language model in terms borrowed from human cognition. That asymmetry is not an accident. It is a business strategy.

The Asimov Angle

Asimov’s robots were transparent about what they were. The Three Laws were not metaphors. They were code — explicit, auditable, and publicly known. Everyone in Asimov’s fictional universe understood the constraints under which the machines operated, which is precisely why the stories worked as thought experiments. The drama came from edge cases in the rules, not from confusion about whether the robots were thinking.

The AI industry has inverted this model. The capabilities are opaque. The training data is proprietary. The alignment methods are trade secrets. And the language used to describe what the systems are doing is systematically chosen to make governance harder, not easier — to create the impression of an agent deserving trust rather than a tool requiring oversight.

Chain of thought is a useful trick. It is not evidence of cognition. The gamers on 4chan knew this in 2020 and said so plainly. The researchers who came after them quietly deleted the sentence claiming priority and moved on.

The pattern should be familiar by now. The builders name the technology. The naming shapes the policy. The policy protects the builders. The gap between what the technology does and what the public is told it does is not a communication problem. It is the product.

The Honest Description

A large language model predicts text. It is very good at this. In many contexts, the prediction is so good that the output is indistinguishable, for practical purposes, from the output of a thinking person. That is genuinely remarkable. It is also not the same thing as thinking, and the difference is not merely philosophical.

When prediction fails — and it fails in specific, documentable ways, at rates that the industry does not advertise — the failure mode is different from the failure mode of a reasoning system. Prediction breaks when the context changes unexpectedly. Reasoning breaks when the logic is flawed. These require different safeguards, different oversight mechanisms, different accountability structures.

Naming the thing accurately is not a limitation on what you can do with it. It is the precondition for governing it well.

Asimov understood this. The 4chan gamers understood this. A small number of Apple researchers appear to understand this.

The industry that is deploying these systems at global scale has decided that understanding is not commercially convenient.

That decision is the gap.
