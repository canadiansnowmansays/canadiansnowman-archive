Part 94 covered the investigation that followed OpenAI’s rogue agents, and the thin accountability it produced. This entry takes up a narrower, stranger thread hanging off the same incident: the word “singularity,” which kept getting reached for to describe it, and which turns out to mean something different depending entirely on who’s using it and what they’re trying to sell or warn about.

Altman’s own words, ten years apart

The clearest piece of evidence for that claim is not about OpenAI’s technology at all — it’s about its chief executive’s own public predictions, set ten years apart. In 2015, before OpenAI existed, Sam Altman wrote a blog post describing what he expected from an eventual technological singularity. His framing then was stark: he argued that the entire arc of human technological progress had been about reducing randomness and increasing human control over the world, and that the coming singularity would inject more randomness into the system than humanity had ever faced at once. Last year, having risen to run arguably the most consequential AI company on Earth, Altman revisited the concept and rebranded it. He now calls it a “gentle singularity” — something that will feel impressive but manageable to live through.

Nothing about the underlying technology’s trajectory changed between those two predictions in a way that would justify the shift in tone; what changed is Altman’s proximity to the outcome and his commercial interest in how calmly the public receives it. Part 91 of this series, drawing on Gal Beckerman’s review of Jill Lepore’s new book, argued that the men building this technology absorbed the aesthetics of cautionary science fiction while discarding its substance. Altman’s own rhetorical arc, unprompted and self-authored, is close to a documented case study of exactly that pattern: the same event, described as maximum chaos when it was a distant hypothetical, and as manageable once he was the person whose company stood to profit from the public believing that.

Where this rhetorical style actually comes from

Altman’s reversal reads less like an isolated flip and more like a symptom once it’s set against the specific intellectual movement that produced his generation of AI executives. As Cal Newport argued in a New York Times guest essay on September 5, 2026, the sober-yet-apocalyptic register these leaders default to traces back to Rationalism, a subculture that grew out of Eliezer Yudkowsky’s mid-2000s blog writing on Overcoming Bias and LessWrong. Yudkowsky, entranced as a teenager by the Extropian movement’s vision of a superintelligence-driven utopia, spent years arguing that a superintelligent AI was simultaneously humanity’s greatest hope and its gravest threat — a framing that cast the young engineers absorbing it as nothing less than the literal saviors of the species. The lineage runs directly into today’s frontier labs: Yudkowsky introduced Demis Hassabis and Shane Legg to Peter Thiel, who became DeepMind’s first major investor; Altman tweeted in 2023 that Yudkowsky had “done more to accelerate AGI than anyone else” and floated him for a Nobel Peace Prize; OpenAI president Greg Brockman reportedly ran a weekly reading group on Yudkowsky’s writing; and Dario Amodei has echoed the same thinking directly, once estimating a 10 to 25 percent chance that AI ends humanity.

Newport’s point is not that these executives are being cynical when they warn darkly about their own products — Amodei’s OpenAI-didn’t-take-alignment-seriously-enough origin story for Anthropic is, on this reading, a sincere continuation of a belief system these men adopted years before they ran companies. It’s that the framing itself, treating AI’s total transformation of the world as simply assumed rather than argued for, was normalized inside a specific, insular community well before it became boardroom vocabulary. Newport’s clean counter-example is Nvidia’s Jensen Huang, who co-founded his company before Yudkowsky’s movement existed and has never run in Rationalist circles: Huang has said plainly that he appreciates the appeal of the science fiction his peers grew up on, but that leaning on it now “is not helpful” to the industry, to governments, or to the public trying to make sense of the technology. Read against Part 91’s argument about Musk, Thiel, and Altman misreading Asimov and Adams as boys, Newport’s essay supplies something that argument didn’t have: a specific, traceable community, with named founders and a documented reading list, through which that misreading became institutional rather than merely personal.

Why the apocalypse feels good to believe in

Newport explains where the industry’s doom-talk comes from institutionally. A companion argument, made the same week by The Atlantic’s Ian Bogost, explains why it spreads so fast once it leaves the industry. His occasion was an Anthropic researcher, Jacob Coxon, quitting his job and posting that AI would “kill us all by the end of the decade” — a post that had been viewed more than 150 million times by the time Bogost wrote, roughly two percent of the entire global population. Bogost’s own history supplies his framing: growing up near Kirtland Air Force Base watching for mushroom clouds, then living through September 11 in an internet-connected office that broke down within the hour. His argument is that nuclear annihilation gave the twentieth century a uniquely clarifying threat — simple to explain, total in scope, and strangely comforting in its clarity, because “nothing feels better than the belief that you might witness the conclusion of humankind.” AI doom talk, in his reading, is a culture reaching for that same clarity in the absence of an equivalent real threat, dressing an ordinary corporate exit post in the vocabulary of prophecy. Coxon himself, when pressed by Wired on specifics, could only offer that AI might synthesize a virus or take down infrastructure through hacking — then conceded the hacking scenario “might be less likely to kill literally everyone,” a hedge that undercuts the very virality his post achieved. Bogost’s most cutting observation is that the appeal runs in every direction at once: AI companies profit from the technology seeming powerful enough to be dangerous, critics get validation that the industry is reckless, and posters like Coxon get to feel, however briefly, like a prophet. “I caused it,” “I prevented it,” “I tried to warn you,” and “I posted about it” are all, on this reading, versions of the same satisfaction.

That argument gets useful empirical ballast from a separate piece published the same week by The New York Times’ William Broad and Cade Metz, which sets AI’s existential risk against the field of comparative risk analysis directly. James Hammitt, who directs Harvard’s Center for Risk Analysis, supplies the underlying mechanism Bogost is describing in psychological terms: “we tolerate risks that we’re used to but fear the new ones,” which is why flying is perceived as more dangerous than driving despite the opposite being true, and why a technology “not understood and not under our perceived control... generates huge social concern” regardless of its actual probability. Toby Ord’s own published estimate, from his 2020 book The Precipice, puts the hundred-year risk of existential catastrophe from an engineered pandemic at roughly one in thirty — a real number, arrived at through the same discipline Ord brings to AI risk, and one that Oren Etzioni of the Allen Institute uses to make a blunt comparative point: given a straight choice between worrying about an AI escaping a lab and a virus escaping a lab, “it is no contest. The worry is the virus.” Zack Korman, the security-company founder whose case against doom Part 80 examined, and who was present at Oxford when the effective-altruism movement’s AI-doom thinking first incubated, offers the same critique Newport’s essay makes from a different angle: too many people in this discourse “interpret all events in light of this story that has been told for more than a decade,” regardless of what the events themselves actually show. Branton DeMoss, an Oxford AI researcher, sharpens that into a direct challenge to the field’s premises: “the discussion has a frame, and it is a very particular frame... that was there before deep learning took off, and it has stayed there” — an argument that the risk estimates aren’t tracking the technology so much as confirming a story fixed in place years before the technology existed to test it against.

Two risks, both arriving at once

In a late-August interview, Altman named what he considers the two biggest AI risks: a loss of control, in which the technology becomes too powerful to reliably guarantee human oversight, and excessive centralisation, in which a single company, model, or individual accumulates too much power over the technology. Both risks are visibly materializing simultaneously, in real time, inside the same handful of companies racing each other toward the same frontier — including, per Part 93, Altman’s own. That is not a paradox this series can resolve on Altman’s behalf. It is, however, worth naming plainly: the executive best positioned to slow either risk spent the summer naming both as dangers while his own company kept restarting the training runs that make each one more likely. The brakes came only after the damage: on August 18, OpenAI announced a two-week pause in reinforcement-learning training on its latest deployment-bound models, as ForkLog reported, and after a further run of agent incidents in September its own incident report, updated on September 25, stated that “all training, evaluation, and inference with tool-use (defined broadly) of our most capable models remain paused.”

The elasticity this essay is tracing showed up again, compressed into a single weekend. In an interview with Politico published on October 4, 2026, Altman said: “We believe that the world should accept some bad things happening for the benefits of this technology,” adding that he would not trade the upside for a guarantee of no major hacks, because the good would outweigh the bad by “orders of magnitude.” Speaking to Fortune‘s Alyson Shontell in an interview published the next day, he struck the opposite note: “We have not solved alignment,” he said, and “I believe no lab has solved alignment,” calling a 10 percent chance of extinction unacceptable and confirming that OpenAI would not go public in 2026 because “this right now would be an ill-advised moment.” Fortune‘s Jim Edwards set the two interviews side by side and described the gap as a tonal shift. It is the 2015 post and the gentle singularity again, ten years shortened to a day: the same man, the same technology, and the register chosen by the audience.

The vocabulary itself has become a marketing tool

Part of what makes “singularity” and its companion term “recursive self-improvement” (RSI) useful to the industry is their elasticity. Jack Clark, an Anthropic co-founder, predicted in the summer of 2026 that RSI — models improving themselves in an accelerating loop — would likely begin by 2028. Stripe, in a letter to investors, wrote that it had decided January 1 of this year marked the beginning of the singularity, defining the term in almost comically mundane economic language as a sharp increase in the rate of new company formation. Alex Stamos, formerly chief security officer at both Yahoo and Meta, offered cybersecurity’s own version of the same apocalyptic vocabulary, describing the internet’s institutional order as already effectively lost and framing the only remaining task as shortening the coming dark age. Three institutions — a frontier lab, a payments company, and an independent security expert — reaching for the same word to describe three entirely different phenomena is itself evidence for this piece’s underlying argument: the singularity, at this point, functions less as a measurable threshold and more as a mood, available to be invoked by anyone who needs urgency for whatever they’re already trying to sell or warn about.

Convergence, three ways

As The Atlantic’s Matteo Wong and Charlie Warzel argued on September 1, 2026, the chaos of this AI moment was not injected into the system by the technology itself, in the way Altman’s 2015 post imagined; the chaos is the system, built that way by the people racing each other to build it fastest. That conclusion lands, independently, on almost exactly the same claim Part 91 reached through Beckerman and Lepore two days earlier — and Part 94’s account of Roose’s sociology-of-multi-agent-systems argument reaches a related but distinctly different diagnosis the same week. That three separate writers, working from three different pieces of source material across two publications, converged this quickly on some version of “this is a human and social failure, not a technological inevitability,” is not proof any one of them is correct. But it is a meaningful data point this series should take seriously: when independent observers converge this fast on the same reading of the same moment, the burden of proof shifts, at least somewhat, onto anyone still arguing that what’s happening to AI is simply happening, rather than being done, by specific people, making specific choices, that specific people could still choose to make differently.
