There is a mound of dirt in Indiana that has been there for over thirty years.

It sits along a property line next to a mosque, put there during the mosque’s ground-breaking by a neighbour who did not want to look at it. Children were warned not to chase soccer balls onto that neighbour’s lawn. The dirt is covered in grass now, and trees have grown on it, but it is still there — a small, deliberate act of exclusion that outlasted the anger that built it, quietly waiting for the culture to catch up to it again.

As Asma Khalid reported for The Atlantic on 18 August 2026, that mound sits behind her childhood mosque in Indiana, and the story of how a private grudge became a public licence to hate is, in miniature, the story this series has been telling for fourteen months.

The founding promise, again

This series began in June 2025 with a simple, stubborn argument: every group currently marked as “unassimilable” — immigrants, LGBTQ+ Americans, and now, with fresh intensity, Muslims — was once a stand-in for Irish Catholics, Chinese railroad laborers, or Jewish refugees fleeing a continent that had already decided it did not want them. The targets rotate. The rhetoric does not.

What makes the Muslim American story distinct, and worth its own instalment rather than a paragraph folded into Part XVI, is how far back the “founding promise” actually reaches — further than most of the immigration waves this series has already covered.

Muslims were not latecomers to the American story. Historians estimate that somewhere between 10 and 15 percent, and by some accounts as much as 30 percent, of the enslaved people transported to North America practised Islam. One of them, Yarrow Mamout, was sold into slavery in Annapolis in 1752, spent decades in bondage on Maryland plantations, and eventually purchased his own freedom and a plot of land in what is now Georgetown. He was well known enough in his lifetime that the Revolutionary-era portraitist Charles Willson Peale painted him — a portrait that now hangs in the Philadelphia Museum of Art — and wrote admiringly in his diary of Yarrow’s faith and character. When Yarrow died in 1823, his obituary ran in more than three dozen newspapers.

Thomas Jefferson, drafting the Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom, made a point of naming who the new nation’s tolerance was meant to cover: “the Jew and the Gentile, the Christian and Mahometan, the Hindoo, and infidel of every denomination.” He invoked Islam specifically to demonstrate how far religious liberty was supposed to reach — even as tens of thousands of enslaved African Muslims were denied that liberty in the same breath.

This is the pattern this series keeps finding underneath every “who really belongs” argument: the promise was broad from the start, and the betrayal of it is not a deviation from American history but a recurring feature of it.

The decade the mask came off

Khalid, now a journalist, traces the shift herself, having covered American politics through the exact period this series has spent fourteen months excavating. She first noticed the change in the run-up to the 2016 election. At a Florida Republican Party summit in 2015, a Muslim woman in a hijab approached her in the audience, delighted to have found a fellow Republican in an unwelcoming crowd, and was startled to learn Khalid was there as a reporter. Within days, Donald Trump was on stage claiming — falsely — that “thousands and thousands” of Muslims in New Jersey had cheered when the Twin Towers fell. Soon after, he called for a “total and complete shutdown of Muslims entering the United States.”

At a Trump campaign event in Florida that same cycle, a volunteer named Jared cornered Khalid to explain, earnestly and at length, why a Muslim ban would actually be good for her. “True believers” of Islam, he told her, had a mission to come to America and commit “heinous things,” and if that happened, she personally could end up in an internment camp — so the ban was simple “risk mitigation.” She did not tell him that she considered herself a true believer in exactly the sense he meant: someone who prays, fasts, and holds her faith seriously. Shortly afterward, Trump told Anderson Cooper on CNN, “I think Islam hates us.” The pronoun did a great deal of quiet work.

Trump did not invent this suspicion. He located it, already present, and gave it a microphone. What matters for this series’ argument is what happened next: rhetoric that would once have ended a campaign instead became campaign strategy, and strategy curdled into governance, and governance has now curdled into the casual cruelty of a state’s second-highest elected official telling a Christian talk show that his faith community deserves “permission to hate.”

From surveillance to celebration

Khalid’s account of the years after 11 September 2001 will be familiar in shape to anyone who has read this series’ earlier instalments on the officer corps purges or the preschool inspections: harassment that was real but treated as aberrant, a scattering of vandalism and violence — a car driven into a mosque door in Ohio, a Molotov cocktail thrown at worshippers in Texas, gunfire at a Seattle mosque parking lot — met with public disapproval even as government surveillance of Muslim communities expanded quietly in the background.

What has changed by 2026 is not the underlying suspicion. It is that the suspicion no longer needs to hide.

Representative Andy Ogles of Tennessee posted in March that Muslims “don’t belong in American society.” Representative Randy Fine of Florida, the same month, wrote that the country needed “more Islamophobia, not less.” Representative Nancy Mace of South Carolina described every Muslim officeholder in America as “a trojan horse.” These are not anonymous accounts or fringe commentators. These are sitting members of the United States Congress, saying in public what earlier generations of bigots at least had the residual shame to say only to a reporter’s face, off the record, in a strip-mall Republican headquarters in the Mahoning Valley.

And then there is Micah Beckwith, the lieutenant governor of Indiana, who went on a conservative talk show in May and said: “I hate Islam. It’s a demonic death cult.” He later added, apparently as a form of consolation, that he loves Muslims because they make “great Christians” once they convert. He said this days after a shooting killed three people at a mosque in San Diego, without mentioning the shooting or the security measures — guards, gates — that American mosques increasingly require. Asked by The Atlantic whether he regretted the comment, his office replied that he did not, and had “not had a change of heart.”

Beckwith is also the man who, in his own words, told Americans they needed “permission to hate again.”

That phrase is the hinge this entire series turns on. It is not a claim that hatred exists — hatred has always existed, in the dirt mound and the bullet holes and the harassed schoolgirl. It is a claim that the culture’s restraint on expressing that hatred was the problem, and that the restraint itself needed to be lifted. Every exclusionary movement this series has documented, from the Chinese Exclusion Act’s defenders to the architects of the 1924 immigration quotas to the officers purged from the military for the wrong politics, has eventually arrived at some version of this same demand: not tolerance for prejudice, but permission for it.

“We were here first, okay? You’re second.”

Khalid recounts a conversation from a Republican Party Christmas party in Ohio in 2018, with a longtime local volunteer named Connie, that captures the logic more plainly than any politician’s press release. Connie, a woman in her seventies who had been friendly with Khalid across two prior interviews, told her outright to remove her hijab, said Muslims wanted to “take over America,” and instructed her: “If you want to be an American, be an American. Or be a Muslim.” Then: “We were here first, okay? You’re second.”

This is the exact grammar this series has traced through every prior exclusion. It does not matter that Muslims were present at the country’s founding, transported in chains alongside everyone else who built it against their will. It does not matter that Yarrow Mamout bought his own freedom and his own land while the nation that enslaved him was still deciding what its promises meant. The claim of precedence is not historical. It is a claim about who gets to decide, today, whether the promise applies to you — and it is functionally identical to what was said about Irish Catholics arriving in Boston, about Chinese labourers building the railroads that connected the country they were barred from owning property in, about Jewish refugees turned back from ports because a quota system built in 1924 had already decided how many of them America could stomach.

Khalid’s own reporting supplies one further, uncomfortable data point that complicates the easy version of this story: the hostility is sharpest and most explicit on the right, but the absence of Muslim American voices in foreign-policy decision-making was a bipartisan failure, and many Muslim Democrats remain angry at the Biden administration’s approach to the war in Gaza. This series has never argued that only one party is capable of betraying the founding promise — only that one party, at this particular moment, has stopped bothering to disguise it.

Reading it through the TACO framework

Run Beckwith’s arc through the Threaten-Accommodate-Capitulate-Obfuscate framework this series has used to track other institutional collapses, and the shape is depressingly familiar. The threat came first, on a friendly podcast, low-stakes and low-cost: Islam as “a demonic death cult,” later upgraded to “a more dangerous ideology than even Nazism.” Indiana’s political and religious establishment had its chance to accommodate concern without capitulating to the premise — and largely failed to do so with any force. A coalition of religious leaders, including Jewish clergy, did condemn the remarks, which is more pushback than some of the officials this series has tracked have received. But Beckwith did not retreat; he escalated, accusing his critics on social media of being sheep too naive to recognize a wolf. That is not accommodation forcing a walk-back. That is capitulation dressed up as principle, and it ends, as it always does in this series, in obfuscation: an aide’s flat statement that the lieutenant governor has “not had a change of heart,” offered as if steadiness in cruelty were itself a virtue.

The same arc appears in Ogles, Fine, and Mace’s social media posts. None of the three has faced meaningful consequence from their own party. None has walked the comments back. The Republican conference that once might have quietly pressured a member to delete an inflammatory post now treats “Muslims don’t belong in American society” as an unremarkable Tuesday. This is the accommodation-to-capitulation slide this series has already documented inside the military’s officer corps and the federal judiciary — institutions that once maintained at least the appearance of a floor beneath which conduct could not fall, now finding that floor was never load-bearing in the first place.

The berm remains

The mound of dirt behind Khalid’s childhood mosque is covered in grass now. The relationship with the neighbour who built it has, by some accounts, improved. But when Khalid tried to reach that neighbour for this story, the reply she got was blunt: “do I want to speak to you NO,” followed by “you need to research your information a lot better.”

The dirt is still there. So is the pattern.

Two hundred and fifty years into the American experiment, the nation is still deciding, out loud and increasingly without embarrassment, who its founding promise was actually meant for. Micah Beckwith wants permission to hate. Andy Ogles wants Muslims excluded from American society altogether. The lieutenant governor’s office, asked directly whether any of this troubles him, says he has “not had a change of heart.” Somewhere in Indiana, a child is being taught, once again, not to chase a soccer ball across a line someone else drew.
