Almost none of it was true. The presentation he later called “a blot” on his record became the original wound of the Iraq War.

On February 5, 2003, Secretary of State Colin Powell addressed the United Nations Security Council for seventy-six minutes, presenting what the Bush administration claimed was conclusive evidence that Iraq possessed weapons of mass destruction and was concealing them from UN inspectors.

Powell showed satellite photographs. He played intercepted phone calls. He described mobile biological weapons laboratories and underground bunkers and active chemical weapons programs. He was the most credible figure in the Bush administration — the man who had devised the doctrine that bore his name, who had warned against the Iraq invasion in private, who had been overruled, and who had concluded that his duty required him to make the public case for a policy he privately doubted.

“Every statement I make today is backed up by sources, solid sources,” Powell said. “These are not assertions. What we’re giving you are facts and conclusions based on solid intelligence.”

Almost none of it was true.

The weapons of mass destruction did not exist. The mobile biological weapons laboratories were a fabrication, based on the testimony of an informant who had been assessed as unreliable and whose claims had been flagged as doubtful by the Defense Intelligence Agency before Powell’s speech. The nuclear program that Dick Cheney had described with such certainty — the aluminum tubes, the uranium from Niger — was a combination of misread intelligence, deliberate exaggeration, and outright forgery.

The Iraq War that followed Powell’s speech — the invasion launched on March 20, 2003, the fall of Baghdad on April 9, the “Mission Accomplished” declaration on May 1, and the subsequent eight years of occupation, insurgency, sectarian civil war, and strategic catastrophe — was built on a lie. It killed approximately 4,500 American service members, roughly 180,000 Iraqi civilians, and cost $2.4 trillion. It destabilized the Middle East in ways that are still being felt in 2026. It handed Iran — Saddam Hussein’s neighbor and nemesis — a strategic windfall by eliminating its most powerful regional adversary. And it did something that Vietnam had not quite managed to do: it broke the back of the post-war international order.

President George W. Bush declares “Mission Accomplished” aboard the USS Abraham Lincoln, May 1, 2003. The banner, the flight suit, and the confident declaration reflected a strategic framework that had planned for the invasion but not for the day after.

The Road to Baghdad

The decision to invade Iraq was not the product of intelligence failure alone. It was the product of a strategic framework — neoconservatism — that had been waiting for its moment since the 1990s and found it in the rubble of the World Trade Center.

The neoconservative argument was coherent, internally consistent, and wrong. It held that the post-Cold War Middle East was structurally unstable — a region of authoritarian regimes that produced the poverty, humiliation, and radicalization that fed terrorism. The solution was not containment or engagement but transformation: the creation of democratic governments in the Arab world that would be stable, peaceful, and aligned with American interests. Iraq, with its educated population, its oil wealth, and its decades of pent-up opposition to Saddam’s tyranny, was the ideal candidate for democratic transformation. A democratic Iraq would be a model for the region, demonstrating that Arab societies were capable of self-governance and inspiring similar transformations in Egypt, Saudi Arabia, and Iran.

This argument was not made in bad faith. The people who made it — Paul Wolfowitz, Douglas Feith, Richard Perle, William Kristol — genuinely believed it. They were wrong not because they were dishonest but because they had no understanding of Iraq, no appreciation of its sectarian complexity, no respect for the institutional knowledge of the State Department and the CIA professionals who were warning that the occupation would be far harder than the invasion, and no recognition that the credibility of American power depended on the multilateral legitimacy they were about to discard.

The UN inspection process that was underway in the months before the invasion — led by Hans Blix and Mohamed ElBaradei, finding no weapons of mass destruction, asking for more time — was not a diplomatic obstacle to be managed. It was the institutional expression of exactly the multilateral legitimacy that the post-war order required. When the United States invaded without UN authorization, over the explicit objections of France, Germany, Russia, and China, it was not merely bypassing a procedural inconvenience. It was demonstrating that the rules-based international order applied to everyone except the most powerful nation — a demonstration that permanently undermined the order’s claim to universality.

The Failure

The invasion itself was a military success. The occupation was a strategic catastrophe.

The Coalition Provisional Authority, led by L. Paul Bremer, made two decisions in May 2003 that together produced the insurgency that consumed the next eight years.

The first was the de-Baathification order — the dismissal of all senior members of Saddam’s Baath Party from government positions. This removed from Iraq’s administrative apparatus the experienced civil servants, engineers, and managers on whom any functioning government depends, regardless of their political affiliations. Iraq’s infrastructure began to collapse within weeks.

The second was the dissolution of the Iraqi army — the immediate disbanding of 400,000 armed men who were sent home without pay, without prospects, and with their weapons. These men — many of them Sunni officers who had served the Baathist regime — became the military leadership of the insurgency that would eventually metastasize into Al-Qaeda in Iraq and, later, ISIS.

Both decisions were made against the explicit advice of the State Department, the CIA, and the military officers who understood Iraq. Both decisions reflected the neoconservative framework’s fatal flaw: the belief that Iraq’s existing institutions were so corrupted by Baathism that they had to be destroyed rather than reformed, that transformation required demolition, that you could build a new order from rubble rather than from existing foundations.

U.S. Marines on patrol in Fallujah, Iraq, 2004. The most expensive, best-equipped military in history could not pacify a country of 27 million against determined resistance. The decisions to de-Baathify and dissolve the Iraqi army produced the insurgency the planners refused to anticipate.

This was the Bush administration’s version of the credibility trap. Having invaded on false premises and against the advice of experts, having destroyed the institutional framework that any successor government would need, and having generated an insurgency that conventional military force could not defeat, the administration faced the same logic that had consumed Vietnam: the price of failure was too high to pay, so the commitment had to be extended, the strategy had to be adjusted, the definition of success had to be refined.

Four years later, in 2007, the “surge” — the deployment of an additional 30,000 troops under the command of General David Petraeus — temporarily reduced violence and created conditions that looked, briefly, like progress. But it did not resolve the fundamental problem: that Iraq had been shattered as a state, that the sectarian civil war that the invasion had unleashed had created grievances that would outlast any military intervention, and that the regional consequences — the empowerment of Iran, the creation of ISIS, the destabilization of Syria — could not be contained within Iraqi borders.

The last American combat troops left Iraq in December 2011. They returned in 2014 to fight ISIS. American troops remain in Iraq today. The mission accomplished banner has not aged well.

What Iraq Did to the Order

Vietnam had damaged American credibility. Iraq broke it.

The difference was scale and context. Vietnam was fought during the Cold War, when the Soviet threat gave American power a strategic rationale that survived the specific failure. Even after Vietnam, American allies in Europe and Asia could argue that American commitments were generally reliable — that Vietnam was an aberration, a specific case of strategic overextension, rather than evidence of a systematic tendency toward recklessness.

Iraq destroyed that argument.

Iraq was not a Cold War conflict fought in the context of superpower competition. It was a war of choice, launched in the unipolar moment, by the most powerful nation in the history of the world, against a country that posed no credible threat to American security. It was launched on the basis of intelligence that was fabricated and assessments that were cherry-picked to support a predetermined conclusion. It was launched without UN authorization, against the explicit opposition of the majority of the Security Council, and in violation of the international law framework that the United States had spent sixty years building and championing.

And it failed — visibly, catastrophically, undeniably — in front of the entire world.

The failure had several dimensions.

The first was the WMD failure — the revelation that the casus belli had been false, that the intelligence community had been pressured to produce conclusions that supported the war, and that the most credible figure in the administration had been sent to the UN to present a case that his own advisers had flagged as unreliable. The damage to American intelligence credibility was severe and lasting. When American officials subsequently asserted that Iran was developing nuclear weapons, the immediate response from foreign governments and their populations was: we’ve heard this before. The Iraq experience had immunized the world against American intelligence claims in ways that persisted for years.

The second was the occupation failure — the demonstration that American military power, however dominant in conventional combat, was incapable of producing the political outcomes that the strategic logic of the invasion required. The most expensive, best-equipped military in the history of the world could not pacify a country of 27 million people against the determined resistance of a fraction of its population. This was not a new lesson — Vietnam had taught it — but Iraq taught it again, to a new generation, in higher definition.

The third, and most consequential, was the legitimacy failure — the demonstration that American power was capable of acting outside the rules-based international order with impunity, and that the order’s mechanisms had no effective means of restraining it. The Security Council’s opposition, France and Germany’s objections, the global protests of February 15, 2003 — the largest coordinated protest in human history, with an estimated 10 to 15 million people in the streets of cities across the world — had made no difference. The United States invaded anyway.

Millions fill the streets of London (and cities worldwide) on February 15, 2003 — the largest coordinated protest in human history. It made no difference. The rules-based international order was shown to apply to everyone except the strongest nation.

This was the definitive proof that the order’s rules applied to the weak but not to the strong. Every subsequent American claim to be defending the rules-based international order was haunted by this demonstration. When the United States condemned Russia’s invasion of Ukraine as a violation of international law, Russian diplomats had a ready response: Iraq. When the United States insisted on UN authorization for military action, critics pointed to Iraq. The wound was self-inflicted, and it never fully closed.

Canada’s response to the Iraq War deserves a paragraph it rarely receives in American accounts. Prime Minister Jean Chrétien stood in the House of Commons on March 17, 2003, three days before the invasion, and announced that Canada would not join the coalition. Without UN authorization, he said, there was no justification for war. The decision was domestically popular — polls showed approximately 70 percent of Canadians opposed the invasion — and internationally significant: Canada was the first major American ally to explicitly refuse participation. The Bush administration was furious. Paul Cellucci, the American ambassador to Ottawa, said publicly that the United States was disappointed and “upset.” The Canadian economy absorbed retaliatory friction in cross-border trade. And Chrétien was vindicated by history in ways that Blair, Howard, and the other coalition leaders were not. It was, viewed from Toronto, one of the cleaner moments in Canadian diplomatic history — the medium power, living next to the superpower, saying plainly what the superpower did not want to hear. Keenleyside would have recognized the instinct, if not the precise circumstances.

The Iranian Dividend

The Shia Crescent. The Iraq War’s most consequential (and least discussed) outcome: Iran’s greatest strategic windfall in decades. Saddam’s removal, the empowerment of Iraq’s Shia majority, and the opening of influence routes that still shape the region in 2026.

The Iraq War’s most consequential strategic consequence was the one least discussed in Washington: it handed Iran the greatest strategic windfall in the Islamic Republic’s history.

Saddam Hussein’s Iraq had been Iran’s most powerful regional adversary — the country that had fought Iran to a bloody standstill in the eight-year Iran-Iraq war of the 1980s, killing hundreds of thousands on both sides. Saddam’s removal by American forces eliminated this adversary without cost to Iran, converted Iraq’s Shia majority into a politically empowered community with close ties to Tehran, and opened a land corridor between Iran and its Hezbollah proxy in Lebanon through the newly fragmented state of Syria.

The architects of the Iraq invasion had argued that a democratic Iraq would be a model for the region — including, implicitly, for Iran. What they produced instead was an Iran with more regional influence than it had held at any point since the 1979 revolution.

This consequence was not unforeseen by everyone. State Department and CIA analysts had warned that Saddam’s removal would benefit Iran. The warnings were ignored, because the neoconservative framework had no category for strategic consequences that the market for democracy could not correct. If Iraq became democratic, Iran’s citizens would demand the same. The logic was elegant and wrong.

Twenty years later, in 2026, the United States is at war with an Iran whose regional position was strengthened by the American invasion that was supposed to demonstrate American power. The proxies Iran built with its Iraq windfall — Hezbollah, the Houthi movement, the Shia militias in Iraq — are the same proxies that are now expanding the Iran war to five simultaneous fronts. The connection is direct.

Senator Lindsey Graham — Trump’s most influential adviser on the Iran war of 2026 — said of the doctrine that what you break, you own: “I don’t buy that.” He helped authorize the Iraq War. He is, in this respect, one of the most consequential figures in the chain of decisions that led from Baghdad 2003 to Tehran 2026. He has never acknowledged the connection.

Powell’s Epitaph

Colin Powell in his later years. The soldier-statesman who warned privately, presented the case publicly, and carried the regret for the rest of his life. “A blot,” he called it. The institutions had the information. The warnings were given. They were overridden.

Colin Powell died on October 18, 2021, of complications from COVID-19. He was 84 years old.

In his final years, he spoke with increasing candor about Iraq. The February 2003 UN speech, he said, was “a blot” on his record — the presentation he wished he had never given, based on intelligence he should have questioned more aggressively, in service of a policy he had privately opposed.

Powell represented something important about American power at its best: the soldier-statesman who understood that military force had limits, who had built a doctrine around those limits, and who had warned — too quietly, too privately, too deferentially — against the war that destroyed his doctrine’s legacy.

He is also a figure who illuminates American power’s most persistent failure: the inability of its most capable and principled servants to prevent its most destructive impulses. The institutions of American democracy — the intelligence community, the State Department, the professional military, the Congress — had the information and the judgment to prevent the Iraq disaster. The information was available. The judgment was expressed. The warnings were given.

They were overridden by a small group of political appointees who were certain they were right and who had the president’s ear.

This is the pattern that runs from Vietnam to Iraq to the Iran war of 2026: not a failure of information but a failure of institutional restraint. The multilateral frameworks that the post-war order built were designed to provide that restraint — to embed American power in structures that would check its excesses and distribute the costs of its commitments. When those frameworks were bypassed — as they were in Vietnam, as they were in Iraq, as they were again in Iran — the results were consistently catastrophic.

The lesson is not that American power is malign. It is that unrestrained power of any kind produces malign outcomes — and that the institutional scaffolding the post-war generation built was not an obstacle to American effectiveness. It was its precondition.
