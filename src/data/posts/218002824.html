In January 1901, an Englishwoman named Emily Hobhouse arrived at the camp outside Bloemfontein with a railway truck of supplies and a letter of introduction to the High Commissioner. She was forty years old, the daughter of a Cornish archdeacon, and she had come to South Africa to distribute relief to the families of the Boers the British Army was fighting. She found about two thousand women and children living in bell tents on the open veld, a dozen or more to a tent, in summer heat, without soap, with too little water, on rations that did not include vegetables or fresh milk, and with typhoid and measles moving through the camp.

One of the children she wrote about was Lizzie van Zyl, a little girl whose father was still fighting in the field. Because of that, her family received the lower of the two ration scales the camp used, the one for families of men who had not surrendered. Lizzie was moved to the camp hospital, where Hobhouse recorded that the staff treated her as a nuisance and did not understand her, since she spoke only Dutch. She died there. A photograph of her, emaciated, holding a doll, became one of the images by which Britain’s war in South Africa is remembered.

The ration scale is the whole chapter in miniature. The women and children in the camps were not combatants. They were being fed less because their men had not yet given up.

A war for voters, or for gold

Southern Africa in the 1890s had two independent Boer republics, the South African Republic, usually called the Transvaal, and the Orange Free State, founded by Afrikaans-speaking farmers who had trekked inland in the 1830s and 1840s to get out from under British rule at the Cape. Britain had annexed the Transvaal once, in 1877, and lost it again after a short war in 1881, when the Boers defeated a British force at Majuba Hill.

Then, in 1886, the largest gold deposits ever found were discovered on the Witwatersrand, inside the Transvaal. Johannesburg grew from a mining camp into a city within a decade, and by the late 1890s the Rand was producing about a quarter of the world’s gold. The capital, the engineering and most of the miners came from outside, many of them British. The Transvaal government under President Paul Kruger called them uitlanders, outsiders, taxed them heavily, and made them wait fourteen years for the vote, which they complained was designed to keep them from ever outvoting the burghers.

That was the stated grievance: British subjects denied their political rights by a backward republic. It was not an invented grievance. It was also a very convenient one.

The first attempt to exploit it was an outright conspiracy. At the end of December 1895, Leander Starr Jameson led some five hundred armed men from Rhodesia into the Transvaal, expecting an uitlander uprising in Johannesburg to greet them. It did not happen. Jameson was surrounded and captured within days. The raid had been planned with the knowledge of Cecil Rhodes, mining magnate and Prime Minister of the Cape Colony, who was forced to resign; whether the Colonial Secretary, Joseph Chamberlain, had also known in advance remains one of the long-running questions of imperial history. The raid failed, but it convinced Kruger’s government that Britain meant to take the republic one way or another.

The second attempt was diplomatic, and it was run by Sir Alfred Milner, the High Commissioner in Cape Town. In a dispatch in May 1899, published to build public support, Milner described the uitlanders as helots, the enslaved underclass of ancient Sparta. At a conference in Bloemfontein that June, Kruger offered to reduce the franchise waiting period to seven years. Milner broke off the talks. British troop reinforcements were sent to South Africa over the summer.

On 9 October 1899, Kruger issued an ultimatum demanding that British troops be withdrawn from the republic’s borders. It expired two days later, and the Boers invaded Natal and the Cape. Lord Salisbury’s government insisted that the quarrel was about the rights of British subjects, not the gold. By 1902 Britain had the gold, and both republics with it.

The war the empire nearly lost, then won badly

The Boers were superb mounted riflemen fighting on their own ground, and they humiliated the British Army in the war’s first months. In a single “Black Week” in December 1899 the British lost at Stormberg, Magersfontein and Colenso. The government responded by sending out Lord Roberts, with Lord Kitchener as his chief of staff, and an army that would eventually number close to half a million men from across the empire against Boer forces that numbered, over the whole war, perhaps sixty to eighty-five thousand men.

Canada was part of that army. It was the first time Canadians had been sent to fight an overseas war as a national contingent. Wilfrid Laurier’s government, caught between English Canadians eager to support the empire and French Canadians who saw no reason to fight Britain’s war against another small people, agreed to equip and transport volunteers rather than send an official force; Henri Bourassa resigned his seat in protest. About seven thousand Canadians went. At Paardeberg, in February 1900, the Royal Canadian Regiment’s night advance on the Boer trenches helped force the surrender of General Piet Cronjé and some four thousand men, on the anniversary of Majuba. Canadians took Pretoria with Roberts that June, and won three Victoria Crosses in a single rearguard action at Leliefontein in November 1900.

Pretoria fell, both republics were formally annexed, and the war should have ended. It did not. The Boer commandos broke into small mobile units and went on fighting as guerrillas, living off the farms of the veld. Kitchener, now in command, decided to take the farms away.

Scorched earth, and the camps

Over the next eighteen months, British columns burned something like thirty thousand Boer farmhouses, destroyed crops, and slaughtered or seized livestock across the two former republics. Canadian units took part in the sweeps. The country was fenced with barbed wire and dotted with some eight thousand blockhouses. And the families left homeless, along with the Black African farm workers and tenants who lived on the same land, were moved into camps.

By the war’s end there were more than forty camps for white Boer civilians and more than sixty for Black Africans. The best modern estimates put Boer deaths in the camps at about twenty-eight thousand, the great majority of them children under sixteen. Deaths among the more than one hundred thousand Black Africans interned, in camps that received even less attention and fewer rations, were at least fourteen thousand recorded and probably closer to twenty thousand. Black Africans were also expected to work, often on the military’s own projects, for their food.

The term itself had a short history. The Spanish had used reconcentración against the rebellion in Cuba from 1896, and the American press wrote about “reconcentrado” camps there. The Boer War is where the English phrase “concentration camp” came into general use, which is a more accurate way to put what the Introduction said. The camps were not designed as death camps, and they bear no moral comparison with what the phrase came to mean in the 1940s. They killed through neglect, overcrowding, disease and indifference, in a policy aimed at breaking an enemy’s will by holding his family.

The whistle-blower

Hobhouse’s report, published in June 1901, caused a political storm in Britain. The Liberal leader, Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman, asked in a speech that month when a war was not a war, and answered that it was when it was carried on by methods of barbarism in South Africa. The government sent out an all-women commission under the suffragist Millicent Fawcett, which confirmed most of what Hobhouse had found, and conditions improved sharply once the camps were placed under civilian control and properly supplied. Death rates fell dramatically in the war’s final months.

Hobhouse herself was not thanked. When she tried to return to South Africa in October 1901, she was refused permission to land at Cape Town, placed under military custody, and shipped back to England.

The peace, and who paid for it

The Treaty of Vereeniging was signed on 31 May 1902. The Boers accepted British sovereignty in exchange for generous terms: a promise of eventual self-government, £3 million for reconstruction, and one more clause that matters as much as anything else in this chapter. Article 8 provided that the question of granting the vote to Black Africans would not be decided until after self-government was introduced, which in practice meant it would be decided by the Boers.

That was the price of reconciliation between the two white communities, and it was paid by the people who had no seat at the table. The Union of South Africa, created in 1910 as a self-governing dominion, restricted the franchise, and the Natives Land Act of 1913 confined Black land ownership to a small fraction of the country. The road to apartheid does not start at Vereeniging, but it passes through it.

The gold, meanwhile, went back into production under British sovereignty. When the mines ran short of labour after the war, the colonial administration imported more than sixty thousand indentured Chinese workers between 1904 and 1910 to dig it, housed in compounds, a scheme so unpopular in Britain it helped the Liberals win the 1906 election.

Scoring the Boer War against the rubric

Overwhelming asymmetry. The British Empire against two farming republics with a combined white population smaller than that of many British cities. The early Boer victories show the asymmetry was not total on every battlefield, but the final outcome, with half a million imperial troops committed, was never in doubt. Satisfied.

Coercion as first resort. This is the point where the case needs care, because the Boers issued the ultimatum and fired first. But the sequence before that included an armed raid planned with the Cape Prime Minister’s knowledge, a negotiation broken off by Britain after Kruger made a substantial concession, and a troop build-up on the republics’ borders. The ultimatum was a response to coercion already under way. Satisfied, on the full sequence.

Extraction is the point. Salisbury’s government said the war was about the franchise, not the gold. Britain ended it owning the largest one on earth, and immediately imported indentured labour to work it. The stated purpose, uitlander voting rights, was not the operative goal, and no one argued afterwards that the war had been fought for the franchise. Satisfied.

Self-justifying rhetoric. Rights, representation, and the spreading of good government, from Milner’s helots to the government’s insistence that gold had nothing to do with it. Satisfied.

Resistance gets punished. Thirty thousand farms burned; families held on reduced rations because their men were still fighting; the critic who exposed it deported. Satisfied.

Five for five.

What the Boer War tells us

This is the chapter where the Introduction’s promise about Canada first comes due. Canada did not start this war and did not design the camps. But it sent its young men, cheered them home as heroes, built monuments to them in towns across the country, including the South African War memorial on University Avenue in Toronto, and for more than a century mostly did not ask what the sweeps they joined were for. The rubric does not score Canada as the bully here. It does ask Canadians to notice that the empire’s bullying was a group effort.

It also shows the pattern’s most dangerous feature: the way a bully’s war can end in a peace between the strong parties, paid for by the people with the least power in the room. That happened at Vereeniging, and it will happen again in Part VII, when the great powers of Europe divide Africa at a conference table without inviting any Africans.

Before that, Part VI brings the British story to its end, at Suez in 1956, when Britain tried the whole routine one last time, pretext and gunboats and all, and found that the world it had helped build would no longer let it work.
