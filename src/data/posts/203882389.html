Norman Stalker has edited the Elsinore Beacon for twenty-three years.

He arrived from Kitchener in 2003 under circumstances he has never fully explained and which nobody in Elsinore has pressed him to explain, because in a town of thirty-three thousand people there are certain arrangements that work and certain questions that, once answered, tend to complicate the arrangement. Norman editing the Beacon works. The question of how he came to be there has remained, by general agreement, in the category of things that do not require examination.

He is sixty-one. He wears three sweaters on rotation — burgundy, dark green, faded blue, all from a store that no longer exists. He keeps his reading glasses on a chain: on his nose means he is processing information; off his nose and in his hand means he is forming a judgment; in his hand and pointed at you means the judgment is not good. He has published, in twenty-three years, approximately sixty percent of what he knows about Elsinore.

The other forty percent is in a locked filing cabinet behind his desk.

Nobody has asked what is in the cabinet. This is either professional courtesy or self-preservation, and in Elsinore these have never been considered mutually exclusive.

The question of what Norman knows and does not publish came to general attention in October, when Bradley Fenn — who had been unusually quiet since the heritage minutes affair and the subsequent Beacon coverage, which had been accurate, complete, and filed on page one — submitted a letter to the editor.

The letter was about the Beacon’s coverage of the Smart Community Initiative. Bradley noted that the Beacon had covered the initiative’s launch, the Swan Management Optimization Report, and Gertrude’s response to the report, but had not covered the initiative’s subsequent developments, which included a follow-up presentation to council and a partnership announcement with a technology firm in Waterloo.

He said he felt the Beacon’s coverage had been selective.

Norman read the letter. He removed his reading glasses from his nose. He held them in his hand. He pointed them, briefly, at the letter.

He published the letter in full, with a four-line editor’s note beneath it:

The Beacon has covered the Smart Community Initiative as events have warranted. We covered the launch, the swan report, and its reception. We will cover subsequent developments as they warrant coverage. Readers who feel coverage has been insufficient are welcome to submit letters, as Mr. Fenn has done. — Ed.

Bradley submitted a follow-up letter saying the editor’s note was itself an example of selective presentation.

Norman did not publish the follow-up letter. He filed it. He did not explain why he had not published it. He did not need to. He is the editor.

Carly Meadows had been at the Beacon for fourteen months. She had covered the Easter egg hunt, the paddle boat incident, the election, the heritage minutes, the Presidential Package launch, the Community Ticket committee meeting, and Reverend Faye’s sermon on artificial intelligence. She had filed every piece accurately and on time. She had received, from Norman, three texts that said Good and one that said Very good, which she had not told anyone about because she understood, by now, that Norman’s vocabulary operated on a scale that most editors would consider severely compressed and that Very good occupied a position most editors reserved for This is the best piece I have ever read and you should win a prize.

She knocked on Norman’s office door on a Tuesday morning in November and asked about the filing cabinet.

Norman looked at her over his reading glasses.

She said she had been at the Beacon for fourteen months and she had noticed the cabinet and she had not asked about it because she had not been ready to ask about it, but she thought she was ready now.

Norman was quiet for a moment. He removed his reading glasses. He held them in his hand.

He said: “What do you know about prior restraint?”

Carly said it was the legal doctrine that prevented governments from stopping publication of material before it was published, as distinct from punishing publication after the fact. She said the Pentagon Papers case was the foundational American precedent. She said the Canadian framework was somewhat different.

Norman said: “Good. What do you know about sources?”

She said a journalist’s obligation to protect a source was absolute. She said it was not negotiable under commercial pressure, legal pressure, or social pressure. She said it was the thing that made the whole enterprise possible.

Norman looked at her for a long moment. He put his reading glasses back on his nose.

He said: “The cabinet contains twenty-three years of information provided to me by people in this community who trusted me to know it and to decide when and whether it served the public interest to publish it. Some of it I have published, when the time was right and the public interest was clear. Some of it I have not published, because the time was not right or the public interest was not clear or because publishing would have caused harm to people who did not deserve harm.”

He paused.

“Some of it,” he said, “I have not published because I made a judgment that the information was true but that the cost of publishing it exceeded the benefit, and I made that judgment alone, as editors do, and I have lived with it.”

Carly said: “And the other forty percent?”

Norman looked at her.

“That,” he said, “is an overestimate. It’s closer to thirty-five.”

He went back to reading the copy on his desk. The conversation, in Norman’s vocabulary, was over.

Reading glasses on nose: processing. Off nose, in hand: forming judgment. In hand, pointed at you: the judgment is not good. Carly asked about the other forty percent. He said it was closer to thirty-five.

Gerald Fitch had known, in a general way, that Norman had information he did not publish. Gerald had known this since approximately 2007, when a matter had arisen in Elsinore that Gerald had also known about and had also decided not to put in his notes, on the grounds that certain things existed in a category between the official record and the public interest that required individual judgment and that his judgment, on that occasion, had aligned with Norman’s.

They had never discussed this directly.

They had sat at the window table at the Tim Hortons on several Tuesday mornings over the years — Gerald with his large coffee and two sugars, Norman with his medium black — and read the news on their respective phones and occasionally mentioned things to each other that they had both already known, and the conversation had proceeded in the comfortable register of two people who understood that what was said was approximately what was meant and that the gap between the two was the space in which Elsinore operated.

Gerald had a folder in his filing system labelled Beacon — Editorial — Norman Stalker — General. The folder contained clippings of Norman’s editorials, a copy of the editor’s note about Bradley’s letter, and one handwritten note Gerald had made in 2019 that said, in his small precise handwriting: N. knows. Filed appropriately.

He had not shown this folder to anyone. He had not been asked.

Constance Hay had been reading the Beacon since before Norman arrived. She had been reading it since the Beacon had a different editor whose name she remembered and whose judgment she had considered adequate but not distinguished. She considered Norman’s judgment distinguished, which was a word she used rarely and only when she meant it.

She had, over twenty-three years, noticed the things Norman did not publish. She had formed views about some of them. She had kept those views in her notebook, where they remained.

She attended the November council meeting at which Bradley Fenn raised the question of the Beacon’s coverage of the Smart Community Initiative formally, asking council whether the municipality should consider its communication strategy in light of what he described as “editorial selectivity at the local press level.”

Constance waited until the floor opened.

She said: “The Elsinore Beacon has published accurate, fair, and complete coverage of this municipality for twenty-three years. If Councillor Fenn has a specific factual error to identify, I would encourage him to do so. If he is suggesting that the editor’s judgment about what to publish constitutes a problem requiring a municipal communication strategy, I would suggest he consider what it would mean to live in a municipality where the answer to that question was yes.”

She sat down.

Bradley said he was simply raising the question of balance.

Constance did not respond. She had said what she had to say. She wrote one sentence in her notebook.

Mayor Doug thanked everyone for their contributions to the discussion.

The Note for the Record section of the Moonbeam Chronicle does not usually discuss the Moonbeam Chronicle itself, because a column about its own editorial decisions is either honest or self-serving, and in either case it is the wrong column.

But I will say this.

Norman Stalker told me, when he agreed to publish the Moonbeam Chronicle eight months ago, that he would run it for a month and see. He said it in the tone of a man who had already made a longer commitment and was offering a shorter one in case the longer one proved wrong. He has not mentioned the original arrangement since, because in Norman’s vocabulary a silence of eight months on the subject of a column’s continuation is as close to an enthusiasm as he is likely to express.

He has not edited a word of the Moonbeam Chronicle. He has not asked me to change anything. He has not told me what to write or what not to write. He has published every column I have given him and filed the ones he thought were good and said nothing about the ones he thought were less good, which is also a form of editorial communication that I have learned to read.

I do not know what is in the cabinet. I have not asked. I do not intend to ask.

But I will say that a man who has spent twenty-three years deciding what this community needs to know, and has been right more often than he has been wrong, and has never once been caught publishing something he knew to be false, and has never once been caught suppressing something the public needed to know for reasons that served him personally — that man’s judgment about the locked cabinet is the kind of judgment that a community should be grateful to have and careful not to disturb.

To the south, the question of what the press is permitted to know and publish has taken on a different quality. Journalists have been barred from briefings. Signal chats have been accidentally shared with reporters and then the reporters have been investigated rather than the officials who shared classified material. A television network’s parent company has settled a lawsuit brought by the president for two hundred million dollars in a deal that required no admission of wrongdoing and produced a foundation grant. The Pentagon has restricted press access. The question being asked in Washington is not whether the press publishes too little. The question is whether the press should be permitted to publish at all, and by whom that permission should be granted.

In Elsinore, Norman Stalker decides what the Beacon publishes. He decides alone, in an office whose windows he last cleaned in a year he cannot precisely recall, with three sweaters on rotation and a locked cabinet behind his desk.

He has published sixty percent of what he knows.

The other thirty-five percent is his judgment, held in trust, for whenever it is needed.

Constance Hay trusts his judgment.

In twenty-three years, she has not been wrong about that kind of thing.

The Moonbeam Chronicle: Sketches from Elsinore publishes every Sunday morning in the Elsinore Beacon. Norman Stalker is the editor. He has approved this column. He has not commented on it. This is, in Norman’s vocabulary, the same thing.
