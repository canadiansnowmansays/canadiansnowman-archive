Winston Spencer Churchill was born on October 10, 1940, at Chequers, the prime minister’s official country residence, in the same month the Royal Air Force secured the victory in the Battle of Britain that kept Nazi Germany from forcing an armistice — a birth timed, by pure accident of history, to coincide almost exactly with his grandfather’s single most consequential wartime achievement. He was the son of Randolph Churchill and Pamela Digby, whose marriage collapsed in divorce in 1946 when the boy was five, and he was raised largely in the aftermath of that collapse — his mother going on, across the following decades, to a career considerably more consequential in its own right than anything this series has yet had occasion to mention. Pamela’s trajectory after the divorce ranks among the more remarkable individual reinventions this entire series has recorded: she built a series of relationships across postwar European and American high society substantial enough that biographers have credited her with real informal influence over transatlantic politics and finance for decades, before marrying the Broadway producer Leland Hayward and then, in 1971, the American businessman and elder statesman Averell Harriman — the same Harriman who had served as Roosevelt’s own special envoy to Churchill and Stalin during the war this series has already covered in detail. As Pamela Harriman, she became a major Democratic Party fundraiser and power broker in Washington through the 1980s, and was appointed United States Ambassador to France in 1993, serving until her death in 1997. It is worth sitting with the shape of this for a moment: Winston Jr.’s own mother ended her life more directly embedded in the actual machinery of the special relationship — as a sitting American ambassador to a European capital, married to a man who had personally shuttled between Churchill and Stalin — than either her ex-husband Randolph or her father-in-law’s own son ever managed at any point in their considerably more conventional Churchill-name political careers. The name did not travel with her when she remarried out of it. The proximity to actual power did. Anthony Scaramucci’s “The Long Trade”, the essay this series set out to answer, treats the special relationship as Churchill’s personal wager on America; the one person from this family who ended up actually serving the American government in a European capital was the woman his son had divorced.

Educated, like his father and grandfather before him, at Eton and Christ Church, Oxford, Winston built his own early career in journalism rather than politics, working first as an unpaid copy reader at the Wall Street Journal before becoming a foreign correspondent covering conflicts across Yemen, the Congo, Angola, Borneo, Vietnam, and the 1967 Six-Day War for The Times and other publications — genuine, dangerous war reporting in an era before satellite communications made frontline journalism considerably safer than it once was, and a body of professional work that stands, on its own terms, as more substantive than most accounts of his life bother to credit. His first attempt at Parliament came at a 1967 by-election in Manchester Gorton, which he lost by a margin of only 577 votes despite standing against a deeply unpopular sitting Labour government — a defeat narrow enough to sting, and one that arrived, fittingly enough given this series’ running theme, with a joke attached: visiting an engineering firm during the campaign, he was greeted with a crack comparing him to Rommel, the German general his own grandfather had spent years fighting, a reminder, as his father pointedly told him at the time, of exactly how much of a disadvantage as well as an advantage the family name could represent.

He won a seat at last in the 1970 general election, entering the Commons as Member for Stretford at not quite thirty years old, and was still working as a Daily Telegraph journalist covering his own family’s history when his father Randolph died in 1968 — the paper’s proprietor, Lord Hartwell, made the decision at that point to hand the unfinished official biography of Winston’s grandfather over to the historian Martin Gilbert rather than to the family’s third consecutive generation of would-be biographers, closing off, at least officially, the specific inheritance of narrating the great man’s life that had passed from Winston senior to Randolph and might otherwise have passed to Winston junior in turn. His journalism had already produced one substantial book of its own — a study of the 1967 Six-Day War, co-written with his own father Randolph in what may be the closest the two men ever came to genuine professional collaboration rather than competition — published the year before Randolph’s death and drawing directly on Winston’s own frontline reporting from the conflict. As a backbench MP he cultivated a lighter public profile alongside the policy work, serving as chairman of the House of Commons Flying Club and a member of the parliamentary ski team, small diversions that suggest a man who had, by his thirties, found a comfortable enough niche inside Westminster’s clubbable social life even as his actual ministerial prospects steadily narrowed.

He served briefly as assistant defence spokesman for the Conservative opposition from 1976 to 1978, but that modest frontbench post proved to be the professional ceiling of his entire twenty-seven-year parliamentary career — he never held ministerial office of any kind, and lost even the frontbench post over Rhodesia. In November 1978, when Margaret Thatcher, then Leader of the Opposition, instructed Conservative MPs to abstain on the annual renewal of sanctions against Ian Smith’s white-minority regime, Churchill defied her and voted against the sanctions outright, joining more than a hundred Conservative rebels. Thatcher dismissed him from the frontbench for it, and the vote effectively ended whatever remained of his prospects for advancement within his own party; he spent the rest of his time in the Commons, through all of Thatcher’s eleven years in government, on the back benches. His parliamentary record shows a man who used his backbench freedom mainly to advocate hawkish, staunchly anti-communist positions considerably further to the right than his own party’s leadership was generally willing to go — he spoke out consistently against nuclear disarmament and in support of the Afghan mujahideen during the Soviet occupation of Afghanistan, and chaired an organization called the Committee for Peace with Freedom that campaigned along similar lines, positioning himself, across three decades in Parliament, less as a rising minister-in-waiting than as a fixed ideological voice whose views were considerably more predictable than his career trajectory.

There is a specific, almost too-neat irony worth pausing on in that Rhodesia vote, because it connects directly back to Part X of this series. Winston Jr.’s aunt, Mary Soames, had stood alongside her husband Christopher at the literal ceremony ending white minority rule in Rhodesia in 1979 and 1980, managing the diplomatic delicacy of that transition with real skill under considerable danger. Her nephew, barely a year earlier, had sacrificed his own frontbench career to vote against the very sanctions that had helped force that transition — siding, in effect, with the white-minority government whose surrender his aunt and uncle would shortly be sent to manage. Two members of the same family were, within roughly twelve months, positioned on opposite sides of the identical historical hinge point: one paying a political price to ease the pressure on Smith’s regime, the other standing at the ceremony that ended it. It is not clear either one thought of the coincidence in quite those terms at the time. It is exactly the kind of detail an inheritance audit exists to notice regardless.

His personal life generated the kind of public attention his political career mostly did not. In 1979, he became entangled in a genuinely sensational scandal when a court case involving the prosecution of three police officers for blackmailing Soraya Khashoggi, the former wife of the Saudi arms dealer Adnan Khashoggi, revealed that Churchill had been conducting a two-year affair with her — the defence in the case had argued that police surveillance of Khashoggi stemmed from her relationship with a prominent politician, and Churchill was subsequently named as that politician in open court, generating exactly the kind of tabloid coverage his grandfather’s name virtually guaranteed would attach to it. He married twice, first to Minnie d’Erlanger in 1964, a marriage that lasted until 1997, and then to Luce Engelen; he had four children.

The episode that most directly earns Winston Jr. a place in this series’ running argument about inheritance, though, arrived in 1995, two years before he lost his seat when the Davyhulme constituency was abolished ahead of that year’s general election. Winston Churchill’s own extensive personal papers — the private correspondence, wartime documents, and working materials that had passed down through Randolph to his son — were sold that year to the nation for £12.5 million, roughly twenty million dollars at the time, a transaction that drew sharp public criticism at the moment it was announced. The sale was structured, in the end, as an acquisition by the national Churchill Archives Centre at Cambridge rather than a private sale to some anonymous foreign buyer, which softened the criticism considerably — the papers remained in Britain, accessible to scholars, housed at an institution built specifically to preserve them. But the underlying transaction was still, stripped to its essentials, a grandson converting the family’s actual documentary inheritance into a substantial personal payment, a full circle this series has been tracing since Leonard Jerome first built and lost fortunes on Wall Street: the family’s core asset, whatever form it took in a given generation, eventually got monetized by whichever descendant happened to be holding it when the market — in this case, a national archive with public funds to spend — was ready to pay.

He left Parliament in 1997 and spent his remaining years as a public speaker and lecturer, drawing on his family heritage and his own journalism career to address audiences on defence policy, history, and international affairs, and remaining a vocal supporter of the 2003 Iraq War and a hardline critic of Islamic terrorism in his later public commentary — political positions that placed him, by the final years of his career, considerably to the right of the mainstream Conservative Party he had spent nearly three decades sitting inside without ever quite leading it anywhere. He died of cancer at his home in London on March 2, 2010, at sixty-nine, and was buried, like every Churchill this series has examined so far, at St. Martin’s Church in Bladon, Oxfordshire — the same small churchyard holding his grandfather, his great-grandfather, his father, and by then most of the family this series has spent eleven parts following across a century and a half.

Winston Jr.’s life, considered against the men this series began with, completes a specific and rather melancholy arc. Leonard Jerome built and rebuilt fortunes through sheer audacious risk-taking on a market with no rules. Winston senior converted every personal setback into a public comeback narrative on a stage genuinely large enough to make the recoveries mean something to the whole world. By the time the family’s political inheritance reached its third generation of Winstons, the stage itself had shrunk to a single marginal parliamentary seat in greater Manchester, the recoveries had shrunk to narrow by-election defeats and a brief, ultimately terminal frontbench posting, and the family’s most consequential remaining asset was not a political achievement at all, but a filing cabinet of paper that could, when the moment came, be sold.

Part XIII turns to his cousin, Nicholas Soames — Mary’s son, and, until his own retirement from Parliament, the last Churchill-adjacent presence in the House of Commons at all.
