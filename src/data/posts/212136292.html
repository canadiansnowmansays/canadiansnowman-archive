Ralph Kramden never got Alice to the moon. That was always the joke — the bus driver’s threat was a fantasy of escape he could never fund, never engineer, never actually attempt. Somewhere between his cramped Bensonhurst apartment and a patch of South Texas coastline seventy years later, the fantasy got a budget, a president, and a launch tower named after a movie monster. On July 24, 2026, it also got something Ralph never had: proof that the thing might actually come back in one piece.

I. The Scrub That Kept Scrubbing

Regular readers of this series will recall where we left off. Starship’s thirteenth flight test scrubbed at T-minus zero on July 16, four of the booster’s thirty-three Raptor engines failing to ignite in the final second of the countdown — the automated abort sequence catching what human reflexes never could. Musk announced two engines would be swapped before the next attempt. I chose to wait rather than write into an unresolved cliffhanger. It turned out to be a longer wait than either of us expected.

The second attempt, targeted for July 20, slipped to July 23. That attempt scrubbed too — this time with weather rather than hardware to blame, as Tropical Storm Bertha made landfall near the Texas coast. The date moved a final time, to July 24, the fifty-seventh anniversary of the Apollo 11 crew’s splashdown back on Earth. SpaceX, not normally a company inclined toward poetic scheduling, noted that its original July 16 target had itself fallen on the anniversary of Apollo 11’s launch. Sometimes the calendar hands you a metaphor you didn’t ask for.

Third time held. At 6:51 p.m. Eastern, all thirty-three engines lit. Booster 20 and Ship 40 climbed off Pad 2 into a Texas evening, and for the first time since May, a Starship V3 vehicle was actually going somewhere.

II. A Booster Lost, a Ship Found

The flight itself was a mixed report card, which is more or less the house style for this program. The booster’s ascent, stage separation, and boostback burn all went cleanly — the vehicle turned around and headed back toward a splashdown zone in the Gulf as planned. The landing burn is where it came apart: of the thirteen engines meant to relight for descent, only ten did, and five of those failed again within seconds of reignition. The booster hit the water harder than intended and was lost. That’s twice now that Booster 20’s line of vehicles has failed to survive its own landing attempt, and it’s a reminder that the “routine” in SpaceX’s routine reusability is still very much aspirational for the first stage of this particular design generation.

The upper stage told a different story. Ship 40 reentered the atmosphere, gathered the heat-shield performance data the mission was designed to collect, executed a deliberate banking maneuver meant to simulate the trajectory a future return-to-Starbase flight would fly, and then did something no Starship upper stage had done before: it splashed down in the Indian Ocean intact. Every prior Ship — including its immediate predecessor on Flight 12, which lost an engine mid-flight and toppled over on impact before exploding, the expected outcome for that stage of testing — had gone into the water and stayed there in pieces. Ship 40 didn’t.

What followed was somehow more compelling than the launch itself. SpaceX’s recovery team spent twenty-four days towing a hundred-and-seventy-one-foot stainless-steel spacecraft across roughly 1,200 miles of open ocean, through what the company itself described as “challenging conditions and increasingly rough seas.” At one point the effort looked likely to fail outright — Musk himself was less than optimistic about the tow succeeding. It didn’t fail. On August 18, Ship 40 arrived off the coast of Christmas Island, an Australian territory whose chief claim to fame, until that week, was an annual migration of forty million red land crabs to the sea. A vehicle designed to survive reentry from space had outlasted the ocean instead, and washed up beside one of the more improbable wildlife spectacles on the planet, waiting for an inspection team to come tell it what it had learned.

I want to sit with that image for a moment, because it’s a better metaphor than anything I could invent. SpaceX didn’t plan for an intact splashdown on this flight — the entire test architecture assumes the Ship dies at the end, that whatever data it gathered on the way down is worth more than the hardware itself. Ship 40 refused the assignment. It survived an outcome nobody engineered it to survive, then had to be dragged home by a boat, arriving not as a triumphant recovery but as an unplanned research windfall that happened to wash up near some crabs. There is something very true about American technological ambition in that image: it doesn’t fail cleanly and it doesn’t succeed cleanly either. It just keeps floating, longer than anyone expected, until somebody has to go deal with it.

III. The Number That Comes Next

The reason Ship 40’s survival matters goes beyond the poetry of the thing. Six days after the flight, Musk confirmed that the splashdown had been precise enough — positionally exact enough — that Mechazilla’s tower arms could have caught it, had this been a catch attempt rather than an ocean landing. That single data point cleared the engineering gate SpaceX had set before it would even consider trying the far harder version of that maneuver: catching the upper stage, not just the booster, out of the sky.

Flight 14, pairing Booster 21 with Ship 41, is now built around exactly that attempt. It has no precedent in spaceflight. SpaceX has caught the Super Heavy booster with the tower’s chopstick arms multiple times since October 2024 — booster catches are, relatively speaking, a solved problem now. Catching the Ship is categorically different. Every previous Starship test, all thirteen of them, deliberately flew a suborbital arc for exactly one reason: a suborbital vehicle that runs into trouble during its coast phase reenters over water regardless of what goes wrong. A vehicle that reaches orbit and then can’t deorbit on command doesn’t have that fallback. It’s a fifty-two-meter steel problem with no safe place to land.

Flight 14 removes that safety margin on purpose. Ship 41 has to fly all the way around the Earth and come back from the east, arriving at Starbase from an actual orbital trajectory, then thread itself between the tower’s arms with what the engineering literature politely calls “centimeter-level accuracy” — coming in at orbital-return velocity, having just survived the thermal load of reentry, with its heat shield needing to remain intact enough that the catch points themselves don’t fail under the strain. Musk told SpaceX’s first-ever quarterly earnings call, on August 4, that the company would attempt it “assuming we receive regulatory approval,” tentatively before the end of August. As of this writing, that regulatory approval — an FAA review of the environmental and safety implications of bringing an orbital-velocity vehicle back over populated coastline — is still pending, and no static fire date for Ship 41 has been confirmed. The booster, notably, will not be caught this time; Booster 21 splashes down in the Gulf as a known quantity while all the risk gets loaded onto the Ship.

It’s worth noting, too, what Flight 14 is meant to carry along the way: the first batch of upgraded V3 Starlink satellites, deployed en route to demonstrating that this vehicle can do useful work and not just heroic test-flight theater. NASA is watching closely as well — Starship still owes the agency several unchecked boxes before it can be certified to fly astronauts, and reaching orbit reliably is one of the largest of them, with Artemis III’s practice rendezvous and Artemis IV’s lunar landing both downstream of whatever Flight 14 proves or fails to prove.

IV. Reinvigorating America’s Leadership, By Presidential Memorandum

Which brings me to the news that actually sent me back to this thread. On August 20, four days before I sat down to write this, the Trump administration released an update to something called the National Space Transportation Policy — a document with a lineage stretching back to the Clinton administration in 1994 and last revised under Obama in 2013. This version, issued as National Security Presidential Memorandum 17, sets a headline target that deserves to be read slowly: more than 1,000 rocket launches and spacecraft reentries per year on American soil by 2030.

For context, the entire global orbital launch total for 2024 — every country, every company, every mission — came in well under half that number. The policy wants America alone quadrupling or better its current pace within four years, and it wants to do it by leaning harder into exactly the model this series has been tracking all year: commercial companies carrying the government’s ambitions on their books, at their own operational risk, with the state supplying the regulatory runway and the language of superiority.

Phil Larson, who advised the Obama White House on space policy and helped write the 2013 version of this same document, offered the mildest possible assessment when the New York Times asked him about it: “The new policy mostly updates it for today’s scale and maturity. I’d call it evolution rather than a major policy reset.” He’s not wrong on the substance — buying commercial launch services, streamlining regulation, encouraging private investment: these are decades-old bipartisan priorities, restated rather than reinvented. But Larson’s calm is a policy analyst’s calm, measuring the document against its predecessors. It undersells what the document’s own language is doing.

Keith Cowing, who has run NASA Watch since 1996 and has read more of these memoranda than almost anyone alive, noticed something Larson’s assessment didn’t capture: in the new policy’s text, the Secretary of War — the newly renamed successor to the Secretary of Defense — is mentioned fourteen times, ahead of NASA’s eleven mentions, and appears earlier in the document’s own sentences. Cowing’s read is blunt: the two agencies are “surgically attached” to one another in this policy. The memorandum’s own language backs him up. It calls access to space “a vital national and economic security interest,” ties America’s space transportation capabilities directly to “global economic” underpinning, and closes its stated purpose with the line that these policies “will secure America’s continued superiority in space.” This is not, notably, a document primarily concerned with science, exploration, or even commerce for its own sake. It is a document about primacy, written in the register of a nation that intends to win something.

Set that language against Flight 13. The policy imagines a 48-hour turnaround for high-priority launches and a tenfold increase in national launch cadence by the end of the decade. The vehicle SpaceX is currently building toward that future needed three attempts and eight days just to leave the ground once, then spent three and a half weeks being towed home by a boat because nobody was quite sure it would make it back on its own. I don’t say this to mock the engineering — Ship 40’s survival was a genuine and unplanned achievement, and Flight 14’s catch attempt is a real and serious next step. I say it because the gap between the policy’s rhetoric and the program’s current reality is the whole story here. Washington has decided what the future of American spaceflight should look like in volume and posture. The rocket meant to deliver that future is still working out how to land.

V. The Float Unwinds

Longtime readers will remember what Part IX of this series was actually about. Not the rocket — the float. SPCX priced its IPO at $135 on June 12, and within two trading sessions the stock had climbed more than 40 percent, briefly making SpaceX more valuable than Microsoft and Amazon combined market caps would suggest is reasonable for a company that still loses money launching things. I wrote at the time about an engineered scarcity: a free float of roughly four percent of shares outstanding, index-inclusion mechanics pulling passive money in behind it, a flywheel that fed on its own momentum precisely because there was almost nothing to buy. The thinnest float in history, I called it, and the number kept climbing on the strength of that thinness alone — a peak just above $225 a share, a valuation near $2.6 trillion, briefly the most valuable company on Earth.

I noted three doors that would eventually let more supply back onto the market: the overallotment, the earnings-driven unlocks, and the lockup expiry. All three have now opened, and the float thesis is running in reverse with the same mechanical logic that drove it up. An 911.5-million-share unlock landed on August 6 and, strangely, the stock rose six percent — cooling demand hadn’t yet caught up with the new supply. It has since. A smaller release in the days that followed broke a six-session streak of closing above the IPO price, and by Wednesday, August 19, SPCX had crossed back below $135 for the first time since its debut, a decline Reuters attributed to profit-taking, a reassessment of the valuation, and the unwinding of the same bullish positioning that built the mania in the first place. It closed near $131 the following day, down about four percent on the session — one down day in a stretch that has erased something like $900 billion of peak valuation. And there is more supply still coming: another 1.3 billion shares unlock in November alongside Q3 earnings, the full 180-day lockup expires in December, and Musk’s own 6.42 billion shares don’t become eligible to trade until June of 2027. The float that was once too thin to hold a rational price is now, gradually, becoming a normal one — and a normal float, it turns out, does not support a $225 share price built on the premise that almost nobody could sell.

It would be too cute to call this a metaphor for the rocket program and leave it there, so I won’t insist on it. But the shape is hard to miss. A vehicle that flew on scarcity — of engineering margin, of tested hardware, of successful landings — spent July finding out what happens when the thin thing gets tested by reality: two more scrubs, a lost booster, a ship that barely made it home under its own improbable luck. A stock that flew on scarcity — of tradable shares, of index float, of patience — spent August finding out the same thing: the unlocks arrive on schedule whether or not the underlying company has grown into the price, and gravity, mechanical and financial both, does not care how good the story was on the way up. Neither collapse is a verdict on SpaceX’s engineering or its long-term prospects. Both are simply what happens when a thin float — of shares or of margin — meets the schedule it was always going to meet.

VI. What the Crabs Know

There’s a version of this essay that treats the policy and the launch as two separate stories — one about Washington issuing memoranda, one about a company towing hardware across an ocean. I don’t think they are separate. The policy exists because companies like SpaceX have made a target like “1,000 launches a year” sound plausible rather than absurd; the company’s test program exists, in no small part, because a permissive and enthusiastic federal posture has made room for engines to fail publicly, boosters to be lost repeatedly, and a program to keep flying anyway. Each needs the other’s confidence to keep moving. Neither one, on its own evidence this month, has quite earned it yet.

Ralph Kramden threatened the moon and never left the room. Seventy years on, his descendants have a launch tower, a presidential memorandum, and a spacecraft currently floating off an island best known for its crabs, waiting to be inspected for what it can teach the next attempt. The thousand-launch future is a target on a piece of paper signed in Washington. The intact splashdown is a fact that happened in the ocean, slowly, over twenty-four uncertain days, while nobody was entirely sure it would hold. I know which one I trust more right now. But I also know which one gets to write the policy documents.

To the moon, Alice. We’re not there yet. But for the first time in a while, something came back in one piece to tell us about it.
