On election night, as the House slid into Democratic hands, Representative Robert Garcia of California put out a statement two words long: “Lawyer up.”

Garcia is set to chair the House Oversight Committee in January. Plenty of people read his statement as a threat aimed at the president, and plenty of Republicans will spend the next two months saying so. But that isn’t quite what it was. Donald Trump has lawyers. He has had lawyers his entire adult life, and the Supreme Court gave him the best one of all in 2024, when it granted him criminal immunity for official acts. The people who need to hear “lawyer up” are the ones around him: the aides, the family members, the contractors, the crypto partners, the ballroom donors, the people who said yes when something was asked of them and assumed nobody would ever ask them why.

Isaac Stanley-Becker reported in The Atlantic on 10 November that Trump-aligned defence attorneys are already expecting a boom. White-collar law has been in a slump for almost two years, thanks to an administration that went easy on corporate fraud, pardoned dozens of well-connected convicts and cut the enforcement staff that used to chase them. Congressional investigations change that. One person close to the White House told him the lawyers hoping to represent the Trump circle are “positively salivating.”

That tells you who Garcia was talking to. It also tells you the warning landed.

Every road runs through the Justice Department

The trouble is that a warning is only as good as what stands behind it, and on paper what stands behind a congressional subpoena is not much.

If a witness simply refuses to show up, Congress has three options. The first is criminal contempt: the House votes, and the matter goes to a U.S. attorney, who takes it to a grand jury. In Washington, that U.S. attorney is Jeanine Pirro. No Democrat expects her to indict a Trump aide for snubbing Robert Garcia. Steve Bannon and Peter Navarro both served time for defying the January 6 committee, but they were prosecuted by Joe Biden’s Justice Department, not Trump’s.

The second is civil contempt: Congress goes to court and asks a judge to order compliance. That works, eventually. The last time Democrats held the House, they spent years in litigation over Trump’s tax returns and finally got them in 2022, when the information was useful mainly to historians.

The third is inherent contempt, under which the House directs its sergeant-at-arms to arrest a defiant witness. It is constitutional, it is real, and it has not been used for almost a century. Picture the Capitol Police trying to take a sitting deputy chief of staff into custody in 2027 and you can see why.

Then Attorney General Todd Blanche went on CNN over the weekend and closed the circle. The Justice Department, he said, will represent administration officials who come under Democratic scrutiny. So the department that would refuse to prosecute contempt will also supply the lawyers defending against the subpoenas. The Blanche Files took that Sunday apart in Part XIII, “It’s Me.” The question here is what Democrats can do about it. Matt Viser reported in The Atlantic in October that Trump is widely expected to be more dismissive of oversight than any president in history, and that he may simply issue broad pardons to protect the people closest to him.

Put all that together and it is easy to conclude that “lawyer up” was bluster. I think that’s the wrong conclusion. But getting to the right one means looking hard at the limits of each obstacle, starting with Blanche’s.

The fine print in Blanche’s promise

The Justice Department represents executive-branch officials all the time. That is normal. What’s less well known is that its own regulations limit the practice. Department lawyers can’t defend an official whose conduct falls outside the scope of their employment, or where representing them wouldn’t be in the interest of the United States.

That limit matters because so much of what Democrats want to investigate is, by its nature, not government business. The crypto fortune the Trump family has built through World Liberty Financial isn’t an official act. Neither is a Russian oligarch paying for a party after Donald Trump Jr.’s wedding. Neither are alleged kickbacks on government contracts, payments for pardons or donations to a White House ballroom made by companies with business before the government. If the question is “what did you get, and from whom?” rather than “what policy did you carry out?”, the answer may well fall outside anyone’s job description.

That gives Democrats a design principle. Every subpoena should be written to force the department into a choice: either decline to represent a witness, which leaves that person on their own with private counsel and private bills, or claim that profiting from public office is within the scope of federal employment and in the interest of the United States. The first choice isolates the witness. The second is a sentence the department would have to put in writing, in a court filing, where judges and voters can read it.

Blanche’s promise was meant to reassure people inside the administration. Read closely, it should worry anyone whose exposure is personal rather than official, which is most of the people Democrats most want to talk to.

Going around the front door

The more useful lesson from 2019 is one Democrats seem to have learned: don’t spend two years knocking on the door of an administration that has decided not to answer.

Viser reported that Democratic leaders expect the administration to fight every request for documents, so they are planning to get the same information from third parties. That means subpoenaing contractors rather than the White House, and banks rather than the Treasury Department. A bank has no executive privilege. A defence contractor has no immunity. A crypto exchange has no interest in going to the Supreme Court on behalf of a lame-duck president’s family. Each of them has compliance officers, shareholders and regulators to think about, and each will run the numbers.

Senator Ron Wyden, expected to chair the Senate Finance Committee, plans to drop the courtesy under which the chair issues subpoenas only with the ranking member’s agreement. Subpoenas will go to a majority vote. That one procedural change removes the Republican veto that slowed Senate investigations for years.

Senator Richard Blumenthal, who is set to lead the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, has already moved. Stanley-Becker reported that last week Blumenthal sent letters to World Liberty Financial, tech companies and known donors to the White House ballroom, asking for records by early next year and making clear that subpoenas would follow if they didn’t comply. A Democratic aide summed up the theory: “Companies that went MAGA are going to be doing penance.”

Blumenthal’s own version was blunter. Trump “is now or will be within months a lame-duck president,” he said. “It’s a sinking ship, and the rats are gonna jump.” He doesn’t expect top officials to start cooperating. He expects some of the people below them to decide they don’t need the criminal risk or the civil exposure, and to leave. Whistleblowers, former employees and nervous business partners don’t need a subpoena enforced. They need a reason to talk and a place to do it.

The pardon problem, and its limits

The pardon is the obvious counter-move. A president who expects his aides to be investigated can pardon them first, and Trump has shown he’s willing to use the power freely.

But pardons are less of a shield than they look. A federal pardon covers federal crimes only. It does nothing about state law. And a witness who has been pardoned for a federal offence generally can no longer refuse to testify about it on Fifth Amendment grounds, because there is no longer any federal jeopardy to protect against. A blanket pardon can make a witness easier to question, not harder.

That’s where the election’s other results come in. Democrats did more than flip Congress. They won every statewide race in Texas, including attorney general, where Nathan Johnson takes office in January. Johnson told Stanley-Becker he plans to expand his office and hire experts in consumer protection, antitrust and public contracting. He insisted he isn’t on a mission to take down Elon Musk, whose companies are rooted in Texas and who spent more than $445 million this cycle trying to elect Republicans. But, Johnson said, “there’s not going to be a force field around him maintained by his loyal right-wing allies while I’m attorney general.”

That sentence is the state-level version of “lawyer up.” No presidential pardon reaches a Texas courtroom. Congressional investigators who find evidence of fraud in contracting or consumer deception have somewhere to send it now, and it isn’t Jeanine Pirro’s office.

Why this time has to be different

Adam Serwer argued in The Atlantic in February that the story of Trump’s impunity is bigger than Trump. Since Richard Nixon resigned, he wrote, powerful people in both parties have worked to make sure their leaders escape the consequences of what they do. Gerald Ford pardoned Nixon. George H. W. Bush pardoned almost everyone implicated in Iran-Contra. Barack Obama chose to “look forward” rather than prosecute the lawbreaking of the war on terror or the financial crisis. The Supreme Court narrowed bribery law until it barely works, and then gave the president immunity for official acts.

Serwer’s point was that other democracies don’t do this. Brazil convicted Jair Bolsonaro for trying to overturn an election. South Korea convicted Yoon Suk Yeol for his attempt to seize power. In America, he wrote, elite impunity has become a national project.

That’s the real stake in Garcia’s two words. The question isn’t whether one more set of hearings embarrasses one more administration. It is whether the American system can still hold anybody powerful to account, or whether the honest answer, after fifty years of pardons and immunity and looking forward, is no.

What accountability can mean in 2027

It’s worth being clear about what Congress can and can’t do. Representative Yassamin Ansari of Arizona told Viser that voters “want to see people in jail.” Congress can’t put anyone in jail. When Stanley-Becker asked Garcia whether he could imagine people ending up there through his committee’s work, Garcia demurred and said it would be up to the Justice Department or the courts. “But,” he added, “if you have broken the law, there should be accountability.”

Accountability in 2027 will mostly mean a record: documents, testimony, bank records, sworn statements and referrals. That sounds modest, but it isn’t. A record is what turns a vague public sense that something is wrong into a specific account of what happened, who did it and who paid. The Watergate hearings didn’t convict anyone. They made the convictions possible.

A record also lasts. The general federal statute of limitations is five years. Contempt of Congress committed in 2027 could still be prosecuted after January 2029, by a Justice Department that does not answer to Donald Trump. Every aide weighing whether to ignore a subpoena has to reckon with that. Defying Congress might be safe under Blanche. It is a much bigger bet that it will be safe under whoever succeeds him.

So “lawyer up” isn’t really about next month. It’s about the next five years. It tells everyone in Trump’s orbit that the free pass ends with his term, that the routes around the Justice Department are open, that state attorneys general now have the jurisdiction and the will, and that a written record will be waiting for whoever runs the department next.

Democrats will have to resist the temptation Ansari described and Senator Bernie Sanders warned against, of letting investigations crowd out an economic agenda. Garcia himself has framed the work as being about the cost of living: stopping, as he put it, the Trumps “from stealing from you and giving to themselves.” That’s the right frame. But they will also have to resist the old instinct Serwer described, the one that says it’s time to heal and look forward. That instinct has had fifty years to prove itself, and the country’s political system has never been more openly for sale.

Two words on election night. Now comes the hard part of making them mean something.
