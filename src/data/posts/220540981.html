Chagrin is an underrated word. It means the particular discomfort of being caught: not guilt, which is private, but the public flush of having been seen. In most governments chagrin does a great deal of quiet work. An official caught out feels it, the boss notices, and either the official reforms or the official departs. It is the mechanism by which embarrassment becomes accountability.

In Part I we watched that mechanism work, briskly, on five women. Kristi Noem was gone two days after a bad hearing. Kimberly Guilfoyle was gone six days after a bad story. Pam Bondi, Lori Chavez-DeRemer and Tulsi Gabbard went in the eleven weeks between March 5 and May 22, 2026, each replaced by a man.

This essay is about the men for whom the mechanism never engaged. I have chosen three, because they are the three the Democrats in Congress kept naming, and because their cases line up uncomfortably well against the women’s. In each, I set out what happened, what would ordinarily follow, and what actually did.

Hegseth: the war plans in the group chat

In March 2025, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth shared operational details of imminent American strikes on Houthi targets in Yemen in a Signal group chat. The chat had been set up by national security adviser Mike Waltz, who had inadvertently added Jeffrey Goldberg, the editor of the Atlantic. Goldberg read the attack timings before the bombs fell.

That alone would end most defence ministers’ careers in most democracies. It is the kind of breach that, done by a junior officer, produces a court-martial.

What happened next was an inspector general’s investigation that ran for more than eight months. As NPR reported on December 3, 2025, the report concluded that Hegseth had risked compromising sensitive information that could have endangered American forces. He had used his personal phone for official business, in breach of departmental rules. He had declined to be interviewed, responding only in writing. He had handed over only a few of his messages. He told investigators he regarded the inquiry as political.

The expected reaction to findings like that is resignation, or at least contrition. The actual reaction came from the Pentagon’s chief spokesman, Sean Parnell, who called the report a “TOTAL exoneration.” Hegseth remained in office. He has since renamed his department the Department of War, overseen a war with Iran, presided over a Pentagon press blackout, and on September 30, 2026 delivered his “State of the Force” address at Quantico. He has not, in the year and a half and more since the chat became public, been seen to blush.

Someone did pay for that chat. It was Waltz, who added the journalist. Trump removed him as national security adviser in May 2025, and in the same breath nominated him as ambassador to the United Nations. That was the post Trump had already promised to Elise Stefanik, whose nomination he had withdrawn two months earlier for reasons of House arithmetic. The Senate confirmed Waltz 47 to 43 in September 2025, as the Washington Free Beacon reported.

Consider the sequence once more, slowly. A man commits a security blunder. He is punished by being given the job a woman was promised. The woman, having done nothing wrong at all, ends up out of Congress and out of government. If a novelist wrote that, an editor would send it back as too on the nose.

Set Hegseth beside Noem. She was removed after contradicting the president about an advertising budget. He kept his job after an inspector general found he had risked American lives. Hers was a political embarrassment. His was an operational one, with troops on the other end.

Patel: the drinking, the jet and the SWAT team

Kash Patel’s file is longer, and I have covered it at length in its own series. The short version will do here.

In November 2025, the Boston Globe, running New York Times reporting, described how the FBI director had assigned SWAT-qualified agents to protect his girlfriend, the country singer Alexis Wilkins. They guarded her at the National Rifle Association convention in Atlanta and on several occasions in Nashville. When the Atlanta team stood down after judging her safe, Patel reportedly tore into its commander. The same reporting documented his use of a government Gulfstream for personal travel, including a golf trip to Scotland. A former FBI agent who once led high-risk teams said it showed Patel’s “lack of leadership experience, judgment and humility.”

Then, in April 2026, the Atlantic published an investigation built on more than twenty anonymous sources, alleging episodes of excessive drinking and unexplained absences. It described meetings postponed and aides unable to rouse him. Patel denied it and sued the magazine for $250 million. At a Senate appropriations hearing on May 16, 2026, he agreed to an alcohol test. At the Senate Judiciary Committee on September 15, it emerged that no one had followed up.

Now set Patel beside Chavez-DeRemer, because this is the comparison that ought to stop a reader cold.

Chavez-DeRemer faced allegations that she drank on the job. Patel faced allegations that he drank on the job. Chavez-DeRemer faced allegations that she used official trips for personal purposes. Patel faced allegations that he used the bureau’s jet for personal purposes. She faced an inspector general. He faced congressional hearings and a sustained run of investigative journalism. Both denied everything.

She was gone on April 20, 2026, announced by a spokesman in a two-line statement. He is still the director of the Federal Bureau of Investigation.

To be fair to the record, the Chavez-DeRemer file contained something Patel’s did not: allegations involving messages from her and her family to young staff, which are of a different and more serious order. I do not want to flatten that. But strip it out, and you are left with two officials accused of the same two kinds of misconduct. One received the Trump reflex. The other, when reports surfaced in late 2025 that Trump was tiring of the headlines, received a White House photo opportunity in the Oval Office to show the press he still had the president’s confidence.

Lutnick: lunch on the island

The third case is Howard Lutnick, the Commerce Secretary.

For years Lutnick told the story that he and his wife had cut off Jeffrey Epstein around 2005, after finding him disgusting. On January 30, 2026, the Justice Department released its largest tranche of Epstein files. As Reuters reported via CTV News, among them were emails showing that Lutnick, his family and another family had arranged to visit Epstein on Little Saint James for lunch on December 23, 2012. That was seven years after the supposed break. The day after the visit, an Epstein assistant forwarded Lutnick a note from Epstein that read, in part, “Nice seeing you.”

Remember what ended Pam Bondi. Two months after those emails surfaced, she was fired, in large part over her handling of the Epstein files. The attorney general who mishandled the files lost her job. The cabinet secretary whose name was in the files, and whose account of his relationship with Epstein the files contradicted, kept his.

In April, Representative Madeleine Dean put this to Lutnick directly at a House hearing. She asked whether the president was concerned about his relationship with Epstein. He replied that he would not discuss his conversations with the president. Dean pointed out that Trump had just fired three cabinet secretaries, all of them women, and said that if he cared at all about holding Epstein’s enablers to account, Lutnick would be next. He was not next. As of this writing, he has not been anything.

The ledger

Put the cases side by side and the pattern is hard to argue away.

The table needs one more row, and it is the most telling. On November 6, 2026, the president removed his chief of staff after an election that voters treated as a verdict on his war, his tariffs and his deportation campaign. The chief of staff was the woman in the row above. The war was run by Hegseth, who greeted her replacement on social media with "Patriots in control." The tariffs were Lutnick's brief. The deportations were Miller's, and Miller got her job. As of that evening, Hegseth, Patel and Lutnick were all still in office.

The Democrats saw it early. After Bondi and Noem had gone, Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez was asked whom the president should fire next. She replied with a question: “Is he out of women?” Representative Jasmine Crockett, whom Trump mocks with some regularity, said the same thing less economically. Incompetent women, she wrote, get thrown under the bus a great deal faster than incompetent men.

Partisans say things, and the line between a sharp observation and a talking point is not always clear. But here the partisans and the Brookings researcher are counting the same heads, and arriving at the same number.

Why the mechanism fails for men

So why does chagrin work on women in this administration and not on men?

I can offer three explanations, in rising order of discomfort.

The first is that the men are simply better at shamelessness. Hegseth answered the inspector general by calling him political. Patel answered the Atlantic with a quarter-billion-dollar lawsuit. Lutnick answered Representative Dean by declining to discuss it. Shamelessness is a survival skill in Trump’s Washington, and it is the house style. The women, by contrast, tended to fight their battles on the president’s terms and lose them. Noem tried to share credit for the ads with him. Bondi tried to deliver the prosecutions he wanted. That is a real difference in behaviour. But it raises the question of why the same shamelessness, when it does appear in a woman, is not rewarded. Guilfoyle denied everything too. She lasted six days.

The second explanation is functional. Trump needs Hegseth at War, Patel at the FBI and Lutnick at Commerce, the argument runs, and the women’s departments were more easily handed to deputies. It does not survive contact with the facts. Justice is hardly a minor department. Homeland Security runs the president’s signature policy. The intelligence directorate was central to a war. These were not peripheral posts.

The third explanation is the one I started with, before I had done any counting. It is that Trump extends to men a presumption of usefulness that he does not extend to women. A man in trouble is someone to be defended, or reassigned, or photographed with in the Oval Office. A woman in trouble is a liability to be cut loose. The man’s embarrassment becomes the president’s fight. The woman’s embarrassment remains hers alone.

The evidence in this essay does not prove that third explanation. Evidence of this kind rarely proves motive. What it does is make the first two explanations very hard to sustain. And when the innocent explanations fail one after another, the remaining one does not need proving so much as it needs facing.
