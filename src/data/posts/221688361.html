Canadians who were adults on the night of October 30, 1995 tend to remember where they were, in the way an earlier generation remembered where they heard about Kennedy. Much of English Canada watched from its living rooms, having spent most of the autumn assuming the matter would sort itself out. By the end of the evening it had, by a margin smaller than a full house at Montreal’s Olympic Stadium. The No side won 50.58 per cent of the vote. The country survived by 54,288 ballots.

This essay is about how Canada got from a comfortable win in 1980 to that coin toss fifteen years later. It is a story of promises made in arenas, deals made in kitchens, one eagle feather, and a premier who said too much in defeat. It is also, for English Canada, a story of almost losing the country while looking the other way.

The First Ask

Part II ended with a question: by the spring of 1980, what would independence add to what nationalism had already won? René Lévesque’s answer was cautious to the point of being apologetic. The question on the ballot of May 20, 1980 did not ask Quebecers to leave. It asked for a mandate to negotiate sovereignty-association, a new agreement with the rest of Canada “based on the equality of nations,” and promised that any change in political status would go back to the people in a second referendum. It was a referendum on whether to hold talks about whether to hold another referendum.

The campaign turned on two things. The first was an own goal. In March, the PQ minister Lise Payette mocked the No side by likening federalist women to “Yvette,” the obedient little girl of a school reader, and then applied the label to Claude Ryan’s wife. The reply was a brunch of some 1,700 women in Quebec City on March 30, and then, on April 7, a rally of close to 15,000 “Yvettes” at the Montreal Forum.

The second was Pierre Trudeau. On May 14, at the Paul Sauvé Arena in Montreal, six days before the vote, Trudeau promised that a No would not be read as a vote for the status quo. He and his Quebec MPs, he said, were putting their seats on the line to deliver change: a renewed federalism and a reformed constitution. Quebecers would spend the next decade arguing about what exactly had been pledged.

The result, as The Canadian Encyclopedia‘s “Québec Referendum (1980)” by R. Hudon, updated by Dominique Millette and Maude-Emmanuelle Lambert, records, was 59.56 per cent No and 40.44 per cent Yes, on a turnout of 85.61 per cent. Roughly half of francophones voted Yes. That evening, at the same Paul Sauvé Arena, Lévesque told a weeping crowd: “À la prochaine fois.” Until next time.

English Canada heard 60-40 and concluded the matter was closed. Quebec heard “until next time,” and so, as it turned out, did history.

The Night of the Long Knives

Trudeau kept his promise, in his fashion. He renewed the constitution. He did not renew it in the way Quebec nationalists, or even many Quebec federalists, had imagined.

The first ministers met in Ottawa beginning November 2, 1981 to settle patriation: bringing the constitution home from Westminster with an amending formula and a Charter of Rights and Freedoms. Lévesque had joined a “Gang of Eight” provinces opposed to Trudeau’s plan. The gang did not hold. On the night of November 4, the federal justice minister, Jean Chrétien, worked out a compromise with Saskatchewan’s attorney general, Roy Romanow, and Ontario’s, Roy McMurtry, in a kitchen at the Government Conference Centre, then sold it through the night to the other premiers, as The Canadian Encyclopedia‘s “Patriation of the Constitution” by Robert Sheppard, updated by Stephen Azzi and others, describes. The package combined the provinces’ preferred amending formula with a “notwithstanding” clause allowing legislatures to override parts of the Charter.

Lévesque was not told. He learned of the deal the next morning. In English Canada it became “the Kitchen Accord.” In Quebec it became la nuit des longs couteaux, the night of the long knives, and the name has never worn off.

Queen Elizabeth II proclaimed the Constitution Act, 1982 on Parliament Hill on April 17, 1982. Quebec’s government refused to sign it. On December 6, 1982 the Supreme Court ruled that Quebec had never had a veto over constitutional change in the first place. Every Quebec government since, federalist or sovereigntist, has declined to endorse the 1982 constitution, which governs Quebec perfectly well without its consent.

Meech Lake

The next attempt to fix it came from a Quebecer who had promised to bring the province back “with honour and enthusiasm.” Brian Mulroney and the ten premiers met at Willson House on Meech Lake, in the Gatineau Hills, on April 30, 1987, and agreed in a single day to five conditions set out by Quebec’s Liberal premier, Robert Bourassa: recognition of Quebec as a distinct society, a veto over constitutional amendments, a role in Supreme Court appointments, limits on federal spending power, and more control over immigration. The final text was signed on June 3, 1987, after an all-night session in Ottawa. Quebec’s National Assembly ratified it on June 23, 1987, which started a three-year clock. Every legislature had until June 23, 1990 to agree.

Trudeau, retired, savaged the deal in print in May 1987. Governments changed in New Brunswick, Manitoba and Newfoundland. In December 1988 Bourassa used the notwithstanding clause to preserve French-only outdoor signs after a Supreme Court ruling, and English Canada’s enthusiasm for distinct society cooled to freezing. Newfoundland’s new premier, Clyde Wells, a constitutional lawyer of considerable rectitude, argued that the accord created two classes of province and would make future reform impossible; in April 1990 his legislature revoked its earlier approval.

Then came Elijah Harper. The Cree member for Rupertsland in the Manitoba legislature, holding an eagle feather, beginning on June 12, 1990 repeatedly withheld the unanimous consent Manitoba needed to rush the accord through without the public hearings its own rules required. His objection was simple: a constitutional deal recognizing one founding people as distinct had been negotiated by eleven white men without a word about the people who were here first. Part VI will come back to him. In Newfoundland, Wells then cancelled the planned free vote, and the deadline passed on June 23, 1990, as The Canadian Encyclopedia‘s “Meech Lake Accord” entry recounts. Eight provinces holding most of the country’s population had said yes. Unanimity is a cruel arithmetic.

In Quebec, the death of Meech was received as a rejection, not of a legal text but of Quebec itself. Polls put support for sovereignty above 60 per cent.

Bouchard and the Bloc

One man had already jumped. Lucien Bouchard, Mulroney’s old law-school friend and environment minister, quit cabinet on May 21, 1990 over a parliamentary committee report proposing changes to Meech that he regarded as a betrayal, according to The Canadian Encyclopedia‘s “Lucien Bouchard” biography. Mulroney always insisted he had been fired. Within weeks Bouchard had gathered a handful of Tory and Liberal MPs from Quebec into a new caucus. The Bloc Québécois was formally founded on June 15, 1991, at Sorel-Tracy, with Bouchard as leader.

The Bloc was the oddest creature Parliament had ever housed: a federal party whose purpose was to leave the federation, running candidates only in Quebec. In the federal election of October 25, 1993, which reduced Kim Campbell’s Progressive Conservatives to two seats, the Bloc took 54 of Quebec’s 75 seats with 49.3 per cent of the provincial vote. With Preston Manning’s Reform Party two seats behind, at 52, the Bloc became Her Majesty’s Loyal Opposition, as The Canadian Encyclopedia‘s “Bloc Québécois” entry notes.

Charlottetown, or Everybody Says No

In between came one more try. The Charlottetown Accord, agreed on August 28, 1992, was Meech plus everything English Canada and the West and Indigenous leaders had complained Meech lacked: distinct society, yes, but also a Canada clause, an elected and “equal” Senate, a guaranteed quarter of Commons seats for Quebec, and recognition of an inherent right of Indigenous self-government. It was put to a national referendum on October 26, 1992, and Quebec held its own vote the same day.

It lost almost everywhere, for opposite reasons. Western voters thought it gave Quebec too much. Quebec voters, 56.7 per cent of them, thought it gave Quebec too little. Nationally about 54 per cent voted No, and six provinces rejected it, according to The Canadian Encyclopedia‘s “Charlottetown Accord” by Gerald L. Gall.

The lesson most politicians took was to stop trying. Constitutional reform became the subject no federal leader would touch. The lesson Quebec’s sovereigntists took was that Canada had been asked twice and had twice declined. It was time to ask Quebec again.

Parizeau’s Referendum

Jacques Parizeau was everything Lévesque was not: an economist trained at the London School of Economics, a three-piece suit, a man who wanted independence plainly and said so. He led the PQ back to power on September 12, 1994 and became premier on September 26, promising a referendum within a year. He was not interested in sovereignty-association. To a group of foreign diplomats he compared a Yes vote to a lobster trap: once in, Quebecers could not get out.

But a plain question would lose, and he knew it. The question finally put asked whether Quebec should become sovereign “after having made a formal offer to Canada for a new economic and political partnership,” under a tripartite agreement signed on June 12, 1995 by Parizeau, Bouchard and Mario Dumont of the Action démocratique, as The Canadian Encyclopedia‘s “Québec Referendum (1995)” sets out. The hyphen in “sovereignty-association” had been replaced by a comma and a partnership, but it was still there.

For the first weeks the No side, led in Quebec by provincial Liberal leader Daniel Johnson and quietly confident in Ottawa, held a lead. Then, on October 7, Parizeau named Bouchard the chief negotiator of the partnership that would follow a Yes, which in practice handed him the campaign.

Bouchard had lost his left leg to necrotizing fasciitis in December 1994 and nearly died. He returned to public life on a cane, and in Quebec the effect was something close to a secular resurrection. He was warmer than Parizeau, and he talked about partnership rather than rupture. Within days the polls moved, and before long, as the Wikipedia account “1995 Quebec referendum” summarizes, surveys showed the Yes side ahead.

English Canada Wakes Up

For most of the campaign, the rest of the country did what it had done since 1980: it assumed. Quebecers would grumble, vote No, and carry on. It was the purest form of the Tim Hortons shrug, “Again? Must be Tuesday,” the country has ever produced, and it very nearly cost the country.

The panic, when it came, came late. On October 25, Chrétien went on national television to plead with Quebecers and to promise recognition of Quebec as a distinct society and a veto over future constitutional change. Bouchard took the airwaves the same night.

On Friday, October 27, three days before the vote, tens of thousands of people filled Place du Canada in downtown Montreal for what became known as the Unity Rally. Canadian Airlines and Air Canada, Via Rail and bus companies cut fares, some to a fraction of normal, and people arrived from every province with flags and homemade signs. Estimates ranged from about 35,000, the sovereigntists' figure, to 150,000, the organizers', as Barry Came reported in Maclean's, "Crusade for Canada," November 6, 1995; a later CBC analysis of footage suggested around 60,000, according to the Wikipedia article "Unity Rally". Quebec's chief electoral officer questioned whether the corporate discounts broke Quebec's spending limits; the courts eventually concluded that rules made in Quebec did not reach sales made outside it.

It was also a little like rushing to the hospital with flowers for a spouse ignored for fifteen years. Some Quebecers were moved by it, some irritated, and nobody could prove what it did to the count.

Monday Night

On October 30, 1995, 93.52 per cent of registered Quebecers voted, one of the highest turnouts in any Canadian vote. The No side took 2,362,648 votes, or 50.58 per cent. The Yes side took 2,308,360, or 49.42 per cent. Roughly six in ten francophones had voted Yes; non-francophones voted No by overwhelming margins. The James Bay Cree and the Inuit of Nunavik, in their own referendums held days earlier, had voted roughly 96 per cent to stay in Canada, a fact that will matter a great deal in Part VI.

That night, Parizeau, visibly bitter, told his supporters that they had been beaten, “by money and the ethnic vote.” Even within the Yes camp, which had worked for years to present Quebec sovereignty as civic rather than ethnic, the reaction was mortification. The next day, October 31, he announced his resignation, as CBC News recalled in “Jacques Parizeau, former PQ premier, remembered as ‘formidable opponent’”. Bouchard succeeded him as premier on January 29, 1996.

There was also the matter of the spoiled ballots. In all, 86,501 ballots were rejected, more than the margin of victory. In three heavily federalist Montreal-area ridings the rejection rates were conspicuously high: about 12 per cent in Chomedey, 5.5 per cent in Marguerite-Bourgeoys and 3.6 per cent in Laurier-Dorion. Federalists alleged that PQ-appointed scrutineers had disqualified No ballots on the flimsiest grounds. An inquiry under Chief Justice Alan B. Gold of the Quebec Superior Court found irregularities but no organized fraud; deputy returning officers who were charged were acquitted. There was another scandal from the other direction, too, over federal money channelled through a group called Option Canada. Neither side came out of 1995 with entirely clean hands.

What Fifty Point Five Eight Bought

Here is the uncomfortable sum. In 1980 the country won by nearly twenty points and treated it as a verdict. In 1995 it won by barely more than one point and, after a few weeks of shock, treated that as a verdict too.

Yet the leverage thesis of this series holds even here. Quebec lost two referendums and still collected. After 1995 the House of Commons passed a motion recognizing Quebec as a distinct society, and Ottawa lent its own constitutional veto to Quebec and the other regions by statute. The near-death experience also persuaded Ottawa that the rules of leaving could no longer be left to whoever wrote the next referendum question. That is the subject of Part IV.

The shrug was defensible in 1980. In 1995 it was very nearly fatal, and the difference was 54,288 votes and a rally the country organized three days before the deadline. A nation that survives by that margin has not proven its wisdom. It has proven its luck.
