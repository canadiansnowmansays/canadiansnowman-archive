Lamar Alexander’s new memoir, The Education of a Senator (From JFK to Trump). The man who helped end Trump’s first impeachment trial now delivers the verdict the Senate wouldn’t.

Lamar Alexander played a crucial role in short-circuiting the first impeachment trial of Donald Trump. As an influential Republican senator from Tennessee, he opposed calling witnesses and argued that the president’s attempts to pressure Ukraine did not meet the test for removal from office. His vote helped end the trial early. Trump was acquitted.

Alexander left the Senate in January 2021 — weeks before the second impeachment trial. He did not have a vote. He has said it would be “self-righteous and disingenuous” to declare how he would have voted on a jury he did not sit on.

But in his new memoir, The Education of a Senator, published this month, Alexander makes clear what he believes the evidence showed.

“If those actions do not constitute a ‘high crime or misdemeanor,’” he writes, referring to Trump’s exhortation of the crowd that stormed the Capitol on January 6, 2021, “I do not know what does.”

The man who helped end the first trial now says the second one had the goods.

What He Said — and When He Said It

The timing matters. Alexander is eighty-five years old. He has been out of office for five years. He is a former governor, a former cabinet secretary, a two-time presidential candidate, and one of the most experienced Republican legislators of his generation. He chaired the health committee, partnered with Barack Obama on bipartisan legislation, and spent eighteen years navigating the institution he now accuses of failing.

He is saying this from retirement. He is saying it in a book. He is not saying it on the Senate floor, where it might have cost him something. He is not saying it in a primary campaign, where it might have changed something.

This is the Cassidy problem in reverse. Cassidy said too little too late while still in office, and it destroyed him anyway. Alexander is saying everything clearly — from a position where it costs him nothing.

The pattern is now unmistakable across the Loyalty Trials and this series alike: the honest assessment of Trump’s conduct arrives only after the senator is beyond the electorate’s reach. Romney said it while declining to run again. Burr said it and retired. Sasse said it and left for a university presidency. Toomey said it and went to private equity. Alexander is saying it from the pages of a memoir.

The Senate apparently has a clear-eyed view of what happened on January 6. It just can’t say so while the voters are listening.

Article One

Alexander’s memoir does not stop at the impeachment. He renders a verdict on his former colleagues’ conduct during Trump’s second term — and it is scathing by his deliberately understated standards.

“To me, the most disappointing difference between the first and second Trump terms was not what Trump did, but what the Senate majority did not do. Republican senators rarely checked abuse of presidential authority.”

From Alexander — a die-hard institutionalist, a loyal Republican, a man who spent decades chairing committees and building bipartisan coalitions — this amounts to a formal indictment of the chamber he served in.

His prescription is characteristically Alexander: methodical, procedural, and almost comically earnest. He says he would organise a bipartisan breakfast meeting every week and have senators read Article One of the Constitution to each other. Article One — the one that says Congress shall be in charge of spending, taxes, tariffs, and the declaration of war. The one that has been systematically ignored as the executive branch has expanded its authority and the Senate has declined to resist.

“The least a Senate can do is preserve its constitutional prerogatives,” he said in a recent interview.

It is the mildest possible version of the most urgent possible argument. The Senate has surrendered its core constitutional functions — the power of the purse, the power to declare war, the power to provide advice and consent that is actually advice and not rubber-stamping — and Alexander’s solution is a breakfast club and a reading assignment.

He is not wrong about the diagnosis. The prescription feels like bringing a reading group to a gunfight. But then, the Senate has not even managed the reading group.

The 21st Century Cures Act

There is a detail in Alexander’s memoir that connects his story directly to the Cassidy story — and to the broader vaccine crisis that Part XXVIII of this series documented in Louisiana.

In December 2016, during the lame-duck session after Trump’s election, Alexander shepherded the 21st Century Cures Act through Congress. It was a bipartisan, multibillion-dollar infusion of federal money for health research. Obama signed it into law and, according to the book, told Alexander: “If it had been just you and me these eight years, everything would have been fine.”

Four years later, that legislation helped provide the means and the money to develop the COVID vaccines — under Trump’s administration. Operation Warp Speed was built on the research infrastructure Alexander’s bill funded.

Trump has never taken credit for the vaccines. His party has turned against them. His health secretary, Robert F. Kennedy Jr., is actively undermining public confidence in immunisation. Kennedy’s advisory panel voted to remove the recommendation for the hepatitis B vaccine at birth — the vaccine that defined Bill Cassidy’s medical career. Louisiana’s vaccination rates are plummeting. Two infants died of whooping cough.

Alexander says he is puzzled that Trump “would not take satisfaction and credit for such a great accomplishment.”

He should not be puzzled. The vaccines work. The anti-vaccine politics work better. The party has made its choice, and the choice is not the one that saves lives. It is the one that wins primaries.

The View From Retirement

Every instalment of this series asks the same question: where is democracy fighting back?

In Lamar Alexander’s memoir, democracy fights back from the safest possible position — a book written by a man who cannot be primaried, cannot be censured, and cannot lose anything except the regard of people who stopped regarding him the moment he left office.

This is not courage. It is testimony. There is a difference, and the difference matters.

But testimony has value even when it arrives late. Alexander is now on the record. The man who ended the first impeachment trial has declared that the second one met the standard. The man who spent eighteen years in the Senate has declared that his former colleagues have failed to defend their constitutional authority. The man whose legislation funded the vaccines has declared his confusion that his own party has turned against them.

He has also, in his understated way, told every sitting Republican senator what they already know and will not say: “Your oath is not to Trump. Your oath is to the Constitution. You swore that on your first day as a senator. You should follow it.”

Bill Cassidy followed his oath on February 13, 2021. On May 16, 2026, 73 percent of Louisiana Republicans told him it didn’t matter. Lamar Alexander is making the same argument Cassidy made — from retirement, where the voters can’t reach him.

On Tuesday, Thomas Massie found out whether Kentucky’s 4th District agreed. It did not. Brad Raffensperger found out whether Georgia Republicans valued the man who defended their vote count. They did not. Shane Massey cast his vote in South Carolina knowing the price. The honest men keep losing. The honest argument keeps arriving from retirement.

The question is no longer whether anyone will make it while it can still cost them something. A few have. The question is what it costs them when they do.
