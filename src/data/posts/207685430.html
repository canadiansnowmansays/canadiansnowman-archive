Robert F. Kennedy Jr. did not resign in July, contrary to a rumor that consumed a corner of the internet for about seventy-two hours. He did, however, spend the same general period freezing over a billion dollars in Medicaid funding to two states run by his political opponents, while an agency two rungs beneath him fumbled a lettuce recall so badly that it issued, and then un-issued, a false positive for a parasite. Taken separately, these are three stories. Taken together, they are a portrait of a department where the secretary’s attention goes exactly where it is politically useful, and nowhere else.

The Rumor That Wouldn’t Die, Briefly

On June 12, 2026, Dr. Robert Malone — the mRNA researcher and former vice chair of Kennedy’s own reconstituted ACIP, appointed by Kennedy himself the previous June — posted on social media that he had it “from senior USG employees” that Kennedy would be leaving HHS sometime after the Fourth of July, with Dr. Mehmet Oz reportedly tapped to head a transition. HHS’s rapid-response account called it “utterly false” within hours. Malone, undeterred, posted again that he was “not ready to concede” the rumor was wrong, citing a pattern he didn’t fully specify.

The following Wednesday, on a MAHA Action Zoom call, Kennedy’s wife, the actress Cheryl Hines, delivered what amounted to the administration’s real statement on the matter: “You don’t need to worry about Bobby. He is stronger than ever and he will continue the fight.” It is a measure of how thoroughly Kennedy has fused his cabinet role with his movement’s mythology that the definitive denial of his resignation came not from a press secretary but from his spouse, at a rally for supporters, phrased as reassurance to the faithful rather than an answer to the press.

Whether the rumor had any substance is beside the point. What it revealed is that a sitting cabinet secretary’s job security is, at this stage, a matter of speculation stable enough to make national news and shaky enough that his own department felt obligated to deny it twice. Malone’s original post claimed the transition had been “reportedly widely and openly discussed” at the Congressional baseball game the week before — the kind of detail that is either a very specific fabrication or a sign that people inside the tent have started talking about the exits out loud. Either possibility is worse for Kennedy than a straightforward denial would suggest. This is not what confidence in one’s position usually looks like.

A Billion Dollars, Deferred

Kennedy did not need to leave in July to make news. On July 21, 2026, HHS announced it was deferring more than a billion dollars in Medicaid payments to California and Minnesota — $867.5 million from California, $199 million from Minnesota — citing high-risk claims flagged in what the department is calling a fraud crackdown. At the news conference announcing the freeze, Kennedy dropped the department’s usual careful hedging and went personal, telling reporters the two states had “violated the social contract that makes this country strong” and naming Governors Gavin Newsom and Tim Walz directly, saying all either man had to do was hand over “basic documentation.” CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz was, if anything, blunter: “CMS is done trying to chase down stolen and misused funds after they’ve already left the building.” Oz also cited claims billed for at least one deceased patient and raised questions about the immigration status of some California recipients, without offering supporting evidence for either claim at the podium.

Both governors’ offices pushed back hard and immediately. Newsom’s office called the announcement “the same recycled political stunt we’ve seen before,” while Walz has separately described an earlier version of this same freeze as a “campaign of retribution.” That earlier version matters: this is not the administration’s first pass at Minnesota’s Medicaid funding. In February, Vice President JD Vance and Oz froze $259.5 million from the state using nearly identical language about fraud and accountability. July’s billion-dollar action isn’t a new tactic — it’s the same tactic, run twice on the same state and now extended to a second one, with the number large enough to make headlines on its own. Kennedy used the announcement to unveil a further expansion of HHS’s “exclusion authority,” which lets the department permanently bar providers it suspects of fraud from Medicare and Medicaid altogether — a broadening of his own discretion delivered in the same breath as an accusation he declined to substantiate.

It is a sentence that would sound perfectly reasonable coming from an administration that had applied the same scrutiny evenly. It arrives instead as the latest chapter in a fraud crackdown running almost exclusively against states led by Democratic governors. Meanwhile the secretary’s department has, by its own admission, canceled task force meetings, allowed a preventive-care panel to shrink by half, and left roughly a third of its own leadership positions filled by people with the word “acting” in their titles. The rigor is selective. The scrutiny travels in one direction.

The Lettuce Problem

And then there is the outbreak that has nothing directly to do with Kennedy and everything to do with the agencies that report to him.

Over the past two weeks, the FDA and CDC have been managing a multistate cyclosporiasis outbreak traced to iceberg lettuce grown by Taylor Farms de Mexico and served at Taco Bell locations across five states. As of this writing, more than 1,600 people who tested positive for the parasite have reported eating at Taco Bell. On its face, this looks like a public health system working as intended: track the illness, trace the source, recall the product.

What actually happened was messier. Taco Bell’s own recall notice urged “all relevant restaurants, retailers, and foodservice operators” — not merely the ones in the five states federal regulators had named — to act, suggesting the company itself believed the problem was bigger than what the government had disclosed. Walmart pulled bagged lettuce from shelves in fifteen states. Then, on a Saturday, the FDA posted an announcement that it had detected cyclospora on Taylor Farms lettuce outside the original recall — only to delete the announcement and reverse itself the next day, calling the result a false positive after agency staff re-examined the sample. Taylor Farms issued a statement claiming the FDA had apologized to the company. The FDA denied it had apologized at all.

Meanwhile, North Carolina reported an 83 percent weekly spike in cyclosporiasis cases, reaching 561 total since May, while insisting — without much elaboration — that its outbreak was unrelated to the Taco Bell cluster, and might instead involve parsley or cilantro. The FDA’s own website carries a warning not to eat recalled lettuce “at any Taco Bell location or other restaurants,” without specifying which other restaurants. Neither HHS, Taylor Farms, nor Taco Bell responded promptly to requests for comment.

Kennedy himself finally weighed in on July 21, at the same briefing where he announced the Medicaid freeze, declaring the outbreak “under control” and the source “identified.” Asked whether cuts to FoodNet, the CDC’s longstanding foodborne-illness surveillance network, had slowed the investigation, he called the criticism “invalid,” saying the eliminated monitoring had been “redundant.” Sen. Jon Ossoff was not persuaded, writing to Kennedy that his “self-indulgent demolition of critical public health programs puts Americans at risk.” The timing was awkward: the FDA had confirmed just two days earlier, on July 19, that none of the cyclospora samples tested in this entire investigation have actually come back positive — meaning Kennedy declared the source “identified” days after his own agency admitted it hadn’t confirmed anything in a lab. Mexican authorities, for their part, have publicly cast doubt on whether the outbreak traces to the Taylor Farms facility at all. Even FDA Commissioner Kyle Diamantas stopped well short of Kennedy’s certainty, telling reporters only that the evidence “strongly supports” Taylor Farms as the source — a noticeably softer claim than his boss’s.

None of this began this month. The CDC was slow to sound the alarm even as cases climbed sharply in Michigan and other states in the weeks before the Taylor Farms link was confirmed — the same institutional sluggishness this series has already traced back to a CDC that has had a confirmed director for twenty-nine days total since Kennedy took office. The practical result of two weeks of contradictory announcements has been foot traffic to chain restaurants nationwide — not just Taco Bell — dropping noticeably, as consumers do the risk assessment the government won’t do clearly for them.

This started as agency dysfunction rather than a Kennedy scandal in the narrow sense — a false-positive test posted and retracted over a single weekend, the kind of small, fixable communications failure that compounds when the person atop the org chart is elsewhere. It has since become something closer to both. The FDA and CDC report, ultimately, to a secretary whose Tuesday leadership briefings he attends once a month, scrolling his phone when he does show up, according to colleagues who spoke to the New York Times for the reporting that opened this series’ previous installment. Now that same secretary has put his own name on a claim of certainty — “under control,” source “identified” — that his own agency’s lab results don’t yet support. That is no longer a lower-level communications failure. It is the secretary himself getting ahead of the facts, in public, on the record.

Three Stories, One Department

None of this required Kennedy to resign, and none of it required him to be caught doing anything as dramatic as the leucovorin debacle or the ACIP purge chronicled elsewhere in this series. It only required the ordinary operation of a department where attention is a scarce resource, spent almost entirely on food dyes, vaccine skepticism, and enforcement actions against political opponents — and where a lettuce recall, a Medicaid freeze, and a resignation rumor can all occupy the same news cycle without anyone in charge appearing to notice the pattern connecting them.

He is, for now, still the secretary. The salad is still, technically, off the menu. Both facts should worry you about equally.
