NARRATOR Sir David Attenborough (in the hushed, British-accented tones of a prestige wildlife documentary):

And here, in the temperate wetlands of the District of Columbia, we observe one of nature’s most fascinating territorial behaviours: the dominant male, having secured his habitat through a combination of display, aggression, and the enthusiastic support of approximately 74 million members of his species, turns his attention to what truly matters.

Not governance. Not diplomacy. Not the war in Iran, the collapsing economy, or the $1.776 billion he has just allocated to compensate members of his pack who attacked the Capitol.

The grass.

NARRATOR: The crisis began in September 2018, when the new VH-92A presidential helicopter — a $215 million aircraft produced by Lockheed Martin’s Sikorsky division, one of 23 units procured at a total cost of $5 billion — was discovered to occasionally scorch the grass with its engine exhaust during landing.

The grass. Was scorched.

In the hierarchy of presidential concerns — nuclear proliferation, economic stability, the continued existence of democratic norms — scorched grass would appear, to the untrained observer, to rank somewhere below the wine selection at state dinners and slightly above the font choice on the White House Christmas card.

The trained observer, however, understands that for this particular specimen, the lawn is not decoration. It is territory. And territory must be defended.

NARRATOR: The solution, as with all solutions proposed by the dominant male, is vast, expensive, and bears his personal insignia.

A 100-foot helipad. Black granite. Presidential seal inlaid in the stone. Permanent. Immovable. A landing pad that says, in the universal language of territorial display: I was here, and I mattered, and the helicopters landed on me.

One hundred feet in diameter. For reference, that is approximately the size of a professional basketball court, or roughly half the area of the Oval Office’s ego, or precisely the amount of space required to ensure that no blade of grass is ever again inconvenienced by the exhaust of a $215 million helicopter.

The cost has not been disclosed. One suspects it will be discovered in a footnote, like the IRS audit provision, on a Monday afternoon, on a government website that nobody checks.

The East Wing reduced to rubble. Construction continues despite judges, parliamentarians, and basic notions of historical preservation. The grass, however, will be protected.

NARRATOR: The helipad is, of course, merely the latest in a series of territorial improvements the dominant male has undertaken during his second occupancy of the habitat.

He has paved the Rose Garden. He has renovated a White House bathroom in marble and gold. He has added gold flourishes to his West Wing office. He has demolished the East Wing — a structure that has stood since 1942, housed the First Lady’s offices for eight decades, and was most recently the workplace of Dr. Jill Biden — and is replacing it with a $400 million, 90,000-square-foot ballroom.

The ballroom has faced legal challenges. A federal judge stopped the work, requiring congressional approval. A Republican effort to include $1 billion for the project in an immigration funding bill was struck down by the Senate parliamentarian on procedural grounds.

Construction continues.

On Monday, May 19, the dominant male summoned the press corps to the construction site. He stood before the workers, the cranes, the exposed steel, and explained that the ballroom is not a ballroom. It is a security upgrade.

“It’s a shield,” he said. “Impenetrable steel, and also impenetrable glass.”

Impenetrable glass. The mind, as they say, boggles.

NARRATOR: In the natural world, territorial display serves a well-documented purpose. The bower bird builds elaborate structures of twigs and stolen objects to attract a mate. The gorilla beats his chest to establish dominance. The frigate bird inflates a brilliant red throat pouch that serves no practical function whatsoever but is, the bird insists, very impressive.

The dominant male of the District of Columbia combines all three strategies. He builds (the ballroom). He beats his chest (”I’m a really good builder. The thing I do best in life is build.”). And he inflates — not a throat pouch, but a budget, a schedule, a narrative in which the demolition of the East Wing is a security measure and the installation of a granite presidential seal on the South Lawn is groundskeeping.

“President Trump has continued to make improvements at the White House and all around D.C. to benefit future presidents and Americans,” a spokesman said, in a statement that manages to be technically a sentence while conveying no information that corresponds to observable reality.

Marine One in all its glory. Soon to be spared the indignity of landing on mere vegetation thanks to 100 feet of black granite and a presidential seal.

NARRATOR: And so we return to the grass.

The president who will not protect the Constitution is protecting the lawn. The man who created a $1.776 billion slush fund for insurrectionists is concerned about helicopter exhaust. The administration that withdrew from the Paris Climate Accord, rolled back emission standards, and has presided over the warmest years in recorded human history is worried that its aircraft are leaving scorch marks on the decorative greenery.

There is a metaphor here. It is not subtle. The dominant male, surveying his territory, sees threats in precise inverse proportion to their actual significance. The grass, which will grow back, must be preserved at any cost. The democracy, which may not grow back, is someone else’s problem.

One hundred feet of black granite. A presidential seal. A helicopter pad for a $5 billion fleet.

The lawn will be fine.

NARRATOR: In the natural world, the dominant male’s territorial improvements often outlast the dominant male himself. The bower bird’s structure remains after the bird departs. The gorilla’s beaten path persists in the forest floor.

The granite helipad, if built, will remain on the South Lawn long after this president has gone. Future presidents will land on it. Future groundskeepers will polish the presidential seal. Future historians will note that in the spring of 2026, while the republic burned, the 47th president of the United States spent a Monday morning showing reporters his construction site and explaining that what America really needed was impenetrable glass and a very large rock with his name on it.

The grass, we are assured, will be magnificent.
