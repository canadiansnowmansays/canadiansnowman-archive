Parts IX and XII of this series traced the AI boom’s cost transmission through property-tax breaks and Gen Z activism against data-centre build-out. There is a second, quieter transmission channel running through the same boom, and it doesn’t need a single new data centre to operate: it runs through the ordinary rental market of a city where AI wealth is now bidding up rents and home prices fast enough to make evicting a decades-long tenant financially rational.

As The New York Times’ Kami Rieck reported on August 28, 2026, San Francisco landlords are increasingly offering rent-controlled tenants cash to leave voluntarily, rather than risk a no-fault eviction that would legally entitle those tenants only to the smaller relocation payment set by law. Behind the negotiating tables sits a fairly simple arithmetic problem: the gap between a decades-old rent-controlled lease and the current market rate has become, thanks to AI-driven demand, wide enough that buying a tenant out and re-renting at market rate can pay for itself within a few years.

The numbers behind the pressure

San Francisco’s median asking rent hit $4,395 in August 2026. Bernard Dethiers, 74 and retired when Rieck interviewed him, pays around $1,100 a month for the North Beach two-bedroom he has occupied for more than forty years. When new owners bought his building in 2025, they offered him $40,000 to leave within roughly three months. He countered at $600,000 — a figure built, he explained, from the rent differential he’d otherwise absorb over twenty years. The owners rejected it and turned to eviction under the Ellis Act, the California law that lets landlords clear a building of all rental units at once, provided they genuinely intend to remove it from the rental market. Mr. Dethiers and five other households in the same building took the fight to court. Under California law, his age and tenure entitle him to a full year to move rather than the standard 120 days — cold comfort, since he has told the Times he doesn’t know where in the city he’d go.

Other reporting fills in how the building reached that point. NBC Bay Area reported on August 13, 2026 and Hoodline reported on August 14 that the Powell Street building was bought by seven new owners as a tenancy in common, that Ellis Act notices were filed on November 3, 2025, and that the households, paying between roughly $600 and $1,300 a month, faced a move-out deadline in November 2026. Among them are Yok Lin Chui, there more than fifty years, and Meagan Cunningham and her adult son Max, there since 1987. The owners sued in April 2026 to resolve the dispute and narrow the tenants’ defences; in August the tenants filed a cross-complaint alleging housing discrimination, claiming that only Caucasian tenants had been offered a chance to buy into the ownership group. Mr. Dethiers put his view of the law in six words: “It’s perfectly legal. But it’s immoral.” Steve Collier of the Tenderloin Housing Clinic, which represents the tenants, described its typical user: “The Ellis Act primarily is used by people who just bought a building.” No ruling in the case has been reported.

The scale is not anecdotal. San Francisco Rent Board data shows landlord pre-buyout declarations — the disclosure filing required before buyout negotiations can begin — rose roughly 17 per cent in the first half of 2026 compared with the same period a year earlier. Ellis Act and owner-move-in notices together now account for about 10 per cent of all eviction filings with the board. Daniel Wayne, a tenant-side lawyer, put the underlying problem plainly: relocation payments and buyout sums simply don’t reflect what a long-tenured tenant gives up, particularly once current rents and moving costs are factored in.

The pressure did not ease after Rieck’s piece ran. As Io Yeh Gilman reported for Mission Local on September 10, 2026, Mayor Daniel Lurie declared a “rent emergency” that day at Alamo Square Park, citing a 26 per cent rise in median rents over the previous year and a 44 per cent rise in eviction notices, and attributing the spike to the AI boom. “Rising rents are making it harder for the people who have built their lives here to stay here,” he said. His package proposed a 25 per cent increase in relocation payments for tenants displaced by Ellis Act evictions, which goes directly to the gap Mr. Wayne describes, and more money for the city’s Tenant Right to Counsel programme. Meg Heisler, policy director of the Anti-Displacement Coalition, called it an “incomplete package,” saying “too many tenants are being forced out of their homes before cases even get to court.” The market kept moving regardless. Tim Fang reported for CBS Bay Area on September 29, 2026 that Zumper’s September report put San Francisco’s median one-bedroom rent at a record $4,400 a month, up 25.4 per cent in a year, and its median two-bedroom rent at $6,340, the highest in the United States and up 26.8 per cent.

Buyouts and the Ellis Act are not the only levers being pulled. Jesse Alejandro Cottrell reported for The San Francisco Standard on October 5, 2026 that more than 1,000 evictions had been filed in the city so far in 2026, putting the year on course for the most in a decade, and that, according to the Rent Board’s annual report, “nuisance” claims had risen 41 per cent, from 288 to 405 cases. One notice went to Sue Yeng Yan, 92, in her rent-controlled unit since 1982, on the grounds that her cluttered apartment was a nuisance; her landlord relented after press attention. Supervisors Myrna Melgar and Jackie Fielder proposed legislation requiring the conduct behind a nuisance eviction to “be severe in nature, or must be continuing and recurring.” When the gap between a controlled rent and the market rate is wide enough, every ground for eviction the law allows is worth more to an owner than it was before.

Three negotiations, three outcomes

The piece’s other cases show the negotiation itself functioning as a kind of informal price discovery for displacement. Craig Lipton, a 59-year-old real-estate veteran of 35 years in the city, wanted to sell part of his Nob Hill building and needed five tenants — splitting an $11,000-a-month rent-controlled unit — gone first. His opening offer of $5,000 was declined; he raised it to $55,000, they countered at $152,000, and the two sides settled at $100,000. He listed the building for $7.25 million at the end of July 2026. Mr. Lipton’s own framing of the experience — that being made to pay to reclaim possession of his property felt like a form of hostage-taking — is worth holding next to the tenants’ framing on the other side of the same table: for them, the money is compensation for the one thing a buyout can’t actually replace, which is a specific home in a specific city they can no longer afford to re-enter once they leave it.

John Alioto, a 47-year-old Hyatt Regency doorman born and raised in San Francisco, has lived in his Nob Hill rent-controlled unit for more than two decades, splitting roughly $2,600 a month in rent across four housemates. His landlords offered $70,000 in April, then $104,000 after the first offer was declined; the tenants have declined both. Mr. Alioto’s own words capture the asymmetry better than any rent-board statistic: no sum handed over outside the building, he said, would be enough to make leaving feel like a good outcome rather than a loss with a price tag attached to it. Upstairs, Mike Dineen — 53, a worker at the employee-owned Rainbow Grocery Cooperative, in his unit for nearly thirty years — received a similar offer and considered it too low to seriously weigh. His apartment is where he cared for his mother during the final year and a half of her life, and where three decades of neighbours became, in his words, the greatest source of community he has. He does not own the building. He was careful to specify, though, that it is still his home.

The landlord’s-eye view, and why it isn’t wrong on its own terms

Scott Freedman, a lawyer who represents property owners in these negotiations, offers the industry’s own explanation without much apology: the current wave resembles past tech booms, in which renewed excitement about what’s happening in the city pulls in demand against a fixed supply of units, and owners — some returning to San Francisco themselves, some with grown children wanting to move in — respond rationally to the resulting price signal. That account is not dishonest. It is simply a description of a rent-controlled market doing exactly what economic theory predicts it will do once the gap between controlled and market rents grows wide enough: it creates a permanent, escalating financial incentive for owners to remove long-tenured tenants by whatever legal mechanism is available, Ellis Act included, regardless of how long any individual tenant has lived there or what leaving would cost them in terms this series’ balance sheets don’t capture.

Why this belongs in the ledger

This series has so far measured AI’s costs to ordinary people mainly through tax breaks diverted from schools and roads (Part IX), water and power strain triggering youth organising (Part XII), and rising borrowing costs flowing through to mortgage rates (Part X). Rent-controlled eviction pressure is a fourth channel, and arguably the most direct one: it requires no new construction, no utility hookup, and no municipal vote. It requires only that enough capital arrive in a city fast enough to widen the gap between what a longtime tenant pays and what a landlord could otherwise charge — at which point a state law originally designed to let owners exit the rental business altogether becomes, in practice, a tool for turning that gap into cash. Nobody in this story has done anything illegal. That is precisely what should trouble a series built on tracking how AI wealth moves through an economy without anyone along the way needing to break a law to extract it.
