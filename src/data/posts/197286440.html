Day 75. The chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff cannot define the center of gravity of the war he is fighting. The head of Central Command says the military has “met every military objective” — while the Strait of Hormuz remains closed. The Senate blocked a war powers resolution 50-49. The House blocked one 212-212. Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates have secretly bombed Iran. In Doha, a stranded gas tanker named Rasheeda circles endlessly in the Persian Gulf, and residents track her on their phones like a pet that can’t find its way home. In Beijing, the president of the United States asked the president of China for help. Brent crude closed at approximately $127/barrel. The Consumer Price Index rose 3.8 percent in April — the fastest rate since May 2023. Diesel is six dollars. Gas is five. American farmers cannot afford fertilizer. The war is eleven weeks old.

I. The Silence

In public, Gen. Dan Caine has avoided discussing with lawmakers the broader U.S. military strategy.Credit...Kenny Holston/The New York Times

On Tuesday, General Dan Caine sat before the Senate Armed Services Committee for his fourteenth hour of congressional testimony since the war began and was asked by Senator Gary Peters, Democrat of Michigan and a Navy veteran, to define the “center of gravity” in the war with Iran.

The term is foundational. Carl von Clausewitz defined it as the enemy’s primary source of strength — “the hub of all power and movement on which everything depends.” In 1991, General Colin Powell defined it as Iraq’s Republican Guard. In 2010, Admiral Mike Mullen defined it as the Afghan people. In 2026, General Caine declined to answer. The decision, he said, should be made by “U.S. political leaders.”

Greg Jaffe’s analysis — “Gen. Caine’s Silence on Iran War Leaves Questions About Military Strategy” — is the frame for Week Eleven. Senator Peters offered his own diagnosis. The center of gravity, he said, was the Strait of Hormuz. “We’re not going to bring this war to an end until we seize control of the straits in a way that opens them back up.”

In Situation Room meetings before the war, General Caine raised the prospect of weapons depletion and flagged the risk of Iran blocking the Strait. But he has said that as the top military adviser to the president, his job is to present options — not take a firm stand. Stephen Biddle, a professor of international affairs at Columbia and a frequent Pentagon adviser, pushed back: concepts like center of gravity are “normally understood as a military determination, not a presidential prerogative.” Heidi Urben, a retired Army colonel at Georgetown, warned that the silence sends a signal downward through the ranks: “When military leaders only talk about tactics, it reinforces this fallacy within the ranks that they don’t need to worry about strategy.”

Eliot Cohen, a historian and former senior State Department official, defended General Caine as “remarkably discreet, and appropriately so.” But discretion is a peacetime virtue. Eleven weeks into a war with no defined center of gravity, no public strategy, and no exit criteria, it begins to look like something else.

II. The Hypothetical

On Thursday, Admiral Brad Cooper, the head of U.S. Central Command, testified before the same committee for the first time since the war began on February 28. He told the senators that the U.S. military had “met every military objective.” He did not address the Strait of Hormuz, through which twenty percent of the world’s oil supply passes and which remains closed.

He then told the committee, as documented in the NYT live updates — “Top Commander Dismisses Reports of Civilian Deaths in Iran” — that across more than 13,600 strikes, he was aware of only one civilian casualty incident: the February 28 strike on the Shajarah Tayyebeh elementary school, which Iranian officials said killed 175 people. He described the prevention of civilian deaths as “a matter that I’m passionate about.”

The New York Times has verified damage to 22 schools and 17 health care facilities. The Iranian Red Crescent Society reported on April 2 that at least 763 schools and 316 health care facilities had been damaged or destroyed. At least 1,700 Iranian civilians have been killed, according to the Human Rights Activists News Agency. Airwars, the British nonprofit, has documented more than 300 civilian casualty events. Admiral Cooper conceded his staff had investigated none of them.

Senator Kirsten Gillibrand, Democrat of New York, asked how he explained the publicly available evidence. “There’s no way we can corroborate that,” he replied. “No indication of that whatsoever.”

Emily Tripp, executive director of Airwars, called the claim “pretty ridiculous.” Annie Shiel of the Center for Civilians in Conflict called it “patently absurd.”

The civilian harm team at Central Command has been reduced from ten officers to one. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth terminated dozens of positions across the Pentagon focused on preventing and responding to civilian casualties — the office created by his predecessor, Lloyd Austin. William McRaven, a retired admiral and former commander of U.S. Special Operations Command, broke ranks: “It was frankly pretty clear from the very beginning that we were probably responsible.”

Senator Mark Kelly, Democrat of Arizona, pressed Admiral Cooper to reinstate the officers removed from civilian harm mitigation. “If you were to find out that there was an error in the targeting process, would you reinstate some of those people?”

“Sir, it’s a hypothetical,” Admiral Cooper replied. “We’ll just see what the investigation says.”

Seventeen hundred dead civilians are not a hypothetical.

III. The Margins

The war powers clock expired on May 1. The administration’s position: the ceasefire pauses the countdown. Legal scholars across the ideological spectrum reject this view. On Monday, Trump said the ceasefire was on “life support.” On Wednesday and Thursday, both chambers voted on whether that mattered.

In the Senate, as Megan Mineiro reported — “Senate Again Rejects Bid to End Iran War, but G.O.P. Opposition Grows” — the seventh Democratic attempt to halt the war failed 50-49. Senator Lisa Murkowski of Alaska became the third Republican to flip, joining Susan Collins and Rand Paul. Senator John Fetterman of Pennsylvania again cast the deciding vote to block, voting with Republicans.

Senator Lisa Murkowski, Republican of Alaska, during a committee hearing. She previously opposed similar resolutions.Credit...Eric Lee for The New York Times

Murkowski was blunt: it did not appear that hostilities had ceased, “nor that anything has really changed much other than the words that are being used.” She is working with like-minded Republicans on a draft authorization that would force exit criteria — paused pending a ceasefire lapse. Senator John Curtis, Republican of Utah, wobbled: “I wouldn’t say I’m totally resolved in my mind.” He voted no.

Senator Tim Kaine did not mince words: “The White House has lied to the American public, and every time they say this war is justified to stop Iran’s nuclear program, they are telling the American public a lie.”

In the House on Thursday, as Robert Jimison reported — “The House again blocks a war powers vote to halt the Iran war” — the vote tied 212-212. Two new Republican defectors: Brian Fitzpatrick of Pennsylvania and Tom Barrett of Michigan, both facing difficult re-elections, joined Thomas Massie of Kentucky. Representative Jared Golden of Maine was the lone Democrat to oppose, casting the deciding vote as his colleagues chanted “one more.”

The symmetry is precise. In both chambers, the margins are single votes. In both chambers, the decisive vote blocking the resolution is cast by a lone member of the opposing party — Fetterman in the Senate, Golden in the House. In both chambers, the Republican cracks are widening.

Thomas Massie explained why his colleagues flipped: “It indicates that diesel is six dollars and gas is five, and people can’t afford fertilizer because of this war.”

The Republican defector count now stands at: Kennedy of Louisiana, Collins of Maine, Murkowski of Alaska, Paul of Kentucky in the Senate; Massie of Kentucky, Fitzpatrick of Pennsylvania, Barrett of Michigan in the House. Seven. The number was one in March.

IV. The Summit

In Beijing on Thursday, the president of the United States sat across from the president of China and asked for help.

Anton Troianovski reported — “The U.S. says Trump and Xi discussed the need to reopen the Strait of Hormuz” — that the White House released a statement saying the two leaders had “agreed that the Strait of Hormuz must remain open to support the free flow of energy.” There was no signal that China would do anything about it. Guo Jiakun, a spokesman for China’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs, said only that the country’s policy regarding the Strait had been “consistent and clear.”

Secretary of State Marco Rubio, aboard Air Force One, told Fox News the United States hoped to “convince” China “to play a more active role in getting Iran to walk away from what they’re doing now.” He painted a bleak picture of the global fallout — without acknowledging that it was an American war that precipitated Iran’s closure of the Strait in the first place.

The price of help is now openly stated. Henry Huiyao Wang, president of the Center for China and Globalization, said the United States should toughen its rhetoric against Taiwan independence “if they expect China to do more.” If China is to give an “extra push” to clinch an Iran deal, he said, “it certainly needs more encouragement from the U.S.”

Taiwan for Hormuz. The Formosa trade. It is now on the table, spoken aloud by Chinese analysts in Beijing on the eve of the summit. Rubio said nothing about it.

V. The Toll Booth

Ships in the Gulf of Oman near the Strait of Hormuz early this month.Credit...Fatima Shbair/Associated Press

Hours after Trump and Xi met, Iran demonstrated exactly who controls the Strait.

As Euan Ward reported — “As Trump meets Xi, Iran lets Chinese ships through the Strait of Hormuz” — Fars and Tasnim, two semiofficial Iranian news agencies, said Iran had approved the passage of some Chinese vessels under rules set by Tehran. The crossings began Wednesday night, following diplomatic outreach by China’s foreign minister and ambassador to Iran.

Tomer Raanan, a maritime risk analyst at Lloyd’s List, delivered the verdict: Iran gets to dictate who goes through the Strait, and as long as that is the case, it is “not really open.”

This is the Landler toll booth made operational. Iran is not reopening the Strait. It is running selective transit — deciding by flag, by relationship, by leverage, who passes and who waits. China gets the golden ticket on the day its president meets the American one.

Meanwhile, at the same hour, United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations reported that a vessel anchored near Fujairah had been seized by “unauthorized personnel” and was bound for Iranian territorial waters. An Indian-flagged ship was attacked off Oman. Windward, a maritime data company, described a “broader pattern of coercive maritime activity.”

The Strait is not closed and it is not open. It is curated.

VI. The Combatants

Eric Schmitt and Vivian Nereim reported from Washington and Riyadh — “Saudi Arabia and U.A.E. Carried Out Secret Attacks in Iran, U.S. Officials Say” — what the series has been circling since March. Two current and one former senior U.S. official confirmed that both Gulf monarchies had separately struck Iran in retaliation for attacks on their territory. It is the first known time either country has directly attacked Iran.

Neither government has acknowledged the strikes. Both host U.S. military installations. Both have maintained publicly that they are bystanders. The strikes make them direct combatants.

Firas Maksad of the Eurasia Group framed the logic: “They realize that the war is unlikely to topple the Iranian government, and any U.S.-Iran deal is unlikely to decisively eliminate the threat.” Their retaliatory strikes were “calibrated so as not to slide into further escalation.”

Iran’s foreign minister Araghchi singled out the Emirates as “directly involved in the aggressive action against my country.” The Abraham Accords, once a diplomatic breakthrough, are now a military alliance tested under fire — with Israel’s Iron Dome deployed on Emirati soil and Netanyahu claiming a secret visit that Abu Dhabi denied hours later, as Pranav Baskar and Johnatan Reiss reported in “Israel Says Netanyahu Made a Secret Trip to U.A.E., Which Says He Didn’t.”

Trump told reporters in March that Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman was “fighting with us.” He was, it turns out, telling the truth. The Gulf bystander fiction is over. Parsi’s non-negotiated status quo just became harder to sustain — Iran now faces three named adversaries, not two.

VII. Poor Rasheeda

In Doha, the stranded gas tanker Rasheeda has been circling in the Persian Gulf for more than two months. Residents track her on maritime apps and ask one another: “Where is Rasheeda today?”

River Akira Davis reported from Qatar — “In Qatar, Energy Sector Damage Is Severe, and the Way Back Will Be Long” — the most devastating economic assessment of the war to date. Three of twenty Iranian projectiles penetrated Ras Laffan’s defenses in March, striking LNG liquefaction trains. The damaged units accounted for seventeen percent of production. The critical bottleneck: cryogenic heat exchangers, precision machines whose manufacturing is dominated by a single Honeywell subsidiary. Replacement units take four to five years to procure.

A satellite view of Qatar's Ras Laffan Industrial City after Iranian strikes damaged LNG liquefaction trains in March. Engineers estimate full recovery could take three to five years

Even if the Strait reopened tomorrow, Qatari LNG exports would remain crippled for months and impaired for years. Qatar is uniquely vulnerable — all of its maritime infrastructure sits deep inside the Persian Gulf, with no alternative route to open water. Sixteen hundred vessels remain trapped near Hormuz. Crews hand-stitched Pakistani flags from scraps of cloth found on board, hoping ties to the mediating nation might secure safe passage. It did not work.

The Philippines has stopped sending sailors into the conflict zone. Insurance companies will not cover the route. Storage tanks may overflow, forcing shutdowns that risk permanent damage. Henning Gloystein of the Eurasia Group: “We’re talking reduced production until the end of the decade.”

Rashid Al-Mohanadi, a former QatarEnergy engineer, watched the March attack from his home outside Doha. The sky over Ras Laffan flashed with interceptor missiles. The shock waves rattled his windows. When he stepped outside, the horizon was thick with black smoke.

Near the end of Davis’s interview, Al-Mohanadi opened a maritime tracking app and typed in “Rasheeda.” Out came the tanker, still circling. “Poor Rasheeda,” he said. “At this point, she must be so tired.”

This is the Eyre doomsday weapon thesis in its fullest expression — not a theoretical argument about geography but a measured engineering assessment. The damage will outlast the war by years. The color has drained not from a bag of Japanese snack chips but from the world’s energy map.

VIII. The Price

Lydia DePillis reported — “Inflation Accelerates After Weeks of War in Iran” — that the Consumer Price Index rose 3.8 percent in April from a year earlier, the fastest rate since May 2023. Energy costs have replaced tariffs as the primary driver. Diesel is six dollars. Gas is five. Farmers cannot afford fertilizer. Senator Chris Murphy of Connecticut: “Nothing matters more to our constituents than doing something about these spiraling gas prices, which are bankrupting families and farmers all across the country.”

Trump was asked this week whether Americans’ financial situation was motivating him to make a deal with Iran. His answer: “Not even a little bit.”

Admiral Cooper told the senators that the military had “met every military objective.” He did not mention gas prices. General Caine could not define the center of gravity. Neither could name what the war had cost.

Representative Greg Casar, chairman of the Congressional Progressive Caucus: “Trump’s war with Iran has cost thousands of lives and spiked the price of everything from gas to groceries.”

The ceasefire vocabulary, updated: “Holding” (Rubio, May 5). “Nominal” (Iran FM, May 8). “Life support, one percent chance” (Trump, May 11). “Piece of garbage” (Trump, May 11). “90% enrichment option” (Rezaei, May 12). “Sir, it’s a hypothetical” (Cooper, May 14).

IX. The Numbers

The war: Day 75. Eleven weeks. More than 13,600 U.S. strikes. Iran retains approximately 70 percent of its mobile launchers and 70 percent of its prewar missile stockpile, per published intelligence reports Admiral Cooper declined to confirm. At least 1,700 Iranian civilians killed. 22 schools and 17 health care facilities with verified damage. 763 schools and 316 health care facilities reported damaged or destroyed by the Iranian Red Crescent. More than 300 civilian casualty events documented by Airwars. One officer remaining on the civilian harm team at Central Command, down from ten.

The Strait: Closed to general commercial traffic. Selectively open to Chinese-flagged vessels as of Wednesday night. Iran operational at 30 of 33 missile sites along the Strait. 1,600 vessels trapped. One vessel seized near Fujairah on Thursday. One Indian-flagged ship attacked off Oman. The Rasheeda still circling.

The vote: Senate 50-49. House 212-212. Republican defectors: seven across both chambers. Kennedy, Collins, Murkowski, Paul, Massie, Fitzpatrick, Barrett. Democratic crossovers holding the line: Fetterman, Golden.

The economy: CPI 3.8 percent in April. Brent crude approximately $127/barrel. Diesel $6. Gas $5. Fertilizer unaffordable. Qatar LNG recovery: three to five years minimum. “Reduced production until the end of the decade” — Eurasia Group.

The summit: Trump and Xi “agreed that the Strait of Hormuz must remain open.” No Chinese commitments obtained. Taiwan-for-Hormuz quid pro quo openly floated by Chinese analysts.

The Gulf: Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates confirmed as direct combatants. Iron Dome deployed in the Emirates. Netanyahu’s secret visit claimed by Israel, denied by UAE. Araghchi: “collusion with Israel is unforgivable.”

The 90 percent claim: Admiral Cooper: “The large-scale volleys that we’ve seen over the last couple of years, Iran is no longer capable of executing those.” Also Admiral Cooper: declined to comment on reports Iran retains 70 percent of its missile stockpile. These two statements do not occupy the same reality.

The school: Shajarah Tayyebeh elementary. February 28. 175 killed. Investigation ongoing after more than two months. McRaven: “It was frankly pretty clear from the very beginning that we were probably responsible.”

The center of gravity: Undefined. General Caine declined. Senator Peters defined it: the Strait of Hormuz. The chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff is fighting a war whose center of gravity he will not name for a president whose war aims change daily.

The ceasefire: On “life support, one percent chance” — the president’s own words, Monday, May 11.

Narges Mohammadi: Still in Evin Prison.

Days of war: 75.
