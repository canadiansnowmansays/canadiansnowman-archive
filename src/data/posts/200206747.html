In March, I wrote a postscript to The American Contradiction — my ten-part examination of how exclusionary politics betrays America’s own founding promise. I documented Pete Hegseth’s purge of decorated Army officers from promotion lists, the cancellation of a Navy captain’s command over a special operations unit, and the reported comment from Hegseth’s chief of staff that President Trump would not want to stand next to a Black female officer at military ceremonies.

I ended that postscript the same way I ended the original series: with the observation that exclusion weakens rather than strengthens. That history, consistently, confirms this.

Here we are again.

The Mechanism, Revealed

What was speculative in March is documented in June. The New York Times reported this week that Pete Hegseth blocked the promotions of at least seven Navy officers selected by a board of senior admirals for advancement to one-star rank — and that the officers removed appear to have been targeted not for any professional failing, but because their names appeared on an external watchlist website maintained by private citizens cataloguing what it called “woke” military officers.

Let that sentence sit for a moment.

The United States Secretary of Defense is consulting a crowdsourced blacklist operated by anonymous civilians and using it to override the judgments of the most competitive promotion process in the American armed forces — a process in which only five percent of eligible officers are selected, evaluated over weeks by panels of senior admirals reviewing hundreds of service records.

The Navy’s senior leadership and promotion architecture. Hegseth’s interventions bypass the competitive board process designed to identify merit at the highest levels.

One of the women removed from the list had served as a “diversity liaison officer” — twenty years ago. Her job, two decades prior, had been to help the Navy recruit and retain women and minorities. She had subsequently built a distinguished career as a surface warfare officer, completed advanced nuclear power school, and been selected to serve as an aide to a four-star admiral. None of that mattered. The watchlist had her name. Hegseth acted.

Another female officer served as a Navy pilot and foreign area officer. The rationale for her removal was not stated. It did not need to be. The pattern is its own rationale.

The result is a new Navy one-star list that includes no women — none — despite women comprising twenty-one percent of the active-duty Navy. It includes approximately two non-white officers in a service where sailors identifying as racial minorities represent thirty-eight percent of the force.

The Arithmetic of Accountability

Diverse U.S. Navy personnel in formation. Women comprise 21% and racial minorities 38% of the active-duty Navy — talent the current promotion interventions appear to be systematically sidelining

Senator Jack Reed of Rhode Island has been tracking the numbers. His finding, presented in recent Senate testimony: nearly sixty percent of the senior officers Hegseth has fired or sidelined are female or Black. Women and minorities currently account for fewer than twenty percent of all generals and admirals.

The math does not require interpretation. It is a statistical signature. When sixty percent of the casualties fall on twenty percent of the population, the cause is not coincidence and it is not merit. It is targeting.

Hegseth’s spokespeople continue to repeat the same formulation: that promotions go to those who have earned them, that the department will “never consider the color of a service member’s skin or their gender as a factor.” The New York Times report makes clear that this statement is false. The watchlist site’s own criteria are racial and gender-coded. The demographic outcome of Hegseth’s interventions is demographic. To say the department does not consider gender while producing a one-star list with no women is a kind of institutional gaslighting — and it is, by now, a familiar one.

The Lie Told to Congress

There is a second element to this week’s reporting that demands attention, because it moves from policy dispute into something more serious.

Representative Chrissy Houlahan, a Democrat from Pennsylvania and an Air Force veteran, asked Hegseth directly at a House Armed Services Committee hearing whether he had ordered the Navy to add a special operations officer lacking the necessary command experience to the Navy’s promotion list for admiral.

“I’m not aware of what you’re referring to,” Hegseth replied.

He was aware. The Times reports that Hegseth had himself urged senior Navy officials to include Captain William Francis Jr. — his own personal assistant — on the one-star list, despite Francis lacking the command time required under the board’s eligibility rules. Navy officials declined, citing those rules. Francis was not selected.

Hegseth then told a congressional committee he was unaware of what the representative was describing.

This is not a misremembrance. A secretary of defense who personally intervenes to push his own aide onto a promotion list does not subsequently forget that he did so. The response to Houlahan was a choice. It was made in a congressional hearing, under oath, about a specific and material fact.

The Classroom Test, Revisited

A diverse group of young children — the future service members who will inherit the military we are reshaping today

In April, I wrote about a photograph from a Bronx preschool — Barack Obama and Zohran Mamdani seated on a carpet among twenty children, none of whom had yet learned to sort each other by race. I proposed what I called the Classroom Test: if your political philosophy requires you to walk into that room and explain to those children why some of their friends are problems, your philosophy has failed the most basic field test a civilized society can offer.

I applied it to gerrymandering. It applies here with equal precision.

Those children will grow up. Some of them will want to serve their country. Some of them will want to fly naval aircraft, or lead special operations units, or complete nuclear power school and earn a position beside a four-star admiral. What they will find, if the current trajectory continues, is a military that has been explicitly redesigned to tell them that ambition of that kind — ambition dressed in a particular body — is the wrong kind of ambition.

Jessica Ruttenber noticed. She retired as a lieutenant colonel. She flew Air Force refueling tankers in Iraq and Afghanistan. She saw the new Navy one-star list. She did not know, when she wrote her essay, that Hegseth had personally removed female officers from it. She knew only what was visible on the surface: that no women appeared.

“The military I left in 2021 feels very different from the one we are watching today,” she wrote. “In some ways, it feels like we are watching hard-won progress move backward in real time. That is the part I cannot shake. Because if I am honest, I now find myself wondering: Would I want my own children to enter a system like this?”

What History Says, Again

Tuskegee Airmen, 332nd Fighter Group, World War II. Their combat record proved that drawing from America’s full talent pool produced excellence, not compromise

I have now written fourteen essays arguing the same thing: America has always been strongest when it drew from its full talent pool, and weakest when it chose fear over capacity. The Tuskegee Airmen flew. The Buffalo Soldiers served. Truman desegregated the military in 1948 in part because segregation was a strategic liability. The modern officer corps — diverse, combat-tested, professional — is the product of that long, imperfect, real arc.

What Pete Hegseth is doing is not restoring meritocracy. He has dismantled the Command Assessment Program, which used double-blind reviews to allow officers of any background to compete fairly for top positions. He replaced its oversight with officials who include a retired general who once called Barack Obama a “terrorist leader” and a man who pleaded guilty to dereliction of duty. He is consulting anonymous watchlist websites to override the judgments of senior admirals. He is lying to Congress about his interventions.

The officer removed because she served as a diversity liaison officer two decades ago had, in the intervening years, completed nuclear power school and risen to serve a four-star admiral. In any rational assessment of merit — any — her record is extraordinary. She was not removed for failing to meet a standard. She was removed because someone on a website remembered who she used to be.

In March, I wrote that the argument I made across ten essays had not been complicated by events. It had been confirmed.

That sentence needs only one edit for June.

It has been confirmed again.
