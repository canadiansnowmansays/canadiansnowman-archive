There is a special kind of insult in winning a lawsuit and then discovering the money still isn’t yours. That is roughly where several thousand authors found themselves this month, as the largest copyright settlement in American history — $1.5 billion, approved in July 2026 by a federal judge in the Northern District of California — moved from a headline number into the much less glamorous business of actually being paid out.

The settlement resolves a class action brought after authors discovered that Anthropic, in the course of building the training data for its Claude chatbot, had downloaded and stored millions of pirated books. A judge ruled last year that the piracy itself gave the plaintiffs cause to sue, even while separately finding that Anthropic’s training on books it had legally acquired counted as fair use — a split ruling this series has tracked closely, and one that continues to define the boundary regulators and courts are drawing around AI training more broadly. Under the settlement, authors can collect up to $3,000 for each of the more than 482,000 books a court found Anthropic had illegally obtained. Anthropic’s deputy general counsel, Aparna Sridhar, said in May that more than 91 percent of authors and publishers covered by the deal had already filed claims, and that the company looked forward to closing the matter out.

Closing it out is turning out to be the easy part. Getting the money to the people whose books were actually stolen is not.

A contract clause nobody wrote for this

The mechanics of the dispute are almost mundane, which is exactly why they matter. Authors do not simply collect their $3,000 per book. They must split it with whichever publisher holds the rights, and with any co-authors, at percentages a class-action council worked out in consultation with publishers and the Authors Guild. For most trade authors that split runs close to even. For textbook authors, it is far worse — in some cases 10 to 15 percent of the total, set entirely by the terms of decades-old publishing contracts that were never written with an AI settlement in mind.

Mary Rasenberger, chief executive of the Authors Guild, which represents more than 18,000 members, does not describe this as a deliberate squeeze by publishers so much as an inevitable collision between a settlement built for the present and record-keeping built for an earlier era. Her concern, as she put it, is that not every publisher has kept clean track of which rights it has already reverted back to authors — and a publisher that hasn’t updated its own catalog will file a claim on a book it no longer owns simply because nobody told it otherwise.

That is precisely what happened to April Henry, a bestselling author of young-adult mysteries and thrillers, who logged into the settlement’s claims portal to find that HarperCollins — her publisher on a book released in 1999, whose rights had reverted to her in 2007 — had filed a claim asserting partial ownership anyway. Henry produced a letter from her agent confirming the reversion, uploaded it to the portal, and was subsequently cleared to collect the full $3,000 rather than splitting it. She does not believe HarperCollins tried to cheat her. She has twenty-two titles on the list of Anthropic-pirated books and expects to net somewhere in the mid-$20,000s once co-authors and publishers take their share — a number that would have been considerably higher without the correction.

Not every author’s dispute has resolved so cleanly. Amy Lupold Bair, who has written guidebooks on blogging and family life online, posted on Threads that her publisher was fighting the settlement’s default 50/50 split and wanted her — the author whose work was actually stolen — to accept just 10 percent, a post that drew more than a thousand likes and a lengthy public discussion before Bair declined to comment further. The post did not name the publisher, but two of her titles are published under Wiley’s “For Dummies” imprint; John Wiley & Sons would say only that it filed claims for all Wiley-published works covered by the settlement in accordance with its terms, without confirming what percentage it was seeking on educational titles specifically, or whether it considered the “Dummies” series to fall under the harsher educational-title category at all.

The pattern across both disputes is the same, even though the underlying disagreements are different in kind. Henry’s dispute was a records problem — a publisher claiming rights it no longer held, correctable with a letter from an agent. Bair’s dispute, if her account is accurate, is a leverage problem — a publisher entitled by contract to negotiate its own split, using that entitlement to push for far more than the settlement’s own default terms anticipated. Rasenberger’s read of the broader landscape is that most of these fights are the first kind rather than the second: sloppy catalog-keeping rather than deliberate extraction. But from an author’s chair, the distinction between “our records are wrong” and “we are exercising our contractual leverage” is cold comfort either way. Both produce the same result — a smaller check, a longer wait, and a claims-portal dispute the author did not choose to be fighting.

The industry’s streaming moment

Kristelia García, a copyright law professor at Georgetown, put her finger on why this keeps happening: publishing contracts, unlike modern music contracts, were mostly never drafted with a clause contemplating what happens if a third party pirates the work and later has to pay damages for it. That gap is what turned a defined, court-approved payout into thousands of individual negotiations over money nobody’s contract anticipated. García compared the dynamic to a 2007 lawsuit brought by the producers who discovered Eminem, who sued a Universal Music subsidiary arguing they were owed a larger cut of digital download royalties than their old physical-sales contracts specified — a dispute that took five years to settle, in 2012, precisely because nobody had written the contract for the format that made the money. Publishing, García argues, is now having its own version of that moment, a full generation after music had its own reckoning with streaming.

The dispute is not confined to old contracts, either. Rasenberger described one author who contacted the guild this week with a genuinely unresolved legal question: the rights to that author’s books reverted only this year, long after the underlying infringement occurred in 2021 and 2022. Whose claim controls — the rights-holder now, or the rights-holder at the time Anthropic pirated the book — is a question the settlement’s own claims process was not built to answer cleanly, and Rasenberger says a court-appointed arbitrator will ultimately have to decide contested cases like it one at a time.

That timing question matters more than it might first appear, because it goes to the theory of harm the entire settlement rests on. The $3,000-per-book figure is compensation for an infringement that happened when Anthropic downloaded and stored the pirated file — a discrete act, datable to 2021 or 2022 in most of the litigation’s core allegations. A rights holder who acquired the book after that date was not the party harmed by the piracy; they are, at most, the party who now happens to hold the rights to a claim that vested years earlier. Settlement architecture built around present-day rights ownership, rather than rights ownership at the moment of infringement, was always going to produce exactly this kind of dispute once enough time passed between the harm and the payout — and with the underlying infringement now four to five years in the past for the oldest claims, “enough time” has already arrived for a meaningful number of authors.

A preview for every AI company still fighting instead of settling

Anthropic is not the only company facing this exposure, and the split dispute now playing out across thousands of individual claims is a preview of what awaits any other AI company that settles a similar case rather than litigating it to a verdict. Meta’s own author lawsuit and OpenAI’s still-unresolved copyright fights, discussed elsewhere in this series, are both built on the same underlying theory — pirated or improperly licensed creative work used to train a commercial product — and any eventual settlement in either case will run into the identical contract-language gap that is currently tying up Anthropic’s payout. The number attached to a settlement is the easy part to negotiate, because it is a single figure both sides can argue over in mediation. Distributing that number correctly across a century of publishing contracts nobody wrote with AI in mind is the part no settlement agreement can pre-solve, no matter how large the headline figure is. Every company currently calculating whether settling is cheaper than trial should be pricing in not just the settlement figure itself, but the multi-year administrative tail of disputes like Henry’s and Bair’s that follows it.

Everyone opted in except the people actually harmed

Part III of this series was built around a different but related irony — that while courts fight over whether AI companies stole creative work to train on, the industry’s more urgent problem was turning out to be who was stealing from the compensation funds now flowing back the other way. The Anthropic settlement adds a subtler variant to that pattern. Nobody here is accused of fraud. Publishers largely appear to be following the letter of contracts signed years or decades before generative AI existed, and the settlement’s own architecture — negotiated with input from publishers and the Authors Guild alike — set the default splits everyone is now fighting over. And yet the practical result, case after case, is the same: the authors whose books were actually pirated are the ones most likely to end up arguing for a bigger share of the money a court ordered paid specifically because their work was stolen.

That is not a flaw unique to Anthropic’s settlement. It is what happens whenever compensation for a genuinely new kind of harm gets forced through contract language written for an entirely different world — the same gap García points to in the Eminem case, replayed now for a generation of authors who never imagined their backlist would end up as AI training data in the first place. The $1.5 billion headline number was never really the hard part. Figuring out, book by book and contract by contract, who that number actually belongs to — that is the fight that will still be running long after the settlement itself is closed out.
