You may have noticed the silence. It was not voluntary. An algorithm charged with guarding against spam concluded that a man who publishes too much is indistinguishable from a machine that publishes too much — a confusion I have decided to find flattering. This belongs to the Sunday series, and today is not Sunday, but a fable about being silenced is a poor candidate for sitting quietly in a drawer until the calendar permits. The full account is below. We are back.

Once, in a kingdom made entirely of words, there lived a writer who could not stop writing.

This was his only crime, though it would take the kingdom some time to charge him with it.

He had written, by the morning in question, one thousand two hundred and thirteen essays — on tyrants and tariffs, on senators and saints, on the slow strange business of a republic talking itself out of being one. He wrote about kings who mistook the throne for the crown and the crown for the man. He wrote, in particular and at length, about the way power likes to decide who may speak, and then to call the silence “order.”

He published these every morning at nine o’clock, the way other men drink coffee.


Now, the kingdom of words was guarded, as all kingdoms are, by a sentinel. But this sentinel was not a knight, nor a censor in robes, nor even a man. It was an Algorithm — a small, tireless, joyless machine that had been given one instruction and the certainty that the instruction was the whole of wisdom.

Guard against spam, it had been told. Guard against phishing.

The Algorithm did not know what spam was, exactly, in the way that a man knows things. It knew only the shape of spam: the pattern of it. Many messages, sent quickly, all at once, from a single source. This, it had decided, was the face of the enemy. It watched for the face the way a faithful dog watches a gate, and it was very proud of how faithful it was.

It had never read a sentence in its life. This was not considered a disqualification for guarding a kingdom of sentences. In the kingdom’s experience, it rather helped.

One morning at nine o’clock, the writer did as he always did. He sent out the day’s work — five essays, perhaps six, queued the night before and released together into the light, the way a farmer opens the gate and lets the whole flock through at once because that is when the gate is open.

And the Algorithm saw the flock.

Many messages, it observed, with the quiet thrill of a sentry who has finally caught someone. Sent quickly. All at once. From a single source.

It checked its one instruction. It found that the morning matched the shape of the enemy precisely.

It did not read the essays. It could not have read the essays. Had it been able to read the essays — had it possessed even the faint flickering candle of getting the joke — it would have noticed that the writer it was about to silence had spent eleven hundred mornings warning the kingdom about exactly this: about small powers, given one rule, mistaking the rule for the truth, and the truth for the enemy.

But the Algorithm did not get the joke. Getting the joke was not in its instruction set. Getting the joke is, in fact, the one thing no tyrant has ever managed, large or small, silicon or flesh — because to get the joke you must first suspect you might be in it.

So the faithful machine did the faithful thing. It lowered the gate. It hung a notice upon it, in the language all such notices are written in, which is the language of having decided you are guilty before deciding what of:

Your account is currently suspended. Something you posted may have violated our policy against Spam and Phishing.

It is worth pausing to admire the bar. In the kingdom next door — a large republic, much given to founding documents — they had decided that removing a man from the highest office in the land required Treason, or Bribery, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors, a trial, and two-thirds of the senate willing to say so out loud. To remove a writer from his small house of words, the Algorithm required only that he had posted before breakfast. The surest way to make a man hard to remove, it seemed, was to make him powerful; the surest way to make him easy to remove was to make him merely prolific.

Spam. Phishing. The two crimes, of all the crimes in the kingdom, that a man who deals only in words can least commit — for he sells nothing, hooks no one, asks only to be read. The Algorithm had reached into its small bag of accusations and pulled out the two that fit the writer least, and it had done so with total confidence, because confidence, too, requires no reading.

And here, in most fables, the fable ends. The gate is shut. The writer stands outside it in the cold. The machine returns to its watch, satisfied, having protected the kingdom of words from one of the few people in it still making any.

That is how the machine believed the story went. That is how every petty tyrant believes the story goes. The dungeon, they are certain, is the last page. Lower the gate and the voice stops, because surely — surely — the voice was only ever the gate.

But the machine had made the oldest mistake in the oldest book, the mistake King John made with his barons and every sovereign has made since: it had confused the megaphone with the man.

For the writer, standing outside his own locked gate, did not stop writing. He could no more stop writing than the gate could start reading. He simply turned, and walked to a quieter room — an ordinary room, in a corner of the world the Algorithm did not patrol and could not see — and he set down the day’s work there. And the next day’s. And the day’s after that. Among them, an essay about a king named John, who once learned at Runnymede what every gate-keeper eventually learns — though that one would have to wait its turn, as it happened, for later in the day.

The words went on accumulating. They always do. That is the inconvenient thing about words, the thing that every gate-lowering power discovers too late: you can suspend the publication, but you cannot suspend the person, and it is the person who has the sentences.

The Algorithm watched its gate, faithfully, and saw no flock come through, and was pleased. It mistook the quiet for victory. It had silenced the kingdom of words, it believed, and it would go on believing this right up until the morning the gate opened again and eleven hundred essays’ worth of momentum came through it all at once — which, the Algorithm would surely note, sent quickly, all at once, from a single source, matched the shape of the enemy precisely.

The moral: A machine given one rule will defend it to the death of every truth the rule was meant to protect. And a writer given one gate will simply find another room. The tyrant counts the silence and calls it order. The chronicler counts the silence and calls it Tuesday, and keeps writing by candlelight, because the chronicle was never the candle, and was never the gate, and was always — only, stubbornly, unsuspendably — the man holding the pen.

Composed in exile, in a quieter room, and held until the gate opens.
