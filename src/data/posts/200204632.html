This series has tracked, across thirty-three previous installments, the places where democracy’s immune system has activated — the courts that blocked discriminatory maps, the state senates that refused to redraw them, the individual officials who calculated that some things were worth more than their careers.

New York Times reporting from a federal appeals court in Washington provides the foundation for this entry, and it concerns not redistricting but the United States military. The principle, however, is the same: when executive power overreaches, the courts occasionally find language precise enough to name what is happening.

On Monday June 1, a divided panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit blocked the Trump administration from removing 28 transgender service members from the military while their lawsuit proceeds. The ruling was 2-to-1. It applies, for now, only to those 28 plaintiffs. A hearing is scheduled this month on whether the protection should extend to all transgender troops.

The majority opinion, written by Judge Robert L. Wilkins and joined by Senior Judge Judith W. Rogers, found that the government had shown little evidence that transgender troops harm military operations. It found something else as well — something more specific, and more damning.

The Trump administration’s policy, the majority wrote, appeared to be “driven by the bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group.”

The Phrase That Cannot Be Walked Back

“The bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group” is not routine judicial language. It is a finding. It says, in the precise and consequential vocabulary of constitutional law, that the court examined the government’s justifications and found them insufficient to conceal the actual motivation.

In constitutional jurisprudence, this matters enormously. Government actions that are “driven by the bare desire to harm” a group — without legitimate governmental interest behind them — fail constitutional scrutiny. The majority did not say the policy was merely unwise, or poorly implemented, or in tension with military readiness. It said the policy appeared designed to harm people the administration dislikes.

Judge Justin Walker, appointed by President Trump, dissented. Courts, he wrote, lacked the expertise and authority to make decisions about the military. “The Constitution assigns that authority to Congress and the Commander in Chief.”

This is a coherent position, and it may ultimately prevail — particularly if the case reaches the Supreme Court, which last year moved quickly to overturn a similar ruling in the parallel Shilling case. But it is worth noting what the dissent does not dispute: it does not contest the majority’s characterization of the policy’s motivation. It contests the court’s authority to act on that characterization.

Two Cases, Two Arguments

Monday’s ruling comes in the case of lead plaintiff Nicholas Talbott, a second lieutenant in the U.S. Army Reserve. It is one of two major legal challenges to the transgender ban — and the distinction between them matters for what comes next.

The Shilling case, brought by Emily Shilling, a decorated Navy aviator, focused on whether discrimination against transgender people constitutes sex discrimination. That case reached the Supreme Court last year, which moved swiftly — in a pattern this series has documented repeatedly — to allow the ban to take effect while litigation continued.

The Talbott case makes a different argument: not that the ban is sex discrimination, but that it reflects unconstitutional hostility directed at a politically unpopular group. This argument draws on a more established line of constitutional precedent — the principle, developed across decades of Supreme Court decisions, that government cannot act out of “mere disapproval” or “animus” toward a group of people.

Shannon Minter, one of the lawyers representing the troops, called the distinction significant. The animus argument, he said, has “simpler reasoning with a more established precedent.” Whether the Supreme Court would accept that reasoning — given its 2025 intervention in Shilling — remains genuinely uncertain. But the legal architecture of the Talbott case is built on different ground.

The Arithmetic of Those Remaining

About 4,200 openly transgender people were serving in the military when the ban took effect, according to government court filings — roughly 0.1 percent of the armed forces. They were infantry platoon leaders, pilots, nuclear engineers, doctors, intelligence analysts, and police officers.

In the past year, many left voluntarily rather than face the uncertainty and potential harm of contesting the policy. Hundreds of others stayed — insisting that the military formally convene retention boards and force them out rather than allowing the administration to accomplish their removal through bureaucratic pressure. Many of those members remain in uniform, unable to work, still drawing government pay, suspended in institutional limbo while the litigation proceeds.

Monday’s ruling allows the 28 plaintiffs currently in uniform to continue serving while the case moves forward. It bars the administration from removing transgender recruits, however — a distinction that leaves the next generation of potential service members outside its protection.

This is the texture of what “winning” looks like in the current legal environment: narrow, contingent, subject to reversal, and purchased at enormous personal cost by the individuals willing to put their names on a lawsuit and wait.

What the Immune System Looks Like From Here

This series began with redistricting — with the specific, mappable ways that democratic representation is being dismantled one congressional district at a time. It has expanded, over thirty-four installments, to encompass the broader question of where democratic institutions hold and where they fracture.

What Monday’s ruling adds to that picture is both encouraging and clarifying.

Encouraging: two federal judges, appointed by presidents of both parties, looked at a policy affecting 4,200 people representing 0.1 percent of the armed forces — people who flew combat missions, survived IEDs, led SEAL teams, and served in nuclear engineering roles — and found that the government had produced little evidence they harm military operations. The policy’s actual driver, the majority concluded, was something else entirely.

Clarifying: the ruling applies to 28 people. The Supreme Court, which overturned the Shilling ruling within months, remains the final arbiter. Judge Walker’s dissent — courts should not second-guess military command decisions — reflects a view held by at least four current justices. The path from Monday’s ruling to durable protection for transgender service members is long, and the terrain is hostile.

What the courts have given, in this instance, is time and language. Time for the case to develop. Language — “the bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group” — that names what is happening with the precision that makes it harder to pretend otherwise.

In a series that has spent thirty-three installments documenting the moments when democratic accountability functions despite determined efforts to disable it, that is not nothing.

It is, in fact, exactly what democracy’s immune system looks like when it is working at partial capacity under severe stress: imperfect, contested, contingent, and absolutely necessary.
