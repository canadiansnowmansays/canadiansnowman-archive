Postmark: A laboratory, somewhere on Earth. A six-legged robot named SCAR-E, built for microgravity and rugged alien terrain, spends its actual working hours crawling the hull of a docked ship. It has never seen an asteroid.

Todays dispatch comes to you from the edge of the solar system, on a frontier that exists almost entirely in the future tense — and I want to be honest about that up front, because this postcard is as much about the discipline of hope as it is about the robots themselves. The asteroid-mining industry, as of 2026, has extracted precisely nothing from any asteroid. As one careful piece of reporting put it, with a bluntness this series has come to appreciate, the market for actually mined asteroid material currently stands at zero dollars, compared to a nearly thirteen-billion-dollar market for the mundane, Earth-bound industrial inspection work the same robots do to keep their companies solvent in the meantime. Every postcard this series has filed has described a machine already doing something. This one describes machines built to do something that has, quite literally, never been done by anyone, ever, in the history of the species.

And yet the preparation for that first act is real, well-funded, and further along than the industry’s science-fiction reputation suggests.

The robot that inspects hulls while it waits for the asteroid

The most vivid character in this postcard is a six-legged machine called SCAR-E — the Space Capable Asteroid Robotic Explorer — built by the UK-based Asteroid Mining Corporation. As Undark reported, SCAR-E is designed to operate in microgravity, crawling across a rugged, irregular surface no wheeled vehicle could navigate, gathering data and physical samples as it goes. Its manufacturer plans a demonstration mission analyzing lunar soil, and eventual deployment testing on the International Space Station and the Moon’s surface in the years just ahead. But for now, and for the plainly stated foreseeable future, SCAR-E’s actual paycheck comes from a job that has nothing to do with asteroids at all: as the same reporting noted, the company deploys its ruggedized, autonomous, six-legged design to inspect ship hulls here on Earth, in the hazardous, cramped, corrosion-prone spaces a human inspector finds tedious and sometimes dangerous to access. This is, in its way, one of the most honest business models in this entire series — a robot engineered for a trillion-dollar dream, earning its keep in the meantime doing unglamorous industrial work that happens to share the same core capability. The asteroid is the mission. The ship hull is the mortgage payment.

Other players in the field are pursuing the same patient, incremental logic from different angles. As Interesting Engineering has reported, the Los Angeles company TransAstra is advancing a patented process it calls Optical Mining, paired with orbital logistics systems meant to enable sustainable extraction and transport of material once a target asteroid is actually reached — technology it is validating step by step rather than betting everything on in a single dramatic mission. A company called AstroForge sent a mission named Odin this year specifically to image metal-rich, M-type asteroids and identify which ones might actually be worth the enormous cost of visiting a second time, with a follow-up Vestri mission planned to test in-orbit refinery systems for extracting metal directly at the source rather than hauling raw rock home. None of this is mining, strictly speaking. All of it is the patient groundwork mining will eventually require — the equivalent, in this series’ recurring vocabulary, of the recalibration and latency problems the surgical humanoid of Part XV is still working through before anyone lets it near a human patient.

The swarm logic, again

Readers of this series will recognize the design philosophy emerging across several of these companies, because it is the same one this series has now traced through the garage humanoid, the disaster-response robot, and the field robot’s small-and-cheap counter-current: not one enormous, singular, irreplaceable machine, but many small, redundant ones working in concert.

As one 2026 industry analysis put it with unusual clarity, the economic logic of a robot swarm in this environment is almost embarrassingly simple once stated plainly. Lose one robot out of a hundred-unit swarm mining an asteroid, and you have lost one percent of your capability. Lose your only spacecraft, built as a single irreplaceable unit, and the entire mission is over. Need to process more material as a job grows? Add more robots rather than redesigning the one you have. Encounter unexpected terrain or conditions? The swarm reconfigures itself on the fly, some units scanning while others extract or transport, adapting the way Part XX’s disaster-response swarms adapt to an unpredictable rubble field. A California-based company called OffWorld is developing exactly this kind of autonomous industrial robot swarm explicitly for off-planet excavation, with stated applications spanning both lunar and asteroid environments — the same machine, the same logic, deployed wherever digging needs doing far from any repair shop. Even Blue Origin, a company built on the very different, very singular architecture of large rockets, has acquired Honeybee Robotics specifically to bring its robotic-arm and sample-collection expertise, developed originally for Mars missions, into this swarm-based asteroid future.

There is a genuinely elegant engineering answer buried in this postcard that connects directly to a problem this series first raised on the battlefield in Part XVI: the communication lag between Earth and anything beyond low orbit. A signal to an asteroid can take minutes each way, which makes real-time human piloting of a mining robot essentially impossible — by the time an operator on Earth sees a problem and sends a correction, the robot may have already driven off a ledge that no longer exists in the operator’s outdated picture of the scene. As one 2026 industry assessment described it, this has been addressed by equipping mining robots with onboard edge-computing systems capable of real-time decision-making without waiting for instructions from Earth at all — full autonomy, adopted here not as an ideological preference for machines over humans but as the only physically possible solution to the plain fact that light itself takes too long to make the round trip. This is the clearest, least contestable case for full machine autonomy this entire series has encountered: not a choice between trusting a human or a robot with a decision, but a situation where no human decision can arrive in time to matter at all.

The nations reaching for the same rock

This frontier, like every other this series has visited, is not merely a private commercial venture — a state actor is already in the race, and its presence changes the character of the whole endeavour.

As one 2026 report on the industry noted, China’s Tianwen-2 mission is targeting 2026 for a rendezvous with the near-Earth asteroid Kamo’oalewa, attempting to collect a physical sample and return it to Earth by late 2027, before continuing on to a decade-long observation mission at a distant comet. This is not a private company chasing venture capital on a speculative timeline; it is a national space program committing years of mission planning and a state budget to a sample-return effort, run in parallel with the same private ventures this postcard has otherwise described. A Chinese company called Origin Space has, separately, developed robots specifically for asteroid observation and resource acquisition, supported by a dedicated space telescope constellation built for asteroid detection — an integrated observation-to-extraction pipeline that echoes, in miniature, the whole-of-nation approach to humanoid robotics Part XIV documented on the ground. The pattern this series has traced from the factory floor to the seabed cable repeats here on the largest possible scale: wherever there is a frontier worth claiming, a state with the patience for a multi-year program tends to arrive alongside the venture-funded startups, and often outlasts them.

The number nobody can quite believe

No account of this frontier is complete without confronting the single figure that made asteroid mining a headline story in the first place, and it belongs to an object NASA itself is already flying toward — not a private company’s speculative target, but the subject of an active, funded, government-led mission.

The asteroid in question is 16 Psyche, a metal-rich body in the main belt between Mars and Jupiter roughly the size of Massachusetts, and early estimates of its metal content produced a valuation so large it stopped making intuitive sense: ten thousand quadrillion dollars, a figure with eighteen zeroes, more than the entire world economy could produce in tens of thousands of years. As University of Arizona researchers reported, that number was always closer to a thought experiment than a market forecast, and more recent laboratory work — recreating Psyche’s surface material and matching it against telescope observations — has already revised the asteroid’s estimated composition down from a presumed solid iron core toward something closer to eighty percent metal mixed with rock and carbon-rich material likely deposited by other, smaller impacts over billions of years. NASA’s own Psyche spacecraft, launched in 2023 and now scheduled to arrive at the asteroid in 2029 after a Mars gravity-assist flyby, exists specifically to resolve this uncertainty with direct measurement rather than telescope inference — and its stated scientific purpose has nothing to do with mining at all. The mission’s actual goal is to examine what may be the exposed metallic core of a shattered protoplanet, offering scientists a way to study, at a distance of hundreds of millions of kilometres, the same kind of core that lies unreachably deep beneath our own feet, sealed under Earth’s rock and mantle where no drill will ever go.

This is worth sitting with, because it complicates the tidy commercial narrative this postcard has otherwise told. The most valuable asteroid target anyone has publicly discussed is being visited first not by a mining company’s robot but by a pure science mission, seeking not profit but an answer to one of planetary geology’s oldest open questions: how did a rocky planet like Earth end up with a metal heart in the first place. The mining industry’s entire commercial case rests, at least in part, on scientific groundwork it did not fund and does not control, laid by researchers whose interest in Psyche has nothing to do with platinum futures. If SCAR-E and its private-sector cousins ever do reach a metal-rich asteroid to extract anything at all, they will very likely be walking a path that a government space agency, chasing a wholly different kind of value, mapped for them first.

The rock that could break a market

There is one more honest complication to this postcard, and it is worth stating clearly because it cuts against the industry’s own optimistic pitch: the very abundance this frontier promises could, if it ever fully arrives, undermine the economics that make it worth pursuing at all.

Near-Earth asteroids are estimated by industry optimists to contain platinum-group metals worth trillions of dollars in aggregate — the same metals used in catalytic converters, electronics, and a range of industrial processes on Earth, where they remain rare and expensive precisely because so little of the planet’s crust contains them in workable concentration. But a market’s value depends on scarcity, and platinum’s price today reflects a global supply constrained to a handful of terrestrial mines. A single successfully mined asteroid, delivering even a modest fraction of the metal some estimates project, could flood that market and collapse the very price that made the mission economically attractive to fund in the first place — the asteroid-mining equivalent of striking oil so successfully that oil becomes worthless. This is not a reason to doubt the engineering described in this postcard. It is a reminder that the “trillion-dollar” framing so many industry reports reach for assumes a market structure that the industry’s own success would immediately begin to dismantle — one more layer of uncertainty stacked atop the legal and technical ones, and one more reason the patient, incremental pace of this frontier may be wiser than the breathless headlines suggest.

What the empty ledger tells us

From the balcony, this postcard’s honest ledger — years of investment, genuine engineering progress, and precisely zero dollars of asteroid material actually mined — is not a story of failure. It is the most candid picture this series has offered of what a genuine frontier actually looks like while it is still being built, before the triumphant headline arrives, if it ever does.

The legal and economic obstacles are as real as the engineering ones and deserve to be stated plainly rather than waved away. The Outer Space Treaty, the foundational law governing activity beyond Earth, was written in 1967 with no clear answer to who, if anyone, owns a mineral extracted from an asteroid that belongs to no nation — an ambiguity that has not stopped companies from proceeding, but that leaves every dollar of investment resting on a legal foundation nobody has fully tested. The costs remain almost unimaginably large, the insurance questions largely unsolved, and the industry’s own promotional history carries an honest and slightly embarrassing paper trail — a 2015 headline confidently predicted asteroid mining would be a commercial reality by 2025, a decade of overpromising this postcard’s own honesty requires acknowledging rather than repeating.

And yet the incremental, patient work described here — the hull-inspecting robot earning its keep, the imaging missions quietly identifying which rocks are actually worth the trip, the swarm logic solving the redundancy problem, the edge-computing solving the communication-lag problem one engineering decision at a time — is precisely the kind of unglamorous groundwork every genuine frontier requires before its first real breakthrough, whether that breakthrough arrives in five years or fifty. This series closes its current dispatches here, at the very edge of what any of these machines can presently claim to have accomplished, because it is worth remembering, after twenty-three postcards of dancing robots and surgical robots and marathon-winning robots, that the frontier furthest from home is also the one still most honestly in its beginning — a six-legged robot, built to conquer a rock a hundred million kilometres away, presently earning its living inspecting the hull of a ship that never left port.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one has not been mailed yet. It is still being written, one hull inspection at a time, by a robot patiently waiting for its actual assignment to begin.
