Every debate about single-payer healthcare in America eventually hits the same Republican talking point: “Government-run healthcare doesn’t work. Look at the VA! Look at the DMV! Government can’t run anything efficiently!”

Except for one inconvenient fact: America already runs one of the world’s largest single-payer health insurance programs. Medicare enrolls about 69 million people. In its traditional form, it spends a far smaller share of its budget on administration than private insurers do. And Americans who have it rate their coverage more highly than Americans with employer insurance.

It’s called Medicare.

One caveat belongs up front, because the rest of this essay depends on it. More than half of Medicare enrollees, 54% of those eligible in 2025 according to KFF’s “Medicare Advantage in 2025: Enrollment Update and Key Trends” (July 28, 2025), are no longer in the government plan at all. They are in private Medicare Advantage plans, paid for by Medicare but run by insurance companies. The genuinely single-payer part, traditional Medicare, covers about 34 million people (CMS enrollment data, November 2025). That is still one of the largest single-payer programs on earth.

This is Part 7 of Code Blue, our investigation into America’s 80-year healthcare failure. We’ve documented the broken promises (Parts 1-2), followed the money (Part 3), examined international models (Part 4), described the bipartisan compromise nobody wants (Part 5), and exposed the lobbying apparatus that prevents reform (Part 6).

Now we examine the elephant in the room: America already runs a single-payer program that works. We just restrict it, with a few exceptions for people with disabilities or kidney failure, to people over 65.

The Numbers Don’t Lie

Let’s start with what makes Medicare different from private insurance:

Administrative overhead:

Traditional Medicare: roughly 1-2% of spending by the Medicare Trustees’ accounting

Private insurance: commonly estimated at 12-18% of spending

The ACA’s medical-loss-ratio rule lets private insurers keep up to 15-20% of premiums for administration and profit

Translation: For every $100 spent, traditional Medicare spends a dollar or two on administration (claims processing, billing, overhead). Private insurers spend many times that.

Where does the extra go?

Marketing departments

Profit margins for shareholders

Executive compensation (UnitedHealth CEO: $23.5 million in 2023)

Complex network management

Claims denial departments

Prior authorization bureaucracy

Traditional Medicare needs very little of that. You turn 65, you sign up (or are enrolled automatically if you already draw Social Security), you show your card, you get care. No networks. Little prior authorization. Far less fighting about coverage.

Satisfaction rates:

KFF’s 2023 Survey of Consumer Experiences with Health Insurance asked insured adults to rate their coverage:

Medicare: 91% rated it excellent or good

Medicaid: 83%

Employer insurance: 80%

Marketplace (ACA) plans: 73%

Medicare enrollees were also the least likely to report a problem with their insurance in the past year: 51%, against 60% of people with employer coverage.

The gap is not new. A Commonwealth Fund survey back in 2007 found that 8% of Medicare beneficiaries rated their coverage fair or poor, compared with 18% of people with employer plans.

Why? Because Medicare delivers most of what it promises:

No exclusions for pre-existing conditions

No networks in traditional Medicare

No prior authorization for most care in traditional Medicare

No annual or lifetime dollar limits on most covered care

Coverage that continues regardless of employment status

What it doesn’t promise is a ceiling on what you pay. Traditional Medicare has no out-of-pocket maximum. After the deductibles, Part B generally leaves you 20% of the bill, with no upper limit. That is why only 13% of traditional Medicare enrollees go without some form of supplemental coverage, according to KFF’s “A Snapshot of Sources of Coverage Among Medicare Beneficiaries” (December 2025, using 2023 data). Most of the rest buy a private Medigap policy, keep a retiree plan from an employer, or qualify for Medicaid.

Coverage comprehensiveness:

Medicare covers:

Hospital care (Part A)

Physician services (Part B)

Prescription drugs (Part D, through separate private plans; out-of-pocket drug costs capped at $2,000 in 2025)

Preventive services (no cost-sharing)

What it doesn’t cover well:

Dental (limited)

Vision (limited)

Hearing (limited)

Long-term care (not covered)

A cap on your own hospital and doctor bills (none in traditional Medicare)

But for core medical care, the difference between life and death, health and sickness, Medicare covers it. With a supplement to close the out-of-pocket gap, it covers it more reliably than most private insurance, without the networks and coverage denials.

The Political Third Rail

Here’s where it gets interesting: Try cutting Medicare benefits in a red state and watch the town hall revolt.

2017: The Republican Healthcare Debacle

Remember when Republicans tried to repeal Obamacare? The town halls were brutal. Angry constituents shouting at their representatives.

But notice what they weren’t angry about: Nobody was demanding Medicare cuts. Nobody was saying “please reduce my Medicare benefits.” Nobody was arguing that Medicare doesn’t work.

Some of those defending the ACA were themselves on Medicare, speaking up for children and grandchildren who needed coverage.

2011: Paul Ryan’s Medicare Voucher Proposal

Representative Paul Ryan (R-WI) proposed converting Medicare, for people then under 55, into a “premium support” system: a fixed amount of money to buy private insurance instead of guaranteed government coverage.

Republican reaction: The House passed it on April 15, 2011, by 235 to 193, with almost every Republican voting yes and no Democrats (PBS NewsHour).

Then the politics caught up with it. On May 25, 2011, the Senate rejected the Ryan budget 40 to 57, with several Republicans joining Democrats against it (NPR, “Senate Republicans Get 40 Votes For Paul Ryan Medicare Plan”). Democrats spent the next campaign reminding seniors who had voted for it.

Why? Because seniors vote. And seniors love Medicare. And telling seniors you’re taking away Medicare is political suicide.

Ryan’s plan never became law. Not because it wouldn’t save federal money (it probably would, largely by shifting costs to seniors). But because it was politically toxic.

The “hands off my Medicare” paradox:

In the summer of 2009, at a town hall in Simpsonville, South Carolina, a man told Republican Representative Bob Inglis to “keep your government hands off my Medicare,” as Philip Rucker reported in the Washington Post on July 28, 2009. Inglis tried to explain that Medicare is the government. The man wasn’t having it.

The cognitive dissonance is stunning. Many seniors on Medicare, a single-payer, government-run, tax-funded program, oppose single-payer, government-run, tax-funded healthcare for everyone else.

Why? Because they have theirs. And they’re afraid that expanding it to younger people will somehow diminish what they receive.

What Medicare Actually Costs

2024 Medicare spending: $1.12 trillion (CMS National Health Expenditure Fact Sheet) 2024 Medicare enrollment: about 68 million beneficiaries Per capita spending: roughly $16,400

Wait. CMS puts national health spending at $15,474 per person in 2024. Why is Medicare more expensive?

Answer: Because Medicare covers the oldest and many of the sickest Americans. People over 65 use more healthcare. That’s expected.

The miracle isn’t that Medicare spends about $16,400 per beneficiary. The miracle is that traditional Medicare delivers care to tens of millions of older and disabled Americans at a small fraction of the administrative overhead that private insurers carry while covering younger, healthier people.

Funding sources:

Payroll taxes: 2.9% (1.45% employer, 1.45% employee)

Premiums (Part B): $185/month for most beneficiaries in 2025, rising to $202.90 in 2026 (CMS, November 14, 2025)

General revenue: Federal budget

Part D premiums: Varies by plan

Is Medicare sustainable? That’s the wrong question. The right question is: “Is it more sustainable than the current private insurance system?”

Answer: Yes.

Medicare pays hospitals and physicians substantially less than private insurers do. Traditional Medicare has lower administrative costs. It doesn’t extract profit margins.

The “Medicare is going bankrupt” scare is about the Hospital Insurance Trust Fund (Part A). The 2025 Medicare Trustees Report projects that fund will be depleted in 2033, after which incoming revenue would still cover most scheduled benefits. The Trustees put the 75-year shortfall at 0.42% of taxable payroll. In other words, raising the combined payroll tax by less than half a percentage point, from 2.9% to roughly 3.3%, would close it on the Trustees’ own numbers.

Compare that to private insurance. KFF’s 2025 Employer Health Benefits Survey found average family premiums up 6% in a single year, to nearly $27,000.

Medicare Advantage: The Privatization Experiment

Here’s where it gets complicated.

Medicare Advantage: Private insurance companies receive payments from Medicare to provide coverage to seniors instead of traditional Medicare.

Current enrollment: About 54% of eligible Medicare beneficiaries, 34 million people, chose Medicare Advantage over traditional Medicare in 2025 (KFF, July 28, 2025).

Why? Partly marketing. Medicare Advantage plans often offer:

$0 premiums beyond the Part B premium (Medicare pays the insurer)

Gym memberships

Dental and vision coverage

An annual cap on in-network out-of-pocket costs, which traditional Medicare lacks

Sounds great, right?

The catch:

Networks restrict which doctors you can see

Prior authorization required for many services

Higher out-of-pocket costs when you’re actually sick

Wrongful denials: In 2022, the HHS Inspector General reviewed a sample of Medicare Advantage denials and found that 13% of prior-authorization denials, and 18% of payment denials, were for care that met Medicare’s coverage rules and would likely have been approved under traditional Medicare (HHS OIG, OEI-09-18-00260, April 27, 2022)

The cost to taxpayers: The Medicare Payment Advisory Commission estimates that Medicare spends about 20% more for Medicare Advantage enrollees than it would if they were in traditional Medicare, roughly $84 billion in 2025, mostly because of coding intensity and favorable selection (MedPAC, March 2025 Report to the Congress). The federal government pays extra for coverage that comes with networks and prior authorization.

Why does this exist? Lobbying. Insurance companies lobbied for Medicare Advantage (originally called Medicare+Choice in 1997, rebranded in 2003). They argued “private competition will reduce costs.”

Result: Costs went up. Insurance company profits went up. Seniors got networks, prior authorizations, and claim denials.

And leaving isn’t simple. Someone who joins an Advantage plan and later tries to return to traditional Medicare can, in most states, be medically underwritten for a Medigap supplement. Without one, they face traditional Medicare’s lack of an out-of-pocket cap on their own.

But more than half of those eligible still choose Medicare Advantage because the marketing is better and the upfront costs are lower (until you get sick).

The Expansion Debate

If Medicare works so well, why not expand it?

Medicare for All proposals:

Bernie Sanders version: Expand Medicare to cover everyone, eliminate private insurance for core coverage

Public option version: Let people under 65 buy into Medicare, keep private insurance as an option

Medicare at 50 version: Lower eligibility age gradually (60, then 55, then 50)

Arguments for:

Traditional Medicare already works

Administrative costs would fall toward traditional Medicare’s level

Negotiating power of about 340 million people instead of a fragmented market

Would sharply reduce medical debt, if cost-sharing is kept low and capped

Simplifies billing for doctors and hospitals

Arguments against:

Higher federal spending: the Mercatus Center’s 2018 estimate put the added federal cost of the Sanders bill at $32.6 trillion over ten years, much of it replacing what households and employers now pay in premiums

Physician and hospital payment rates might decrease

Some people would pay more in taxes than they now pay in premiums

Insurance industry would lose most of its business, and many of its jobs

Political difficulty of passing it

The honest answer: Medicare for All is achievable. Other countries run single-payer systems successfully. The question isn’t feasibility. It’s political will.

The German Alternative

Remember from Parts 4 and 5: You don’t have to eliminate private insurance to achieve universal coverage.

Germany’s model:

Statutory health insurance, compulsory for most workers below an income threshold, covers about 88% of Germans

Private insurance available for high earners, civil servants and the self-employed (about 11%)

Both systems regulated for price and coverage

Why this matters for Medicare expansion: America could:

Let anyone buy into Medicare (public option)

Keep private insurance for those who want it

Regulate both systems for price controls

Achieve universal coverage without eliminating private insurance

This gives:

Republicans their “market choice”

Democrats their “universal coverage”

Insurance companies a smaller but still profitable market

Americans the coverage they need

But neither party will propose it (see Part 5).

What Seniors Actually Think

The best recent comparison of the two halves of Medicare comes from the Commonwealth Fund’s 2024 Value of Medicare Survey, as reported by Richard Eisenberg in Fortune on March 15, 2024:

About two in three people in each group, traditional Medicare and Medicare Advantage, said their coverage fully met their expectations

Delayed care while waiting for prior approval: 22% of Medicare Advantage enrollees, against 13% in traditional Medicare

Couldn’t afford care because of co-pays or deductibles: 12% of Medicare Advantage enrollees, against 7% in traditional Medicare

And the comparison with working-age coverage has been consistent for years. In the Commonwealth Fund’s 2007 survey, only 12% of Medicare beneficiaries had gone without care because of cost, against 26% of people with employer plans.

Seniors like their coverage. Whether they would welcome sharing it with everyone else is a different question, and one the polling hasn’t settled.

The International Comparison

Some of the largest single-payer or tax-funded systems in the world:

United Kingdom (NHS): the whole population, roughly 69 million people

United States (Medicare): about 69 million enrolled, but only about 34 million in traditional, single-payer Medicare

Canada (combined provinces and territories): the whole population, about 41 million people

Taiwan National Health Insurance: the whole population, about 23 million people

Systems like these avoid the multi-payer billing apparatus that drives up U.S. private insurers’ overhead.

America already runs one of the world’s largest single-payer programs. Medicare as a whole covers about 20% of the U.S. population. The traditional, single-payer part covers about 10%.

The Absurdity Highlighted

Let’s say you’re 64 years old:

You might have diabetes, requiring insulin that can cost hundreds of dollars a month without insurance

You might need a hip replacement, which can cost tens of thousands of dollars without insurance

You might have high blood pressure, requiring daily medication

Say your private plan has a $6,000 deductible and 20% coinsurance

Say your premiums are $800/month

You live in fear of medical debt

You turn 65:

Part B premium: $185/month in 2025 (most people pay no Part A premium)

Insulin copay: $35/month (IRA cap)

Hip replacement: a $1,676 Part A deductible for the hospital stay, plus 20% of the surgeon’s and other Part B charges, with no annual cap unless you have a supplement

Medications: through a separate Part D plan, with out-of-pocket costs capped at $2,000 a year in 2025

Far less exposure to medical debt, if you can afford a Medigap policy or have other supplemental coverage

Little prior authorization

Your choice of almost any doctor, since about 98% of non-pediatric physicians participate in Medicare

The difference? One birthday. One arbitrary age cutoff.

The healthcare didn’t change. The system changed. From private insurance designed to maximize profit to public insurance designed to deliver care.

The Question Nobody Wants to Answer

If Medicare works so well for seniors, why not for everyone?

Republicans say: “Medicare is different because seniors earned it.”

Response: Everyone who works pays Medicare taxes. Everyone over 65 who qualifies gets the same Medicare regardless of how much they paid in. It’s already a universal system for its age group.

Democrats say: “We support Medicare for All!”

Response: Then why didn’t you pass it in 2021-2022 when you controlled Congress? (See Part 5.)

Insurance companies say: “Medicare only works because it piggybacks on private insurance pricing.”

Response: Medicare pays lower rates than private insurance. Hospitals accept Medicare rates. Doctors accept Medicare rates. The system works.

Hospital administrators say: “Medicare doesn’t pay enough to cover costs.”

Response: There is something to this. MedPAC put hospitals’ overall Medicare margin at about −13% in 2023 (HFMA, March 21, 2025). But hospitals still compete hard for Medicare patients. The case for expansion is that one set of rates, set high enough to sustain hospitals and paid without the billing apparatus private plans require, would cost less overall than today’s patchwork.

The truth nobody wants to say:

America already has a working single-payer program. Traditional Medicare delivers higher satisfaction and far lower administrative costs than private insurance. It needs an out-of-pocket cap to finish the job.

We just restrict it to people over 65 because apparently only seniors deserve decent healthcare.
