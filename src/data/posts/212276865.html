From the Illuminated Registers of the Court, Newly Transcribed

In Which the Herald Announces the Terms

Let it be recorded that on the appointed evening, the Herald of Trade — a functionary of middling rank but considerable self-regard, one Master Greer — stood before the assembled court and declared the terms generous beyond any vassal’s deserving. The northern lord’s steel would be taxed at but half its former burden, he announced, though only up to a certain measure, beyond which the old burden would resume in full. The northern lord’s carriages, too, would be taxed more lightly, though the discount applied only to such parts as were forged within the King’s own borders. This, the Herald said, was “the best treatment afforded any exporter to the realm,” and he seemed, by all accounts, to believe it.

Of the Prophecy Spoken Three Days Prior

It should be noted, for the completeness of the record, that three days before the reckoning, the King’s own Regent had spoken at a gathering of young lords and their patrons in a coastal manor, and had made sport of the northern lord’s pretensions. The Regent said — and this was later repeated well beyond the hall where he said it, as such things are — that the northern lord made a great show of puffing out his chest and declaring he would out-match the King, when in truth, the Regent said, he always climbed down in the end. The gathered lords laughed, as gathered lords do at such things, and the Regent returned to his wine satisfied that the matter was settled before it had even properly begun.

Of the Northern Lord’s Answer

The northern lord did not climb down.

He sent word that the terms offered called into question “the reliability of any deal” struck under them, and that progress, while real, had “not been enough to meet our objectives.” He suspended the talks himself, before the King’s own deadline could suspend them for him — a distinction the court’s chroniclers would do well to note, as it is not nothing to leave a table before you are made to leave it.

The Herald, for his part, informed the assembled press that the northern lord had “walked away,” and blamed him for “new demands and walk backs” that had upended a “careful balance.” Both descriptions may be true. It is a peculiar feature of these chronicles that two men can watch the same door close and each insist the other one shut it.

Of the Metals of the Northern Hills, and Whence the Tax Upon Them Came

Here the scribe permits himself a digression, for the tax on the northern lord’s steel and silver-grey metal did not spring newly from this week’s quarrel. It was first laid eight years past, when the King’s counselors declared — under an ancient statute meant for matters of the realm’s very defense — that foreign metal endangered the kingdom’s ability to arm and build itself. The northern lord’s smiths were, in time, excused from that tax entirely, the King’s own counselors having concluded that metal forged in the northern hills posed no threat to anyone. That excusal held for six years. Then, this past winter, the King revoked it without ceremony, citing the very same ancient statute, as though the six years of excusal and the reasoning behind it had simply never occurred. The scribe leaves it to the reader to judge whether a threat to the realm’s defense can be declared settled, then unsettled, on no new evidence but the King’s changed mood.

Of the Reckoning Itself

At the stroke of midnight, the new taxes fell upon some twenty billion in northern goods, cheese and hockey sticks among them, and the northern lord announced he would answer measure for measure, coin for coin, “to protect our workers and businesses.” A poll taken among his own people found more than half opposed to any further tribute of any kind — which is to say the northern lord did not stand alone so much as he stood where his people had already told him to stand. Even the lord of the western marches, a man given to public affection for the King’s subjects if not the King himself, declared that “everything needs to be on the table” and that his own province was “ready to do its part.”

A Note on the Sling

The scribe is aware that comparisons to smaller shepherds and larger champions are much overused in chronicles such as this one, and resists the urge except to note one thing: the stone was never the point. The point was that the shepherd showed up to the field at all, when everyone at court — the Herald, the Regent, the assembled young lords with their wine — had already agreed among themselves how the day would end.

Closing Entry

The scribe does not know how the next chapter reads. Kings have reversed themselves before, and shepherds have been known to tire. But the record must show what happened on this day, not what everyone at court was so certain would happen instead. On the twenty-first of August, in the year of the King’s second reign, the northern lord did not kneel. Let that much stand, whatever follows it.

— Filed by the Royal Scribe, under protest from the Herald’s office, which requested several corrections that the Scribe has declined to make.
