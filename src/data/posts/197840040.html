Zhongnanhai. Xi offered Trump rose seeds. Trump accepted. The war in Iran continued.

I. The Notebook

Between the toast and the tiramisu, the cameras caught something the protocol hadn’t planned.

At the state banquet Thursday evening, footage circulated on social media showing Trump reaching toward what appeared to be Xi’s private notebook while the Chinese leader had stepped away from the table (Trump and Xi Play Up Stability Without Resolving Major Tensions). Secretary of State Marco Rubio, seated nearby, appeared to notice the cameras noticing — and turned his face away immediately.

In the intelligence community, this is called HUMINT. At the Great Hall of the People, it’s called getting caught.

The irony writes itself. While Trump’s own Treasury Department was sanctioning Chinese firms for providing satellite imagery to Iran — intelligence gathering in service of a foreign military (Spies, Sanctions, Cyberattacks: China and the U.S. Clash Behind the Scenes) — the president of the United States was caught on camera trying to read his host’s private notes at a state dinner. The scale was different. The instinct was the same.

Whether Beijing chose to let the footage circulate is itself a signal. In the Great Hall of the People, there are no private moments. Only moments Beijing chooses not to release.

II. The Declining Nation

Trump woke up Friday morning in Beijing and did what he does: he posted.

“When President Xi very elegantly referred to the United States as perhaps being a declining nation, he was referring to the tremendous damage we suffered during the four years of Sleepy Joe Biden and the Biden Administration, and on that score, he was 100% correct,” he wrote on Truth Social.

The New York Times reporters on the ground noted that Xi never used the phrase “declining nation” in public. But as David Pierson observed, Xi’s signature theme — “great changes unseen in a century” — has always meant exactly that: the demise of the post-war American order. Xi didn’t need to say the words. He built the entire summit around the premise. The Thucydides Trap. The once-in-a-century transformation. The new paradigm for great power relations. Every phrase presupposed that one power was rising and the other was not.

Trump heard the subtext. He couldn’t let it pass. But he also couldn’t push back on Xi directly — not after calling him “a great leader” and “really a friend.” So he did what the TACO framework predicts: he accommodated the insult by redirecting it at a domestic target. Biden did it. Not me. Not on my watch.

The cycle completed before breakfast.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio at the state banquet. China sanctioned him in 2020. Their solution: the sanctions applied to the senator, not the cabinet official. Same man, different title.

III. Behind the Walls

On Friday morning, Xi took Trump somewhere almost no outsider goes: Zhongnanhai (What to Know About the Secretive Compound Where Trump Met Xi).

The walled compound adjacent to the Forbidden City has been the residence and workplace of China’s ruling elite since Mao chose it in 1949. It was here that Mao met Nixon in 1972, ending a quarter-century freeze. It was here that Obama walked with Xi on Yingtai Island in 2014, in what Xinhua described as a “defining moment in bilateral ties.” Putin has been inside. Lukashenko has been inside. Tim Cook and Elon Musk have been inside, multiple times.

Xi told Trump he chose Zhongnanhai to reciprocate for the Mar-a-Lago visit in 2017. The equivalence was flattering. The implication was not. Mar-a-Lago is a private club in Palm Beach with a membership fee. Zhongnanhai is the nerve centre of a party-state that has governed 1.4 billion people for seventy-six years without interruption. One is a business. The other is a civilization’s operating system.

They sat in a pavilion. They drank tea. They had a working lunch. Xi walked Trump through the gardens, pointed out the trees and the roses, and offered to send rose seeds to Washington as a gift.

“I love that, that’s great,” Trump said, smiling.

Rose seeds. While Iran burns. While the Strait of Hormuz leaks oil through spoofed transponders (How Iran’s Energy Exports Are Still Headed Toward China). While Taiwan arms itself with $25 billion in weapons it hopes will never be needed. While samarium sells for $2 a kilogram inside China and $500 outside. Xi gave the president of the United States rose seeds, and the president of the United States said he loved it.

Xi also repeated the line from the banquet: “The great rejuvenation of the Chinese nation and making America great again can go hand in hand.” Again, the rejuvenation came first. Then he added something for the Chinese record: “Through strengthened cooperation, both China and the United States can promote their respective development and revitalization.” The word he chose was revitalization — a word you use for something that has declined.

The gardens of Zhongnanhai. Xi pointed out the roses. Trump admired them. The November rare earths deadline was not mentioned.

IV. The Trophies

Trump flew home with trophies. Whether they are real is a different question.

He told Fox News that Xi had agreed to order 200 Boeing jets (Trump Announces Boeing Jet Order From China. Beijing Stays Silent). Boeing’s chief executive was in the delegation. Boeing did not confirm the order. When asked at the foreign ministry’s daily briefing, a Chinese spokesman said only that trade ties were “mutually beneficial.” Numerous predictions of large Boeing deals have preceded recent summits. None have materialized. The last major Chinese Boeing order — 50 planes from a state-owned leasing arm — happened in September 2024, separately from any summit. China has fewer than 200 outstanding Boeing orders, compared with nearly 500 from Airbus.

Jamieson Greer, Trump’s trade representative, said he expected China to buy more than $10 billion in U.S. agricultural products and confirmed a “Board of Trade” to oversee tariff reductions on roughly $30 billion in goods (China Starting to Fulfill ‘Promises,’ U.S. Trade Representative Says). “We’re already seeing them start to fulfill some of their promises,” he said. China’s foreign ministry did not confirm the numbers. It said China was ready to work on “economic understandings.”

Promises and understandings. These are not contracts. They are not signed documents. They are the kind of words that two governments use when they want to announce progress without committing to anything that can be measured, audited, or enforced. Trump has said that foreign investment pledges during his second term total $20 trillion. His own White House tracker puts the number at $10.6 trillion (Trump Might Welcome Chinese Investment, but America Is Wary). Foreign leaders have learned that they can win favour with Trump by promising whopping numbers they might not fulfil.

Trump also said Xi told him Beijing would refuse to provide Iran with military equipment while America is at war. “He said he’s not going to give military equipment, that’s a big statement,” Trump told Sean Hannity. He added that Xi wanted to see the Strait of Hormuz opened. But there was no public commitment from China to pressure Tehran, and the Times’ analysis of shipping data shows Iranian crude still reaching Chinese waters through spoofed transponders, false flags, and ship-to-ship transfers in the Gulf of Oman. Fifty-one million barrels of Iranian oil sit floating on tankers near Malaysia, down from 85 million in February, but far from zero.

And on the one thing China cares about most — Taiwan — Trump said nothing at all. Not on Day 1. Not on Day 2. Not on the plane home.

Zhongnanhai. ‘We have established a new bilateral relationship, based on constructive strategic stability,’ said Xi. The cage has a name now.

V. What Didn’t Happen

The summit ended without:

A rare earths extension. Keith Bradsher reported no visible progress (How China Could Wield Its Control of Rare Earths Against Trump). Greer gave China “a passing grade” and said there was “willingness” to extend, but no deal was announced. The November deadline stands. Samarium, yttrium, and dysprosium remain almost exclusively Chinese products. Prices remain stratospheric outside China. American aerospace and defence manufacturers remain dependent on a supply chain controlled by the country their own Pentagon identifies as the primary threat.

Any clarity on Nvidia. Despite Jensen Huang travelling with the delegation, the two sides did not discuss chip export controls (Nvidia’s Future in China Remains Unclear After the Trump-Xi Summit). China has not approved purchases of the H200 chip that Trump greenlit in December. DeepSeek announced its latest AI model runs on Huawei chips. The technological decoupling that Huang has warned about is happening in real time, and the summit did nothing to reverse it.

Any movement on Jimmy Lai. Rubio confirmed Trump raised the case. Beijing’s response, after Trump departed: Lai is an “instigator” and “Hong Kong affairs are China’s internal affairs.” A 78-year-old man remains in prison for twenty years. More than 100 bipartisan lawmakers had urged Trump to press for his release. The press was pressed. The release was not.

A joint statement. There was no communiqué, no signed agreement, no document that binds either party. What emerged instead was a phrase — “constructive strategic stability” — that Xi will now use as the baseline for every future interaction. As Christopher Buckley noted, Chinese leadership “often uses broad concepts like that to try to set the terms for bilateral relations.” Doshi’s warning from Day 1 proved prophetic: the cage is built. Any future American effort to challenge China — on overcapacity, on the Indo-Pacific, on export controls — can now be framed as destabilizing the “strategic stability” that both leaders supposedly agreed to.

VI. The TACO Verdict

The Beijing Opera ran for two days, five acts, and approximately fourteen hours of formal meetings, tours, banquets, and garden walks. Here is what happened, scored against the four pillars that have structured this series from Part I:

Trade. Threaten, then Accommodate. Trump came with tariff leverage. He left with unconfirmed Boeing numbers, unconfirmed agricultural pledges, and a Board of Trade that has no structure, no membership, and no enforcement mechanism. China gave beef licenses it can revoke at any time. The price was right: symbolism for substance, exactly as Julian Gewirtz predicted on Day 1. Score: Accommodate.

Taiwan. Xi Threatened. Trump was silent. The $13 billion arms package remains on hold. The word “oppose” was never tested. The word “Taiwan” does not appear in the White House readout. Taipei expressed gratitude for American support with the conviction of a country that knows it is being traded. Score: Accommodate.

Tehran. Obfuscate. Both sides agreed the Strait of Hormuz “must remain open.” China made no commitment to pressure Iran. Trump claimed Xi promised no military equipment to Tehran — a claim China has not confirmed. Meanwhile, Iranian oil continues to reach Chinese ports through a blockade that the NYT’s shipping analysis shows is leaking from multiple points. Rubio argued on Fox News that China should help because it’s in China’s interest — “without acknowledging,” as the Times noted, “that it was an American war that precipitated Iran’s closure of the strait in the first place.” Score: Obfuscate.

The Relationship Itself. Capitulate — to the frame. Xi achieved what Yun Sun of the Stimson Center predicted: peer-status validation. He hosted the American president in the Great Hall, the Temple of Heaven, and Zhongnanhai. He received an invitation to the White House. He established “constructive strategic stability” as the governing concept. He gave a history lesson, quoted Thucydides, invoked the century of humiliation, co-opted MAGA into the service of Chinese rejuvenation, and sent the president home with rose seeds and an unconfirmed plane order. Trump called him a great leader, a friend, and said their relationship was the longest between any two presidents in the history of U.S.-China relations. Score: Capitulate to the frame.

VII. The Curtain Falls

Amanda Hsiao was right. They just wanted time.

The Beijing Opera was never about trade deals, or Boeing orders, or beef licenses, or even Taiwan. It was about buying time — time for China to build the semiconductor industry that will make American export controls irrelevant, time to refine the AI models that will run on Huawei chips instead of Nvidia’s, time to complete the military buildup that the Pentagon catalogues but the White House ignores, time to wait out an American president who is fighting a war in Iran he cannot finish, presiding over an economy he cannot stabilise, and leading a country whose own allies are hedging their bets.

Xi paid for that time with pageantry. Red carpets, honour guards, choreographed children, imperial porcelain, Peking duck, rose gardens, and a Thucydides lecture. The cost was negligible. The return was strategic stability — his phrase, his frame, his cage.

Trump paid with flattery, silence on Taiwan, and the willingness to call a man who has imprisoned a million Uyghurs, sentenced Jimmy Lai to twenty years, and threatened to invade a democracy of twenty-three million people “really a friend.”

As Air Force One lifted off from Beijing Capital International Airport on Friday afternoon, flag-waving students lined the tarmac one last time. Sanger, who has covered five presidents inside the Great Hall, noted that Trump “wasted no time” — a quick wave, up the stairs, doors closed, wheels rolling. Next stop: Alaska, for refuelling.

The philosopher king stayed behind, in his walled garden, among the roses. The stage was cleared. The costumes were returned. The reviews, such as they were, had already been written — in two languages, describing two different meetings, for two different audiences, in two different countries that agree on almost nothing except that the Strait of Hormuz should probably be open and that neither of them should let the other one win.

The Beijing Opera is over. The curtain falls. But the building — as always — remains.
