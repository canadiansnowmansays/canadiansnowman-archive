I. The Sound of Silence

It has now been six months since the Department of Justice released the first major tranche of Epstein files under the Epstein Files Transparency Act. In that time, the following has happened: a restaurateur named as backup executor of Epstein’s will was documented across seven thousand pages of files as the man who housed, employed, and managed the women in Epstein’s orbit. Epstein’s longtime personal assistant testified before Congress and named two men who sexually assaulted her. The former Attorney General of the United States sat for a four-hour deposition and declined, under privilege, to answer questions about her conversations with the president regarding those files. The man she named as responsible for the entire redaction process — the man who approved the memo telling the American public there was no client list worth seeing — was formally submitted to the Senate this week for confirmation as the permanent Attorney General of the United States.

You would be forgiven for not knowing most of that. The networks have been busy.

This is not an accident. Donald Trump understands, with the instinct of a man who has spent fifty years manipulating media cycles, that the most effective suppression tool is not denial — it is noise. Start a war. Pick a fight with a trading partner. Float a cabinet firing. The sound of silence suits Donald Trump perfectly — and the Epstein story does not require active burial if there is always something louder happening in the adjacent lane.

So let’s make some noise.

The New York Times published a detailed investigation into Stephen Hanson, a Manhattan restaurant mogul whose name appears on more than seven thousand pages of DOJ files and who served for years as something the files never quite call him but the record makes unmistakable: Epstein’s wingman. The Times piece is excellent. It is also, notably, the first sustained piece of new Epstein investigative reporting to break through the news cycle in weeks. It should not have taken this long, and it should not be standing largely alone.

Here is what else has been happening while the cameras pointed elsewhere.

II. What a Wingman Does

The two men at Little Saint James, Mr. Epstein’s private island in the Caribbean, in an undated photo from the Justice Department files.Credit...U.S. Department of Justice

Jeffrey Epstein needed hot dogs. Not just any hot dogs — Zweigle’s Pop Open hot dogs, frozen, packed in a white freezer at his Upper East Side townhouse, ferried by private jet to his Zorro Ranch compound in New Mexico. And there was one person he knew he could count on to make the delivery himself.

Stephen Hanson had built one of the most successful restaurant empires in Manhattan history. At its peak, BR Guest Hospitality served more than twenty thousand people a day across twenty-five theatrical, high-volume restaurants — Blue Water Grill, Dos Caminos, Ruby Foo’s. Bon Appétit named him restaurateur of the year in 2003. His company was valued at $300 million by 2007. He was a figure of genuine consequence in the world of New York hospitality.

He was also the man Epstein called when he needed his hot dogs, his beef jerky recipe tested, his Caribbean island staffed, his apartment renovations coordinated, his women housed, fed, employed, and managed.

The DOJ files reveal that when Epstein wanted provisions delivered to New Mexico, his chef emailed an assistant: “In the white freezer in downstairs kitchen of 71st street there are packages of frozen hot dogs. JE would like to have hot dogs for lunch tmrw and these are the new dogs he likes.” Hanson, records show, was happy to make the delivery himself — along with a woman Epstein had asked him to bring, who was later paid settlements from funds established for Epstein’s victims.

Hanson’s name appears on more than 7,000 pages in the Epstein files — about as often as Woody Allen’s and the former Prince Andrew’s. He was once designated as a backup executor in Epstein’s will. The two men traded Valentine’s Day greetings and gifts, including a take-a-number machine of the kind used in delis, which Epstein placed prominently in the room where people waited to see him.

Think about that image for a moment. The man who raped children installed a deli ticket dispenser in his waiting room. And his close friend, the restaurateur of the year, thought this was charming enough to give as a gift.

Hundreds of messages between the two men involve plans for “the girls” — a term they used for whichever group of women happened to be in Epstein’s orbit at a given moment, whether as assistants or sexual prospects. Hanson lent the women one of his two houses in East Hampton for getaways and extended invitations to parties. He got young women tables in his dining rooms, arranged cooking classes for them, and when Epstein asked him to help women land visas or find jobs in his restaurants, the answer was almost always yes.

When women in Epstein’s entourage needed employment, Hanson sometimes hired them as hosts, servers, or in one case a chef. In 2009, shortly after Epstein had finished his jail sentence, a woman who would later be paid a settlement wrote to Epstein saying she was broke. Epstein replied: “my friend steve hanson, owns a buch of restaruants, he will be helpful, call him directly.” Hanson got her a job as a host. Within three months, she was fired. “Between us,” Hanson wrote to Epstein, “the girl was a nervous wreck and just couldn’t handle it.”

There is something almost banal about that exchange. Two powerful men discussing a trafficked woman’s employment performance, as though she were a disappointing line cook. “We tried,” Epstein replied, and the matter was closed.

One victim told the FBI in a 2021 video interview that when she was in her early twenties — she had first met Epstein at seventeen — Epstein had sent Hanson to her New York City apartment, where Hanson paid her for oral sex at least a dozen times. Her name was redacted in the files. Her lawyer, Gloria Allred, declined to comment. The DOJ has not indicated whether the agency investigated further, and Hanson has never been charged with a crime.

In response to questions from the Times, Hanson issued a statement through his lawyer — who was a former attorney for Epstein — that read in part: “Mr. Epstein was adept at deception and manipulation. He pulled the wool over the eyes of leading academics, scientists, and titans of business. The suggestion, assumption, or insinuation that there was anything untoward about, in relation to, or concerning, any connection between Mr. Hanson and Mr. Epstein is untrue.”

A former attorney for Epstein, writing on behalf of Epstein’s backup executor, denying wrongdoing in Epstein’s network. One almost admires the consistency.

III. The Transaction

Mr. Hanson in an undated photo in the files.Credit...U.S. Department of Justice

What did Hanson get in return?

Epstein gave advice, lent money, and most valuably, helped Hanson navigate a brutal corporate divorce. When Starwood Capital’s Barry Sternlicht — who called his partnership with Hanson “among the two worst investments of my career” — was looking to exit their restaurant group, Epstein reviewed Sternlicht’s offers behind the scenes and wrote the responses that helped Hanson walk away with more than double the initial offer on the table. “Dam are u good,” Hanson emailed Epstein after the deal was done. “Learned a lot from you.”

The relationship was, in other words, precisely the kind Epstein specialized in constructing: mutually beneficial, transactional, and quietly compromising. Hanson provided logistics and access. Epstein provided money, strategy, and the reflected glow of his peculiar orbit. Both men got what they needed. The women got whatever they could survive.

Hanson was one of the last people Epstein texted on July 6, 2019, before boarding his Gulfstream in Paris for what would be his final flight to freedom. Epstein explained he had been keeping a low profile because his press had been “toxic.” Hanson replied: “No different then before. Nyc understand a bitch. But palm is easy. Enjoy paris.”

Hanson is now 76. He recently opened a restaurant called the Polo Room in Palm Beach with celebrity polo player Ignacio Figueras. Former colleagues report it is a raging success. You can’t get into it.

IV. Sarah Kellen Speaks

While the Times was excavating Hanson’s decade of favours, a more consequential development was unfolding in Washington.

Sarah Kellen — Epstein’s longtime personal assistant, whom prosecutors considered charging before ultimately deciding not to pursue the case — testified in a closed-door session before the House Oversight Committee on May 21. The transcript was released this week. In it, Kellen accused Frédéric Fekkai, the French celebrity hairstylist, and Philip Levine, the former mayor of Miami Beach, of sexually assaulting her in separate incidents during her years working for Epstein. She alleged that a third individual, fashion photographer Patrick Demarchelier, exposed himself to her.

Levine’s name appears in the Epstein files more than 600 times.

Kellen also told the committee that she was aboard Epstein’s private plane with former President Bill Clinton for all of his trips with Epstein to Asia and Africa, and that she saw Bill Gates, former Prince Andrew, Ehud Barak, and Leon Black interacting with Epstein. She did not allege wrongdoing by any of those men.

Kellen’s attorney stated that she would not testify about other victims’ experiences, citing her own trauma and constitutional protections. She indicated she would be more willing to discuss other victims if granted immunity by either the DOJ or the Oversight Committee.

That last sentence deserves to sit in the silence for a moment. The woman who for years was described as Epstein’s scheduler, his fixer, his recruiter — the woman who showed police surveillance footage from inside his mansion in 2003, the woman whose name appears in settlement agreements as a facilitator — is now, herself, a named victim. And she is offering to say more, if someone will protect her first.

No one has offered.

House Oversight Chair James Comer sent a letter to Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche asking the DOJ to investigate the allegations against Fekkai and Levine. The committee has been conducting a broad series of depositions: former President Bill Clinton, former Secretary of State Hillary Clinton, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, former AG Pam Bondi, Les Wexner, Darren Indyke, and Richard Kahn have all been interviewed. Bill Gates and former Epstein associate Lesley Groff are next.

The Oversight Committee has done more genuine accountability work in six months than the FBI and DOJ managed across thirty years. The question of why that is remains the central question of this series.

V. Bondi’s Deposition and the Permanent Attorney General

Former Attorney General Pam Bondi arrives for her deposition at the Rayburn House Office Building on Capitol Hill, Friday, May 29, 2026, in Washington. (AP Photo/Manuel Ceneta)

On May 29, former Attorney General Pam Bondi sat for her long-awaited deposition before the House Oversight Committee. Democrats had demanded it be conducted under oath, on camera, and released publicly. They got most of what they asked for. What they got from Bondi was considerably less.

During the roughly four-hour session, Bondi invoked privilege and declined to answer questions about her interactions with Trump regarding the Epstein files, accompanied throughout by Assistant Attorney General Harmeet Dhillon, who rebuffed similar lines of inquiry.

On the critical question of accountability for the files review process, Bondi told lawmakers that Todd Blanche had overseen recruiting the attorneys who reviewed the documents and approved the redaction process. She also said she had no role in drafting or releasing the joint FBI-DOJ memo stating that the review “revealed no incriminating ‘client list’” and that “no further disclosure would be appropriate or warranted.” The memo that told the American public there was nothing to see — Bondi says she didn’t write it and wasn’t involved in it. Blanche, apparently, was in charge of that too.

Bondi acknowledged that the DOJ improperly released documents that included victims’ names and identifying information, though she argued her office moved quickly to address the errors. She praised Blanche as “one of the most highly ethical individuals I know” and said he managed the Epstein investigation “with very little error.”

Then, the day after Bondi’s deposition transcript was released, Trump announced at a White House dinner that he will nominate Blanche to be attorney general permanently. “We are going to make him permanent attorney general,” Trump said. The formal nomination was submitted to the Senate on Thursday. A confirmation battle is expected, with some Republican lawmakers already expressing hesitancy — largely over Blanche’s proposed $1.776 billion “Anti-Weaponization Fund,” not, it should be noted, over anything to do with Epstein.

Consider the architecture of that sequence. The man who was Trump’s personal criminal defence attorney becomes Deputy AG. He oversees the Epstein files review process, approves the redactions, and authorizes the memo declaring there is no client list worth seeing. Bondi, who was nominally his superior, says she had no role in any of it. Bondi is then fired — ousted over her failed efforts to prosecute Trump’s perceived political opponents — and Blanche is elevated to acting AG, where he has since pursued indictments against Trump’s critics and rolled back gun control measures. And now, having demonstrated his loyalty to the president’s satisfaction, he has been formally submitted to the Senate for permanent confirmation as the chief law enforcement officer of the United States.

The man who controls the remaining withheld Epstein files is the man who used to control Donald Trump’s criminal defence. The White House calls this “advocating for sanity, law and order.”

The survivors are still waiting for someone to tell them what that phrase means.

VI. The Void

Here is the thread that connects Hanson’s hot dog deliveries to Bondi’s stonewalling to Blanche’s nomination: in each case, the mechanism is the same. Powerful men arranging things for other powerful men, at the expense of women whose names remain redacted or whose testimony remains locked behind immunity negotiations no one is willing to offer. The logistics change. The structure does not.

And the mainstream media, with honourable exceptions, has largely allowed that structure to operate in the quiet. Outside the Times, outside independent journalists like Jim Acosta broadcasting from Substack after being pushed out of CNN, outside the dedicated Epstein reporters at NPR and CBS, the story has not received the sustained, front-page, lead-segment treatment that the nomination of a president’s former personal defence attorney to permanently oversee the suppression of files implicating that president would, in any previous era, have demanded.

The politicians are equally absent. Outside the Oversight Committee hearing rooms, the elected silence is deafening. No floor speeches. No press conferences on the Capitol steps. No one standing at a microphone saying clearly, on the record, what Robert Garcia says in committee and what Jasmine Crockett said at West Palm Beach City Hall six months ago: this is obstruction of justice, and the people committing it have names and are now being submitted to the Senate for lifetime appointments.

The survivors testified. The assistant named her abusers. The wingman’s favours are documented across seven thousand pages. The acting attorney general was the president’s defence lawyer. The former attorney general says she had nothing to do with any of it. The Senate has been asked to make it permanent.

The words of the prophets, it turns out, are not written on the subway walls. They are in sealed court filings, redacted beyond recognition, in a DOJ database last updated February 1, 2026.

And the country is watching something else.

That is exactly what Donald Trump wants. It is the only thing on this list that is going according to plan.
