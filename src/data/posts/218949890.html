Picture a Tim Hortons, any Tim Hortons, on the morning of Monday, October 5, 2026. It could be in Rimouski or Red Deer or on Yonge Street, a few blocks from where I am writing this. The radio behind the counter says that Quebecers are voting today, and that the Parti Québécois is favoured to win on a promise of a third referendum on leaving Canada. It adds that in two weeks, on October 19, Albertans will vote on whether to begin the legal process toward a referendum of their own.

A man in a fleece vest looks up from his double-double, considers the news, and says the most Canadian sentence available to him.

“Again? Must be Tuesday.”

Then he goes back to the sports section.

It is not an unreasonable response. This country has been threatening to come apart for roughly as long as it has been together. Nova Scotia tried to leave within a year of Confederation. Quebec has held two referendums on sovereignty, in 1980 and 1995, and lost both. Alberta has nursed a grievance against Ottawa since before it was a province, and has periodically dressed it up as a movement. Each time the newspapers have warned of the end of Canada, and each time Canada has turned up for work the next morning, a little grumpier but intact.

So the man in the fleece vest has history on his side. This series asks whether he is right.

Two votes, fourteen days

Start with Quebec, where the first vote is already in.

In Quebec, as Norimitsu Onishi reported for The New York Times on October 5, 2026, in "Separatists Lead as Voters in Quebec Go to the Polls," the Parti Québécois under Paul St-Pierre Plamondon went into election day leading the polls and within reach of a majority of the National Assembly's 127 seats. St-Pierre Plamondon, 49, broke with a generation of PQ leaders who kept the referendum question deliberately vague so as not to frighten the voters they needed. He said plainly that a PQ government would hold one. He then spent most of a 39-day campaign talking about something else: taxes, immigration levels, homelessness, the cost of living, and a health system that nobody in Quebec defends.

He also attached a condition that would have astonished René Lévesque. There will be no referendum, he pledged, until Donald Trump has left office and the economy has steadied. The man who promises to break up Canada has, in effect, made the timing of the break-up conditional on the American presidential calendar. Lévesque would have understood the caution. He might not have enjoyed the company.

Then Quebecers voted, and the arithmetic was stranger than the campaign. The PQ won 59 seats, five short of a majority, on 28 per cent of the popular vote. The Liberals took 40 seats and became the official opposition. The Conservative Party of Quebec won 19, and Québec solidaire 9. The governing Coalition Avenir Québec, which held 79 seats going in and was now led by Christine Fréchette after François Legault’s resignation, won none at all. Fréchette lost her own seat in Trois-Rivières.

Read that again with the referendum in mind. The party promising to break up Canada will govern with the support of just over a quarter of the people who voted, in a province where support for independence sits around 30 per cent, the lowest in decades. Quebecers wanted change, and the party offering change happened to have a referendum in its platform. They did not hand it a mandate to hold one alone.

St-Pierre Plamondon seemed to understand that on election night. Quebecers, he said, had asked the PQ to work with the other parties, and he had heard the message. He also repeated the promise: there will be a referendum, once Trump is gone. Since the only other sovereigntist party in the Assembly is Québec solidaire, with nine seats, the referendum now depends on a minority government surviving long enough to call one.

Prime Minister Mark Carney’s reply was a model of federalist calm. He congratulated the new premier and said that building a stronger Quebec was core to building a stronger Canada, then turned at once to tariffs, jobs and housing. Somewhere, a man in a fleece vest nodded.

Alberta’s vote, on October 19, is even more peculiar. It is not a vote on leaving. It is a vote on whether to begin the process of holding a vote on leaving, a “referendum on a referendum,” as critics and even some separatists have called it. The question offers two options: that Alberta remain a province of Canada, or that the Government of Alberta “commence the legal process required under the Canadian Constitution” to hold a binding separation referendum. It sits on a ballot alongside nine other questions, most of them about immigration and provincial programmes.

It got there by a winding road. Separatist groups gathered more than 300,000 signatures for a citizen-led referendum. Then, on May 13, 2026, an Alberta judge sided with First Nations who argued that putting separation to a vote without consulting the peoples whose treaties cover the province was unconstitutional. Premier Danielle Smith responded on May 21 by putting a question on the ballot herself, while announcing that she would personally vote to stay. That is a sentence worth reading twice.

The polls say Albertans will, too. As Agence France-Presse reported in “Alberta separatists make their case before referendum on leaving Canada” on October 3, 2026, only about a quarter of the province appears to want out. Research Co.’s late-August survey put remain at 74 per cent and the process option at 22.

So, two votes in fourteen days, neither of them likely to end the country. The man in the fleece vest can finish his doughnut.

Why this series, and why now

And yet.

Separatism in Canada has never really been about whether a province leaves on a given Monday. It is a long argument the country conducts with itself, sometimes quietly, sometimes with bombs in mailboxes, and it has shaped Canada far more than any single referendum result. During the 1990s, when it seemed the votes would never stop, a word for it entered the Canadian vocabulary: the neverendum. The name stuck because it described something true. Canada does not resolve this question. It manages it, renegotiates it, and passes it to the next generation, like a family heirloom that nobody likes but nobody will throw out.

This series is the history of that heirloom. Over the coming weeks it will try to answer four questions, which are the ones I think a reasonable reader in a Tim Hortons would ask if he put down the sports section long enough.

Where did it come from? Not, as most of us assume, from Quebec. The first province to try to leave was Nova Scotia, in 1868, and it was not a fringe affair. Anti-Confederates won almost every seat in sight. Newfoundland, meanwhile, joined only in 1949, by the narrowest of margins and with grievances it still recites.

Why does it keep coming back? The reasons differ by region, and that difference is the most important thing to understand about the whole subject. In Quebec, it is language, identity, and the memory of conquest. In the West, it is oil, freight rates, equalization, and the conviction that Ottawa is a machine for sending Alberta’s money to Ontario and Quebec. In both, it is the suspicion that the federation was designed by somebody else, for somebody else.

What has it actually achieved? This is the question that most coverage skips, and it is where the series makes its main argument. Measured as an exit strategy, Canadian separatism has failed every time. Measured as leverage, it may be the most successful political movement in the country’s history. Quebec won Bill 101, its own pension plan, a large say in choosing its own immigrants, and recognition by the House of Commons as a nation, all without leaving. Nova Scotia was bought off with “better terms” within a year. Alberta has extracted concessions on resources and equalization by threatening, from time to time, to take its oil and go home. The threat has paid. The departure never comes.

Could it ever actually happen? Here I want to correct an assumption I brought to this project myself, and which I suspect many readers share. I began by believing that Ottawa could stop anything regardless of the vote count. That turns out to be wrong, in an interesting way. In 1998, the Supreme Court of Canada ruled that no province can secede unilaterally. But it also ruled that a clear majority on a clear question would oblige the rest of the country to negotiate in good faith. Ottawa cannot simply ignore a clear result. What it can do, under the Clarity Act of 2000, is decide what “clear” means, and it controls the constitutional amendment any departure would require. Mark Carney has already signalled that 50 per cent plus one would not be enough. The real barrier, in other words, is not a veto. It is a negotiation so complicated, over borders, debt, currency, the military and Indigenous lands, that nobody has ever wanted to start it.

The ingredient that is new

If the old pattern holds, the man in the fleece vest is right, and this series will end as a history of a threat that worked precisely because it was never carried out.

There is one ingredient this time that has not been present before, and it is the reason I do not entirely share his calm. For the first time, a foreign government has taken a visible interest in the outcome. U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has described Alberta as a natural partner for the United States. Alberta separatist leaders have met officials of the State Department to discuss support for an independent Alberta. The Trump administration has insisted these were routine meetings with civil society groups. Perhaps they were. But the neverendum has always been a family argument, conducted in our own two official languages, with the rest of the world politely looking away. A neighbour leaning over the fence to offer one side a loan is a new thing, and the series will give it a part of its own.

The other new thing is quieter. As Onishi noted, many Quebec separatists now believe the next referendum could be their last chance. Francophone Quebecers are wealthier and better educated than in 1995 and markedly less interested in seceding, and immigration is changing the province’s electorate. A movement that thinks it is running out of time behaves differently from one that thinks history is on its side. Desperation is not usually good for clarity.

How the series will run

The Conversation observed on June 17, 2026, in “Why Albertans are seeking a referendum on separation from Canada,” that Alberta’s October vote will not settle the independence question, but will show how seriously the rest of Canada needs to take it. That is a fair description of the whole project.

The first six parts are history, and they can be written now, because the past is mercifully fixed. Part I goes back to Joseph Howe and Nova Scotia’s revolt, and on to Newfoundland. Part II covers the Quiet Revolution and the October Crisis, when Quebec nationalism became a governing force and, for a few terrible weeks in 1970, a violent one. Part III tells the story of 1980 and 1995, and of the night English Canada discovered how close it had come. Part IV sets out the legal rules of leaving, which are less a wall than a maze. Part V turns west, to the National Energy Program, “The West wants in,” and the 2026 petitions. Part VI gives the floor to the nations inside the nation: the Cree, the Inuit and the Treaty First Nations, who have voted twice, overwhelmingly, to stay in Canada, and whose treaties were signed with the Crown and not with Quebec City or Edmonton.

The last three parts will wait for events. Part VII examines the American interest. Part VIII will look hard at what Quebecers decided on October 5 and what Albertans decide on October 19. Part IX will try to answer the question the man in the Tim Hortons asked.

Is the national shrug wisdom, or luck?

My provisional answer is that it is mostly wisdom, with an asterisk the size of 1995. On October 30 of that year, the No side won by 54,288 votes out of nearly 4.7 million valid ballots, a margin of 50.58 per cent to 49.42. For most of that campaign, English Canada said “Again? Must be Tuesday,” and went back to the sports section. It got a fright it has spent thirty years trying to forget. Stephen Leacock, who knew a thing or two about Canadians assuming that everything would work out, would have recognised the type.

We will see what Tuesday brings this time.
