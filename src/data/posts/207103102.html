There is a legal doctrine at the center of this entire story that almost nobody outside a law school seminar had reason to know about eighteen months ago. It is called supremacy-clause immunity, and it may end up determining whether any American can hold a federal agent accountable in a state courtroom for as long as this administration remains in office.

I want to walk through it carefully, because the concept is easy to confuse with something more familiar — qualified immunity — and the difference between the two is exactly where this fight will actually be won or lost.

Not Qualified Immunity — Something Older and Narrower

Most people who have followed American policing debates over the past decade know the term qualified immunity. It is a doctrine, built up by federal courts over decades, that shields law-enforcement officers from civil lawsuits when they violate someone’s rights, so long as the right in question was not clearly established at the time. It gives officers what courts have called wiggle room — the idea being that you do not want officers frozen with fear of personal liability every time they make a split-second judgment call. Qualified immunity applies to civil suits, and it applies regardless of whether the officer in question is state, local, or federal.

Supremacy-clause immunity is a different animal entirely, and a narrower one. It applies only in one specific circumstance: a state criminal prosecution of a federal official. The doctrine flows from the Constitution’s supremacy clause, which establishes federal law as the supreme law of the land. The underlying logic is intuitive enough, and has a genuinely honorable history — you do not want a state prosecutor able to criminally charge a federal agent simply for doing their job as federal law requires. The clearest historical example is school desegregation: federal marshals enforcing court-ordered integration in the South could not be hauled into a hostile state court and prosecuted for the act of enforcing a federal court’s own order. The doctrine exists, at its best, to prevent states from using their own criminal law as a tool of resistance against legitimate federal authority.

The legal standard that has developed around this immunity holds that when a federal officer is carrying out duties authorized by federal law, and when the specific actions taken were necessary and proper to fulfilling those duties, the officer is immune from state criminal prosecution. Two conditions, both of which have to be met: authorized duty, and necessary-and-proper conduct within that duty. It is that second condition — necessary and proper — where every one of these cases will actually be fought.

The Argument Minnesota Has to Win

If Hennepin County moves forward with charges in the Good and Pretti cases — something County Attorney Mary Moriarty has told me her office has not yet decided, pending the outcome of the state’s own Bureau of Criminal Apprehension investigation — the argument the state will need to make is specific and narrow. It cannot simply argue that the shootings were wrong, or even that they were unjustified under ordinary use-of-force standards. It has to argue that the conduct in question fell outside the scope of the officers’ federal duties altogether, or that it was not necessary and proper to carrying out those duties — that the officers, in other words, breached the boundaries of what a federal officer is legally entitled to do, even while nominally on the job.

This is already the theory underlying the state’s charges against Christian Castro in the Sosa-Celis shooting, and it is worth remembering how that case actually developed: federal prosecutors’ own account of the underlying encounter fell apart badly enough that they moved to dismiss their own charges with prejudice, and two of the agents involved are now under investigation for lying under oath about what happened. If an officer’s account of an encounter does not hold up, the necessary-and-proper defense gets much harder to sustain, because the state’s argument becomes not “this was excessive,” but “this did not happen the way you said it did, which means we cannot even evaluate whether it was within the proper scope of your duties.” Good’s case and Pretti’s case have not been tested against real evidence yet, for the reasons described in Part V. That is precisely why the evidence fight and the immunity fight are the same fight.

What “Necessary and Proper” Actually Asks a Court to Decide

It is worth being concrete about what a judge is actually being asked to determine when this defense is raised, because the phrase necessary and proper sounds like it should have a clean, mechanical answer and it does not. Courts applying this standard have historically looked at whether an officer’s conduct bore a reasonable relationship to the duty being performed — not whether it was the only possible way to perform that duty, and not whether, in hindsight, a different choice would have been wiser. That is a real degree of latitude, and it is one reason federal officers have historically fared well when they can invoke it.

But the standard has limits, and the limits are precisely where these cases will live. An officer conducting a lawful arrest is exercising a federal duty. An officer who, in the course of that arrest, uses force disproportionate to any threat actually presented is arguably no longer acting within the necessary-and-proper scope of that duty — the arrest itself may be authorized, while the specific conduct used to carry it out is not. That is the distinction Hennepin County will have to persuade a federal court to draw in each of these cases: not that ICE and CBP lacked authority to be present, which nobody disputes, but that what specific individual officers did once they were present exceeded what the underlying duty could plausibly justify. It is a narrower argument than it might first appear, and a more winnable one than blanket immunity might suggest — which is exactly why the fight over evidence in Part V matters so much. A court cannot draw that line without a factual record precise enough to draw it on.

The closest historical precedent for a state trying to prosecute a federal agent is the Ruby Ridge case — an Idaho county prosecutor’s criminal charges against an FBI agent over deaths during the 1992 standoff at Ruby Ridge. It is worth knowing this case in some detail, because it cuts in two directions at once.

On one hand, it establishes that a state genuinely can bring criminal charges against a federal agent, and that supremacy-clause immunity is not an absolute bar to doing so. On the other hand, the case took a decade to litigate, and it was eventually dropped — not because the agent was acquitted, not because a court definitively ruled the prosecution invalid, but because a new county prosecutor took over years later and simply decided the fight was not worth continuing. Ruby Ridge did not end in a clean legal answer about the scope of supremacy-clause immunity. It ended in exhaustion.

That is the honest lesson of the closest available precedent: these cases are winnable in the sense that they can be brought, and losable in the much more mundane sense that time, resources, and political will run out long before a court delivers a final answer. A state pursuing this path today needs to understand it may be signing up for a decade-long fight against a federal government with far greater resources and no obligation to move quickly.

Why the Political Landscape Has Changed

Here is what makes this moment different from 1992, and different, frankly, from most of the recent past. Bryna Godar, a staff attorney with the State Democracy Research Initiative at the University of Wisconsin Law School who studies exactly this kind of state prosecution of federal officials, made a point to me that I think is the sharpest available framework for understanding why this is happening now. States tend to bring these prosecutions, she noted, specifically when there is friction between state and federal priorities over law enforcement. For most of the recent past, state and federal governments have been broadly aligned on enforcement priorities, which is exactly why these cases have been rare. Ruby Ridge was itself an exception precisely because Idaho and the federal government disagreed sharply about what had happened and who was responsible.

What we are seeing now is that same kind of friction, reproduced at a much larger scale, because the federal government’s aggressive immigration enforcement agenda has produced violence that many state governments — not uniformly, but in specific places, with specific prosecutors — consider genuinely intolerable. That friction is the precondition for these cases existing at all, and it is intensifying rather than fading, which is why Minnesota is not likely to be the last state to test this doctrine.

There is a second, more structural shift working in the states’ favor, and it has nothing to do with immigration policy directly. Federal judges — including in Minnesota, where courts have already had to grapple with serious allegations of rights violations coming out of Operation Metro Surge’s immigration detentions — have grown visibly more skeptical of this Justice Department’s representations generally. That skepticism has been earned the hard way, case by case, as judges have found themselves less willing to simply take DOJ’s word at face value and more inclined to demand the department actually prove what it asserts, in person, under oath. It is not difficult to imagine that same skepticism extending to how a federal court evaluates a supremacy-clause immunity defense raised by ICE or CBP officers — a court less willing to grant the benefit of the doubt to a department it has already learned not to trust reflexively.

That is the real stake in this fight, and it goes well beyond Good and Pretti. If Minnesota succeeds in getting a court to allow a state prosecution to proceed against the necessary-and-proper defense these officers will raise, the doctrine of supremacy-clause immunity narrows for every state, in every future case, for as long as the precedent holds. If Minnesota loses — through an adverse ruling, or through the quieter mechanism of exhaustion that ended Ruby Ridge — the federal government’s practical immunity from state accountability, in immigration enforcement and beyond, gets that much harder to dislodge.
