Every month, two deposits arrive in my bank account from the Government of Canada. The first is my Canada Pension Plan benefit, earned through decades of payroll deductions I barely noticed at the time. The second is Old Age Security, which arrives because I am old and Canadian and for no other reason. Neither one makes me rich. They form part of my retirement. They are the part I never have to think about when the markets wobble or the gas bill climbs.

I mention this not to boast about my country’s arrangements, which have their own flaws, but because of what I read on October 12, 2026. Tara Siegel Bernard, writing in the New York Times, described what happens when the floor beneath tens of millions of American retirees develops a crack. I recognised the feeling she was describing. Anyone who has reached the age where the monthly deposit matters more than the stock ticker would recognise it.

Her piece laid out the arithmetic plainly. Social Security’s retirement trust fund is projected to be depleted in the fourth quarter of 2032, according to the 2026 Trustees Report. After that, incoming payroll taxes would cover only about 78 per cent of scheduled benefits. If Congress does nothing, more than 60 million Americans would see their monthly cheques cut by roughly 22 per cent, all at once.

To show what that means in practice, the Times worked with Jack VanDerhei of Morningstar’s Center for Retirement & Policy Studies to model four hypothetical households. The one that stayed with me was a single man who earned about $34,000 a year over his working life and, like roughly half of American households, has nothing in a retirement account. His Social Security benefit at 67 would be about $1,543 a month. After the cut, it would fall to about $1,204. For him, Social Security is not one leg of a three-legged stool. It is the whole stool.

A middle-income woman in the same analysis fares only somewhat better. She earned about $75,000 a year, saved around $20,000, and would rely on Social Security for nearly all of her monthly income. Even the higher-earning couple, with a comfortable home and a respectable nest egg, would lose about $1,470 a month.

This series is about that cut, why it is coming, and how it could be stopped. It is also, I hope, about something larger: whether a democracy can still fix a problem it has seen coming for forty years.

The Problem Everyone Saw Coming

I spent the final stretch of my working life in the Office of the President at Rogers, handling the complaints that had already failed everywhere else. They came up from frontline support, down from executives, and sideways from regulators and reporters. Over the years I learned something about how institutions get into trouble.

The costliest problems were rarely the surprises. A surprise forces action because nobody can pretend it was expected. The expensive problems were the ones everybody knew about: the known defect, the policy that obviously could not hold, the fix that sat in a queue because it was awkward, unpopular, or someone else’s budget. They waited until the awkwardness was overtaken by a crisis, and the crisis always cost more than the fix would have.

Social Security’s shortfall is that kind of complaint. It is not a hidden flaw that a clever analyst has just uncovered. The programme’s trustees publish a report every year, and for decades those reports have projected the same basic shape. The baby boom generation was large, and it is now retiring. Americans are living longer and collecting benefits for more years. Birth rates have fallen, so fewer workers pay in for each retiree drawing out. Wages at the top have grown much faster than wages in the middle, and more of that top-end income escapes the payroll tax entirely, because the tax stops at an annual ceiling.

None of this is mysterious. All of it was foreseeable. And for most of the past four decades, the political response has been to hand the problem to whoever holds office next.

Why It Is Called the Third Rail

The phrase is usually credited to Kirk O’Donnell, an aide to House Speaker Tip O’Neill in the early 1980s. Social Security, he said, was the third rail of American politics, a reference to the electrified rail that powers a subway train. Touch it and you die.

The metaphor has lasted because it describes something true. Older Americans vote in large numbers, and they vote with particular attention to anything that touches their cheques. Any politician who proposes cutting benefits hands an opponent a ready-made attack advertisement. Any politician who proposes raising taxes to pay for them hands an opponent a different one. The safest course, for each individual politician in each individual election, is to say nothing specific and promise to protect the programme in general.

The trouble is that the safest course for each politician is the most dangerous course for the programme. Every year of delay makes the eventual fix larger, because the gap grows and the time available to phase changes in gently shrinks. Changes that could once have been spread over decades now have to work in less than six years.

There is a further irony. The default outcome, the one Congress gets by doing nothing, is itself a benefit cut, and a brutal one. It falls on every retiree at once, rich and poor alike, with no phase-in and no protection for the most vulnerable. Inaction is not neutral. It is a decision, just one that nobody has to cast a recorded vote for.

The Last Time Someone Touched It

It is worth remembering that the rail has been touched before, and the people who touched it survived.

In the early 1980s Social Security faced a cash crunch far more urgent than today’s. Benefits were at risk within months, not years. President Ronald Reagan, a Republican, and Speaker Tip O’Neill, a Democrat, could hardly have been further apart on the role of government. They agreed on very little except that they could not let the cheques stop. A bipartisan commission, chaired by Alan Greenspan, produced a package that neither side loved.

The package spread the pain around. Scheduled payroll-tax increases were brought forward. For the first time, part of the benefits paid to higher-income retirees became taxable. Newly hired federal employees were brought into the system. The annual cost-of-living adjustment was delayed by six months. Most famously, the full retirement age was scheduled to rise slowly from 65 to 67, so slowly that the change took decades to complete.

Reagan signed the amendments on April 20, 1983. Both men took criticism from their own side. Both men’s parties survived. And the programme was secured for roughly half a century, which is about as good an outcome as public policy ever produces.

That is the model this series will hold up, and it is an American model. Americans do not need to look abroad to find proof that this can be done. They need only look back about forty-three years.

Why a Canadian Is Writing This

A fair reader might ask what business a retired Canadian has writing about American retirement policy. It is a reasonable question, and I want to answer it honestly.

I am not writing because I think Canada has the answers and the United States does not. Canada faced its own pension reckoning in the mid-1990s, when the Canada Pension Plan’s chief actuary warned that the fund was headed for trouble. Ottawa and the provinces spent several difficult years negotiating a fix. In 1997 they agreed to raise contribution rates from 5.85 per cent of earnings to 9.9 per cent over six years, and to create an independent investment board to invest the fund in markets rather than leave it sitting in government bonds. It was unpopular, and it worked.

But Canada had advantages Washington does not. The country is roughly one-eighth the size of the United States. The provinces held a formal veto over changes to the plan, which forced the governments at the table to compromise. And Canada has made its own mistakes. In 2012 the federal government announced it would raise the eligibility age for Old Age Security from 65 to 67. In 2016 the next government reversed the change. Retirement policy can become a political football on either side of the border.

So I write as a neighbour, not a teacher. I am a retiree who lives on public pensions, looking across the fence at a neighbour whose roof has a leak that both of us can see. Where Canada’s experience offers a useful lesson, I will say so. Where it doesn’t, I will say that too.

The Arithmetic Is Not the Hard Part

The tools for closing Social Security’s gap are well known. They fall into three broad groups.

The first is more revenue. Congress can raise the payroll-tax rate, raise or remove the ceiling on earnings subject to the tax, or dedicate new revenue from elsewhere. Notably, the Times reported that even some Republicans, who usually resist tax increases of any kind, have recently proposed raising that ceiling so that higher earners contribute more.

The second is lower spending. Congress can change the benefit formula, slow the growth of benefits for higher earners, change how cost-of-living increases are calculated, or raise the retirement age again.

The third is new money from investment. Some proposals would create a fund invested in markets, alongside the existing trust fund, to earn returns that payroll taxes alone cannot provide.

Every serious plan is some mixture of these. The real question is not whether the gap can be closed; it can. The question is who pays, how much, and how soon.

VanDerhei made a point in the Times piece that I intend to take seriously throughout this series. A reform package, he argued, should be judged on “what it does to household retirement outcomes”, not merely on whether the trust fund balances. A plan that balances the books by quietly impoverishing the lower-income man with no savings is not a solution. It is the same cut with better paperwork.

What This Series Will Do

Over eight parts, I want to walk through the problem from beginning to end, and then propose an answer.

Part I looks closely at the cliff itself: the four households, the 2032 date, and what a 22 per cent overnight cut would mean in practice. Part II goes back to 1935 and asks why Franklin Roosevelt built Social Security as earned insurance rather than charity, and why that design choice still shapes every argument about it. Part III returns to the 1983 rescue in more detail. Part IV looks across the fence at Canada’s own reforms, mistakes included.

Part V sets out every lever available to Congress and, as plainly as I can, who would bear the cost of each one. Part VI examines the politics: why both parties have found the cliff more comfortable than the vote. Part VII returns to the lower-income retiree and asks what fairness should mean when a programme this large has to change.

Part VIII is the reason I wanted to write this series at all. It will present a draft bill, written in the form Congress uses, that sets out one way to close the gap. I will not pretend it is painless; no honest fix is. My aim is a design that I believe is genuinely fair and that is also built to pass, in the spirit of 1983: something each side has asked for, and something each side will dislike, with both clearly labelled.

This will not be the first bill I have sent south from my desk in Toronto. In December 2025, my series Code Blue: America’s 80-Year Failure to Solve Healthcare closed with the American Health Security Act of 2026, a full draft of ten titles and fifty-two sections for universal coverage. Tellingly, it was not modelled on Canada’s single-payer system. It drew on Switzerland and Germany, which reach universal coverage through regulated private insurance, because that design has a better chance of surviving American politics. The same principle applies here: the right model is the one that can pass in the country that has to live with it, not the one a foreign neighbour happens to prefer. Congress has not yet taken me up on the first offer. I am making a second one anyway.

I am not legislative counsel, and I cannot produce an official score from the Social Security actuaries. Where I estimate what a provision would accomplish, I will say that it is an estimate and where it comes from. But a neighbour’s draft has one advantage over a politician’s: it does not have to survive a primary.

A Choice With a Due Date

Every household knows the pattern. The small repair put off for a season becomes the large repair put off for a year, and then becomes the emergency that costs more than both. Governments are households with bigger budgets and longer memories, and they fall into the same habit.

The cliff is in the fourth quarter of 2032. It can be found on a calendar. The members of Congress Americans elected on November 3, 2026, will very likely be the ones who either write the fix or let the default cut happen. A deadline you can read on a calendar is not really a crisis. It is a choice with a due date.

A word to the new majority, offered as a neighbour. Within hours of the polls closing on November 3, Representative Robert Garcia of California, who is expected to chair the House Oversight Committee in January, warned Trump officials to “lawyer up” and named four investigations: the president’s family, immigration enforcement, corporate corruption and the Epstein files, as Helen Jeong reported for NBC Los Angeles on November 4. Much of that deserves scrutiny, and the war with Iran, and Pete Hegseth’s conduct of it, is not something any Congress should leave alone. But a majority’s time is finite, and so is the public’s patience. Chuck Schumer and Hakeem Jeffries have said that lowering costs comes first, as Spectrum News reported. Few things cost American families more than health care, and nothing they count on more than Social Security. A Congress that spends 2027 evening the score will have little left for either. Oversight is a committee’s job. Legislating is everyone’s, and the votes to meet the 2032 deadline will have to come from both parties.

This series is about making that choice before it makes itself.
