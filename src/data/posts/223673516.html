Let me introduce four Americans who do not exist.

They were built by Jack VanDerhei, director of retirement research at Morningstar’s Center for Retirement & Policy Studies, for an analysis that Tara Siegel Bernard published in the New York Times on October 12, 2026. Each one pairs a Social Security benefit calculated from a realistic career with the actual median savings of real American households at a similar income, drawn from the Federal Reserve‘s survey of household finances. They are composites of millions.

All four were born in 1962. All four are 64 years old as I write. All four plan to stop working and claim Social Security at 67, in 2029, which is when people of their generation qualify for a full benefit. And if Congress does nothing, all four will be somewhere around their seventieth birthday when the cheque shrinks.

I want to spend this essay with them, because the debate about Social Security is usually conducted in trillions of dollars and percentages of taxable payroll, and nobody has ever paid a gas bill with a percentage of taxable payroll.

Four Households, One Calendar

Start with the man at the bottom. He is single and earned about $34,000 a year, on average, over his working life. Like roughly half of American households, he has nothing in a retirement account. His monthly Social Security benefit at 67 would be $1,543 in today’s dollars. That is his income. Not the foundation of his income; the whole of it.

Then there is the woman in the middle. She is also single and earned about $75,000 a year over her career, a respectable professional salary, and saved about $20,000, which at a cautious four per cent withdrawal rate produces roughly $70 a month. Her Social Security benefit would be $2,548, so the programme provides about 97 per cent of what she has to live on. About three-quarters of similar households own their homes, but you cannot buy groceries with home equity unless you are prepared to sell the kitchen.

Next is a married couple who each earned about $75,000, for a combined $150,000. They have done better on savings, with about $171,700 tucked away, producing around $572 a month. Their combined Social Security benefits would be $5,096, for a total monthly income of about $5,668.

Finally, the higher-earning couple. One spouse earned at or above the maximum amount subject to the payroll tax every year of a long career, which in 2026 is $184,500, according to the Social Security Administration, rising to $192,300 in 2027. The other earned about $75,000. They have about $435,000 in retirement savings, producing roughly $1,450 a month, and their combined benefits would be $6,681. Their monthly income comes to about $8,131.

If you are American and near retirement, there is a reasonable chance you are standing close to one of them.

What the Cut Takes

Now move the calendar forward to the fourth quarter of 2032 and let Congress do nothing. Here is what happens to each household’s monthly Social Security income, in today’s dollars, using the Times’s illustrative 22 per cent reduction.

The man with no savings goes from $1,543 to $1,204. He loses $339 a month, or about $4,070 a year.

The middle-income woman goes from $2,548 to $1,987. She loses $561 a month, or about $6,730 a year.

The middle-income couple goes from $5,096 to $3,975. They lose $1,121 a month, or about $13,450 a year.

The higher-earning couple goes from $6,681 to $5,211. They lose $1,470 a month, or about $17,640 a year.

In raw dollars the couple at the top loses most. But a cut is felt as a share of what you live on, and by that measure the order reverses. For the higher-earning couple, the lost $1,470 is about 18 per cent of their total monthly income. For the middle-income couple it is about 20 per cent. For the single woman, it is about 21 per cent. For the man with no savings, it is the full 22 per cent, because there is nothing else in his income for the cut to be diluted by.

That is the cruelty built into the default. The law, as it stands, does not ask who can afford the cut. It simply pays everyone the same reduced fraction. The household with $435,000 in savings, and very likely a paid-down house, absorbs the cut by trimming a holiday. The man with $1,204 a month absorbs it by deciding which of his bills is least likely to have him evicted.

I spent years in the Office of the President at Rogers dealing with people whose problems had already been mishandled several times before they reached me. One thing I learned is that the same billing error lands very differently on different customers. A $300 mistake is an irritation for one household and a crisis for another. A good institution notices the difference. The default outcome for Social Security does not.

Depletion Is Not Bankruptcy

Before going further, it is worth clearing away a word that does a great deal of damage in this debate. Social Security is not going bankrupt. It is not going to run out of money. The cheques will not stop.

To see why, it helps to understand what the “trust fund” actually is. There are really two. The Old-Age and Survivors Insurance trust fund, known as OASI, pays retirement and survivor benefits. The Disability Insurance trust fund, or DI, pays benefits to workers who become disabled before retirement age. Together they are referred to as OASDI. Workers and employers each pay 6.2 per cent of wages, up to the annual ceiling, and that money is credited to the funds. Whatever is not needed to pay current benefits is invested in special interest-bearing U.S. Treasury securities.

For decades the programme took in more than it paid out, and the reserves grew. According to a summary of the 2026 Trustees Report by the Congressional Research Service, the combined reserves peaked at $2.91 trillion in 2020 and began 2026 at about $2.56 trillion. They are now being drawn down, because the baby boom is retiring and more is going out than is coming in.

“Depletion” means that reserve runs to zero. It does not mean revenue stops. Payroll taxes keep flowing every payday from roughly 186 million covered workers. What depletion means is that the programme can then pay only what comes in. As Alicia Munnell of the Center for Retirement Research at Boston College put it in a June 16, 2026 brief, depletion “does not mean that OASI has run out of money.”

So the honest description is not bankruptcy. It is a household whose savings account has been drained but whose paycheque still arrives. It can still pay most of its bills. It cannot pay all of them.

There is also a legal tangle. A 2019 report by the Congressional Research Service, written by Barry Huston and William Morton, noted that beneficiaries would remain legally entitled to their full scheduled benefits, while another federal law, the Antideficiency Act, forbids spending money the government does not have. It said the agency’s response was unclear, and described two options: pay full benefits late, or pay reduced benefits on time. Either way, the trust funds have no standing authority to borrow their way past the gap.

What the Trustees Say

The trustees released their annual report on June 9, 2026. Its main findings are worth stating precisely.

The OASI fund, which pays retirees, is projected to be depleted in the fourth quarter of 2032. At that point, incoming revenue would cover 78 per cent of scheduled benefits. That is where the 22 per cent cut comes from.

The DI fund, by contrast, is projected to stay solvent throughout the entire 75-year projection period, through 2100.

If the two funds were combined, the reserves would last until 2034, and about 83 per cent of benefits would then be payable. That is why “2034” is sometimes quoted as the deadline. But as Munnell noted, combining the funds requires Congress to change the law. Without that vote, 2032 is the date that matters for retirees.

Over the full 75 years, the trustees estimate the programme’s shortfall at 4.42 per cent of taxable payroll. In plain terms, it is as if every dollar of wages subject to the tax needed about four and a half more cents to keep every promise. Munnell’s brief put the gap at roughly $30.5 trillion. The deficit was 3.82 per cent a year earlier; her brief attributed most of the worsening to lower assumed birth rates, lower immigration, and the 2025 tax law’s reduction in revenue from taxing benefits.

And the 22 per cent is only where the cut begins. Munnell’s brief noted that the share of retirement benefits payable would fall over time, to 62 per cent by 2100. The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget, citing the Congressional Budget Office, notes that CBO’s own projection puts the average cut from 2032 to 2036 at about 28 per cent. Estimates differ. None of them is gentle. And even these may prove optimistic: as Mark Miller, writing in the New York Times on October 14, 2026 (”Social Security Says Retirees Will Get a 3.6% Raise for 2027”), reported, the trustees’ dates are likely best-case projections, because key assumptions have worsened since June, including lower expectations for the size of the future population.

The Month After

Most retirees who collect Social Security are also enrolled in Medicare, and for many of them the Part B premium for doctors’ services comes straight out of the Social Security cheque before it reaches the bank. In 2026 that standard premium is $202.90 a month, up from $185 in 2025, as the AARP Public Policy Institute reported on January 21, 2026.

Apply today’s premium to our man with no savings. Before the cut, $1,543 less $202.90 leaves about $1,340 to live on. After the cut, $1,204 less $202.90 leaves about $1,001. The cut to his benefit is 22 per cent; the cut to what actually lands in his account is about 25 per cent, because the premium is a fixed bite out of a smaller loaf. (A “hold-harmless” rule shields most beneficiaries from seeing their cheque shrink because of a premium increase. It was not designed to shield anyone from a cut to the benefit itself.)

There is a bitter irony in the calendar. On October 14, 2026, the Social Security Administration announced a 3.6 per cent cost-of-living increase for 2027, the largest since 2023, worth about $75 a month to the average beneficiary, according to the Times. The Medicare trustees project the Part B premium will rise to $209.50, which will absorb part of it. Each January the cheque inches up to keep pace with prices. In 2032, unless Congress acts, it falls by a fifth in a single month.

The damage does not stop at the household door. In a June 3, 2026 analysis titled “No State Spared”, the same committee estimated that the cut would take about $345 billion out of beneficiaries’ pockets in its first year, an average of about $500 a month per person. (It used the 2025 report’s 24 per cent cut, so the 2026 figures would be somewhat smaller.) Retirees spend their benefits locally, so that money is withdrawn from local economies. The loss would exceed one per cent of economic output in 40 states, and would reach 1.9 per cent in West Virginia, with Mississippi, Vermont, Maine and South Carolina not far behind. These are mostly states with older populations and lower incomes, the places with the least room to make up the difference.

There is a further reason the loss would ripple outward: retirees spend guaranteed income far more readily than savings. Jean Chatzky, writing in The Atlantic on October 12, 2026, in a piece titled “Americans Aren’t Spending Their Retirement Savings”, cited the economists David Blanchett and Michael Finke. They found that retired couples spend about 80 per cent of guaranteed income such as pensions and Social Security, but only about half of what they withdraw from investment accounts. People spend money they know will arrive again next month, and guard money they fear may not be replaced. A Social Security dollar is among the most reliably spent dollars in the American economy, so cutting it takes business away from grocers, pharmacies and landlords as surely as it takes income from retirees.

Then there is poverty. On September 15, 2026, the U.S. Census Bureau reported that Social Security moved 28.8 million people out of poverty in 2025 under its Supplemental Poverty Measure. The National Council on Aging said in a statement the same day that almost 21 million of them were older Americans. No other federal programme keeps more older Americans out of poverty. Even with the programme paying in full, the supplemental poverty rate for Americans 65 and older rose to 15.4 per cent in 2025, up from 9.4 per cent in 2020, as Suzanne Blake reported for Newsweek on September 16, 2026. I will not pretend to calculate how many more would fall below the line after a 22 per cent cut. But when a programme is the main thing holding tens of millions of people above poverty, cutting it by a fifth does not leave them where they were. A new AARP survey, reported in the same Times article, asked adults over 50 what they would do if their benefits fell by just 5 per cent. Forty-five per cent said they would have to cut back on groceries and basic household items. The default cut is more than four times that size.

Every Year Costs More

The cliff is not a fixed obstacle. It grows as you approach it. In its analysis of the 2026 report, published June 9, 2026, the committee calculated that closing the 75-year gap with action taken now would require the equivalent of a payroll-tax increase of about 4.25 percentage points, roughly a 34 per cent rise, or a cut of about 25 per cent to all benefits. Wait until 2034 and those become about 4.9 points, a 40 per cent rise, or a 29 per cent cut. The committee’s verdict was that “the existing cost of delay is already very large.”

The reason is simple arithmetic. A fix enacted early can be phased in over many years and spread across more generations of workers and retirees, which is exactly what the trustees themselves urge. A fix enacted at the cliff edge has to hit whoever happens to be standing there. The committee noted that some reforms which would once have restored solvency on their own, such as eliminating the payroll-tax ceiling entirely, would now close only about half the gap.

Munnell’s brief notes that the 1983 Trustees Report, published just after the last great rescue, showed a tiny 75-year surplus. Some decline since was inevitable, because each year the window slides forward to include another distant, expensive year. But every Congress since has received the same annual warning.

A fix exists. Munnell concluded that the required changes are well within the range of shifts the country has absorbed before. Part V of this series will set out every lever. Part VIII will propose a bill. But no bill will be cheaper next year than it would be this year.

Why Only What Comes In?

I receive CPP and OAS, and like most retirees I rarely think about where the money comes from, so long as it arrives. But one question kept nagging at me as I read the 2026 report, and this series must answer it before proposing anything.

Why is Social Security built so that, when its reserve runs out, it can pay only what payroll taxes bring in? Why can’t Congress simply cover the gap from general revenue, the way it covers almost everything else? Why is a programme this important legally fenced off from the rest of the federal budget, so that its fate depends on a single dedicated tax?

None of this was an accident. It was a choice, made deliberately in 1935 by a president who thought very carefully about how to build something his successors could not easily take apart. The design that now produces the cliff is the same design that has protected the programme for nine decades.

That is where Part II begins.
