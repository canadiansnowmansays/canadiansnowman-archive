Stephen Gordon · Reported through October 20, 2026

Empty-chairing is an old trick in democratic politics. In 2012 Clint Eastwood held a conversation with a vacant chair standing in for Barack Obama on the stage of the Republican National Convention. In 2019 Britain’s Channel 4, unable to persuade Boris Johnson to attend a leaders’ debate on climate change, put a melting block of ice at his podium. The empty chair is a way of telling an audience that somebody was invited and declined to come.

On October 20, 2026, Michael Barbaro of the New York Times did a much gentler version on The Daily. A few minutes into the episode, before his first real question to James Talarico, Barbaro mentioned that the show had also asked Ken Paxton for an interview and had so far been unable to lock anything down. Then he spent the rest of the hour talking to the man who did show up.

There was no ice sculpture, only a single sentence. In a race now in its first week of early voting, that sentence may carry more weight than anything else in the episode.

A Pattern, Not an Oversight

Regular readers will know the tally by now. In The Machine, on October 8, this series counted the debates Paxton has agreed to, and there were none. In the second week of October, as the San Antonio Current reported, Paxton explained the absence of debates by saying he had been unable to work out the details with the Talarico campaign. At the San Antonio rally on October 7, the president did most of the talking and Paxton got a walk-on. In The Feed, on October 17, the series noted that the official Republican TikTok account had not mentioned Paxton since early July, and that his own account had about as many followers as a small-town Friday-night football game.

Now add the country’s most listened-to news podcast. It is two weeks from Election Day, the Senate majority may run through Texas, and one candidate gave the Times nearly an hour while the other did not call back.

In fairness, Paxton is not in hiding. On the evening of October 19, the first day of early voting, he rallied in Fort Worth, in the old waiting room of a former train station, under Art Deco chandeliers, with a free bar. As J. David Goodman and Jesus Jiménez reported for the New York Times on October 20, 2026, he read his speech from a teleprompter in a low-key voice, standing at a lectern that said “Ken Paxton Goes After Them.” The opening act was Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who praised Paxton’s recklessness in the use of his office and meant it as a compliment. About fifteen minutes away, in a high-school gymnasium with no bar, Talarico told more than a thousand people that Fort Worth was his slingshot. He used a teleprompter too. The difference is not the teleprompter. A rally is a room where nobody asks a question. A studio has a man who asks the same one three times.

The irony is hard to miss. Only a fortnight ago, the people working for Paxton were spreading the story that Talarico had vanished. When he spent nine days at home in Austin with the flu, made worse by his Type 1 diabetes, the right-wing internet put him in a psychiatric hospital in Ohio, a state he says he has never visited. The hospital denied it, and the Associated Press found that the timeline did not hold together. On The Daily, Talarico called it Flugate and laughed, which is about the right amount of attention to give it. The Last Resort has already dealt with the vice-president’s contribution, and it needs no repeating here.

The point is simpler. The candidate who was supposedly missing has been sitting in front of microphones. The one who is missing has no posters.

Top Versus Bottom

What did Talarico say with the hour? The most interesting part was not a policy but a map.

Most political coverage, he told Barbaro, still draws politics as a line from left to right. He used to see it that way himself. After four terms in the Texas House he now sees it as a contest between the top and the bottom. At the top are the very rich, including the people who own the cable networks, the newspapers and the platforms. At the bottom is everyone else. In his account, the people at the top profit from keeping the people at the bottom angry at one another, sorting them by party, race, religion and culture so that nobody notices whose taxes are going down and whose schools are being cut.

He has a name for the machinery that does this: the rage economy. A conspiracy theorist posts something outrageous, the post gets clicks, everybody on both sides gets angry, and nobody’s life improves. He offered himself as the latest example, without much self-pity, and said the larger damage is to the people who go online looking for connection and come away lonelier.

A Canadian reader will hear something familiar in this. In 1944 Tommy Douglas, the Saskatchewan premier who went on to give Canada public health insurance, used to tell a parable called Mouseland. The mice kept electing governments of cats, sometimes black cats and sometimes white cats, and kept being puzzled that the laws suited cats. Talarico’s vertical politics is Mouseland with an algorithm. It is an old argument, and the fact that it is old doesn’t make it wrong.

It also explains a figure that ought to be impossible. In Times polling, Talarico is favoured by 9 per cent of Republicans and 11 per cent of Trump voters, in a state Donald Trump carried by about 14 points in 2024. Barbaro asked him why. His answer was that people stuck on the left-right map see a Democrat, while people on the top-bottom map see a man who takes no corporate PAC money and is being opposed by roughly $200 million of other people’s, much of it, he says, from Elon Musk. He promised that Texas would show the world’s first trillionaire that it is not for sale. Musk, it should be said, is not yet a trillionaire by most counts. Talarico is rounding up, as candidates do.

The Cringe

Barbaro did not let him off lightly, and the most revealing part of the interview came when he wasn’t let off.

Republican advertising has spent months on a handful of Talarico’s old remarks from the Texas House: that there are six sexes, and that God is nonbinary. Barbaro raised them and then pressed three times. He pointed out that Talarico himself has called some of those remarks cringey, and he set the question beside Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez’s description of that period in Democratic politics as “Woke 1.”

Talarico’s defence had two parts. The first was that he had not changed but the Capitol had. In 2018 he was one of twelve Democrats who flipped Republican seats, and for a while that kept Texas Republicans close to the centre. Then came the near-total abortion ban, permitless carry, the removal of Martin Luther King Jr. and Frederick Douglass from the civics curriculum, and the book bans. He said he was defending constituents against laws that were not theoretical. His best line was about the ban on transgender athletes, which he said treated tee-ball the same as varsity baseball. Whatever you think of the policy, that is a precise criticism of a blunt law.

The second part was the concession. He does not regret the position, he said, but he does regret the phrasing, because it obscured the point he was trying to make. He then declined three times to turn his own regret into a judgment on his party, leaving that, as he put it, to the pundits.

Is that persuasive? Partly. The record bears out the chronology. As Lauren McGaughy reported for the New York Times on October 22, 2026, in a review of his nearly seven years in the Texas House, Talarico’s name appeared on more than 500 bills. More than three-quarters of those he wrote with others had at least one Republican author or sponsor, and 39 became law with him as lead author, among them a cap on prekindergarten class sizes and a path to high-school diplomas for inmates. Rice University’s rankings placed him just left of the middle among House Democrats, and he passed more laws after moving to the liberal Austin seat than before. The line he used in Fort Worth on October 19, that the election “shouldn’t be about the Democratic Party or the Republican Party,” is nearly word for word what he told the Texas House in 2019, weeks after he was first elected.

But the same review shows that more than the phrasing has changed. After the redistricting, his website stopped calling him “a proud Democrat who works with Republicans” and began calling him “the most effective progressive at the Texas Capitol.” This year his campaign literature no longer calls him a progressive at all. In September he told Fox News, “There are not six sexes. There are two,” and he now says he opposes transgender athletes in girls’ sports where safety or fairness is at stake. His campaign says that view predates the Senate race, but it could not point the Times to an earlier instance of him saying so. Tom Oliverson, the chairman of the House Republican Caucus, says he likes Talarico personally but calls much of him “performative theater.” On at least one point, then, this is a change of position, not of phrasing, and voters are entitled to notice. What remains unusual is the manner of it. A politician who says in public that he put something badly, without blaming the reporter, the clip or the algorithm, is a rare species in Texas. Ken Paxton, impeached by his own party in 2023, has never managed even that much.

What He’d Do With It

The policy agenda he laid out is more radical than his tone. Barbaro pointed this out, and Talarico agreed.

On political reform, he would ban corporate PACs and super PACs, ban members of Congress from trading stocks, overturn Citizens United, ban partisan gerrymandering in red and blue states alike, and impose term limits. He has already pledged to serve no more than two terms himself. On the economy, he supports a higher minimum wage, universal child care, cancelling medical debt and higher taxes on billionaires. Canadians, several years into a national programme of ten-dollar-a-day child care, may wonder why the last item on that list counts as daring. In Texas it does.

On the immediate problem of prices, he proposes suspending the federal gas and diesel taxes, which he says would cut about 18 cents a gallon. He frankly called that a stopgap. The real cause, in his view, is the war in Iran, and the remedy is for Congress to take back its constitutional power over war through the War Powers Act. Readers of the Iran Crisis series will recognize the argument. Hearing it from a Senate candidate in Texas is new.

Then there is the filibuster. Talarico wants it abolished. He called it a Jim Crow relic that appears nowhere in the Constitution. Barbaro made the obvious objection: Republicans have held back from abolishing it because they fear what Democrats would do with a simple majority, so shouldn’t a Democrat fear the reverse? Talarico gave the most interesting answer of the hour. The majority should govern, he said, whether or not he agrees with it. Gridlock is what makes voters swing wildly between the parties, and when the choice is between a status quo that never changes and a demagogue who promises to change everything, people will choose the demagogue. On his account, the filibuster does not protect the country from demagogues. It produces them.

You may think that is reckless. It is at least consistent: he is proposing to give up a weapon his own side would very much like to keep.

He was also honest about his ballot. He has voted for Republicans in non-partisan school-board and city-council races, but never, he believes, in a partisan race in Texas. He said the party of the two Bushes was gone before he was old enough to vote. More than twenty current and former Republican officials have endorsed him regardless. One of them, Todd Smith, a former Republican member of the Texas House, spoke at the Fort Worth rally on October 19 and said Talarico’s faith was the reason he trusted him.

The Impeachment He Already Cast

Near the end, Barbaro asked the question every Democrat running in 2026 is asked: if Democrats take the Senate, would he vote to impeach the president, or a member of the cabinet?

Talarico said he would not prejudge an impeachment trial on a podcast, which is the correct answer for someone who might one day sit as a juror. Then he gave the reason, and it was the sharpest moment of the interview.

He has done it before. On May 27, 2023, the Texas House voted 121 to 23 to impeach Attorney General Ken Paxton. Talarico voted yes, alongside a majority of Republicans. He told Barbaro that he had read every document in the case line by line and that at the time he called it the most important vote he would ever cast. The Texas Senate acquitted Paxton on September 16, 2023. Three years later, the man Talarico voted to remove is the man he is running against. In the years between, Talarico worked with Republican state senators on laws to combat opioid overdoses. One of them was Angela Paxton.

So when Talarico says he will weigh the evidence and vote accordingly, he is not offering a hypothetical. He is describing what he has already done to his opponent. Asked whether Trump is central to the corruption he campaigns against, he said the administration’s corruption is unprecedented. He added that members of his own family voted for Trump in 2024 expecting lower prices, a drained swamp, an end to the wars and the release of the Epstein files, and that they have received, in turn, tariffs, a larger swamp, a new war and redactions.

He also returned to faith, the subject of The Wrong Kind of Christian on October 6. Democrats, he argued, have confused the separation of church and state with a separation of faith and politics. He named Jimmy Carter, Martin Luther King Jr., Dorothy Day and Fred Rogers as people who brought their faith into public service without forcing it on anyone. Barbaro asked whether he would win on character or on policy. Talarico said he could not see the difference.

Across Fort Worth the same night, Paxton’s crowd was being told otherwise. Speakers repeated a line from an earlier podcast in which Talarico called himself a Christian who hates Christianity, and Paxton asked the room how many Christians they knew who would say such a thing. The Times noted that in the same conversation Talarico had praised the teachings of Jesus and objected to theology being enlisted for one party’s programme. That context did not make it into the rally, and rallies are not built to carry it.

If He Loses

Barbaro’s last question was the one every interviewer saves for the end: suppose you lose. Paxton goes to Washington, Texas stays red, the Democrats miss the Senate. What would that say?

Talarico did not dodge it. He said there would be more work to do, that he saw himself as one runner in a relay of Texans going back generations, and that whoever came next would take the baton. He conceded that big money is powerful and said his faith tells him it is nothing beside people power, even if the payoff comes long after November 3, or after his own lifetime.

On October 29 this series will ask the same question of the other candidate. Ken Paxton has not answered it on The Daily. He doesn’t need to. His answer has been on file at the Supreme Court of the United States since December 7, 2020.

The Chair

In fairness, the candidate who keeps turning up also chooses his chairs. For McGaughy’s review of his record, published two days after The Daily, the Talarico campaign sent written answers and did not make him available for an interview. Paxton’s campaign answered through its spokeswoman, Madison Cercy, who said Talarico was “scrubbing his website and pretending to be a moderate.” So one candidate gave the Times an hour and a written statement, and the other gave it a spokeswoman. The two of them have yet to sit across from each other.

A long-form interview is the one campaign event a super PAC cannot buy. Paxton’s allies can buy airtime, missing-person posters, a rally with the president and an open bar in an Art Deco waiting room. They cannot sit in a studio for an hour and answer a reporter who keeps asking the same question until he gets an answer. Only the candidate can do that.

Ken Paxton has had two hundred million dollars of other people’s money speaking for him all autumn, and perhaps he thinks he doesn’t need to speak for himself. Perhaps he is right. Texas has not elected a Democrat to the Senate since Lloyd Bentsen in 1988, and the habit of thirty-eight years is a powerful thing.

Early voting continues until October 30, and Election Day is November 3. Between now and then, Texans can decide whether the empty chair reflects a candidate’s confidence or is simply a chair with nobody in it.
