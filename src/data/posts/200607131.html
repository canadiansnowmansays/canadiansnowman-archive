The price is $135.

Not a range. Not a preliminary estimate subject to investor demand. A number. One number, declared by one man, filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission on the evening of June 3, 2026, and distributed to twenty-three investment banks whose names fill the cover page of the amended S-1 like the credits of a film that cost more to produce than most countries earn in a year.

SpaceX will sell 555.6 million shares at $135 each, raising $75 billion, and the company will be valued at $1.77 trillion when it begins trading on the Nasdaq under the ticker SPCX — expected June 12. The underwriters hold an option on an additional 83.3 million shares at the IPO price, worth another $11.2 billion. If exercised, the total raise would make this offering more than triple the size of Alibaba, which held the record for the largest U.S. IPO until this week.

The previous holder of the overall record was Saudi Aramco. Saudi Arabia’s state oil company raised $29.4 billion when it went public in 2019. SpaceX has raised more than twice that amount. Alibaba raised $21.8 billion on the New York Stock Exchange in September 2014, which held the record for years. Facebook raised $16 billion. SpaceX has raised more than all of them combined.

There is a certain irony in the Aramco comparison that the financial press has largely passed over in its excitement. Saudi Aramco went public as the crown jewel of a petrostate attempting to diversify away from the resource that built it — an act of managed transition, oil money funding a post-oil future. SpaceX goes public as the crown jewel of a private empire attempting to consolidate and expand — an act of acceleration, rocket money funding a post-Earth future. Both companies are, in their different ways, bets against the present. The market has decided which bet it prefers. The margin is not close.

The Circus That Arrived First

Before we get to $135, it is worth understanding what $500 million in banking fees looks like when it arrives on the streets of Manhattan.

At Goldman Sachs’s headquarters, rocket ships have taken over the lobby. Bank of America is lighting the spire atop its Midtown offices to resemble a rocket launch. JPMorgan Chase is hosting a SpaceX sales pitch to thousands of clients at its Park Avenue headquarters, broadcasting simultaneously to 90 bank branches and offices across 26 states, some of which closed early for the occasion. Jamie Dimon — the chairman and chief executive of the largest bank in America — will be there in person to sell the deal himself. He will be joined by Mary Erdoes, head of JPMorgan’s asset and wealth management unit. Bank of America is calling its gatherings “launch parties.”

These are not metaphors. This is what the largest IPO in history looks like from the street level: investment banks competing to demonstrate the most elaborate devotion to a rocket company, as if the building that most resembles a launchpad will win the most allocations.

The banks are projecting a take-it-or-leave-it attitude that mirrors their client’s. There was no price range. There was no roadshow in the traditional sense — no crisscrossing the country to pitch investors. Instead, Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs are inviting the largest potential investors to their Manhattan offices for private presentations. The message to everyone else is: $135, accept or sit out.

Fidelity hosted a webinar for its 401(k) customers on how to “get in early” on an IPO. Retail investors are expected to make up roughly 30% of buyers — an unusually large proportion that reflects both Musk’s fan base among day traders and the sheer volume of capital SpaceX needs to absorb. SpaceX has already hosted prospective high-net-worth investors on field trips to its Hawthorne campus to watch rockets being assembled.

It is, in the vocabulary of capital markets, a spectacle. And spectacles, as this series has learned over seven essays, are what happens when the engineering is extraordinary enough to make everyone forget to ask the harder questions.

The Price That Wasn’t a Range

The standard IPO playbook involves a preliminary price range — a band of numbers that signals where the company thinks the market will settle, leaving room for the roadshow to calibrate demand upward or downward before the final number is set. The price range is a negotiation in public form.

SpaceX skipped it. What SpaceX did instead was declare a number and dare the market to disagree. This is consistent with how Musk operates every other negotiation in his life: from a position of maximalism, without apparent anxiety about the counterparty’s reaction. It is the pricing equivalent of landing a rocket on a drone ship — an act designed to demonstrate that things everyone assumed were impossible are not only possible but routine.

SpaceX also chose to forgo disclosing historical quarterly earnings in its registration documents, as companies traditionally do. “For a fast-growing company, you would typically expect to see the quarterly breakdown,” noted Matt Kennedy, a senior IPO market strategist at Renaissance Capital. The absence of quarterly numbers signals that the company’s view should be considered through longer-term lenses. Which is another way of saying: don’t look at what we earned last quarter. Look at where we’re going.

The roadshow begins the week of June 8. The investors who attend it will not be price-discovering. They will be deciding whether $135 is the floor or the ceiling.

To The Moon — And We Mean That Three Ways

There is a phrase in the vocabulary of retail investors — a meme, technically, though it has migrated so far from its origins that calling it a meme undersells its cultural weight — that goes simply: to the moon. It means a stock is going to rise so dramatically that earthly comparisons fail. It means the trajectory is vertical and the destination is beyond imagination. It entered financial slang through cryptocurrency culture but has colonised every corner of retail trading. When a stock goes “to the moon,” it means the people who held it are going somewhere the people who didn’t cannot follow.

SpaceX’s IPO is the first time in the history of financial markets that the phrase is literally true.

The company going to the moon in Wall Street’s vocabulary is the company building the rocket that is actually going to the moon. The ticker symbol SPCX will, if Musk’s Artemis contract and Starship development proceed on schedule, represent ownership in the vehicle that carries human beings to the lunar surface in 2028. And the money being raised — $75 billion, the largest in history — will partly fund the construction of the lunar factory that Musk has written into his corporate filings as a stated objective.

Three meanings. One phrase. Ralph Kramden pointed at the ceiling and said to the moon, Alice as a joke about powerlessness. The financial internet says to the moon as a prayer about gains. Elon Musk says it as an engineering specification.

For the first time, all three are the same sentence.

What $75 Billion Buys

The money raised will help expand the company’s three main businesses: rockets and space travel including Starship development, Starlink satellite internet and mobile service, and artificial intelligence computing following its 2026 tie-up with xAI. Executives called the potential market across these areas “the largest actionable total addressable market in human history,” worth an estimated $28.5 trillion.

That last figure deserves its own paragraph. Twenty-eight point five trillion dollars. The current U.S. GDP is approximately $30 trillion. SpaceX has filed a document with the Securities and Exchange Commission claiming that its addressable market is approximately the size of the entire American economy. Whether this is visionary or delusional depends almost entirely on whether Starship works, Starlink becomes a global mobile carrier, and orbital AI compute infrastructure becomes an industry. None of these outcomes is guaranteed. All of them are physically possible. The market, at $1.77 trillion, is pricing significant probability on each.

Analysts have noted that SpaceX wants investors to believe the company will someday make 93 times what it currently makes in a year. The company lost $4.9 billion in 2025 and $4.3 billion in the first quarter of 2026 alone. The market is not buying earnings. It is buying trajectory.

This is not unprecedented. Amazon lost money for years while the market priced the destination rather than the present. Tesla was unprofitable for most of its first decade as a public company. The pattern — visionary company, patient capital, losses as investment rather than failure — is well established in technology. What is unprecedented is the scale. Amazon and Tesla were valued at tens of billions during their loss-making years. SpaceX is valued at $1.77 trillion while losing money. The patience the market is extending is of a different order entirely.

The Woman Who Built What Musk Priced

Shotwell with Philippine President Bongbong Marcos in 2023

Here is the detail that every launch party, every spire-lighting, every Jamie Dimon sales pitch has managed to obscure: while Elon Musk was dining at the White House, testifying against Sam Altman, and accompanying Donald Trump to Beijing, someone else was running SpaceX.

Her name is Gwynne Shotwell. She has been doing it for 24 years.

Shotwell, 62, is SpaceX’s president and chief operating officer. She was the company’s seventh employee, hired in 2002 after meeting Musk and proposing that he needed a full-time businessperson. She dithered for a month before accepting, then called him and said — in her own words — “I’ve been a bleeping idiot.” She has been there ever since, outlasting every other senior executive in a company whose CEO is not known for his patience with anyone who fails to share his exact vision at his exact pace.

While Musk accumulated headlines over the past six months, Shotwell’s itinerary looked like this: a telecom trade show in Barcelona championing Starlink Mobile; meetings with India’s communications minister in New Delhi, cultivating what could become SpaceX’s largest untapped market; appearances with tech executives at the White House pledging that AI data centres would not raise energy prices for Americans. No chainsaw emojis. No three a.m. posts. No trials.

“Elon represents the brilliant innovation and the vision,” said Peter Diamandis, a SpaceX investor. “And Gwynne is the engine that keeps everything operating on schedule.”

Former executives describe her as the “Elon whisperer” — the one person capable of absorbing Musk’s most ungovernable impulses and translating them into company goals that engineers can actually build toward. When Musk wanted to attack Mark Zuckerberg on social media after a SpaceX rocket explosion destroyed a $200 million Facebook satellite in 2015, Shotwell talked him down. “I like his in-person self better than his Twitter self,” she has said. “In fact, they feel like two different people to me many of the times.”

She is also, as of the IPO filing, a billionaire. The fifth-largest holder of Class A shares. Last year’s highest-paid SpaceX executive at more than $85 million in total compensation. She is not a footnote in this story. She is, in operational terms, the story — the person who converted Musk’s ambitions into a company that twenty-three banks are falling over themselves to sell to the world at $135 a share.

She will be at the JPMorgan event on Thursday, alongside the CFO, addressing thousands of investors. She will be at Bank of America’s launch party. She will do the work of the roadshow, as she has done the work of the company, while Musk’s name is on the marquee.

This is not a complaint on her behalf. By all accounts she has chosen this arrangement and flourished within it. But it is worth naming, as the launch parties light up Midtown and the lobby fills with rocket ships, that the company being sold to the world was built as much by a mechanical engineer from Northwestern who called herself “a bleeping idiot” for hesitating as it was by the man whose compensation package will be denominated in fractions of a trillion.

Control Is Not a Feature. It Is the Product.

Musk will own over 82% voting control after the offering. The share structure — Class B shares carrying ten votes to Class A’s one — means that the 5% of the company available for public trading after the IPO is, in governance terms, nearly weightless. Class B holders, voting separately, elect 51% of the board. Musk’s ability to elect directors through his Class B ownership is enshrined in the filing.

A Tesla shareholder who voted against Musk’s compensation package last year was exercising a right that exists at SpaceX only in theory. The public markets are being offered a financial stake in a controlled company whose controlling shareholder cannot be outvoted, cannot be fired from his three simultaneous executive positions, and whose related-party transactions — the Cybertruck purchases, the Megapack contracts, the Anthropic compute lease — cannot be blocked.

This is the price of admission: $135 buys you a seat on the rocket, but not a hand on the controls. Ralph Kramden sent Alice to the moon. He didn’t ask her to navigate.

The Question Nobody Is Asking Out Loud

Buried in the coverage of the IPO pricing, almost as an afterthought, is the sentence that changes the architecture of everything that follows: as SpaceX heads toward the public market, chatter is building that Musk’s ultimate goal is to combine the company with Tesla.

This is not fringe speculation. Musk has discussed with colleagues the possibility of folding the companies together, according to people familiar with the talks. Tesla and SpaceX already share engineers and collaborate on solving issues tied to power and compute constraints. Wedbush analyst Dan Ives has assigned more than 80% odds that Musk will combine Tesla and SpaceX following the IPO, expecting a merger by 2027.

Tesla’s Q1 2026 filing disclosed a $2 billion equity investment in SpaceX, and the two companies are jointly building a semiconductor research fab at the Gigafactory Texas campus. The merger, if it happens, would not be creating something new. It would be formalizing something that already half-exists in the plumbing. Sum the valuations and you land near $3.4 trillion.

If this happens, Tesla shareholders will be merging their $1.6 trillion company with an entity that Musk controls with 85% voting power — an entity that now includes the wreckage of Twitter, a money-losing AI company, and a rocket business whose valuation requires believing in Mars colonies and orbital data centres simultaneously. In any merger structured as SpaceX acquiring Tesla, public Tesla holders would convert into Class A stock with a fraction of the governance they have today.

The question nobody is asking out loud is a simple one. When Musk declared $135 as the price — one number, no range, no negotiation — was he pricing SpaceX? Or was he pricing the merger that comes next?

The Musk Stack series, which concluded at Part XII and is now receiving a coda in light of this week’s events, examines that question in the detail it deserves. The short answer is that the merger solves a Tesla governance problem more completely and more permanently than any milestone-based compensation package ever could. The long answer involves Delaware courts, Class B voting structures, and the quiet conversion of twenty years of shareholder friction into a single transaction.

The Moon, Reconsidered

Part I of this series opened with Ralph Kramden — the bus driver who couldn’t make rent, whose threat to send Alice to the moon was a joke about powerlessness, a man’s bluster substituting for his inability to change anything at all.

Seven essays later, the joke has a different shape.

The man who built the rocket that will actually go to the moon has just priced its parent company at $1.77 trillion. He is eight days from trading on the Nasdaq. He controls 82% of the votes in a company that is simultaneously a rocket maker, a satellite internet provider, an AI infrastructure business, and — if the merger chatter is correct — the future parent of the world’s most valuable electric vehicle company.

Ralph Kramden was powerless. He knew it. The joke was on him.

The man standing at the launchpad in South Texas is not powerless. He knows it. The joke, if there is one, is on us — the Class A shareholders, the retail investors at Schwab and Fidelity and Robinhood, the pension funds and index trackers who will own a sliver of SPCX at $135 a share and precisely none of the decisions that determine what that sliver is worth.

We are going to the moon.

We are not flying the rocket.
