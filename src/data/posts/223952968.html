This series’ China coverage, above all its companion piece The Diplomat’s Ceiling, has largely examined Beijing through the lens of safety diplomacy and party-security anxiety — Chen Yixin’s warnings, the Trump-Xi summit, Matt Sheehan’s account of China’s regulatory posture. This entry looks at the domestic machine those anxieties sit inside: the industrial strategy Xi Jinping has been building since 2014, and why it operates on a set of assumptions almost entirely different from the ones driving American AI policy.

The speech that started a twelve-year campaign

As The New York Times’ Vivian Wang reported on September 23, 2026, Xi Jinping’s AI ambitions trace to a single moment in June 2014, when he addressed China’s Academies of Engineering and Sciences for the first time as the country’s leader and became fixated on a submitted report predicting AI-powered robots would transform the global economy. His response wasn’t satisfaction that China would be the biggest market — it was a direct challenge: “can our technology and manufacturing capabilities keep up with the competition?” That question set off a strategy-building process still shaping policy today: Pan Yunhe, vice president of the Chinese Academy of Engineering, immediately organised a team to design a long-term national plan, arguing in a subsequent paper that China’s huge population gave it both an unmatched data supply and an unmatched demand for intelligent products — reasoning that surfaced almost verbatim three years later in China’s first official national AI plan, which set 2030 as the deadline for global AI leadership.

The scale of state commitment behind that goal is the piece of this series’ China coverage that has been missing until now. Economists estimate state-led funds poured more than $184 billion into Chinese AI firms between 2000 and 2023, with the government and state banks pledging hundreds of billions more in 2025 alone. That is a fundamentally different capital structure than the one this series’ own Capex Ledger companion thread has documented driving the American AI boom — private US investment hit $285 billion in 2025 alone, more than China’s entire multi-decade state commitment in a single year, meaning the two countries aren’t simply racing toward the same goal with different levels of enthusiasm; they’re running the race with structurally different engines entirely.

Why Beijing isn’t racing toward what Washington fears

The single most important distinction Wang’s reporting surfaces, and one this series’ prior China coverage hasn’t stated this directly, is that Chinese officials have largely declined to frame their own effort around artificial general intelligence at all — the “still-theoretical form of A.I. that could think and learn as well as — or better than — humans” that this series’ own RSI coverage in Part 103 and existential-risk coverage throughout have treated as the central object of American anxiety. Beijing’s stated goals are narrower and more immediate: intelligent robots to offset a rapidly shrinking, aging workforce; digital avatars of top urban doctors extending care to poor rural residents; and, stated with a bluntness no American official has matched in this series’ coverage, an enhanced capacity for the state to track where citizens are, what they say, and “perhaps even how they feel.” The government’s own benchmark — AI permeating 90 percent of Chinese society by 2030, with no stated methodology for how that figure would be measured — reads less like a scientific target than a political one, a number meant to convey totality of adoption rather than a metric anyone expects to be rigorously verified.

That framing difference explains something The Diplomat’s Ceiling’s account of Seth Center’s and Matt Sheehan’s skepticism toward a US-China safety accord could only gesture at from the American side: China’s own resistance to a coordinated global slowdown isn’t necessarily bad faith or a party-security smokescreen, on Wang’s account — it’s a coherent position given Beijing’s premise that it is “already fighting from behind” due to US chip export restrictions, and that agreeing to slow down now would simply cement an American lead China believes it hasn’t caught up to in the first place. Xi’s own embrace of open-source Chinese models, which The Diplomat’s Ceiling has already flagged as a genuine strategic risk from Washington’s vantage point, is in Beijing’s own accounting a rational response to that same competitive disadvantage: open models are winning Chinese companies users globally in a way closed, proprietary American systems structurally can’t match on price or access.

Betting the state can control what it’s unleashing

Wang’s reporting doesn’t treat Beijing’s confidence as unexamined. China has embraced AI despite real, acknowledged risk to the Communist Party’s own legitimacy: displaced taxi drivers, actors, and office workers feeding joblessness anxiety; chatbots posing a genuine threat to the state’s information controls; and advanced foreign models — Claude Mythos named directly, the same model The Diplomat’s Ceiling, in its account of Matt Sheehan’s WeWorm reporting, already identified as a specific Beijing security concern — making sophisticated attacks on Chinese infrastructure more accessible to adversaries. China’s own top spy chief has acknowledged that threat publicly, material The Diplomat’s Ceiling’s coverage of Chen Yixin’s warnings already established from a different angle.

Xi’s bet is that these risks are controllable through the same instruments the party has always used: strict content rules for chatbots, an outright ban on AI companion bots for children, court warnings against companies blindly replacing workers with automation, and, in the starkest illustration of the state’s confidence in its own coercive capacity, barring some leading AI entrepreneurs from leaving the country entirely. That last detail belongs directly alongside The Diplomat’s Ceiling’s account of China’s domestic AI anxiety — the “distillation” fears, the OpenClaw backlash Michael Schuman documented — as evidence that Beijing’s confident public posture coexists with genuine, managed unease about the same technology it’s racing to build.

The industrial playbook, and where it’s already straining

China’s approach to AI, per Wang’s reporting, largely mirrors the state-directed strategy that made it dominant in electric vehicles and solar panels: subsidies, tax breaks, and research money, deployed at a scale this series hasn’t previously quantified. A Shanghai district has offered up to $29 million to any AI company achieving a major “breakthrough”; a Beijing university hub spent roughly $580 million on AI in 2025 alone; Shenzhen spent more than $6 billion building the industrial park housing Xeno Dynamics, a robotic-exoskeleton startup whose founder, Hobo Liang, described the dynamic in terms that capture the whole system’s logic in one line: “We’re beneficiaries of the entire direction — whether it’s about robots, or A.I. You have to follow the direction of major policies.” Liang’s own personal trajectory — an engineer who once idolized Steve Jobs, Elon Musk, and Marvel’s Tony Stark, whose company now sits recognised as a national high-tech enterprise with a reduced tax rate and over $1.4 million in direct government research funding — is a specific, human-scale illustration of state industrial policy translating directly into individual career incentive.

But Cole McFaul, a Georgetown researcher this series hasn’t previously cited, supplies the honest caveat the rest of Wang’s reporting bears out: AI leadership, unlike electric vehicles or solar panels, depends on genuine technological breakthrough rather than subsidized manufacturing scale, and “subsidies can do only so much” against that constraint. China’s own chip shortage remains the clearest bottleneck — DeepSeek’s founder, Liang Wenfeng, has said directly that the company needs better chips, not more money — and the government’s $150 billion, decade-long push to develop domestic cutting-edge chips has not yet produced a major breakthrough, per Wang’s reporting. Beijing’s response to that specific constraint has been telling: rather than micromanaging research further, officials rolled back one of their most burdensome early rules, an initial requirement that generative AI output be guaranteed “true and accurate,” after companies argued the standard was simply impossible to meet — a concession to technical reality this series’ own account of American regulatory debates in Parts 105, 110, and 105 has not documented any US regulator or company successfully winning in reverse.

What the results actually show, and why Washington is responding to them directly

The scoreboard, on the numbers Wang’s reporting compiles, complicates any assumption that Beijing’s state-directed approach is failing to keep pace. China closed 2025 with more than 6,000 AI companies and a core AI industry valued at $171 billion; Chinese models, as of September, consistently outnumbered American ones on OpenRouter, a widely watched global AI-model popularity leaderboard, drawing users even at Western companies like Airbnb; and two Chinese models performed nearly as well as Anthropic’s leading Claude Fable 5 at a fraction of the cost — the same cost-and-capability dynamic The Diplomat’s Ceiling has already flagged as eroding the compute advantage export controls were designed to preserve. Robotics, the sector Xi originally championed in his 2014 speech, is the area where China’s lead looks most concrete rather than aspirational: more than 2 million robots now operate in Chinese factories, more than the rest of the world combined, and some Chinese researchers argue robots trained through direct physical-world interaction may be a more promising route to general-purpose intelligence than the large-language-model approach Silicon Valley has bet on.

That progress has already produced a direct American policy response this series hasn’t previously covered: in July, the US government proposed restricting imports of humanoid robots specifically, a move Beijing has denounced as discriminatory. A congressional advisory group’s warning — that China’s rapid real-world AI deployment generates exactly the kind of practical data that further improves Chinese models, a feedback loop separate from and potentially more durable than raw compute advantage — reads as a genuinely new argument this series hasn’t yet catalogued among the “beat China” justifications running through Parts 102 and 110 and The Diplomat’s Ceiling. Hobo Liang’s own closing reflection captures the shift better than any policy document could: “Before, we all used American technology as the benchmark, and we’d joke about ‘C-to-C’: ‘copy to China.’ I think it’s the mission of this generation of Chinese entrepreneurs to let the world know that some Chinese companies are different.” Whether that confidence survives China’s own chip bottleneck and the safety and legitimacy risks Xi is betting the party can manage is the question this series’ ongoing China coverage will keep testing — but on the evidence Wang has assembled, the premise that America simply leads and China simply follows no longer describes the actual state of the race.

The scorecard, category by category, published the day Xi landed

Wang’s reporting establishes the strategy. The New York Times’ Dustin Volz and Cade Metz, reporting September 23, 2026 — published as Xi’s state visit began — supply the direct comparative scorecard this series’ China coverage has lacked until now: six specific categories where the US and China each hold genuine advantages, rather than a single undifferentiated “who’s ahead” verdict. Western experts generally place the US roughly six months ahead of China on leading models, Volz and Metz report — but that single figure, as their reporting makes clear, obscures a far more complicated balance of trade-offs than this series’ prior coverage has fully captured.

Chips remain the clearest, least contested American advantage. Andrew Feldman, chief executive of chipmaker Cerebras, states it plainly: “The people who have made the fastest chips are here. And they are not in China.” Three successive US administrations have restricted Chinese access to top-tier chips, and Chinese AI researchers themselves, per Volz and Metz, routinely cite chip shortages as their single biggest obstacle — consistent with Wang’s account, earlier in this essay, of DeepSeek’s Liang Wenfeng saying the company needs better chips, not more money. Huawei’s own chip-making effort, meant to close that gap domestically, remains by its own admission years away from matching Nvidia’s current output, and even Chinese firms already using Huawei chips, like Moonshot, are struggling to secure enough of them to meet demand — the same compute bottleneck identified earlier in this essay as Beijing’s most serious unresolved constraint.

Public opinion, by contrast, cuts sharply in China’s favour — a dimension The Diplomat’s Ceiling’s domestic-anxiety coverage didn’t fully capture in comparative terms. A Pew poll released in September found 60 percent of Americans uncomfortable with a new local data center in their area, and on November 3 the backlash reached the ballot box. As Matteo Wong reported for The Atlantic, voters in at least 10 states passed more than 30 local measures restricting data-centre construction, recalled mayors who had backed projects, and elected governors who support statewide data-centre bans. Two days later, technology stocks had their worst day in more than a year. China’s centralised system faces no comparable public reckoning, and no ballot box on which to register one. A KPMG survey of 47 countries found 69 percent of Chinese respondents believe AI’s benefits outweigh its risks, against just 35 percent of Americans — meaning the country with less raw computing power currently has the more politically permissive environment in which to deploy what it has.

Economic resilience currently favours the US, though Volz and Metz flag real fragility on both sides rather than a clean American win. US stock indices sat near record highs in late September, with the predicted AI bubble not yet materializing, even as some evidence points to slower wage growth and fewer job openings in AI-exposed roles, particularly for young workers. China’s own economic backdrop is considerably more precarious — high youth unemployment, consumer pullback, a deflationary spiral — with Chinese economists themselves warning that AI-centered state spending may be deepening those vulnerabilities rather than offsetting them, a domestic economic strain The Diplomat’s Ceiling’s account of Chinese social-media anxiety documented at the individual level without yet connecting it to this macro picture.

The open-source divide is where Volz and Metz’s reporting most directly complicates this series’ own prior framing. American labs, including OpenAI and Anthropic, keep tight proprietary control over their models, building the guardrails this series has covered extensively since Part 93 — and were, by late September, lobbying for government review requirements that would formalize that closed approach. Chinese companies including DeepSeek, Moonshot, and Z.ai do the opposite, releasing their most powerful systems as open source, letting global developers build directly on Chinese infrastructure. Rehaan Ahmad, a Silicon Valley AI founder who’s used the latest Chinese models for months, offers the assessment that should concern this series’ American readers most directly: “These models are still rough around the edges, but there are tasks where they are on par with the leading American systems.” If American regulation ends up constraining domestic open-source projects specifically — a live possibility this series’ Part 110 governance-proposal coverage didn’t examine from this angle — global developers may simply build on Chinese open models by default, making China’s own infrastructure the world’s standard regardless of which country’s closed models remain technically superior.

Electric power is the wild-card advantage this series hasn’t previously weighted properly. Feldman’s own framing captures the mechanism directly: “At a fundamental level, the tech industry is trying to convert electricity into intelligence. It’s like a Dr. Seuss machine. In goes chips and power — and out comes intelligence.” China has spent years modernizing its electrical grid specifically to support this kind of industrial demand, while Feldman describes the American grid bluntly as “1950s technology” — meaning that if China’s own chip-making effort eventually closes the gap this essay has already identified as its most serious constraint, grid capacity, not chips, could become the actual deciding factor in which country can physically run more compute at scale.

Talent is the final category, and arguably the most durable one regardless of how the other five resolve. China has, by some measures, produced nearly half the world’s leading AI researchers as far back as 2024, against roughly 18 percent from the US. Researchers trained in China have long powered American labs directly, but Volz and Metz report a growing number are now choosing to stay home, working for Moonshot, Z.ai, and DeepSeek instead of relocating to Silicon Valley — a reversal of the brain-drain pattern that has favoured American AI development for decades, and one this essay’s earlier account of Hobo Liang’s “copy to China” reversal already flagged from the entrepreneurial side rather than the research-talent side specifically.

Taken together with Wang’s account of Beijing’s strategy, this scorecard resolves the question Wang’s reporting left open: whether China’s twelve-year, state-directed campaign is actually working. The honest answer, on Volz and Metz’s own accounting, is neither yes nor no cleanly — America holds the sharper, more consequential advantage in raw chip access, but China holds real, underweighted advantages in public tolerance, open-source distribution, grid capacity, and talent retention, any one of which could prove decisive depending on which constraint turns out to matter most as the technology matures.
