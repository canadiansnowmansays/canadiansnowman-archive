The Mongol conquests are, by a considerable margin, the most destructive episode this series has had to describe, and any account that moves too quickly past that in order to reach the interesting administrative consequences is committing a kind of quiet dishonesty. The campaigns that began under Genghis Khan in the early thirteenth century and continued under his successors killed people on a scale that medieval sources struggled to convey and modern demographers still argue about. Cities that resisted were destroyed as a matter of announced policy, with the destruction publicized specifically so that the next city would not resist — a strategy that was, on its own brutal terms, extremely effective. Part III of this series described what happened to Samarkand in 1220; the same thing happened at Merv, at Nishapur, at Herat, at Baghdad in 1258, and at dozens of less famous places. The irrigation systems of Khorasan, built up over centuries, were wrecked in ways that took generations to repair and in some cases were never repaired at all.

Having said that plainly, the rest of this installment is about what the Mongols did afterward, because the administrative apparatus they built across the conquered territory was genuinely remarkable and had more to do with the Silk Road’s brief final flourishing than any other single factor in its history.

The first thing to understand is scale. By the 1260s, Mongol rule extended from Korea to Hungary, from Siberia to the Persian Gulf — the largest contiguous land empire ever assembled, and the only political entity that has ever held the entire length of the overland route from the Chinese coast to the Black Sea under a single authority. Every previous arrangement this series has covered involved segmentation: Han China at one end, Parthia or Persia in the middle, Rome or Byzantium at the other, with intermediaries filling gaps and taking margins at every transfer. The caliphates, as Part X described, eliminated a great deal of that friction across the western and central portions. The Mongols eliminated it across the whole thing.

The second thing to understand is that they knew what they had. There is a persistent notion that the Mongols were rapacious nomads who happened to leave trade functioning by accident, and it does not survive contact with the evidence. Mongol rulers actively cultivated merchants, granted them privileged legal status, extended state capital to merchant partnerships, and built the physical and administrative infrastructure that long-distance commerce required. The ortoq system, in which Mongol nobles and the state itself provided capital to merchant associations — frequently Muslim or Uyghur merchants with existing networks — in exchange for a share of profits, amounted to state venture financing for caravan trade, and it operated at considerable scale. A steppe aristocracy with enormous plunder-derived wealth and no traditional mechanism for investing it turned out to be an excellent source of trade capital, and the merchants who took it were exempted from various taxes and given official protection along the routes.

The infrastructure that made this work was the yam, and it deserves the attention this installment is largely built around, because it is the closest thing the premodern world produced to a continental communications utility. The yam was a relay system of post stations spaced roughly a day’s ride apart along the empire’s major routes — estimates of the total number run into the thousands — each maintaining a stock of horses, provisions, and staff. A rider carrying an official paiza, a metal tablet issued as a warrant of authority in gold, silver, or bronze according to the bearer’s rank, could arrive at a station, exchange an exhausted horse for a fresh one, eat, and continue. Because the horse changed and the rider did not have to, sustained speeds became possible that a single mount could never manage: contemporary accounts describe urgent dispatches covering distances in days that would ordinarily have taken weeks, with the fastest couriers reportedly bandaged tightly around the torso to withstand the ride and riding through the night.

The stations were maintained by levies on the local population, which was resented and periodically ruinous — the obligation to supply horses, fodder, and food to an unpredictable stream of official travellers fell on communities that received nothing in return, and complaints about yam abuse appear regularly in the sources, particularly the practice of unauthorized travellers using forged or borrowed paiza to extract free hospitality. But the system worked. It moved intelligence, orders, tribute, and officials across a continent at speeds that no European or Middle Eastern state could approach, and it did so reliably enough that the Mongol high command could actually coordinate campaigns and administration over distances that would otherwise have made a unified empire impossible. Merchants were not the intended users — the yam was a state instrument — but the roads, the security, the wells, and the general suppression of banditry that the system required all served commercial traffic as a byproduct.

Layered on top was a legal and fiscal environment unusually favourable to trade. The yasa, the body of Mongol law attributed to Genghis Khan, survives only in fragments and secondhand descriptions, and scholars disagree about how codified it ever really was — but the provisions that later writers consistently attribute to it are revealing about priorities: severe penalties for horse theft, protections for envoys and merchants travelling under imperial authority, prohibitions on fouling water sources, and exemption of religious personnel from taxation. Whatever its precise form, the practical effect across the empire was a predictable, harshly enforced expectation that people moving on official business or under a paiza would arrive unmolested, and the consistency of foreign travellers’ testimony on this point — that one could cross the entire Mongol realm in relative safety — is the strongest evidence that the expectation was largely met.

The Mongols were, by the standards of the era, close to indifferent on religion: Genghis Khan’s successors patronized Buddhist, Muslim, Christian, and Daoist establishments more or less interchangeably, exempted clergy of all faiths from taxation, and staged formal religious debates at court apparently for the entertainment as much as the enlightenment. This was not tolerance as principle any more than Tang openness had been — it was a shrewd governing posture for a small nomadic elite ruling an enormous and religiously varied population, and it collapsed into partisanship soon enough as individual khanates converted. But while it lasted, a merchant of any faith could operate across the entire route without the confessional frictions that had complicated commerce in every previous era.

The commercial consequences show up in the movement of people, which is the best available proxy. The thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries produced an unprecedented number of long-distance travellers whose journeys are documented: Marco Polo, who has the following installment to himself; the Franciscan envoys William of Rubruck and John of Plano Carpini, sent by European rulers and popes to assess the Mongols and left detailed accounts; Rabban Bar Sauma, a Nestorian Christian monk born near Beijing who travelled west and ended up as a Mongol ambassador to the courts of Byzantium, Rome, France, and England in the 1280s, taking communion from the Pope and meeting Edward I; and Ibn Battuta, whose thirty-year itinerary in the following century covered much of Afro-Eurasia. That a Turkic Christian monk from northern China could be received by the King of England is not a normal feature of medieval history. It happened because a single political authority had made the journey survivable.

Technology and expertise moved with the people, and Mongol policy accelerated this deliberately. Conquered craftsmen were routinely spared and deported to work elsewhere in the empire, which was miserable for the individuals involved and extraordinarily effective at diffusing technique: Chinese siege engineers appear at Persian sieges, Persian and Arab engineers appear at Chinese ones, and the Mongol adoption of counterweight trebuchets from the Islamic world for use against Song Chinese fortifications is a documented instance of a weapon crossing a continent in a few years. Persian astronomers and Chinese astronomers worked at the same observatory at Maragha in Ilkhanid Persia. Chinese medical texts reached Persia; Persian ones reached China. The Ilkhanid vizier Rashid al-Din, himself a physician, produced in the early fourteenth century the Jami’ al-Tawarikh, a history that attempted to cover the Mongols, the Chinese, the Indians, the Franks, and the Islamic world in a single work — arguably the first serious attempt at a world history written from a position that actually had access to sources from all of those places.

Currency deserves a paragraph, because the Mongols attempted something in this area that was centuries ahead of anywhere in Europe and that failed in an instructive way. The Yuan Dynasty in China operated a paper currency system inherited and expanded from Song and Jin precedent, issuing notes backed initially by silver reserves and made legally compulsory for most transactions — a functioning fiat-adjacent currency circulating across the largest economy in the world. European visitors found this genuinely astonishing; the passage in which Marco Polo describes the Great Khan turning mulberry bark into money that everyone accepts as though it were gold is among the details that later readers cited as evidence he had made the whole journey up. The Ilkhanate in Persia, impressed by the Chinese example and facing a fiscal crisis, attempted to introduce paper money in Tabriz in 1294, printing notes that reportedly carried the Islamic profession of faith alongside Chinese characters. The experiment collapsed within months: Persian merchants simply refused the notes, commerce in the city seized up, and the policy was abandoned amid considerable public anger. The contrast is worth noting. A currency instrument works only where the issuing authority has the reach to compel acceptance and the credibility to sustain it, and the Ilkhanate had neither in the way the Yuan did. Even under a single dynastic family spanning a continent, the administrative depth varied enormously from one end to the other.

One further feature of Mongol governance is worth noting because it has no equivalent in the sedentary empires this series has covered: the political prominence of women in the ruling family. Steppe society assigned women substantial authority over the household economy while men were absent on campaign, and at the imperial level this translated into real power. Töregene Khatun governed the empire as regent for roughly five years after the death of her husband Ögedei in 1241, and it was during her regency that the Mongol advance into Europe halted — the armies that had shattered Hungarian and Polish forces in 1241 withdrew eastward on news of Ögedei’s death, and the succession politics that followed occupied the leadership for years. Sorghaghtani Beki, widow of Genghis Khan’s youngest son and mother of both Möngke and Kublai, is described by Persian, Chinese, and Armenian sources alike as one of the most capable political operators of her generation, managing appanage lands in northern China with a light fiscal hand that preserved their productivity while positioning her four sons for power. Rashid al-Din, no sentimentalist, wrote of her in terms he applied to almost no one else. The recurring pattern in which a khan’s death froze the empire’s military operations while the family convened to argue about succession was, in the end, one of the structural weaknesses that fragmented the thing — but it also meant that the individuals doing the arguing, and frequently governing in the interim, included women whose decisions shaped the course of Eurasian history.

None of it lasted long. The empire fragmented into four effectively independent khanates within two generations of Genghis Khan’s death in 1227, and by the mid-fourteenth century those khanates were in open conflict with one another — the Ilkhanate and the Golden Horde fought repeatedly over the Caucasus, and the Chagatai lands split. The Ilkhanate collapsed entirely in the 1330s. The Yuan Dynasty was expelled from China in 1368. The Golden Horde fragmented over the following century. And the friction the Mongols had removed came flooding back: borders, tolls, hostile authorities, banditry, and the requirement to negotiate passage with several mutually antagonistic states rather than one.

Part VII of this series has already described the other thing that travelled on the unified route, and the two collapses are related. The commercial world that the yam and the ortoq system and the paiza had built was, by the 1350s, contending with fragmenting political authority, catastrophic demographic loss, and a European buyer increasingly persuaded that the overland option had become unreliable. Those buyers began looking seriously at ships.

What the Mongol century leaves this series is the cleanest available demonstration of a point the earlier installments have been circling. The Silk Road was never really a road; it was a set of conditions. When those conditions held — tolerable security, predictable tolls, a legal framework a foreign merchant could rely on, and infrastructure someone was paying to maintain — traffic flowed and the volume was limited mainly by demand. When they failed, the same physical geography carried a fraction of the traffic at several times the cost. The Mongols supplied those conditions across a greater distance than anyone before or since, having arrived at them not through any theory of commerce but because an empire that needed to move orders quickly across a continent had to build the apparatus anyway, and the merchants were welcome to use the roads once they existed. It is the same accident that opened the route under Zhang Qian, at the largest scale it ever reached.
