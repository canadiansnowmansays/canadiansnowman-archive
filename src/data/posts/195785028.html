Bart and Amy Snyder are self-described hard-core Republicans who voted for Donald Trump. They did not know what a data center was until they discovered that one had been approved 300 yards from their farmhouse in Wolcott, Indiana. After learning the proposed facility would consume more than four million gallons of water a day, Bart Snyder, 59, worried about the backyard pond where he fishes for largemouth bass. He sued to halt the project and funded a campaign to unseat the three county officials who had supported it.

“Now, I don’t care what affiliation you are,” he said. “If you’re against data centers, we’ll join forces.”

That sentence — from a hard-core Republican farmer in Indiana who had never been politically active before — is the most important thing said about AI governance in America this month. Not because of what it says about data centers. Because of what it says about coalitions.

The Coalition Silicon Valley Didn’t See Coming

The industry that has spent the past decade dismissing its critics as doomers and NIMBYs is confronting something it does not have a label for. The people pushing back against AI in the spring of 2026 include an evangelical pastor in Austin who heard about a marriage nearly destroyed by an AI companion. A Boise musician and his wife — an elementary school band teacher — who discovered AI had been trained on copyrighted music and started a local chapter of PauseAI. A former Tea Party organizer who became chair of a conservative anti-AI group called Humans First. The director of the Sierra Club’s Grand Canyon Chapter. Bernie Sanders. Steve Bannon.

This is not a constituency that Silicon Valley’s political playbook was designed to handle. The industry’s standard response to criticism — pour hundreds of millions into super PACs targeting politicians who question AI, dismiss critics as paranoid Luddites, emphasize the technology’s benefits — was built for a different kind of opposition. It was built for wonky NGOs and European regulators. It was not built for farmers worried about their bass ponds, pastors worried about their congregations’ marriages, and musicians worried about their livelihoods.

“I’ve been called a lot of things over the years working on issues,” said Sandy Bahr, director of the Sierra Club’s Grand Canyon Chapter, “but doomers is a new one.”

A recent Quinnipiac University poll found that 55 percent of American adults now see AI as a force for harm rather than good. The industry has responded by spending more money. OpenAI co-founder Greg Brockman gave millions to a super PAC specifically designed to knock down AI regulation. The industry’s most notable political response to a majority of the American public viewing its technology as harmful has been to fund campaigns against the politicians those people elect.

The Chenoweth Threshold

This series examined the 3.5 percent threshold in an earlier essay on democratic resistance — the finding by political scientist Erica Chenoweth that no government has successfully suppressed a nonviolent movement that mobilized more than 3.5 percent of the population. The threshold matters here because the coalition that Tripp Mickle documents in the New York Times is not a single-issue movement. It is a convergence of separate grievances — water rights, copyright, marriage and family, job security, environmental protection — that are finding each other.

The Snyders in Indiana built relationships with people of different political stripes through their data center fight. Jack and Cathryn Gardner in Boise expanded PauseAI from five cities to thirty in a single year, with 500 signatures on their local petition alone. Pastor Michael Grayston in Austin has been speaking at other churches, writing opinion pieces, and drafting educational materials for faith leaders across the country.

None of them were politically active before AI. All of them are now. The movement looks nothing like the effective altruism circles that fund some of its institutional infrastructure. It looks like the country — which is precisely the point.

Silicon Valley’s dismissal of this coalition as paranoia peddled by doomers and NIMBYs is not just politically miscalculated. It is factually wrong. The Snyders are not afraid of technology. They are afraid of four million gallons of water a day disappearing from a county aquifer. The Gardners are not afraid of the future. They are afraid that the future will be built on stolen songs. The pastor in Austin is not afraid of AI. He is afraid of what it is doing to marriages in his congregation right now.

These are not abstract fears about superintelligence. They are concrete harms, already present, already documentable, already generating lawsuits and recall campaigns and petition drives. The gap between how the industry frames the opposition and what the opposition actually is — that gap is itself a governance failure.

What Sanders Said

Bernie Sanders, in an interview with the New York Times, asked the question that the bipartisan House AI Task Force bill — the deepfakes and whistleblowers bill examined in Part 31 — failed to ask:

“Given AI and robotics are going to impact every man, woman and child in this country, one might think that there’d be a massive debate in the United States Congress: What does it mean? Where do we go? How do we deal with it? There has been minimal, minimal discussion.”

He is right. And the reason there has been minimal discussion is not that Congress lacks the information. The trial in Oakland is generating public exhibits. The Mythos announcement generated global alarm from central banks. The Farrow-Marantz investigation spent a year documenting the governance vacuum at the industry’s leading company. The information exists. The political will to act on it is what the industry’s super PAC money is specifically designed to suppress.

Steve Bannon, who agrees with Sanders on approximately nothing else, said the backlash reflects the fact that “there’s not clarity, there’s not transparency, and there’s certainly not accountability.” Sacha Haworth of the Tech Oversight Project said the same thing in different words after the Oakland trial opened. Max Tegmark said it with a sandwich shop. The diagnosis is not contested. Only the response is.

The TACO Framework Applied Domestically

This series developed the TACO framework — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — to describe how the Trump administration has navigated trade disputes. It applies with equal precision to how the AI industry is navigating domestic resistance.

Threaten: Super PACs targeting politicians who question AI. The “doomer” label applied to anyone raising safety concerns. The implicit threat that slowing AI means losing to China.

Accommodate: Jensen Huang says AI will create more jobs. Sam Altman says AI will cure cancer and give everyone free education. The industry acknowledges concerns and promises to address them — in future voluntary commitments, in future safety frameworks, in future versions of the model.

Capitulate: The industry has not meaningfully capitulated on anything. The Lieu bill — deepfakes and whistleblowers — is the nearest thing to a legislative concession, and it was designed from the outset to avoid controversy.

Obfuscate: The dismissal of opposition as NIMBYism, doomsterism, and technophobia. The framing of regulation as a China-race liability. The replacement of substantive governance debate with the spectacle of the Oakland trial, where the question of whether a public promise matters is being litigated between two billionaires while the public watches from the courthouse steps.

The Snyders in Indiana are not playing TACO. They are playing a different game — the oldest game in democratic politics. They are building a coalition, showing up at county commission meetings, funding recall campaigns, and joining forces with people they disagree with on everything except the thing that is happening to their pond.

That is how democratic resistance has always worked. It is slower than super PACs. It is messier than lobbying. It does not have a billionaire at its centre. And historically, when it reaches sufficient scale, it is harder to stop than any of those things.

The Asimov Frame

Asimov’s robots were not resisted by philosophers or regulators. They were resisted by ordinary people who encountered them in ordinary life — people who found that the machines, despite their Three Laws, were changing the conditions of their existence in ways they had not consented to and could not control.

The resistance in his stories was rarely sufficient. The robots were too capable, too embedded, too economically necessary to simply remove. But the resistance was always legitimate — always a reasonable response from people who understood, correctly, that something consequential was happening to their world without their participation.

The farmer in Wolcott, Indiana, who doesn’t care what your political affiliation is as long as you’re against what’s happening to his water supply — he would have recognized Asimov’s resistant humans immediately. He is one of them.

The question Asimov’s stories always asked was not whether the resistance was right. It was whether the resistance was in time.
