There is a particular kind of confidence required to sit in front of two studio easels holding your own book — the cover a mugshot-orange jumpsuit behind bars, the title screaming that a 84-year-old immunologist must “rot in prison” — and lecture the audience on credibility. On November 24, 2025, Peter Navarro did exactly that on Steve Bannon’s War Room, flanked by cardboard cutouts of Why Fauci MUST Rot in Prison, a QR code inviting viewers to pre-order, and the unmistakable comfort of a man who has never once let the facts of his own record interrupt the performance.

I have spent the better part of a year cataloguing this administration’s ethical vertigo — men and women who denounce in others precisely the sins for which they themselves have already been caught. Navarro deserves his own entry, not because he is unique among Trump’s staff in this respect, but because his particular contribution to the genre is so structurally elegant. He did not merely commit hypocrisy. He built a business model out of it, and now he is selling the sequel.

The economist who did not exist

Start with the joke that tells itself. For more than a decade, in five different books beginning with Death by China in 2011, Navarro built out an intellectual apparatus for his hardline trade views by citing a source named Ron Vara — described as a China expert with dire warnings about the dangers of Chinese manufacturing, right down to the memorable claim that eating Chinese food required a certain nuttiness. Ron Vara does not exist. He never has. The name is an anagram of “Navarro,” and the man behind it eventually admitted, once the joke had outlived its usefulness, that Trump himself would understand the wink.

There is a meaningful difference between an author using a pen name and a policy adviser inventing a fictional authority to lend outside validation to his own arguments, in nonfiction books marketed as serious trade analysis rather than satire. The former is a literary convention. The latter is closer to what regulators call an undisclosed conflict of interest, if regulators bothered to police economic punditry the way they police, say, the safety claims of the electric-vehicle industry. Navarro spent years quoting himself, under an assumed identity, in order to sound less alone in his own convictions. And now — with a straight face, standing in front of a bookshelf of props — he has appointed himself prosecutor, judge, and jury of Anthony Fauci’s credibility, on the theory that Fauci “lied” to the American people about matters ranging from the China travel ban to school closures to masks.

I will grant Navarro this much: a man who has spent a career manufacturing his own expert witness is unusually well positioned to recognize the technique in someone else. It takes one to know one. It does not, however, qualify him to write the definitive account of who deserves prison for institutional failure during a pandemic — a subject on which Navarro’s own record, as it happens, includes an actual criminal conviction, which is more than can currently be said of the man on his book jacket.

It is worth remembering, too, that this is not Navarro’s first attempt to settle the Fauci score in public. Back in November 2021, on the same War Room platform, Navarro told Bannon he had urged Trump to fire Fauci, describing the internal fight in the White House as a Churchill-versus-Hitler scenario and recommending, in his own words, that the “Fauci baby” be strangled in its crib before it could do more damage — a phrase he has never quite lived down and one that makes his current framing of Fauci’s diary as a shocking revelation somewhat less surprising than the marketing implies. Navarro was not a bystander discovering new information about Fauci late in life. He was an antagonist for five years, losing internal battles, who has now found a publishing format in which he always wins the argument.

A record, not an inference

That conviction is worth stating plainly rather than gesturing at, because Navarro’s own promotional material leans hard into martyrdom. In September 2023, a federal jury convicted Navarro on two counts of contempt of Congress for defying a subpoena from the House Select Committee investigating the January 6, 2021 attack on the Capitol — a subpoena seeking documents and testimony about his role in efforts to delay certification of the 2020 election. Judge Amit Mehta sentenced him on January 25, 2024, to four months in federal prison and a $9,500 fine, a sentence lighter than the six months prosecutors had sought. In March 2024 he reported to a federal facility in Miami, becoming the first former White House official in American history imprisoned specifically for contempt of Congress.

Navarro has since converted the episode into a second cottage industry — a memoir co-written with his fiancée framing the prosecution as political persecution, promoted alongside the Fauci book and yet another volume urging support for what allies are calling “the New MAGA Deal.” Donald Trump Jr. has personally solicited pre-orders for that third book on Navarro’s behalf, describing his father’s former trade adviser as a patriot targeted by a weaponized justice system. The output is genuinely impressive from a pure productivity standpoint. Fewer men manage to publish a martyrdom narrative, a prosecutorial indictment of a former colleague, and a policy manifesto within roughly eighteen months of walking out of a federal prison. Whatever else one says about Peter Navarro, he does not appear to suffer from writer’s block.

The trade record behind the theatre

The book tour matters because it obscures the actual policy record Navarro built his reputation on, which is the part I am supposed to be writing about and which deserves equal scrutiny. Navarro entered Trump’s orbit after Jared Kushner discovered Death by China on Amazon in 2016 and recruited its author as a campaign economic adviser. He went on to become the intellectual architect of the administration’s tariff regime — the “tough on China” posture that has now metastasized into a broader trade war touching allies as well as adversaries, Canada very much included.

The April 2025 round of China trade talks that Navarro helped shape did not produce the swift capitulation his rhetoric promised; instead it produced stalled negotiations and escalating retaliatory measures, with international trade bodies citing the resulting policy confusion as a drag on global economic cooperation. This is the pattern with Navarro’s trade theory as practiced rather than published: confident in the book, combustible in the room.

Navarro’s own account of the Canada relationship, offered on social media around the same period, described the United States as having handed Canada “a genuinely fair deal” only to be repaid with retaliatory measures on alcohol and lumber, all so Carney could, in Navarro’s telling, score political points during a Canadian midterm cycle. This is the same move Navarro made with Fauci and with his own prison sentence: recast a dispute in which he holds real leverage and has taken real action as one in which he is purely the aggrieved party, reacting to someone else’s bad faith. It is a rhetorical habit worth naming on its own terms, independent of whether one thinks the underlying tariff or dairy and lumber disputes have merit. Navarro does not appear to have a register for acknowledging that the other side of a negotiation might have a legitimate grievance of its own.

Which brings us to the clip that prompted this essay. On that same War Room appearance, Navarro turned to Canada, describing a trade deal he claimed was “so good it made him nervous,” only for Canadian politics to intervene — his phrase for Prime Minister Mark Carney’s rising popularity after standing up to Trump. Navarro’s characterization of the Canadian public who reward that posture was blunt: useful idiots, rewarding a leader who plays the aggrieved neighbour against an America he described as merely misunderstood.

On being called a useful idiot

I will resist the urge to be purely defensive here, because the more interesting response is comparative. If Navarro wants to measure which electorate is more captured by performative grievance, the data is available and it does not flatter his argument.

As of August 2026, Angus Reid Institute polling puts Trump’s approval in the United States at 34 percent against 58 percent disapproval — a net negative 24 — while Carney’s approval among Canadians sits at 51 percent against 42 percent disapproval, a net positive 9, a 17-point gap in the same comparative reading. Independent American trackers tell the same story without needing Canada in the frame at all: Reuters/Ipsos found Trump’s approval falling to 33 percent in the same window, a new low for his second term, with the Decision Desk HQ polling average landing around 39 percent and Pew Research placing him at 34 percent as of mid-July 2026 — lower, Pew noted, than his own standing at the equivalent point in his first term, and roughly on par with Joe Biden’s least popular stretch.

If rewarding a leader for standing up to an unpopular foreign counterpart makes Canadians useful idiots, the polling suggests they are, at minimum, idiots with unusually good instincts about which leader is actually underwater. The Canadian public looked at a trade dispute, watched their prime minister decline to be pushed around, and approved. The American electorate that Navarro serves has, by his own party’s preferred pollsters, done something closer to the opposite with its own president.

Here Navarro’s coalition deserves its own honest accounting, not as insult but as demography. Exit polling from the 2024 election that put Trump back in office shows a electorate whose core is disproportionately rural and non-college-educated: Trump carried rural voters by 40 points, carried voters without a four-year degree by roughly 13 to 14 points — nearly double his 2016 margin in that group — and drew 62 percent support among non-college voters nationally, according to Pew’s post-election analysis. None of that is a slur; it is simply the coalition Navarro’s own trade nationalism was built to serve, the same voters told for a decade that tariffs on Chinese imports would restore the manufacturing jobs a college degree was no longer required to hold. Whether that promise has been kept, at 34 percent approval and falling, is a separate question from whether Canadians deserve to be called credulous for approving of their own prime minister at rates the American president can currently only dream about.

Projection, not analysis

There is a term for a person who serves a leader’s interests so completely that he mistakes the leader’s convictions for his own — who will fabricate an expert witness rather than admit uncertainty, who will go to federal prison rather than break ranks, who built five books around tariff theories that the leader wanted validated and is now building a sixth around a grudge the leader shares. Navarro has a word for that kind of person too. He used it on Canadians for approving of Mark Carney. He might try it on a mirror.

The useful idiot, properly defined, is not the citizen who rewards a leader for standing up to a bully. It is the adviser who never once, across two administrations, a criminal conviction, and a prison sentence, found daylight between his own judgment and his patron’s. Navarro did not need Trump to tell him China was the enemy — he arrived with the theory already built, in a book Kushner happened to find on Amazon. But everything since has run the other direction: the trade war Trump wanted, prosecuted with the confidence of a man who invented his own supporting citation; the prison sentence taken in Trump’s name rather than the testimony that would have shortened it; the Fauci book keeping alive a fight Trump has already moved on from twice, first by not firing Fauci when Navarro begged him to, then by rehabilitating Fauci’s replacement narrative himself when convenient. That is not strategic loyalty. That is the useful idiot’s whole function — to want, on command, exactly what the boss already wants, and call it independent analysis.

By his own definition, then, the man calling Canadians useful idiots for approving of their own prime minister might reserve a harder look for the one staring back at him from the book jacket.

The Leacock verdict

Stephen Leacock, the humorist whose name I share and whose sardonic eye I try to borrow for occasions exactly like this one, wrote that in Mariposa the failures of great men were forgiven so long as they failed with sufficient confidence. Navarro has clearly absorbed the lesson, if not from Leacock then from some fictional economist of his own invention. He fabricated an expert to agree with himself, went to prison for defying Congress, converted that prison stint into three books in under two years, and used one of his television appearances to call an entire neighbouring country’s population useful idiots for approving of a prime minister more than his own president is approved of by his own countrymen.

None of this disqualifies Navarro from having views on trade, or even on Fauci. It disqualifies him from being taken at his own valuation as the credible narrator of anyone else’s failures. The man who invented Ron Vara to make his own argument sound less lonely does not get to stand behind a cardboard cutout of a Fauci mugshot and call it a prosecution. Call it what it is: the same trick, with a different victim, and a slightly larger print run.

Anthony Fauci may or may not be the national hero his admirers have made him out to be; Peter Navarro, on the current record, is something closer to a settled fact — a national disgrace with a mugshot-style book jacket to prove it. Say what you will about Fauci's contested legacy, but he left government without a criminal conviction. Navarro left it with one, the first White House official in American history imprisoned for defying Congress, and is now selling the story of Fauci's supposed crimes from the vantage point of a man who has already served time for his own.
