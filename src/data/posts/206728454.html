Parts VIII, XIV, and XV of this series traced two very different paths a monarchy facing genuine pressure for reform can take: Charles I’s England, which fought a civil war rather than accept limits on royal power and lost both the war and the king’s own head, and France and Russia, which held their ground against escalating popular grievance until the entire structure broke at once, taking their royal families down with it. Britain’s own monarchy, across the three and a half centuries following Charles I’s execution, offers this series its clearest demonstration of the opposite strategy: a crown that read the same warning signs France and Russia eventually ignored, and chose, at nearly every decisive juncture, to surrender power gradually and voluntarily rather than wait to be forced.

The pattern begins almost immediately after the Restoration this series documented at the close of Part VIII. Charles II’s brother and successor, James II, ascended the throne in 1685 and moved quickly to reassert exactly the kind of unaccountable Catholic-sympathetic royal authority that had cost his own father his head decades earlier, appointing Catholics to senior military and civil positions in defiance of laws explicitly barring them, and suspending Parliament’s own legislation through royal prerogative in a manner this series has already seen provoke catastrophic backlash in Part VIII. When James’s second wife gave birth to a son in 1688, raising the prospect of an enduring Catholic dynasty rather than a Protestant succession through James’s adult Protestant daughters, a coalition of English political and religious leaders, rather than repeat the civil war and regicide this series traced in earlier parts, instead invited James’s own Protestant daughter Mary and her husband, the Dutch prince William of Orange, to invade England and assume the throne jointly. James fled to France almost without a fight, and the episode that followed, known ever since as the Glorious Revolution precisely because it achieved a change of monarch with remarkably little bloodshed compared to the century’s earlier civil war, produced the Bill of Rights of 1689, a foundational document requiring the crown’s regular convening of Parliament, barring the monarch from suspending laws or raising taxes without parliamentary consent, and guaranteeing free parliamentary elections and debate. Where Charles I had died refusing to accept exactly these limits, his own niece and her husband accepted them as the explicit price of the throne itself, a fundamentally different bargain struck within the very same family line.

It is worth noting how narrowly this different outcome was actually secured, since Britain’s own version of the same underlying crisis this series traced destroying France and Russia came remarkably close to producing an identical result only decades before the Glorious Revolution’s more peaceful settlement. Charles I’s own execution in 1649, examined at length in Part VIII, and the subsequent decade of republican experiment under Cromwell, demonstrate that England’s political culture was entirely capable of the same violent rupture this series later documented in France and Russia; what changed by 1688 was less some inherent English preference for peaceful compromise than a set of political actors on all sides who had personally lived through, or been raised on vivid memory of, the chaos and uncertainty that followed Charles I’s execution, and who consequently had strong practical incentives to find a resolution that avoided repeating it. James II’s own flight into exile, rather than a trial and execution, may owe as much to this recent and still-raw memory of regicide’s costs as to any more abstract constitutional principle, a reminder that the specific historical sequence a country has already lived through shapes what its elites consider an acceptable resolution to a later, structurally similar crisis.

The eighteenth century saw this accommodation deepen further through a mechanism no single law or revolution produced, but which nonetheless proved just as consequential: the gradual emergence of a Prime Minister governing with the confidence of Parliament rather than at the monarch’s own personal direction. This shift owed much to simple historical accident. When Queen Anne, the last of the Stuart line, died without a surviving heir in 1714, the throne passed, under the terms of a parliamentary succession act designed specifically to guarantee a Protestant monarch, to a distant German relative, George, Elector of Hanover, who spoke little English and showed comparatively limited personal interest in the day-to-day mechanics of English governance. This created a genuine vacuum that his chief minister, Robert Walpole, filled by default, managing Parliament, the treasury, and government policy so consistently and effectively across two decades in office that he is now generally regarded as Britain’s first Prime Minister in substance, even though the title itself was not yet formally recognized. What began as an accident of a foreign-born king’s limited engagement with domestic politics hardened, over the following century, into an entrenched constitutional convention: the monarch reigns, while an accountable Prime Minister, drawn from and answerable to Parliament, actually governs.

The nineteenth century brought a series of further, deliberately incremental reforms that steadily expanded the electorate and further diluted royal and aristocratic control over government, each one a genuine concession the crown and its allied aristocracy could plausibly have resisted by force, and each one instead accepted, however reluctantly, as the price of continued social peace. The Reform Act of 1832 eliminated a number of notoriously corrupt small parliamentary boroughs controlled by wealthy patrons and modestly expanded the property-based franchise, passed only after the Duke of Wellington’s own government fell and widespread public unrest, including actual riots in cities like Bristol and Nottingham, made the alternative to reform look considerably more dangerous than reform itself. Subsequent Reform Acts in 1867 and 1884 progressively extended voting rights to a much broader share of male citizens, and the Parliament Act of 1911 formally stripped the unelected House of Lords, the traditional bastion of hereditary aristocratic power, of its ability to veto legislation passed by the elected House of Commons, reducing it to a power merely to delay rather than block. Each of these reforms, examined individually, represents exactly the kind of incremental erosion of entrenched privilege that this series has already documented producing violent revolutionary backlash when refused in Parts XIV and XV. Britain’s aristocracy and monarchy, collectively, chose differently each time, ceding formal power gradually rather than risking the kind of total collapse France and Russia eventually suffered.

Queen Victoria’s own long reign, spanning nearly the entire second half of the nineteenth century, completed much of this transformation in practice even as it dramatically expanded the monarchy’s ceremonial and symbolic prominence, a genuinely important distinction this series should draw out explicitly. Victoria wielded meaningfully less direct governing power over the course of her reign than either of the two Georges who preceded her grandfather, deferring increasingly to her elected ministers on matters of substantive policy even as her own reign coincided with the British Empire’s greatest territorial extent and her personal image, disseminated through the new mass media of photography and popular illustrated newspapers, became more globally recognizable than that of any monarch examined anywhere else in this series. This pairing, of steadily declining substantive political authority alongside dramatically expanding ceremonial visibility and popular affection, is precisely the same trade this series will trace again, in a considerably more compressed and deliberate form, when it examines Japan’s own postwar imperial transformation, though Britain arrived at broadly the same destination across generations of gradual accretion rather than through a single negotiated postwar settlement.

The twentieth century tested this pattern of accommodation directly on at least two occasions worth examining closely, because both cases show the monarchy itself, rather than merely Parliament and aristocracy, actively choosing constraint over confrontation. The abdication crisis of 1936 saw the newly crowned King Edward VIII forced to choose between the throne and his intended marriage to Wallis Simpson, a twice-divorced American commoner whose marital history the Church of England, of which the monarch serves as titular head, could not sanction, and Edward, informed clearly by his own Prime Minister Stanley Baldwin that Parliament would not accept the marriage under any circumstances, chose to abdicate within months of his own coronation rather than force a genuine constitutional confrontation between crown and government, a decision that transferred the throne smoothly to his more compliant younger brother, who reigned as George VI. And throughout the Second World War, George VI and his wife, Queen Elizabeth, made a deliberate and well-documented decision to remain in London through the Blitz’s heaviest bombing rather than relocate to safety, including visiting bombed neighborhoods and, when Buckingham Palace itself was struck by German bombs in 1940, the Queen’s own widely reported remark that she could now look the bombed East End of London in the face, a calculated act of shared sacrifice that did enormous work reinforcing the monarchy’s popular standing precisely by demonstrating that the crown, too, would bear the same wartime risks as its ordinary subjects, rather than standing apart from them.

The most recent and, for the purposes of this series, most instructive test of this centuries-long pattern of accommodation has unfolded gradually across the reign of Queen Elizabeth II and her son Charles III, through a series of decisions that have continued the same basic strategy this entire part has traced: reducing the practical scope and privilege of the institution voluntarily, before public pressure or scandal forces the issue. The monarchy’s own finances were substantially reformed in 1993, when Elizabeth II voluntarily agreed to begin paying income tax on her personal wealth for the first time, a concession offered specifically in response to public criticism following a series of costly royal residence fires and a run of embarrassing tabloid coverage of younger royals’ marital difficulties. The death of Diana, Princess of Wales, in a car accident in Paris in August of 1997 produced a genuine and closely studied crisis of public confidence in the institution’s emotional responsiveness, met initially by a royal family retreat to the seclusion of Balmoral Castle in Scotland that the press and a substantial share of the British public widely read as cold and out of step with the country’s own visible outpouring of public grief, expressed through an unprecedented sea of flowers and tributes left outside royal residences in London. The crisis was resolved only after several days of mounting public and press pressure, when the Queen returned to London, ordered the Union flag lowered to half-mast over Buckingham Palace in a departure from strict royal protocol governing when such flags may be flown at all, and addressed the nation directly on live television in unusually personal and emotionally forthcoming terms, a combination of concessions that most historians of the modern British monarchy regard as a genuine turning point in how directly the institution has since been willing to acknowledge and adapt to public sentiment in real time, rather than retreat behind the more distant tradition and protocol that had defined royal conduct through most of the twentieth century.

This same pattern of proactive concession has continued directly into the current reign. Charles III, who acceded to the throne in September of 2022 following his mother’s death, had spoken for years beforehand, even while still Prince of Wales, of his own intention to preside over what palace officials and the press both came to describe as a slimmed-down monarchy, a smaller working group of royals drawing public funds and undertaking official duties, explicitly framed as a response to ongoing public sensitivity about the cost and size of the institution during a period of broader economic strain in Britain. This reforming instinct was tested severely and almost immediately by two separate crises entirely outside the new king’s own direct control. Charles’s younger brother, Prince Andrew, was stripped of his military titles and royal patronages in 2022 following his association with the convicted sex offender Jeffrey Epstein and a civil lawsuit brought by Virginia Giuffre, which Andrew settled without admission of liability, a genuinely severe reputational crisis the monarchy addressed by rapid and visible distancing rather than any attempt to shield a senior family member from consequence. And the departure of Charles’s son Prince Harry and his wife Meghan from official royal duties in 2020, followed by a series of increasingly candid public interviews, a Netflix documentary series, and Harry’s own bestselling memoir detailing private family tensions in unusually frank terms, tested the institution’s tolerance for public internal criticism in a way no prior generation of royal family members had subjected it to, and the palace’s own restrained public response, offering little rebuttal and allowing the controversy to recede on its own timeline rather than escalating a public confrontation, reads as a direct continuation of the same three-century-old instinct this entire part has traced: absorb the pressure, concede what must be conceded, and avoid, wherever possible, the kind of direct confrontation that has destroyed the monarchies examined in Parts XIV and XV.

What this centuries-long British pattern demonstrates, considered as a whole and set directly against the two collapses this series examined in Parts XIV and XV, is that a monarchy’s survival appears to correlate far more closely with its willingness to treat its own power as genuinely negotiable, conceded incrementally and in advance of open confrontation, than with any particular claim to divine sanction, ancient bloodline, or military capability. Britain’s crown has, across three and a half centuries since the Glorious Revolution, given up nearly every substantive governing power a medieval English king would have recognized as central to the office, retaining almost none of the actual authority Charles I died defending, and has, in exchange, kept the throne itself intact and broadly popular through an era that saw nearly every comparably ancient European monarchy either abolished outright or reduced to a status considerably more constrained and more fragile than Britain’s own.

Part XIX crosses the Pacific to a very different kind of monarchy, one that unified a scattered island nation through genuine military and diplomatic skill, only to watch its own crown stolen not by a domestic revolution or an invading army, but by a small group of resident American businessmen backed by United States Marines.
