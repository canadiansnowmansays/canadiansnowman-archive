“You cannot skip a floor that has already booked you. The rat is already in the fur. The ship is already at sea.” — essay 1,313

This is the floor.

⚖️ Serious, one of the gravest, with a single sentence that is personal and no more than that. It is about a date that became a name, a minute that a continent watched together, a memory checked against a clock, and a memorial built so that nobody on it would be counted beside a stranger. There is no wink. The dog stays out of it.

I count my essays, and every so often the count lands on a year that already belongs to someone. At essay 1,313 I wrote a sentence about dread, about the way a catastrophe can be booked long before it arrives, and I promised that the series would quote it back to itself at its gravest numbers. It did so at 1,945. It does so again now. Essay 2,001 is the year of the only catastrophe in this whole series whose name is simply a number.

Every other entry has a name made of words. Hastings. The Black Death. The Somme. Hiroshima. This one is called by its date, and the date is written in numerals, and the numerals are the same three that North Americans dial when they need help. Nobody intended that. It is simply there, every time anyone says it, and it has never once been funny.

The clock

Here is the count of that morning, as the 9/11 Commission reconstructed it, in Eastern time, which is New York’s time and also mine.

At 8:46, American Airlines Flight 11 was flown into the North Tower of the World Trade Center. At 9:03, United Airlines Flight 175 was flown into the South Tower. At 9:37, American Airlines Flight 77 was flown into the Pentagon. At 10:03, United Airlines Flight 93 came down in a field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania, after its passengers, who had learned by telephone what the other planes had done, fought their way toward the cockpit. The South Tower collapsed at 9:59, fifty-six minutes after it was struck. The North Tower collapsed at 10:28, an hour and forty-two minutes after it was struck.

From the first impact to the second collapse: an hour and forty-two minutes. This series has measured wars in centuries and empires in dynasties. The gravest morning in it fits inside a single school period, with time left over.

Seventeen minutes

Between the first plane and the second there were seventeen minutes, and what happened in them is the reason so many people can say what they say about that day.

The first impact was almost unseen. A pair of French brothers making a documentary about New York firefighters happened to swing their camera up as the plane went over, and caught it; a handful of other recordings exist. None of them went out live. For the first several minutes, the people watching at home saw only what came after: a tower with a wound in it and smoke pouring out, and anchors trying to work out whether a small plane had gone off course.

So the networks did what television does. They pointed their cameras at the burning tower and kept them there. And at 9:03, with the whole of the continent’s morning television trained on the North Tower, the second plane came in from the south and flew into its twin, in full view, in real time, in every living room and staff room and hotel lobby where a set was on.

That is the minute the continent watched together. It was not the first plane. It was the second.

The one thing I will tell you

I was watching, live, when the second plane hit.

That is all I am going to say about my own day: not where I was, not who was with me, not what I did with the rest of it. Some of what a man remembers belongs in an essay. Some of it belongs to him, and this series has never asked me to hand over more than I could spare.

But I will do with that one sentence what this series does with every confident count, because I have learned, in the last few essays, that I have to. At 1,963 I audited my memory of the afternoon Kennedy was shot, and found I had the hour wrong by sixty minutes. Then I audited the summer before it and found I had the year wrong. A man who has been caught twice should check the third time. So I checked.

Seven weeks after the attacks, a psychologist in California named Kathy Pezdek surveyed 690 people about how they had learned the news. About three in four said they had seen the first plane hit the tower in footage aired that day. That footage was not shown until the following day. They were not lying. They had watched the replays so many times in the week that followed that the replays had moved back into the morning and taken a seat there.

They were in good company. In December 2001, and again the next month, the President of the United States told audiences that before he walked into the Florida classroom where he was told of the attacks, he had seen the first plane fly into the tower on television. “There’s one terrible pilot,” he remembered thinking. He could not have seen it. No one could. He was not lying either. He was remembering the way everyone remembers.

At Duke University, two researchers, Jennifer Talarico and David Rubin, had students write down their memories of the attacks on September 12, and then asked them again weeks and months later. The memories drifted as much as memories of an ordinary day do. What did not drift was the students’ certainty. The details wandered; the confidence stayed exactly where it was.

So here is my audit. The second plane is the one that could be seen live. It struck at 9:03, Eastern time, and my clock in Toronto read the same as the clock in Manhattan. My memory says I saw it as it happened, and the record says that was possible, and nothing I can find says otherwise. For once in this series, the man has his count right.

I take no satisfaction in it. It is not a memory anyone would choose to have got right.

Who was on the plane

I did not know, that morning, who was on the plane I watched. For years I did not think to ask. When I did, I found a name I should have known.

Garnet Bailey was born in 1948 in Lloydminster, a prairie city that sits squarely on the line between Saskatchewan and Alberta, so that it belongs to two provinces at once. Everyone called him Ace. He played junior hockey for the Edmonton Oil Kings, won the Stanley Cup with the Boston Bruins in 1972, and spent the rest of his life in the game. By 2001 he was director of pro scouting for the Los Angeles Kings. On the morning of September 11 he was flying from Boston to Los Angeles for the team’s training camp, with a young amateur scout named Mark Bavis. They were on Flight 175. Airphone records show that Ace Bailey tried to call his wife four times.

So the plane that a continent watched, the one I watched, carried a Canadian hockey man from a city on a provincial line. I find I cannot write about the minute without him in it now.

The count of the dead

The dead of that morning number 2,977, not counting the nineteen men who killed them. Among them were 24 Canadians. They died in the towers and on the planes and at the Pentagon, and some of them died going up the stairs while everyone else was coming down.

It is, by the standards of this series, an unusually exact count. The Black Death was counted in fractions of continents. Hiroshima was left as a range. The ten million of 1914 are a round number standing in for a number nobody has. This one was counted by name, person by person, and the names were then arranged in a way that I have not met anywhere else in two thousand essays.

At the memorial in New York, the names are cut into bronze around the edges of two pools that sit in the footprints of the towers. There are 2,983 of them, because the memorial also remembers the six people killed when the World Trade Center was bombed in 1993. They are not in alphabetical order. They are not in order of age, or nationality, or floor. They are arranged by what the designers called “meaningful adjacencies.” Families and colleagues were asked whom their person should stand beside, and they sent in some twelve hundred requests: this man with the friend he worked with for twenty years, this woman with the sister who died on another floor, this firefighter with his company. A software artist named Jer Thorp, working with a design firm called Local Projects, wrote the algorithm that fit all of those requests onto seventy-six bronze panels so that every one of them could be kept.

Ace Bailey and Mark Bavis are on the South Pool, panel S-3, side by side, as they were on the plane. In 2012, the year the Kings finally won the Stanley Cup, the team brought it to New York and set it down on the bronze over their names.

Every other count in this series has been an argument about a number. This is the only one I know of that was arranged so that no one in it would be left beside a stranger.

The other count, in Gander

There is one more number from that week, and it is the one my own country should keep.

When American airspace closed that morning, the planes already over the Atlantic and the Pacific had nowhere to land. Canada took them. Transport Canada counted more than 33,000 passengers on 224 flights brought down at Canadian airports in what was called Operation Yellow Ribbon; other agencies, counting a little differently, put it closer to 240. Vancouver took 34 flights. Halifax took around 40.

And Gander, Newfoundland, a town of fewer than ten thousand people, took 38 aircraft and some 6,600 passengers and crew, which is to say it grew by about two thirds in a single afternoon. The people of Gander and the towns around it opened their schools and church halls and Legion halls and their own houses, and fed and bedded and drove and entertained thousands of strangers for the better part of a week, and sent them home with phone numbers and casseroles. Some of those strangers came back every year for the rest of their lives.

I have spent two thousand essays counting what people did to one another. It seems right, at the gravest of the numbers, to enter one count of what people did for one another, and to note that it was kept by a small town on a rock in the Atlantic that had no reason to keep it except that the planes had come down there.

The closer

So 2,001 is the floor. A date became a name, and the name is the number we dial for help. Seventeen minutes turned the cameras toward the second tower, and a continent watched it together, and three in four of the people asked later remembered the first one too. A Canadian scout from a city on a provincial line was on the plane I watched. The dead were counted to the person and then set beside the people they loved. And a town in Newfoundland grew by two thirds in an afternoon and fed everyone who landed.

The count moves on, twenty-five years, and for the first time in this series it moves to a year that has not finished yet. The next landmark is essay 2,026, the year I am writing in, and the count is going to catch up with the calendar and have to stand still beside it. It will be a measure of distance, not of the dead. It is the last of the numbered years.

See you at 2,026.
