You spend 159 years perfecting the art of being the quieter house

This past week, the calendar arranged one of its small cruelties: Canada had a birthday on Wednesday, and three days later the neighbours had one too, and theirs was bigger. It is always bigger. But this year it was aggressively bigger, because the neighbours turned two hundred and fifty, and we turned one hundred and fifty-nine, and there is no graceful way for a person of one hundred and fifty-nine to share a birthday week with a two-hundred-and-fiftieth. You bring a card. You stand near the cake. You try not to be asked your own age.

I have given this some thought, as a Canadian must, because thinking carefully about the neighbours while saying nothing is our entire foreign policy and most of our domestic one besides.

Consider the contrast in how the two parties were run.

The American two hundred and fiftieth — the Semiquincentennial, a word that sounds like a settlement in a class-action suit — had been in planning for years. There were commissions. There was a tall ship component. There was, I am given to understand, a “Salute to America” with a flyover, and somewhere a committee spent real money determining the optimal number of fighter jets to express the precise quantity of freedom. It was enormous and sincere and slightly anxious in the way of all very large birthday parties, where the size of the celebration is doing quiet work to settle a question nobody will say out loud, which is whether everyone is still having a good time.

The Canadian one hundred and fifty-ninth, by contrast, consisted of a man in Ottawa reading a proclamation that no one could hear over the sound of a food truck, several thousand citizens wearing the flag as a cape with no apparent irony, and at least one municipal fireworks display rescheduled on account of fire risk and then rescheduled again on account of the rescheduling. We were not anxious about whether everyone was having a good time. We did not raise the question. To raise the question would be the most un-Canadian thing imaginable. We simply put out the lawn chairs and trusted that adequate contentment would arrive on schedule, the way the mail used to.

The genius of the Canadian birthday — and I use the word “genius” in its loosest available sense, the sense in which one calls a dog who has learned to open the screen door a genius — is that we have built an entire national identity out of being the country that is not having the bigger party.

We define ourselves, almost entirely, by the proximity of a louder nation. We are the polite one. We are the one who apologizes. We are the one with the health care, which we mention the way other people mention a recent vacation, a little too often and watching your face for the reaction. Our patriotism is not a flyover. Our patriotism is a quiet, complicated, faintly competitive relief — relief that we are not them, expressed exactly four times a year and never, ever in front of them, because that would be rude, and rudeness is the only sin in the Canadian catechism that does not come with a path to redemption.

And so the birthday week is, for us, less a celebration than an annual physical. We turn one hundred and fifty-nine and we check the vitals. Is the dollar embarrassing? Is the Prime Minister embarrassing? Has anyone important confused us with a region of the United States this fiscal quarter? Did the Americans, three days later, do something at their own party that we had to watch through our fingers while insisting we were not watching? The answers, this year, were: yes, it depends, twice, and almost certainly.

But I want to say something more generous, because the easy Canada Day essay writes itself and self-deprecation, past a certain volume, is just vanity in a parka.

Two hundred and fifty years is genuinely a long time for a republic, and the thing about the American experiment that we up here are too proud or too injured to say plainly is that it was, and remains, a magnificent and reckless and consequential idea — the idea that a people could simply declare themselves into legitimacy, with a document, in prose, citing self-evident truths. We did not do that. We did not declare ourselves into being. We were, in the characteristic Canadian fashion, assembled — by statute, by railway, by a series of meetings in which the boldest revolutionary act under discussion was the precise wording of a clause on fisheries. The Americans had a Declaration of Independence. We had the British North America Act, which is exactly the birth certificate you would expect from a nation founded by people whose great passion was being left alone to do paperwork in a cold climate.

And yet here we both are. Theirs born in a thunderclap, ours assembled from a kit, and both of us, two and a half centuries and one and a half centuries on, still arguing with our founding documents, still not finished, still — this is the part I actually believe — unfinished on purpose, because the only countries that are finished are the ones in the history books.

I should tell you what happened last night, because it changed the essay I thought I was writing.

I was on the balcony after dark, trying to coax a breeze out of an evening that refused to cool, when the fireworks started. Not one display — several, scattered across the city, blooming over different neighbourhoods at once. It took me a moment to understand what I was seeing, and then I did: it was the Fourth of July, and Toronto’s American expatriate community is apparently large enough, and homesick enough, that the day gets marked here too, hundreds of miles from the country it belongs to. Patriotism, it turns out, is portable. You can carry it across a border in your chest and set it off over a Canadian skyline, and it still means what it meant back home.

I found that genuinely moving, and I want to say so before I say the other thing, because the other thing is less generous and I am not certain it is fair.

The other thing is that I wondered — watching all that transplanted celebration light up a city that is not theirs — how much of the joy was for America, and how much was relief at being at a safe remove from what America has lately become. It is a hard season down there for anyone who has been paying attention, a season in which the man at the top has helpfully clarified that you are either with him or you are, in his rendering, a communist, with no third option on offer and no room to simply be a citizen with doubts. I do not know the answer. I do not think the people setting off the fireworks necessarily know it either. You can love a country and be relieved to be watching it from across a lake, both at once, on the same warm night, under the same fireworks. I suspect that is exactly what I was watching: people celebrating a home they love and are, for now, glad to love from here.

So on Wednesday I put out the lawn chair. I wore no cape, because there are limits, but I stood for the anthem, both verses, in both official languages, and I felt the specific and slightly embarrassed warmth of a man who loves a country he is not permitted, by national temperament, to say he loves. And on Saturday I watched the neighbours’ enormous party from a respectful distance — some of it, as it turned out, from my own balcony — and I noted the jets, and the tall ships, and the great anxious magnificence of it, and I thought what I think every Fourth of July, which is the most Canadian thought there is.

I thought: I hope they’re all right over there.

Because that is the thing nobody tells you about living next to the big party. You spend a hundred and fifty-nine years defining yourself against the neighbours, perfecting the art of being the quieter house, and then one year the music from next door sounds a little forced, and the arguments carry across the lawn a little louder than usual, and you find — to your great surprise, and somewhat against your will — that you are not enjoying it. That you would, in fact, give a good deal for the party next door to go well. That a quiet house is only pleasant if the loud one is happy.

Happy birthday, both of us. One of us said it loudly, with jets. One of us said it quietly, rescheduling the fireworks. But we both, in our own ways, meant it — and we will both, in our own ways, spend a while yet watching the other one’s windows to make sure the lights are still on.
