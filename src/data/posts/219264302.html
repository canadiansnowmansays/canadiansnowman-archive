This is the most delicate instalment in the series, so it begins with a boundary. Pete Hegseth’s faith is his own. A Christian Secretary of Defense who prays, attends church and draws strength from scripture is no different from his many predecessors who did the same, and the Constitution protects him exactly as it protects every soldier under his command. Nothing that follows is a criticism of what he believes.

It is a criticism of what he is doing with the office. The American military serves a country that has never had an established church, and its chaplains serve troops of every faith and of none. The line between a Secretary’s private devotion and the department’s official voice is old, well-marked and, until recently, carefully observed. This essay is about where it now runs.

The Performance

In the Quantico speech, Hegseth wove Christian language and themes through his address, as the New York Times’s Helene Cooper and John Ismay noted in their report that day (”’No Beardos, No Weirdos, No Wimps’: Hegseth Lays Out His Military Vision”). He derided “secular humanism.” And he announced a new Office of Religious Affairs, reporting directly to him.

As LifeSiteNews reported in “Hegseth announces religious affairs office for US military: ‘Real battle is spiritual’,” he told the troops that “the real battle is spiritual” and pledged to place “spiritual health at the same level as physical health.” The office is meant to support the chaplaincy, remove what the department calls policy barriers, and identify unmet religious needs. Hegseth noted that fewer than 5 percent of service members identify as non-religious, and that Catholic chaplains in particular are in short supply.

The department added a reassurance. The office, it said, “will not compel religious participation, favor one faith, or diminish the rights of personnel of any faith or lack thereof.”

That sentence is the paperwork’s starting point. Every word in it is correct. The question is whether the record of the past eighteen months gives a soldier of another faith, or of none, much reason to believe it.

The Paperwork

The prayer services. Since 2025, Hegseth has hosted monthly Christian worship services inside the Pentagon. Previous defence secretaries attended religious services. None, so far as the public record shows, organised regular worship of a single faith inside the building and led it themselves.

The most striking of these came during the war. As Cristina Stassis reported for Military Times on March 26, 2026, in “Hegseth prays for ‘overwhelming violence’ during Pentagon Christian service,” at the first monthly service after the Iran war began, Hegseth prayed for “overwhelming violence of action against those who deserve no mercy” and asked that justice “be executed swiftly and without remorse.” He asked God to “break the teeth of the ungodly” and to “let every round find its mark against the enemies of righteousness.” He said he was repeating a prayer a chaplain had offered to troops during the operation to capture Venezuela’s president, Nicolás Maduro. He closed “in the name of Jesus Christ.”

Hegseth is a member of a church in the Communion of Reformed Evangelical Churches, a small and theologically conservative denomination. Americans United for Separation of Church and State has criticised the services repeatedly. Its objection is not to prayer. It is to the Secretary of Defense using the Pentagon to host worship of his own faith, and then praying from that platform for violence against an enemy with whom the country is at war, an enemy that is overwhelmingly Muslim.

The briefing room. The services have not stayed in the auditorium. On March 10, 2026, in the opening weeks of the Iran war, Hegseth closed a Pentagon press briefing with a prayer for American troops. It drew criticism at the time for blurring an official briefing into a religious occasion, at a moment when officials were already being accused of framing the campaign in civilisational terms. That briefing was among the last the Pentagon held on the war; by early May they had stopped altogether. The prayers continued.

The chaplaincy. The more lasting changes are institutional. As John Vandiver reported for Stars and Stripes on December 17, 2025, in “Hegseth to overhaul chaplain corps, toss ‘unacceptable and unserious’ Army spiritual fitness guide,” Hegseth ordered the Army to discontinue its spiritual fitness guide immediately. He mocked it for mentioning God once and feelings eleven times. “Our chaplains are chaplains, not emotional support officers,” he said.

There is a reasonable argument in there somewhere. Chaplains are clergy, and some troops do want spiritual guidance rather than wellness jargon. But the guide was not written for chaplains alone. It was a resource for all soldiers, including the many who are not religious, and its secular language was deliberate. Throwing it out because it did not mention God enough is a choice about what kind of spirituality the Army endorses.

Other changes followed. The department cut its list of recognised religious affiliation codes from more than 200 to 31, which simplifies the paperwork and also decides, in effect, which faiths count. Chaplains will no longer wear rank insignia, displaying only the symbols of their faith. And now there is an Office of Religious Affairs reporting to the Secretary, not to the chaplaincy’s own chain of command. Tom Nichols noted in The Atlantic on October 1, 2026, in “Pete Hegseth, Idea Guy,” that the new office is also simply more bureaucracy, created by a Secretary who announced the same day that he was cutting the Pentagon’s senior ranks.

The “secular humanism” line. Taken together, these changes give the Quantico phrase its weight. Secular humanism is not an enemy army. It is the private belief of some fraction of the troops who stood in that hall, and of a larger share of the country they serve. The Secretary named it, from a military stage, as something to be overcome.

The precedent the military already learned. The armed forces have been here before, on a smaller scale. In 2004 and 2005, the U.S. Air Force Academy faced complaints that cadets had been pressured by evangelical faculty, coaches and senior cadets to attend services and accept particular beliefs. An Air Force review in 2005 found a religious climate that did not fully accommodate all faiths, and the service issued new guidance on religious expression by those in authority. The lesson was not that faith had no place in uniform. It was that when a superior expresses a religious view, subordinates hear something different from what peers hear. They hear what may be expected of them.

That lesson applies with full force to a Secretary of Defense, the most senior superior there is. When he prays from a Pentagon stage, he is not one worshipper among many. He is the man who decides who gets promoted.

The American founders understood the point. In 1790, George Washington wrote to the Hebrew congregation in Newport, Rhode Island, that the government of the United States “gives to bigotry no sanction, to persecution no assistance.” He was writing to a small religious minority in a new country, assuring them that their standing as citizens did not depend on sharing the faith of the men in charge. Every Jewish, Muslim, Hindu or non-believing soldier in today’s force is owed the same assurance, and it carries more weight coming from conduct than from an announcement.

The law. The legal question is more open than critics sometimes suggest. The First Amendment forbids Congress to make any law “respecting an establishment of religion.” Article VI forbids any religious test for public office. But the Supreme Court has, over the past decade, become far more accommodating of religious expression by public officials. A commentary in The Conversation argued that Hegseth’s services challenge the traditional notion of church-state separation but might well survive under the Roberts Court. That may be right. Constitutional is not the same as wise, and the military has its own reasons for caution that predate any court ruling.

The Bill

The soldier in the next seat. More than a quarter of American adults now report no religious affiliation, and the share is higher among the young adults who fill the enlisted ranks. The military also includes Muslims, Jews, Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs, pagans and, in the Pentagon’s own count, plenty of people who tick “no preference.” Hegseth’s figure that fewer than 5 percent identify as non-religious depends on how the question is asked; either way, those troops exist. Each of them now serves under a Secretary who hosts worship of one faith in the building, prays for violence in Jesus’s name during a war, and calls their worldview a problem. The department says none of this will affect their careers. In an institution where the Secretary has just shown, in Part I of this series, that he will strike names from promotion lists by hand and decline to explain why, a promise in a press release is thin protection.

The chaplains themselves. Military chaplains have a peculiar and honourable job: they are clergy of one faith who are obliged to care for troops of every faith. A Jewish chaplain sees that a Christian soldier gets to Mass; a Catholic chaplain arranges a space for Muslim prayers. Putting the chaplaincy under an office that reports to a Secretary with a clear theological agenda places strain on that bargain. The best chaplains will keep it anyway. The system should not rely on them having to.

The war. The United States is at war with Iran, a theocracy that routinely frames its conflicts in religious terms. Its enemies’ propaganda has long claimed that America wages a crusade against Islam. American officials of both parties have spent a quarter of a century insisting otherwise, because the claim is useful to recruiters. A Secretary of Defense praying in the Pentagon for violence against the “enemies of righteousness” during that war does not prove the propaganda right. It does make the rebuttal harder to deliver.

The allies. The American coalition in the Gulf depends on Muslim-majority partners, among them Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Bahrain, Jordan and Egypt, whose bases, airspace and oil sustain the war. They do not need the Secretary’s theology. They need his discretion, and a sense that the war is being fought for reasons of state.

The institution. The deepest cost is to the idea of the military as everyone’s institution. American soldiers swear an oath to the Constitution, not to a faith. They serve under civilian leaders of every faith and none, and they are entitled to assume that their own beliefs are, professionally speaking, none of their commander’s business. Hegseth has made his faith the department’s house style. He may sincerely believe that strengthens the force. But a military that looks like it belongs to one faith will, over time, be trusted less by citizens who do not share it, and by the troops who are asked to fight for all of them.

The American tradition is, in most respects, more generous to public faith than those of its allies. But it rests on a quiet understanding: the state holds the door open for every faith, and walks through none of them.
