Chuck Rocha has been doing this work long enough to know how men in the Rio Grande Valley think. He is a political consultant advising Democrats in Texas, and he has a phrase for the voters he is after: the “big-ass-truck economy.” These are Latino men who want to provide for their families, buy the truck, fill the tank, drive to the job site. “When I was making a TV ad a couple of weeks ago,” Rocha told The Atlantic on October 26, 2026, “a guy pointed to his truck in the front yard and said, ‘That fucking thing has become a lawn ornament because I can’t afford to drive it.’”

That single sentence may be the defining image of what has happened to Donald Trump’s multiracial working-class coalition in South Texas — the coalition that, two years ago, was heralded as a political realignment. Trump won Hispanic Texans by an estimated ten percentage points in 2024. He carried Starr County, a border county that is 97 percent Hispanic and had voted Republican for president only once since 1892. The MAGA coalition had cracked the Democratic base at its foundation.

On the eve of the November 3 midterms, a late-summer survey of Hispanic Texans who are likely to vote shows Democrats with a two-to-one advantage. Nationally, nearly one-fifth of Latino Trump 2024 voters say they no longer want Republicans controlling Congress. The truck is in the driveway. The tank is empty. The realignment, for now, appears to have been a lease agreement, not a deed.

Benny Melendez, a McAllen contractor we will meet below, said as much on Radio Atlantic on October 29, 2026. He had voted for Biden and other Democrats before he voted for Trump, and in his view they had done nothing for his people. He is voting Democratic this year, but he called the choice a lose-lose. Nick Miroff, who reported the story, concluded that these voters will be up for grabs every single time.

The stakes reach well beyond one seat. No Democrat has won a Senate race in Texas since Lloyd Bentsen in 1988. In its last broadcast before the vote, a panel on PBS’s Washington Week with The Atlantic argued that if Talarico ends that drought, it will be because of turnout in South Texas, the heavily Hispanic border region where many voters backed Trump in 2024 and now show every sign of abandoning his party.

The question is whether James Talarico can collect on it.

The Most Expensive Race in America

The Texas U.S. Senate race is the nation’s most expensive contest of 2026, as Talarico attempts to become the first Democrat to win statewide office in Texas in more than thirty years. He is running against Ken Paxton, the state’s attorney general, whom The New York Times described on October 26, 2026, as “embroiled in multiple unsavory scandals and struggling.” Recent polls show the two candidates in a dead heat.

For Talarico to win, the Rio Grande Valley must swing back. The Valley — a strip of counties along the southern tip of Texas, more than 90 percent Hispanic, reliably Democratic for decades before 2024 — was a breakthrough for Trump two years ago, and it is now, in 2026, his biggest liability in the state. What changed in the Valley is what changed everywhere in South Texas: the policies that were abstract in the voting booth have become concrete at the job site, the gas pump, and the front door.

Diesel is $6 a gallon. Brent crude, the international oil benchmark, closed at $116.72 a barrel on October 23, 2026, up 60 percent since the Iran war began and at its highest level since April, The New York Times reported. Gas nationally averages $4.29 a gallon according to AAA. In a region where men drive their trucks to work because their work is spread across rural job sites, that figure is not an abstraction. It is the reason the truck stays home.

The Construction Economy

Benny Melendez has been a general contractor in the Rio Grande Valley for seven years. He invited The Atlantic‘s Nick Miroff to a half-built house outside McAllen and explained what the last two years have done to his trade. He used to build on spec, investing his own money in a project and flipping a sale quickly. Now he builds only for clients who pay up front. A house that used to take him four months to finish now takes eight to twelve — not because of materials, but because he cannot find enough workers.

The reason is ICE.

At least fifteen men Melendez regularly worked with have been deported. Several were arrested en route to a job site. “If you have a ladder on your truck, they’ll start following you, and they’ll stop you,” he told Miroff. On the morning Miroff visited, a crew scheduled to install kitchen cabinets did not show up. Neither did the men booked to pave the driveway. ICE vehicles had been spotted in the area that morning. Alerts went out across radio stations and social media. The workers stayed home.

Melendez described listening to construction crews disappear, morning after morning, because the night before someone had shared a clip of federal agents at a site a county over. He recognises some of the ICE officers in video footage — Hispanic men who grew up in the Rio Grande Valley, wearing face masks. “I wrote to my friend and said, ‘Dude, you know I love you, but you’re losing my respect,’” he told Miroff. His friend told him the pay was too good to quit.

Melendez voted for Trump in 2024. He does not “hate” him, he said. His views on Trump adviser Stephen Miller, who architected the enforcement surge, were less restrained.

He is not alone. Representative Henry Cuellar, an eleven-time Democratic incumbent who met supporters over cabrito near a pool in his district, told Miroff that two issues have transformed the political weather in South Texas: “immigration and the cost of things.” He quoted constituents in Spanish: “El Trumpo va muy fuerte. Not anymore. The wind has changed.”

Two hundred fishing boats are moored along the Gulf Coast in Cuellar’s district because owners cannot turn a profit with diesel at $6 a gallon. The men who were supposed to be better off under MAGA — the tradesmen, the contractors, the fishermen — have watched their margins compress and their workforce vanish.

The Radio Station

Hector Lepe runs a morning show out of his home studio in Donna, Texas. The show is called El Zorro y Su Pandilla — The Fox and His Gang — and in a different era, it was entertainment. Now callers dial in to report ICE sightings in real time.

Lepe came to the United States without papers as a young man from Oaxaca, worked as an itinerant farmworker, broke into broadcasting, and became a citizen through his marriage. He told Miroff that the Rio Grande Valley used to be “a place where you could take your family to the movies, to the circus, to eat, to dance” without fear. That community has been shattered. Deported tradesmen have been displaced to low-wage assembly plants along the Mexican border, physically close to their children who remain in the United States, unable to cross back.

Lepe is now planning a project he calls La Rescata — The Rescue — a hotline for men detained by ICE to call so that someone can secure their truck and tools until a family member can safely retrieve them. The man who used to run a music programme is now building an emergency infrastructure for a community under siege.

Bobby Pulido and the Quinceañera Strategy

In Texas’s Fifteenth Congressional District, Republican incumbent Monica De La Cruz dismissed her Democratic opponent, Bobby Pulido, as “a washed-up singer from quinceañera parties.” Pulido — a two-time Latin Grammy–winning Tejano-music star — responded by showing up at quinceañeras for free. He is 53, a celebrity in the Valley, and someone who understands that showing up in Spanish, in a living room full of people who are already celebrating and drinking, is a more effective political strategy than a thirty-second television ad.

It appears to be working. De La Cruz won the district in 2024 by more than fourteen points. The latest polls show Pulido ahead.

Pulido’s campaign has focused on high gas prices, tariffs, and ICE cases that have generated local outrage — including the pepper-spraying and detention of two seventeen-year-old U.S. citizens, and the attempted deportation of Giovanna Perez, a mother of four whose husband was at Army basic training when she tried to pick up her family’s military ID cards at a base in Corpus Christi and was turned over to ICE. These are not abstract policy debates. They are the stories that move around Frijoles restaurant in Brownsville and backyard cookouts in Edinburg.

“The thing that the Democratic Party doesn’t understand,” Pulido told Miroff, “is you don’t get anywhere down here calling people ‘the rich,’ as if it’s pejorative. That’s what a lot of people here aspire to be.” His message is populism in the original sense: not class resentment, but a promise that the Valley can return to being the kind of place where you can afford to drive the truck you worked for.

In the Thirty-Fourth District, Representative Vicente Gonzalez — who barely held his seat in 2024 by less than three percentage points — is running ads that show him grilling steaks at a backyard cookout and rationing meager portions to disappointed guests while blaming Trump’s tariffs for the prices. It is direct communication, and in the current climate of the Rio Grande Valley, directness works.

The Counter-Force

None of this is happening without a fight.

Elon Musk donated $158 million in September to the main super PAC supporting Republican Senate candidates, flooding the Texas airwaves with negative advertisements targeting Talarico and other Democratic candidates in the seven tossup Senate races, The Atlantic reported. By the final week, a New York Times analysis put his giving to Republicans this cycle at nearly $475 million, and Musk said he had cast his own early ballot in Texas on October 29. The other side has a billionaire too. Reid Hoffman, once Musk’s close friend and now his rival, has given $16.5 million to support Talarico. Republican outside groups are outspending their Democratic counterparts on advertising in every one of the tossup races, even as Democratic candidates have individually out-raised their Republican opponents, The New York Times reported on October 26, 2026.

The Republican advertising in South Texas has leaned into the region’s social conservatism — accusing Gonzalez of supporting taxpayer-funded “child sex changes,” attempting to link Pulido to a former bandmate with a criminal record. De La Cruz, meanwhile, is running new ads presenting herself as a corruption fighter willing to stand up to her own party — a rhetorical manoeuvre that sits awkwardly alongside her vote, with other Republicans, for two bills that together gave ICE more than $100 billion for immigration enforcement, ten times its annual operating budget.

Paxton, for his part, has adopted the apocalyptic register. “If we lose Texas, we lose America,” he tells crowds. It is the kind of framing that rallies the base and reminds moderates of exactly why they are drifting. It also lands awkwardly for a candidate The New York Times reported has taken about 350 trips outside Texas since taking office in 2015 — including 25 to foreign countries — with a taxpayer-funded security detail that has cost more than $3.1 million. This past summer, in the middle of his Senate campaign, a British tabloid spotted him in Iceland and London. Talarico has made that detail a campaign staple: “He takes lavish vacations in Iceland while Texans can’t afford the basics. Ken Paxton doesn’t serve us — he serves himself.” The charge lands differently when the man in the front yard is pointing at his truck and saying he cannot afford to drive it. The Cook Political Report shifted four Texas House races from tossup to lean-Democrat on October 23, attributing the movement to Paxton’s underperformance at the top of the ticket.

The Senate Map

In the broader fight for the Senate, Texas is one piece of a map that The New York Times described as “still wide and unsettled” on the eve of Election Day. Amy Walter of the Cook Political Report offered the bluntest summary: “This is as good a political environment as Democrats could ever wish, and as bad as Republicans could ever imagine.”

Former Democratic senator Jon Tester of Montana, who lost his own seat in 2024 as his state moved away from the party, was more personal about it. “If you can’t win under these conditions,” he said, “when can you win?”

What the Texas race represents, beyond its outcome, is a test of whether Democratic gains in the Rio Grande Valley are durable or cyclical — whether 2024 was the beginning of a shift or a weather event. If Talarico wins, the party rebuilds its foundation in a state it has not carried statewide in over thirty years and begins building toward 2028. If he loses, the argument that Latino voters have permanently migrated right resumes, this time with more evidence.

Chuck Rocha has spent years constructing political messages for working-class Latino men. He is candid about the limits of what a good cycle can accomplish: many of these voters are not “voting for the Democrats because they love the Democrats.” The party has a great deal of work to do before 2028.

But for this election, with one day left, the truck in the driveway may be doing more campaigning than anyone on the ballot.
