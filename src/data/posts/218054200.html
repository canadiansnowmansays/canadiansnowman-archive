There is a particular silence that falls over a livestream when the person talking has just been handed a note.

On the morning of Monday, September 28, 2026, about twenty minutes into Starship’s fourteenth flight, SpaceX launch commentator Dan Huot told the watching world that the orbital burn was off. One of the upper stage’s six Raptor engines had quit roughly seven minutes after liftoff, and the company, he explained, would only commit to orbit if everything looked healthy. Everything did not look healthy. Starship would coast along the same suborbital arc it had flown thirteen times before and come down in the ocean, as it always does.

Then came the note. “Actually, stand by,” Huot said, as reported by Rob Pegoraro in PCMag on September 28. There was, he added, a good deal of conversation in the room behind him. A fresh go/no-go poll was taken, the poll came back go, and a single Raptor lit for about nineteen seconds. Starship was in orbit.

Pegoraro allowed himself to wonder whose voice in that room had reopened the question, and whether it belonged to the man who owns the room. Nobody has said, and I will not pretend to know. But the scene deserves to sit at the top of this instalment, because in the space of a few minutes SpaceX performed the entire personality of the Starship programme in miniature: caution announced, caution reconsidered, and ambition declared the winner, all on a public feed, with the engineering case for the reversal left for later.

Alice, it must be said, is a little closer to the Moon than she was in July.

What actually happened

For readers who have followed this series since the early scrubs, the bare facts are these.

Liftoff came at 8:49 a.m. EDT from Starbase on the Texas Gulf Coast. All 33 Raptor engines on Booster 21 lit. One quit early on the way up, which the vehicle is designed to tolerate. Hot staging went cleanly. Ship 41 lost one of its own six engines during the ascent burn, and the remaining five burned longer to make up the difference.

After the reversal described above, the ship settled into an orbit of roughly 275 to 290 kilometres (reports range from about 170 to 180 miles, depending on who was reading which telemetry). Minutes later it began releasing its cargo, and this is the part of the day that matters most commercially: as William Harwood reported for Spaceflight Now, a dispenser in the ship’s nose pushed out 26 third-generation Starlink satellites, one after another, each carrying roughly ten times the capacity of the version-two birds that Falcon 9 has been flying. By 9:53 a.m. all 26 were away.

The plan had called for six orbits and nearly ten hours in space, ending in a splashdown off Chile. Instead, with the payload delivered and an engine already missing, flight controllers brought the ship home after a couple of laps. Starship reentered over the Pacific, relit three engines, flipped upright, and settled tail-first into the water north of Hawaii at about 11:57 a.m. EDT, three hours and nine minutes after launch. Huot conceded on the broadcast that it was not quite the soft splashdown of the previous flight. The ship then tipped over and exploded as its leftover propellant found the air, which is what ocean-landed Starships generally do, and which makes the survival of Ship 40 in July all the more of a curiosity.

So the scorecard reads: orbit, yes; payload, yes; full-duration mission, no; recovery of anything, no. Space.com’s live coverage called it a success on every goal except the six planned orbits, which is a generous but not unreasonable reading. The goal of Flight 14 was to get a Starship into orbit carrying something useful, and it did.

Go for orbit, then come home early

What interests me more than the scorecard is the shape of the decisions.

Count them. First, a scrub of the orbital burn on the grounds of caution. Second, a reversal of that scrub on the grounds that the data, looked at again, were good enough. Third, an early return on the grounds of caution once more, cutting seven hours off a ten-hour flight. That is not a straight line. It is a programme that wanted the headline badly enough to reopen a no-go call, and then, having got the headline, wanted to stop pushing its luck.

I do not say this to sneer. There is a respectable engineering argument for exactly this sequence. The orbital burn was short, the ship had engines to spare for its deorbit and landing burns, and once the Starlinks were out, every further hour in orbit was an hour of risk with nothing left to deliver. Bringing the ship down early is precisely what a sensible flight director does with a vehicle that has already lost an engine.

But the argument for the reversal was not made on the broadcast. What the public heard was “no,” then a murmur, then “yes.” Readers of the companion series The Capex Ledger will recognise the problem. SpaceX is now a public company. A flight that ends suborbital on the first orbital attempt is a different quarterly story from a flight that reaches orbit and deploys a revenue payload. When a no-go call is reconsidered in the space of a few minutes, the question of who reconsidered it, and on what evidence, is no longer only an engineering question. It is the sort of thing a securities lawyer would one day like to see in writing.

It may well turn out that the second poll was the correct call made by the correct people for the correct reasons. It would be nice to be told.

The booster that will not relight

Every instalment of this series has had one recurring character that refuses to improve, and this one is no exception. It is the Super Heavy booster’s return to Earth.

On Flight 12, on May 22, 2026, the booster’s engines failed to relight after stage separation, and the Federal Aviation Administration declared a mishap and grounded the rocket while SpaceX investigated. On Flight 13, on July 24, the booster came home with too few engines for a full landing burn and was lost. On Flight 14, by Pegoraro’s account, two of the thirteen engines needed for the boostback burn did not ignite, and the booster came into the Gulf of Mexico faster than it should have, sank and exploded.

In fairness, the accounts do not agree. Harwood’s report describes one engine quitting during boostback and a vertical splashdown a few miles offshore that looked, on camera, more or less normal. Several other outlets use the word “controlled.” At least one fan-maintained flight log says SpaceX set off the booster’s own destruct charges after it hit the water, as a deliberate test of its safety system. The fairest summary I can offer is this: for the third flight running, the booster’s relight sequence was short of engines, and for the third flight running, SpaceX has opted not to try a tower catch. Harwood notes that the company is sticking with ocean landings as a precaution until its upgrades are proven.

As of this writing on September 29, I could find no public mishap determination from the FAA on Flight 14. That may simply reflect the calendar, or it may reflect the fact that a booster planned for a sea landing, which lands in the sea, is hard to call a mishap. Either way, the boosters that SpaceX once caught with apparent ease, on three or four flights depending on whose count you use, have spent the whole of 2026 in the Gulf.

The catch that keeps moving

Which brings us to the promise.

In late July 2026, after Ship 40’s unexpectedly gentle splashdown, Elon Musk announced on X that, barring surprises in the data, SpaceX would try to catch the ship with the tower on the next flight. That was Flight 14. By August 20 the promise had softened to a catch “in a few months.” By launch day, SpaceX’s own flight plan listed no catch attempt for either stage.

After Monday’s flight, NBC News reported that Musk now puts the odds of a successful ship catch at even, or a little better, and expects to refly a caught ship by the end of this year or early next.

Long-time readers will know that the ship catch has been “next flight” for a long while. After Flight 6 in November 2024, Musk suggested Flight 8 might be the one. Flight 8 failed on ascent. The catch migrated to Flight 9, which also failed. It has been drifting forward ever since, a little like the horizon, which likewise gets no closer however fast you walk toward it. I say this with some affection. The Musk timeline is a genre of its own, and no one who reads it as a schedule rather than a mood has been paying attention.

The yardsticks

Pegoraro makes two comparisons worth borrowing.

The first is with SpaceX’s own history. Falcon 1 reached orbit on its fourth attempt on September 28, 2008, eighteen years to the day before Starship did it on its fourteenth. That company was a few hundred people in a hangar, and one more failure would have bankrupted it. This one is a publicly traded giant with an $86-billion IPO behind it and a launch schedule measured in flights per week.

The second is with Blue Origin, whose New Glenn reached orbit on its first flight in January 2025. The comparison is a little unfair, and Pegoraro says so: Starship is trying to be fully and rapidly reusable at a scale no one has attempted, while New Glenn was trying to reach orbit. But fairness cuts both ways. For three and a half years, SpaceX’s defenders have asked the public to judge Starship by its ambitions rather than its results. Monday was the first day that the results began to catch up.

And the word “rapidly” still has not earned its place. The heat-shield tiles on Ship 40, now slowly making its way back to Texas, showed the kind of damage that means weeks of refurbishment, not hours. Two stages lost on Monday, both by design and both in salt water, do not yet add up to reuse of any kind.

The market shrugged

The stock market, which has provided this series with a running subplot since the IPO in June, was underwhelmed.

According to 24/7 Wall St., SPCX edged up to just under $150 in the first hour of trading after launch, then drifted lower and finished the day at $145.47, down 2.16 per cent. For a company that listed at $135 in June, briefly touched $225, and then spent August unwinding below its IPO price, the first orbital flight of its flagship vehicle was worth, in round numbers, nothing.

There are a few ways to read that. The generous one is that the market had already priced in success. The less generous one, which I prefer, is that the valuation was never really about this rocket reaching this orbit. It rests on what Starship is supposed to make possible afterwards, most of all the orbital data centres for which SpaceX’s IPO filing laid out, as Pegoraro reminds us, a market forecast of $26 trillion. One analyst service puts the company’s price-to-sales ratio above 150. At that altitude a stock does not respond to a rocket reaching orbit. It responds to whether the rocket can reach orbit thirty times a day from a swamp in Louisiana, which is the subject of the previous instalment and the unspoken premise of this one.

On Tuesday morning, TD Cowen issued a buy rating and the shares rose a little. The rocket had nothing to do with it.

And the Moon?

This series takes its name from Ralph Kramden’s famous threat, and so we must ask the question every instalment eventually comes back to: how close is Alice to the Moon?

Closer. Not close.

NASA has a great deal riding on the answer. It awarded SpaceX a $2.89-billion contract in 2021 to build a lunar lander version of Starship’s upper stage, since revised to about $4 billion, of which roughly $2.9 billion has already been paid. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman was quick to congratulate the company on Monday, praising the flight as safe, responsible and inspiring, and saying he looked forward to Starship missions becoming routine.

But look at what Harwood lays out as the remaining path. The Artemis III flight next year is now a crewed rendezvous test in low Earth orbit with prototype landers, not a landing. The landing itself has moved to Artemis IV, targeted for 2028. To get there, SpaceX has to launch something like fifteen Starship tanker flights within days of one another to fill the lander’s tanks in orbit, and it has yet to demonstrate transferring a single tonne of propellant between two ships. Flight 14 did not attempt that, and was not meant to. Many veterans of NASA and the aerospace industry doubt SpaceX can deliver a certified human lander by 2028, which is why NASA is quietly paying Blue Origin to build a second one.

Put simply, SpaceX needed fourteen flights to get one ship into orbit for three hours. A Moon landing needs roughly fifteen of them in orbit within days, working together, then a lander that can set down on the lunar south pole and take off again with two people aboard. SpaceX now has two Starship pads in Texas, three more under construction in Florida, and a hundred-billion-dollar spaceport promised for Louisiana. It will need all of them.

Coda: the motto

If the Starship programme ever needs a motto for its mission patch, I would like to propose the three words Dan Huot spoke at about a quarter past nine on Monday morning.

Actually, stand by.

They cover the ship catch, which is always one flight away. They cover the booster, which keeps almost relighting. They cover the 2028 landing, the thirty-flights-a-day spaceport and the $26-trillion orbital data centre market. They cover a stock that did not move when the rocket did. Most of all they cover that moment in the control room, when caution gave way to “go” and the reasons were left for another day.

For all that, the rocket is in orbit. The satellites are in orbit too. Whatever doubts one has about the timelines, the valuations or the voices in that room, something the size of a forty-storey building went round the Earth on Monday and came back down where it was supposed to. Fourteen flights is a long apprenticeship, and SpaceX served every day of it. On September 28 it was also, for once, lucky.

Alice is closer to the Moon. She should keep her coat on.
