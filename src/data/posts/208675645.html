Stephen Gordon

In George Orwell’s 1984, Winston Smith did not spend his days writing propaganda. He spent them adjusting figures.

That is the detail most people forget about the Ministry of Truth. Winston’s actual job — the tedious, clerical, eight-hour-a-day substance of it — was revising production statistics so that the numbers the Party had published in the past would agree with the numbers it needed to be true in the present. Boot output, chocolate rations, tonnage of pig iron. He worked from a chute that delivered slips of paper telling him which figures required correction, and he corrected them, and the old figures went into the memory hole, and nobody who had read the earlier number could prove it had ever existed.

The publication he rewrote was called The Times.

On Sunday, July 26, 2026, the United States Department of Defense revised the number of American service members killed and wounded in its war with Iran. It did not delete the dead. It filed them.

Welcome to Part 11 of the Ministry of Truth.

What Happened, in Order

The sequence matters, because each step was explained, and each explanation made less sense than the silence it replaced.

Earlier in the week of July 20, 2026, as the New York Times reported, the Pentagon lowered the number of service members killed in the Iran war from eighteen to fourteen. Four names came off the Defense Casualty Analysis System — the four killed in Jordan and Iraq in the strikes of July 17 and 18, whose flag-draped transfer cases the president watched come off a C-17 at Dover on July 22, and whose families I wrote about in the last essay of the Iran Crisis series.

Military officials, speaking anonymously, explained the reasoning: those four had died after the ceasefire reached in April. They therefore belonged to a different war.

On Thursday, July 23, the Pentagon’s acting press secretary, Joel Valdez, offered a different explanation. The website was experiencing temporary data disruptions. It would be fixed shortly.

Also on Thursday, a group of Democratic senators wrote to Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth requesting a comprehensive accounting of the dead and wounded, noting that public casualty reporting throughout the conflict had been neither timely nor complete.

As of Saturday morning, July 25, the department’s website still showed fourteen killed under Operation Epic Fury — which is, for the record, the name the Defense Department has given to this war, a fact worth pausing on given how rarely the president who ordered it uses the name.

Then, on Sunday, July 26, the fix arrived. It was not a correction. It was a second webpage.

The Arithmetic

Here is what the new system does.

Casualties occurring since July 7, 2026 — the day the ceasefire began visibly unravelling — are no longer listed alongside the casualties of the war that began on February 28. They now appear on a separate page, labelled, with real artistry, “overseas operations casualties.” The four dead soldiers were restored to the record, in a new and unexplained category, beneath a note specifying that overseas operations casualties begin on July 7, 2026.

And with the new page came new numbers. The count of wounded since July 7 rose by one hundred and forty-two.

The official figure for wounded since July 7 now stands at 207. The total wounded since February 28 stands at 624. The total killed stands at eighteen — the same eighteen it was before anyone touched anything, now distributed across two pages that must be added together by the reader.

Six days earlier, the chief Pentagon spokesman, Sean Parnell, had insisted that fewer than one hundred American service members had been wounded since July 7. The department’s own revised figure is more than double that. Parnell’s statement at the time did not hedge. It went further: “Claims of concealment are fabrications meant to further distress the American people.”

Read that sentence twice, because it contains the whole thesis of this series in one clause. The distress, in Parnell’s construction, is caused not by the wounding of two hundred Americans but by the reporting of it. The injury is the disclosure. The harm is the arithmetic.

When asked on Sunday about the new numbers and the new system, a Pentagon spokesman referred questions to the White House. The White House did not respond.

Two Wars, One Set of Barracks

The reclassification is not a clerical convenience. It is the casualty ledger catching up to a legal argument.

The president has maintained to lawmakers that the fighting since July is a new conflict with Iran rather than a continuation of the war he began with Israel on February 28. That war, in his telling, ended in April when the ceasefire took effect. On July 10, 2026, he formally notified Congress that hostilities had resumed — a notification that, administration officials contend, restarts the sixty-day clock the War Powers Resolution of 1973 imposes on military operations undertaken without congressional authorization.

The utility of this position is obvious in the statute. What is new is its migration into the morgue.

If July 10 began a new war, then the four soldiers killed in Jordan and Iraq died in the new one, and the eighteen dead of the first war become fourteen, and no single page in the federal government’s possession states what the whole thing has cost. Two wars on paper. One set of barracks. The same missile came through the same gap in the same air defences that the Iran Crisis series has been documenting since the interceptor shortages first surfaced, and it does not care which page it is recorded on.

This is doublethink in its original and precise sense: not lying, but holding two mutually exclusive accountings simultaneously and finding both administratively convenient.

Congress, to its limited credit, is not entirely persuaded. On Thursday, July 23, the House voted for the second time to direct the president to end the war in Iran or seek explicit authorization to continue it. The Senate has so far declined to follow, on the reasoning — and this is the part that ought to be carved somewhere in marble — that the sixty-day clock restarted when the ceasefire broke, so the administration should be given more time.

The clock that exists to constrain the war is now the argument for extending it. The clock that resets the war also resets the dead.

The Same Two Men, The Same Room

Regular readers will recognize the names.

Joel Valdez, who attributed the vanishing of four American soldiers to temporary data disruptions, is the same acting press secretary who, in Part 10 of this series, announced that the Pentagon’s press office had been designated a classified space, available by appointment only, because speechwriters handling classified material had been moved in among the public affairs staff.

So the sequence, stated plainly: on June 1, 2026, the office whose function is communication with the public became a room the public’s representatives may not enter. Seven weeks later, statements issued from that room adjusted the number of American dead downward by four and the number of American wounded upward by one hundred and forty-two, and referred all questions elsewhere.

Part 10 argued that you do not classify the room where you announce victories. Part 11 amends that only slightly. You do not classify the room where the numbers are simple, either.

There is a progression across this series that I want to name, because it took ten installments to become visible. Parts 1 through 4 documented restrictions on access — who may hold a press pass, who may walk which corridor, who may be designated a security risk. Parts 6 through 8 documented what access restrictions purchase: operational impunity, the boat strikes conducted in near-total secrecy, two hundred dead and two bodies identified. Part 9 documented vanity. Part 10 documented enclosure — the last unescorted room sealed.

Part 11 is the next logical step, and it is worse than all of them, because it does not require any journalist to be excluded from anything. The Times reported this story. Nobody stopped them. The casualty figures are published on a public website by a federal agency, exactly as the law and decency require.

They are simply published in two places, in two categories, under two theories of when the war began, so that the sum exists nowhere and must be assembled by anyone who wants it. This is a considerable advance on censorship. Censorship leaves a hole a reader can see. Reclassification leaves a number that is technically correct and practically unavailable.

What Was Already Missing

None of this is the first gap in this ledger.

In the week before the strikes that killed three soldiers in Jordan, three other Iranian strikes left dozens of Americans injured. Those casualties were not disclosed when they occurred. Earlier in the month, as the Iran Crisis series noted in Part LXXXVIII, the fact that a dozen or more wounded had been flown to Germany for treatment emerged not from a Pentagon announcement but from journalists reading flight-tracking websites and asking why the aircraft were going where they were going.

And Central Command has, since the campaign resumed on July 14, stopped stating in its nightly summaries how many targets it is striking, on the grounds that the figure would tell the Iranian high command too much. Officials have privately put the total above one thousand targets in a fortnight. The comparable figure for the thirty-eight-day campaign that began February 28 was roughly thirteen thousand.

So the public record of this war now contains: no nightly target count, no timely disclosure of wounded, no single page listing the dead, and a president who declined to answer, in the Oval Office on Friday, July 24, when asked whether he hesitated to bomb power plants and bridges because doing so could constitute war crimes. His answer, as Tom Howell Jr reported, was that he would not respond to that question.

The refusal is at least honest about being a refusal. It is more forthcoming, in its way, than a webpage.

The Pattern, Restated

Part 4 of this series was called “The Body Count.” It was written on the assumption — quaint, in retrospect — that the risk to an accurate casualty record was inattention. That if enough people stopped watching, the numbers would go unexamined.

That was wrong, and I would like to correct it here. The numbers are being examined. Senators are writing letters. The Times is publishing. Veterans and military families and lawmakers, per the Pentagon’s own critics this week, are outraged.

The numbers are being examined and adjusted at the same time, by the same institution, in a room that reporters may enter by appointment.

This series has argued from its first installment that the biggest lie is always in the name itself. The Department of War, which calls itself that now, has stopped pretending its business is defence. Its public affairs office, which is classified, has stopped pretending to be public. And the Defense Casualty Analysis System, which is the last of the three still operating under a name that describes its function, now performs its analysis across two pages that no one has been instructed to add together.

Eighteen Americans are dead. Six hundred and twenty-four are wounded. Neither number appears anywhere in full.

Somewhere in Carrollton, Texas, a family that buried a nineteen-year-old private this month can go to a federal website and find, depending on which page they open, that she counted or that she is filed under overseas operations, beginning July 7, 2026.

Winston Smith’s chute delivered slips of paper telling him which figures required correction. The Pentagon has automated the process and made it public. That is either an improvement or the end of the argument, and I have been writing this series for ten months without yet deciding which.
