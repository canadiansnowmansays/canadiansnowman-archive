Stephen Gordon

October 6 came and went without a debate. WFAA had held the Dallas date since July. Talarico had accepted it. Ken Paxton never did, and by the following morning the Texas Tribune was reporting that he still hadn’t accepted a single invitation, and that Greg Abbott hadn’t agreed to debate Gina Hinojosa either. The state’s two most powerful Republicans have both declined to share a stage. Karl Rove, no friend of Texas Democrats, has put the consequence plainly: if Paxton doesn’t debate Talarico, “he loses.”

Fairness requires the twist. Talarico wouldn’t have made it to Dallas either. He has been off the trail with the flu, diagnosed at a Dallas urgent-care clinic and cancelling at least two events this week, including a Central Texas gathering aimed at Black voters. The Paxton campaign pounced. A spokeswoman said Talarico had time for a New York fundraiser but not for Texans, and accused him of hiding behind recycled rally footage and cancelled events. It is a curious charge from a campaign whose candidate has turned down every debate offer since July. But it isn’t nothing. A campaign whose whole argument is that it shows up has had a fortnight of not showing up, with early voting a dozen days away.

Paxton had his own week. On October 7, Axios published a second recording from a closed-door Washington fundraiser in September, transcribed by Mediaite. This time the drag wasn’t the convention; it was the war. “It’s not tariffs… it’s the war, it’s gas prices, it’s diesel, over $6 per gallon… people are feeling the pinch. So that’s by far our biggest problem.” And: “I don’t know what’s behind some of the decisions.” This from a man who said, in the first week of the war, that he was “very glad” the president did it. His campaign called the recording a “non-story.”

Debates, fevers and leaked tapes come and go. What doesn’t change is the machinery underneath them.

Texas runs on two kinds of optimism. There is the ordinary Lone Star variety, which holds that no ranch is too big, no oil well too deep and no barbecue line too long. And there is the rarer, more fragile Democratic variety, which appears every few years, generates national magazine covers, and then dies on election night somewhere between the suburbs of Fort Worth and the Panhandle.

The second kind is back. As Robert Draper reported in “In Texas, Talarico Inspires a Recurring Blue-Tinted Dream” (New York Times, October 3, 2026), the latest Times/Siena poll, taken September 21–30, has James Talarico leading Ken Paxton by six points. A mid-September ReconMR/Siena survey had Democrat Gina Hinojosa four points ahead of Greg Abbott, a three-term governor who has spent most of a decade looking unbeatable. Texas Southern University has Donald Trump seven points underwater in a state he carried by nearly fourteen.

The last two instalments looked at the drag at the top of the Republican ticket and the war over James Talarico's faith. This instalment is about something less entertaining and more decisive: the machinery underneath it. Polls measure what voters want. Machines decide which of those voters actually get counted.

The Graveyard of Good Polls

James Henson, who runs the University of Texas’s Texas Politics Project, put the Democratic condition better than anyone: “You look at the trends and you have to admit that something’s going on. But you still don’t want to believe your lying eyes, because you’ve been burned for so long.”

The burns are well documented. Tony Sanchez Jr., the self-funding Laredo oilman, lost to Rick Perry by eighteen points in 2002. Wendy Davis, whose filibuster against an abortion bill made her an international name, lost to Abbott by twenty in 2014. Beto O’Rourke came within 2.6 points of Ted Cruz in 2018, and then lost to Abbott by ten in 2022. Each campaign produced crowds, money and hope. None produced a Democratic senator or governor.

The difference this time, and it is a real one, is that the polling lead isn’t a single outlier or a summer bubble. As “The Drag” laid out, every public poll in September had Talarico ahead, and the Times/Siena result is the widest yet. Beto led in two polls across his entire 2018 cycle. Talarico has led in poll after poll since mid-July.

But leads in polls have never been the Texas Democrats’ problem. Turning them into votes has.

The Machine That Abbott Built

“Governor Abbott has built an impressive election machine that has benefited state-level Republicans again and again for a decade and a half,” Henson told Draper. “And the Democratic machinery here is almost nonexistent.”

The Democratic side of that sentence is worth counting out. In recent cycles, as many as a third of state races have gone uncontested by Democrats. A fifth of the party’s county chairs and two-thirds of its precinct chairs sat empty. Until last year, the state party ran a negative operating budget. Veteran consultant Matt Angle calls it “a hollowing-out of the party.” Luke Warford, who chairs the Travis County Democrats, describes the vicious circle: thirty years of losing keeps donors on the sidelines, which keeps good candidates from running, which sends talented staff to real battleground states, which produces more losing.

Then there is the sheer size of the place. Texas has some 32 million people spread across twenty media markets. Katherine Fischer of the Texas Majority PAC put the donor’s dilemma bluntly: in Iowa, “$5 million can fully fund a race,” and Texas is “just so damned big.” Hinojosa has raised about $20 million and says a fifth of the electorate still doesn’t know who she is. Abbott has outspent her roughly ten to one. On the Senate side, Paxton and his allies have now put more than $100 million into ads defining Talarico.

That is what a machine looks like. It isn’t a smoke-filled room. It is the absence of a smoke-filled room on the other side.

The Rules of the Road

A machine also writes the rules of the road, and Texas has spent the last decade rewriting them.

Straight-ticket voting, a staple in the Democratic urban counties, was abolished in 2020. Texas is one of eight states without universal online voter registration, and voting by mail is tightly restricted. Draper reports that in January, Abbott handed the state’s roughly 18.7 million-name voter list to the Trump administration, and that 1.2 million voters have since been placed on the “suspense list” for unverified addresses.

Fairness requires the other half of that story. As Fox 4 in Dallas explained, suspense status usually follows a returned piece of mail, covers about six percent of registered voters, and does not by itself stop anyone voting: a suspense voter can confirm a current address at the polls. The October 5 registration-update deadline has now passed. What the list does is add one more step, one more form and one more line, and every additional step falls hardest on the voters who move most often: renters, students and the young. That is not a coincidence of demography. It is the demography Democrats need.

And then there is Tarrant County.

Tarrant County, Where Texas Elections Go to Be Decided

If Texas has a hinge, it is Tarrant: Fort Worth and its suburbs, purple in a red state, the county that tells you which way the state is turning. On September 1, the Republican-majority Commissioners Court voted 3–2 to cut Election Day polling locations from 316 in 2022 to 224 this November: ninety-two fewer sites, close to a third of the total. County Judge Tim O'Hare said the plan "exceeds the statutory minimum" and that some buildings were no longer usable or ADA-compliant. The two Democratic commissioners voted no; Commissioner Roderick Miles compared the cuts to a modern poll tax. More than two hours of public comment ran almost entirely against. A lawsuit has since been filed.

Wendy Davis, now advising Planned Parenthood Texas Votes and co-founder of the down-ballot group Blueprint Texas, gave Draper the arithmetic: “The reason we’ve lost these statewide races in the past is because we’ve never gotten enough out of Tarrant County to overcome the margins in rural Texas. If we don’t get it to 53 or 54 percent this November, it’s game over. James and Gina can’t win.”

So the most important number in the Texas Senate race may not be in any poll. It may be the length of the line outside a Fort Worth community centre at 5:45 on the evening of November 3.

But Tarrant is also where the Republican machine has shown its first visible crack. In a January special election for State Senate District 9, a district Trump carried by seventeen points and one that hadn’t elected a Democrat since 1991, Taylor Rehmet won with more than 57 percent. Rehmet is a 33-year-old aviation machinist and union-chapter president running for office for the first time. His opponent, Leigh Wambsganss, had led the conservative takeover of several area school boards and had more than $2 million behind her. In her day job she is chief communications officer for Patriot Mobile, a conservative cellphone company. As a retired telecom man, I can report that the market was clear: Tarrant County voters dropped the call.

Alisa Simmons, one of the two commissioners who voted against the polling cuts, is now running to unseat O’Hare as county judge. She says the county’s far-right leadership has “implemented Project 2025 on a county level.” In Tarrant, the machine is literally on the ballot.

Out Where the Ads Are Working

It would be a poor essay that only interviewed the optimists, and Draper’s best reporting is from the places where the dream gets complicated.

In Laredo, Trump’s 2024 win was the first for a Republican in more than a century. Buyer’s remorse is visible: ICE arrests in July were higher there than in any other American city, and the president’s trade policies have unsettled the port of entry the city lives on. But Adrian Zapata, a health-industry executive who voted for Trump, says the $100 million in ads are landing with his family: “They see Talarico as an ultraliberal. They’re done with the ‘they/them.’ They’re going to vote for Paxton just because of that.” Zapata himself is “not ready to vote for Talarico,” but adds: “I don’t like Paxton. He’s never done anything for South Texas.”

That is the shape of the Paxton campaign's bet: more than $100 million in ads calling Talarico "radical" and "weird." They don't need to make Paxton likeable. They only need to make Talarico unthinkable to enough people like Zapata's relatives. (How those ads have turned a Senate race into a referendum on one man's theology was the subject of the previous instalment, "The Wrong Kind of Christian.")

Out in the cattle country near Big Bend, the picture is just as mixed. More than 200 landowners have sued the federal government over the border wall; residents near Fort Stockton are petitioning against a new AI data centre. George Johnson, a feed operator in Alpine, isn’t moved: “It’s Talarico who scares me to death, him and all that socialist talk.” But Thaddeus Cleveland, the Republican sheriff of Terrell County, voted for Trump in 2024 and now says, “Everything he campaigned on, we’re seeing the opposite of, like going to war.” He thinks his neighbours will vote on “the price of groceries and gas, which are compounded here because we’re so far from everywhere else.”

When a Republican sheriff in West Texas is predicting that grocery prices will beat attack ads, the attack ads have a problem.

The Counter-Machine

For the first time in a long time, Democrats have started building one of their own.

Thanks largely to recruiting by the Texas Majority PAC and Davis’s Blueprint Texas, Democrats are fielding candidates in all 150 Texas House races for the first time in half a century. Most of them will lose. That isn’t the point. A Democrat on the ballot in the Panhandle gives the Panhandle’s Democrats a reason to show up, and every one of those votes counts statewide for Talarico and Hinojosa.

“If we win on Nov. 3,” Beto O’Rourke told Draper, “most of those names running down-ballot in really tough areas like East Texas and the Hill Country and the Panhandle will be responsible.” O’Rourke also offered the most candid diagnosis of the reversal: “We adopted a lazy way of campaigning that bit us on the ass, and we’ve learned from that. Now it’s the Republicans who’ve gotten really lazy and complacent and comfortable.”

There is some evidence for that. March primary turnout reached nearly one voter in four, a modern record for a Texas midterm, with Democrats slightly outpacing Republicans. Texas ranked second-to-last in turnout nationally in 2024. If the state’s chronic non-voters are waking up, the polls may be underestimating what’s coming rather than overestimating it.

Which brings us back to Hinojosa, the race national Democrats keep treating as a long shot. As Reid J. Epstein reported in the Times’ October 1 election coverage, the Democratic Governors Association has yet to commit the $10 million her allies have asked for, even though she is within reach of Abbott and is the only Latina running for a major statewide office anywhere this year. Davis is blunt: “Dollar for dollar, she’s running the best statewide campaign we’ve ever seen.” Republicans are spending as though Texas is in play. Some national Democrats are still spending as though it’s 2014.

The Gradualist’s Wager

Henson, the professional skeptic, won’t call the race. What he will offer is a “gradualist” theory: Texas does not turn blue overnight, it turns competitive. “Even if Talarico loses by a Beto-like margin,” he told Draper, “the next cycle is a presidential election, where Trump likely won’t be popular and Democratic turnout will be higher. So there are reasons for Texas Democrats to be optimistic.” He also doubts that the last-minute flood of Paxton advertising will be decisive: “At a certain point, there’s diminishing impact once you get past an absurd level of spending.”

I find the gradualist case persuasive and a little sad, the way sensible advice usually is. It is persuasive because the machinery doesn’t change in a single cycle: the precinct chairs, the full slates, the donors who finally believe, the staff who stop leaving for Arizona. It is sad because it asks Texas Democrats to treat a six-point lead as a down payment rather than a result.

But there is a version of the gradualist theory that cuts the other way. Machines are built slowly; they can also stop working all at once. A Republican machine designed to win elections in a Trump-friendly Texas, fuelled by Hispanic gains in the Valley and quiet suburban loyalty in Tarrant, is now running a Senate nominee who told donors the president was a drag, and has lost a seventeen-point district in the very county where it has since closed nearly a third of the polling places.

Paxton has bet that a Republican in Texas still doesn't need to stand on a stage and explain himself. On October 14, Talarico, flu permitting, will be on CNN doing exactly that, with nobody on the other podium.

The polls have given Texas Democrats their dream back. The next four weeks will show whether they have finally built something to carry it home in, and whether the other side’s machine, after thirty-two years of running unopposed, still remembers how to run against anyone at all.

Postscript

On Wednesday night the president came to San Antonio, his third trip to Texas in a month, to shore up a nominee whose race has gone wobbly. The rally was mostly not about Ken Paxton. Roger Clemens introduced Trump and was promised a Medal of Freedom. Paxton got a brief turn onstage and a compliment about being one of the country’s best attorneys general. The rest of the night was about the border, the Democrats, and Donald Trump, including a repeat of his claim that Iran could take out Los Angeles or San Diego. “We protect our cities, we protect our country,” he said. So he was campaigning on the same war that Paxton, in a recording published that very day, had privately called “by far our biggest problem.”

The crowd showed what the machine has to work with. Julie Funk, a retired first-grade teacher from Bulverde, summed up the mood: “Look, we’re voting for him, let’s just put it that way.” Her friends winced when Paxton’s name came up. That counts as a vote, but not as a volunteer. In a race this close, that difference may decide it.
