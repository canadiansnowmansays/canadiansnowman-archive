On October 9, 2025, this series made a case that was serious in substance and, I will admit, a little mischievous in spirit. With Republican lawmakers lining up to nominate Donald Trump for the Nobel Peace Prize, I argued that George Soros, then 95, a Holocaust survivor who had spent forty-five years and some $32 billion building universities, courts, free presses and civil societies across sixty-odd countries, had a far stronger claim than any recent American president. The heart of the argument fit on a single line: Trump stops wars; Soros prevents them. Ceasefires extracted by threat are a pause. Institutions are the thing that makes the next war less likely.

The poetic justice was the mischief. The man Trump had spent a decade casting as the puppet master behind every protest and prosecutor would receive the honour Trump had coveted since his first term.

The committee announced its choice the next morning, October 10, 2025. It was neither of them.

Exactly one year later, on Friday, October 9, 2026, the committee announced its choice again. It still did not pick Soros. But it picked his argument, and it picked a person who has spent sixty years living it. This is a coda about what happened in between, and about the woman who won.

The Year of the Borrowed Medal

The 2025 laureate was María Corina Machado, the Venezuelan opposition leader, honoured for defending democracy against Nicolás Maduro’s regime. It was a defensible choice, and for a few weeks it looked like an inspired one. Machado had been living in hiding. In December 2025 she slipped out of Venezuela in disguise, crossed military checkpoints, sailed to Curaçao on a fishing skiff and flew to Oslo, arriving hours after the ceremony at which her daughter had accepted the prize and read her speech.

Then the year turned strange.

On January 3, 2026, American commandos raided Caracas and seized Maduro, who was flown to Manhattan and indicted on drug and weapons charges. Machado had broadly supported the American military build-up in the Caribbean. She had also, in 2024, run the successful campaign that Maduro refused to honour. She might reasonably have expected a role in what came next. Trump instead declared that she lacked “respect” inside Venezuela and declined to back her as leader of a new government.

What Machado did next was the most remarkable act of the Nobel year. In mid-January she announced she would present her Nobel medal to Trump, as gratitude, she said, from the Venezuelan people for Maduro’s removal. The Norwegian Nobel Institute replied with the institutional equivalent of a raised eyebrow. A laureate, it said, cannot share the prize or transfer it once announced, and a Peace Prize can never be revoked: “The decision is final and applies for all time.”

A few days later, Machado handed over the medal anyway, mounted in a large gold frame, in the Oval Office. Trump said he was “honored” to finally receive the prize. He posed for photographs.

Here, then, was the TACO cycle applied to a medal. Threaten the committee for years with grievance about being overlooked. Accept a gift that is not a prize. Capitulate to the fiction that the gift is the prize. Obfuscate the difference with a gold frame. By the Institute’s own rules, the 2025 Nobel Peace Prize belongs to María Corina Machado, in the record, for all time. What hangs at the White House is a piece of gold with no standing at all. The medal could change hands. The honour could not.

The epilogue to Machado’s year was crueller still. On June 24, 2026, two violent earthquakes struck Venezuela and killed thousands. Machado asked to return to help with recovery. The Trump administration, by then effectively running the country, refused her repeatedly, with some officials calling her request a “political stunt.” She has spent the months since making appearances in Europe and the Americas. She gave Trump the one thing she had that he wanted, and in return he kept her out of her own country.

If anyone needed a demonstration of the difference between a transaction and an institution, 2026 provided one free of charge.

A Morning in Nuremberg

Which brings us to Friday.

When the committee chair, Jørgen Watne Frydnes, stood up in Oslo at 11 a.m. local time to announce the 2026 laureate, the laureate herself did not yet know. The Institute had not yet managed to reach her. “If you see a Norwegian number calling, please pick up,” Frydnes said to the cameras. “I would love to talk to you very soon.”

Navi Pillay was in Nuremberg, Germany, at an annual conference of human rights lawyers and judges. As Marlise Simons reported for the New York Times on October 9, 2026, in its live coverage “Live Updates: Human Rights Lawyer Navi Pillay Is Awarded Nobel Peace Prize,” she was quietly led out of the room shortly before the news broke. Stephen Rapp, the former U.S. ambassador-at-large for war crimes issues, told the Times that he then shouted out the news to roughly 120 people, and the whole room applauded.

No set designer could have arranged a better stage. Nuremberg is where international criminal law was born, in 1945 and 1946, when the victorious Allies decided that the leaders of a defeated regime would face judges rather than firing squads. The principle established there, that crimes against humanity and wars of aggression are matters of law and not merely of who wins, is the principle Pillay has served for her entire career. She learned she had won the world’s most famous peace prize in the city where her profession began.

What the Committee Actually Said

The committee’s citation was brief: the prize went to Pillay “for her efforts to promote peace and international law.” Its fuller statement was blunter. It called her “one of the most respected international jurists of our time” and said she had been “instrumental in ensuring that war crimes, crimes against humanity and genocide are prosecuted.”

Then came the paragraph that could have been lifted from Part 1 of this series. The committee warned that the system of international law is under heavy pressure, and that a global shift toward “power politics” was eroding respect for law and justice. It said it wanted to remind the world that the rule of law, national and international, underpins the system built to resolve conflicts without violence. And it ended with three words: “Peace requires justice.”

Asked whether the award was also a way of honouring the International Criminal Court, where Pillay served as a judge, Frydnes said it was a message that international law and its institutions are “the cornerstone of a more peaceful world,” and also “a message to those who are attacking” them.

He named no one. He did not need to. Throughout 2025 the Trump administration authorized sanctions against the ICC and then imposed them on its judges and officials, withdrew from the UN Human Rights Council, and sanctioned the UN’s special rapporteur on the occupied Palestinian territories. When the committee speaks of those “attacking” the cornerstone of a more peaceful world, the list of candidates is short, and one of them held the gold-framed medal.

A year ago this series asked which vision of peace the world wanted to encourage: temporary quiet extracted by threats, or the slow, unglamorous work of building institutions that make violence less likely. On October 9, 2026, five Norwegians answered the question in writing.

The Career the Committee Was Describing

It would be a disservice to treat Pillay merely as a rebuke delivered to someone else. Her career is the case for institution-building, made in one human life, and it is worth setting out.

She was born in Durban in 1941 to a family of Indian Tamil origin, the daughter of a bus driver, in a country that was about to formalize racial hierarchy as apartheid. She studied law at the University of Natal, and in 1967 became the first non-white woman to open a law practice in Natal, largely because no white firm would employ her. For nearly three decades she defended opponents of apartheid, and the committee singled out her defence of Nelson Mandela and others who confronted the regime. In 1973 she won a case establishing that political prisoners on Robben Island had the right to see lawyers. It sounds modest. For the men on that island, it was the difference between existing inside the law and disappearing outside it.

In 1995, after apartheid fell, she became the first non-white woman appointed to the High Court of South Africa. The same year she went to Arusha, Tanzania, as a judge of the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda, created after the 1994 genocide, and later served as its president. In 1998 she sat on the trial chamber in the case of Jean-Paul Akayesu. That judgment was the first conviction for genocide by an international court, and it was the first to hold that rape and sexual violence could themselves be acts of genocide. Every war crimes prosecution for sexual violence since stands on that ruling.

She moved to The Hague as a judge of the International Criminal Court from 2003 to 2008. From 2008 to 2014 she served as UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, a job that made enemies everywhere because she criticized everyone: Sri Lanka, Syria, Iran, Russia, China, and, more than a few times, the United States.

Then, from 2021, she chaired the UN Commission of Inquiry on the Occupied Palestinian Territory and Israel. In September 2025, that commission concluded that Israel had committed genocide against Palestinians in Gaza. It was the commission’s most sweeping finding.

The committee summarized the common thread as confronting deep structural discrimination, segregation and exclusion. A more Leacockian summary might be that Navi Pillay has spent sixty years walking into rooms full of powerful men and explaining, politely, that the rules apply to them too. She has done it from the defence table, then from the bench, then from the podium in Geneva. Very few of those men thanked her.

The Two-Step on Gaza

The committee handled the most contentious part of her record with care. Its official statement mentioned the Gaza commission only briefly. In Oslo, asked directly about it, Frydnes said the commission work was “an important part of the reasoning why she received the prize.”

That is a deliberate two-step: a written record restrained enough to withstand attack, and a spoken answer that leaves no ambiguity. Israel’s foreign ministry responded within hours, posting on X that the award amounted to “the grotesque weaponization of an award to legitimize anti-Israel hatred and prejudice.” The Israeli government has rejected the commission’s finding since it was issued, and it rejects it still.

An honest coda should record that disagreement plainly rather than wave it away. The genocide question is being argued in courts, and it will be argued for years. But the committee’s choice says something narrower and harder to dispute: that a finding reached through a legal process, by a jurist with Pillay’s record, deserves to be heard rather than sanctioned. That, once again, was the argument of this series. Institutions are only worth having if they are allowed to reach conclusions the powerful dislike.

What Soros Would Recognize

George Soros turned 96 in August. He did not win, and he is unlikely ever to. A one-year retrospective should be honest about that too: the committee rarely honours philanthropists, and Soros carries political baggage across half the world, much of it manufactured but no less heavy for that. In late August 2025, Trump publicly called for Soros and his son to be charged under racketeering law. The poetic justice this series imagined is not going to happen.

But there is a quieter symmetry that would not be lost on him. Soros’s first major philanthropy, in 1979, was a scholarship programme for Black students at the University of Cape Town under apartheid. The project failed in the form he intended, and he later said it taught him how hard it is to build anything inside a regime designed to prevent it. Pillay was in Durban that same year, a few hundred kilometres up the coast, doing exactly that from inside the courtroom. The financier and the lawyer were working on the same problem from opposite ends: one trying to fund the people who would build a just society, the other trying to keep the law alive long enough for that society to arrive.

The original essay ended with the line about stopping wars and preventing them. A year later, it needs a third clause. Trump stops wars, or announces that he has. Soros spent his fortune trying to prevent them. And Navi Pillay makes the people who commit atrocities in them answer for what they did. Of the three, the committee has now honoured the one whose work cannot be bought, borrowed or framed in gold.

The Prize That Cannot Be Transferred

On December 10, 2026, the anniversary of Alfred Nobel’s death, Pillay will receive her medal in Oslo, along with a diploma and 12 million Swedish kronor. If precedent holds, she will give a lecture about law, which is the only subject she has ever really talked about.

Meanwhile, in Washington, there is a medal in a gold frame that the Nobel Institute has said, in writing, belongs to someone else for all time. It is a fitting object for this past year: a prize obtained as a gift, from a laureate who was then left outside her own country, displayed by a man whose administration spent the year sanctioning the institutions this year’s laureate built.

A medal can be handed across a desk. A career like Pillay’s cannot. It has to be earned slowly, case by case, over decades, in rooms where nobody is applauding. On Friday morning in Nuremberg, for once, the room broke into applause. It took sixty years.

From up here in Canada, a country that was also honoured this week when Anne Carson won the Nobel Prize in Literature on October 8, I find myself less interested in who did not win than in how deserving the winner is. The case for George Soros was always partly a case against something else. The case for Navi Pillay needs no opponent at all.

Postscript, Friday Donald Trump did, in the end, comment, or rather reshare. As Forbes reported on October 9, he amplified someone else’s post on Truth Social, which called Pillay an “unabashed hater of America and Israel.” The day before, he had told reporters it would be a “great discredit” to the committee if he did not win.

Within hours of the announcement, CTV News and The Hill reported, the administration imposed sweeping sanctions on the International Criminal Court itself, cutting the institution off from banking, insurance, software and other services. Secretary of State Marco Rubio explained the policy in a video statement: “Either the ICC will end its threats, or we will end the ICC.”

The committee said its prize was a message to those attacking international law. The message was received, and answered, the same day.

The medal declined to comment.
