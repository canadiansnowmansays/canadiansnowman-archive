Nine months ago I would have told you a war has a shape: it starts, it escalates, it ends, someone signs something. This war has stopped pretending to have one. It now has a heartbeat instead — strike, retaliate, pause, funeral, strike again — and in the space of a single week the heartbeat sped up enough that six countries felt it, an oil pumping station worker died for it, and it turned out the opening morning of the whole war had a second plot running underneath it that nobody outside two intelligence agencies knew about.

Let me be precise about what changed since Part LXXXVI, because precision is the only thing keeping this series honest. That installment closed with Khamenei’s coffin reaching Najaf and Trump declaring, from Ankara, that he thought the ceasefire was over. Everything below happened after that sentence was published.

I. The Silence Broke, and Said the Worst Thing

Since early March, Iranians have had exactly one way of knowing anything about their new supreme leader: written statements, issued under his name, arriving with no voice and no face attached to them. Mojtaba Khamenei did not appear at his own father’s funeral. He has not been seen in public since he was reportedly wounded on the war’s opening day, February 28. For four months, the most powerful office in Iran has been, functionally, a rumor, as Yeganeh Torbati reported for the New York Times in a piece on the leadership vacuum.

On Saturday, the silence finally produced content, and the content was a blood oath. “We pledge that we will avenge your pure blood,” he wrote of his father, “and the blood of all those martyred in these two wars from the criminal and disgraced killers.” He called it “the demand of our nation,” and insisted it “must certainly be carried out,” according to the Times’ live coverage of the U.S. strikes that weekend. By Monday, senior officials were putting names to the target list. Ali Bagheri Kani, deputy secretary of Iran’s Supreme National Security Council, called revenge “the right of the Iranian nation.” Ayatollah Mohsen Araki, a member of the assembly that appoints the supreme leader, called it a “binding command that must be obeyed” — and named Donald Trump and Benjamin Netanyahu as “top of the list,” per the Times’ Monday live updates on Iran’s escalating threats.

Read that against what came before it. Analysts who study Iran’s factional politics say Mojtaba’s only prior substantive statement — a hedge, agreeing to the ceasefire in practice while disagreeing with it “as a matter of principle” — left a gap wide enough for hardliners to drive a funeral procession through. They did. Red flags, a Shiite symbol of vengeance, filled the crowds at Khamenei’s burial, many marked with a phrase invoking the martyrdom of Imam Hussein, Neil MacFarquhar reported for the Times in his dispatch on Iran’s hard-line faction. By Saturday, the new supreme leader had adopted their language as his own first public words. By Monday, the regime’s senior clerics were doing the same, with a president’s name attached.

This is not a man running a government. It’s a man who has been governed into his first sentence by the loudest faction in the room — and the room is getting louder.

II. The Man Israel Tried to Install Instead

While all of that was unfolding in public, Mark Mazzetti, Julian E. Barnes, Farnaz Fassihi, and Ronen Bergman published an investigation in the Times this weekend that reframes the war’s opening morning entirely. For years, Israeli intelligence had been quietly grooming Mahmoud Ahmadinejad — Iran’s own former president, the man once synonymous with Holocaust denial and calls for Israel’s destruction — as an asset who could be installed as Iran’s next leader once the current regime fell.

The recruitment ran through academic conferences in Budapest that functioned as cover for meetings with Mossad operatives, including at least one personal visit from Israel’s own spy chief. Ahmadinejad, disillusioned after being repeatedly barred from running for president again, had reportedly told a small circle of confidants that if he returned to power with foreign help, he’d play a Yeltsin-style reformist role — and would normalize relations with Israel under the Abraham Accords framework.

The plan reached its moment on February 28, the war’s first morning. An Israeli strike hit the compound where Ahmadinejad’s bodyguards and vehicle were stationed. In the chaos, a black Peugeot — driven, according to American and Iranian officials cited in the investigation, by Mossad operatives — pulled him out and took him to a safe house inside Iran. And then the operation simply fell apart: Ahmadinejad, apparently rattled by the frantic extraction and disillusioned with what Israel actually intended for him, left the safe house under circumstances still unclear, and vanished from public view entirely — until he surfaced, briefly and without a word, in the crowd at Khamenei’s funeral procession last week, masked, flanked by what looked like security, staring at the ground.

He is now, according to four senior Iranian officials quoted in the piece, under house arrest, in the custody of the Revolutionary Guards’ own intelligence wing, which has spent the months since February piecing together the extent of his contact with Israel.

I want to sit with what this means for everything already in this series, rather than treat it as a curiosity. Every question I’ve been asking about who actually runs Iran right now — an absent supreme leader, a fractured cabinet, a hard-line faction filling the vacuum with revenge rhetoric — assumed the vacancy was accidental, a byproduct of decapitation strikes that simply hit their targets. This story says otherwise. Someone had a name ready to drop into that vacancy the moment it opened. The plan failed, but the fact that it existed at all tells you Israel didn’t just want Khamenei dead. It wanted a specific successor in the wings, and picked one of the most reviled men in the country’s modern history to play reformer. That the Guards Corps caught him before Israel could finish the job may be the single most consequential intelligence failure of this entire war — for Israel, not Iran.

III. Six Countries, A Water Station, and Sea Drones

The scale is worth sitting with before the politics. Since the heavy strikes resumed last week, U.S. Central Command has hit roughly 310 targets inside Iran, including a fresh wave Monday morning described only as “dozens of targets,” carried out for the first time using sea drones alongside fighter jets and naval vessels, according to the Times’ Monday live coverage of the escalating threats. Monday’s overnight strikes hit a water pumping station in Khuzestan province, killing one employee and shutting down a facility Iranian officials say is critical to regional agricultural irrigation — a strike on civilian infrastructure that lands uncomfortably close to the exact line Tom Nichols warned about in The Atlantic a week ago, in his piece arguing Iran, not Trump, controls this war’s pace.

Iran’s answer has been to widen the map, not narrow it. Over the course of the week, Tehran struck or claimed to strike American-linked targets in Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, Jordan, Oman, and the United Arab Emirates — six countries in a single stretch of days, as the Times’ live coverage of the U.S. strikes following the container ship attack detailed. Jordan intercepted four more missiles Monday alone. Bahrain declared its forces at “advanced combat readiness.”

Ship traffic through the strait, which had climbed to nearly 400 vessels in the week after the June ceasefire, was back down to 14 ships on Sunday — the lowest in a month, and a figure that makes one Kpler analyst’s verdict, reported by Jason Karaian and Lisa Friedman in the Times’ oil-market coverage, feel almost gentle: “We’re back to square one.” Brent crude closed near $79 a barrel Monday, 8 percent above its prewar price; American gasoline hit $3.87 a gallon, diesel $4.88. The oil market, per the same analyst, has essentially stopped reacting proportionally to any of this — prices now move more on rumors of negotiation than on actual strikes, which either means markets have priced in permanent instability, or they’ve simply stopped believing anyone can afford full-scale war.

IV. The Sentence Trump Signed That Broke Everything

Here is where the postscript to Part LXXXVI stopped being a theory and became the entire war, and where Edward Wong, Michael Crowley, and Eric Schmitt’s Times investigation into the failed strait agreement finally shows exactly how the trap was built — by both sides, with their eyes open, in a single paragraph of diplomatic language vague enough for each to sign it believing they’d won.

Paragraph 5 of the June memorandum promised Iran would use its “best efforts” for the safe, free passage of commercial ships for 60 days. Trump read that as Iran’s obligation to open the strait, and declared as much on social media: “Ships of the World, start your engines. Let the oil flow!” Iran read the same sentence, particularly its closing clause about Iran conducting “dialogue” with Oman on the strait’s “future administration,” as formal recognition that Tehran now holds a permanent seat at the head of the table. JD Vance, at the time, insisted Washington’s position would be locked into any future deal: “We have all the cards.” Iran’s negotiators apparently heard something closer to the opposite.

Neither reading was crazy. That’s the actual scandal here, more than any single strike: retired diplomats now openly say the ambiguity was foreseeable and, in Iran’s case, deliberately exploited. “No one should be surprised that Iran views that as explicitly giving them an enduring role controlling passage through Hormuz,” one former U.S. ambassador to Saudi Arabia told the reporters. Another called the document, bluntly, a formalization of the very leverage Iran had spent months building through mines and missile strikes — leverage Trump needed to trade away to relieve domestic pressure over gas prices, and did.

Washington’s own workaround — quiet U.S. Navy guidance routing tankers along Oman’s coast, more than 800 ships escorted since May, transponders switched off to dodge Iranian targeting — was never written into the agreement at all. It worked for exactly as long as Iran tolerated it. The moment Iran decided it wouldn’t anymore, there was no clause to point to, because Washington had negotiated the substance of the dispute and left the resolution of it to a radio channel.

Shipping numbers tell you who’s winning that argument. Prewar, Hormuz carried more than 130 vessels a day. As of Sunday: 14.

V. Whose Regime Is This, Anyway

The most useful thing anyone said last week wasn’t a threat, it was an admission. “We don’t really know who is running the regime,” one Iran scholar told MacFarquhar. “The system is changing; they need time to consolidate power.” A separate strategic read came from David E. Sanger’s Times analysis asking whether Trump has a “Plan C” for Iran at all, which quoted Vice President JD Vance’s blunt framing of the administration’s approach — “if they shoot at ships, we’re going to knock the hell out of them” — and diplomat Richard Haass calling the situation “a strategic dead-end.”

That’s the real story sitting underneath the missiles, and this week’s Ahmadinejad revelation only deepens it. Ali Khamenei’s death on day one of this war didn’t just remove a man, it removed the only mechanism Iran had for settling internal arguments — and, we now know, it very nearly opened the door to a foreign-groomed replacement before the Guards Corps slammed it shut. What’s left is a fight between a negotiating faction — Pezeshkian, Araghchi, both physically attacked by their own mourners, as detailed in the Times’ coverage of the mediation efforts by Adam Rasgon and Euan Ward — and a hard-line bloc now openly naming assassination targets in state media. One camp calls this a bargaining position. The other got a front-page newspaper editorial last week literally titled “We Want Trump’s Head,” per MacFarquhar’s reporting.

Iran’s government has been here before, and lost the argument to the hardliners before. When Obama’s nuclear deal collapsed under Trump’s 2018 withdrawal, it was the reformists who got blamed for having been “duped,” and the hardliners who consolidated power in the aftermath. If this ceasefire dies the same way, Iran has already shown you which faction inherits the wreckage — and this week showed you that faction now has both a body-double conspiracy to point to as proof of Western treachery, and specific names on its enemies list.

VII. Where Is the Off-Ramp, When Nobody Can Afford to Build One

I don’t think this is a war sleepwalking back into itself, the way Nancy A. Youssef, Jonathan Lemire, and Vivian Salama framed it in The Atlantic last week, quoting Iran scholar Vali Nasr’s warning that “we may sleepwalk our way back to war.” Sleepwalkers don’t declare the destination out loud. Iran has told you, in writing, twice now — once through its chief negotiator, once through its supreme leader — exactly what it wants: control of the strait, and blood for its dead, with a named list of whose blood. Washington has told you, through its defense secretary and its own president, exactly what it’s prepared to do about it, which is hit harder and hope that’s a strategy.

Neither side is sleepwalking. They’re just walking toward each other with their eyes open, each convinced the other one will blink first. Which raises the question I suspect is on more minds than mine this week: where, exactly, is the off-ramp supposed to come from, when both governments have a hard-line faction that reads any exit as surrender — and when, we now know, one side already tried and failed to simply replace the other’s leadership rather than negotiate with it at all?

Look at the symmetry the reporting keeps handing us, whether it means to or not. Iran’s hardliners are an estimated one-fifth of its population, and this week they got a newspaper editorial demanding Trump’s head onto a front page, and a supreme leader adopting their vengeance language as his first public sentence. Washington’s equivalent pressure runs through a president facing midterms in four months, whose own rhetoric about these same Iranian negotiators has swung from “very rational people” to “scum” inside a single month — a reversal Nichols dryly cataloged in his Atlantic piece — and a defense secretary who marks American foreign policy with five-word taunts on social media. Both governments are structurally rewarded for looking unyielding to an audience that treats compromise as weakness. Pezeshkian and Araghchi didn’t get pelted with rocks at a funeral procession for negotiating badly. They got pelted for negotiating at all.

Which means any real off-ramp can’t look like retreat to either domestic audience, because neither leadership can survive being seen taking one. It has to look like a win, or at least a draw, on both sides of the strait simultaneously — a much narrower door than the one either side is currently aiming for, and narrower still now that Iran’s suspicion of Western intentions has just been vindicated by an actual, documented regime-change plot.

If there’s a way through it, the analysts covering this have already sketched the outline, even if none of them will say it adds up to an actual plan. One professor’s observation, cited by MacFarquhar, is the sharpest of the lot: Iran’s hardliners aren’t ideologically incapable of accepting a deal, they’re doubtful anyone will actually honor one. A functioning economy grows their own constituency too. The problem isn’t that Tehran’s hard-liners hate prosperity; it’s that nobody in that government believes Washington delivers what it signs, and the oil waiver Trump revoked three weeks after granting it — to say nothing of a covert plan to replace their government entirely — is exactly the evidence they needed. The second possibility is less a negotiation than a waiting game: nobody currently knows who’s actually running the regime, and that opacity may only resolve once Mojtaba Khamenei — or whoever ends up actually holding power — is secure enough to make a decision without his own mourners turning on him for it. Given how the 2015 nuclear deal’s collapse strengthened hardliners rather than reformists last time, there’s no guarantee that consolidation ends up moving toward the negotiating table rather than away from it. And the third option isn’t a resolution at all, just a face-saving fiction — something like Oman’s proposal to jointly administer the strait, letting Tehran claim partial victory over Hormuz without either side formally admitting it blinked.

None of those are things a president or a supreme leader can simply announce on a Tuesday. What actually ends wars like this one, historically, isn’t a negotiated graceful exit — it’s exhaustion, a shock that scrambles the internal calculus on one side or the other, or enough time passing that the audiences watching for weakness simply stop watching as closely. One economist covering the oil markets, quoted by Karaian and Friedman, put the whole calculus in six words this week: the question is which cracks first. Nobody gets to schedule that either. Whoever’s still reading this series by the time we find out will know which it was.
