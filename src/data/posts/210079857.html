A hearing is a spectacle. A committee vote is a mechanism, and mechanisms are worth taking more seriously than spectacles, even when the two are staged by the same people for largely the same audience. One week after Anthony Fauci sat silent through three hours of hostile questioning, on Thursday, August 6, 2026, the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee convened again, and this time it did something with actual, if limited, legal consequence: it voted, along strict party lines, to hold him in contempt of Congress.

Senator Rand Paul framed the vote narrowly at the outset, insisting the committee was not relitigating anything Fauci had said from a podium years earlier, but deciding a single, specific question — whether a witness who has received the benefit of a federal pardon can simply refuse a committee’s direct order to answer and face no consequence for it. Senator Gary Peters of Michigan, the panel’s ranking Democrat, dissented in essentially the opposite direction, calling the whole process a rushed investigation conducted without meaningful Democratic input, and warning that punishing a witness for invoking a constitutional protection would discourage future witnesses from cooperating with the committee at all, regardless of party. Three separate Democratic motions to delay the vote — to send the question to the full Senate, to hear first from outside legal experts, to hear from Fauci’s own attorney, who had been physically removed from the previous week’s hearing before he could speak — were each defeated along the same party line that would go on to decide the vote itself.

It is worth being precise about what this vote actually accomplishes, because the theatrical framing on both sides tends to obscure how limited its practical force really is. A committee contempt resolution does not, by itself, refer anyone for prosecution; it merely clears the way for the committee to recommend that the Justice Department pursue the matter, a recommendation the department is entirely free to decline. For the resolution to carry the full weight of Congress behind it, the entire Senate would need to adopt it, a step that requires sixty votes under current rules. Republicans hold fifty-three Senate seats. Barring a wholesale defection of at least seven Democratic senators — a scenario with no plausible basis given how uniformly the party’s members have defended Fauci throughout this entire saga — full Senate adoption is close to impossible. Even in the unlikely event that it did pass, the resolution would be referred to the United States Attorney for the District of Columbia, who would then have to decide independently whether to bring the matter before a grand jury; a conviction for criminal contempt of Congress, the underlying misdemeanor at issue, carries fines and a prison term of up to a year, but only after a federal judge in Washington first rules on the antecedent question of whether Fauci was entitled to invoke the Fifth Amendment in the first place — the same unsettled constitutional question this essay turns to below. What Thursday’s vote actually produced, in other words, is a political marker rather than a genuine step toward prosecution: a formal, on-the-record declaration of the committee majority’s position, useful for campaign messaging and committee press releases, but almost certainly dead on arrival the moment it would need to travel any further than the committee room where it was passed.

That does not mean the broader investigation is winding down. If anything, the opposite: the night before the contempt vote, Senator Ron Johnson of Wisconsin, who chairs the Senate’s Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, announced that he had separately obtained Fauci’s government-issued cellphone, describing the previous week’s hearing as merely the beginning of what he called the accountability process rather than its conclusion. State-level investigations have opened as well, in Alabama, Louisiana, and Florida, whose officials each vowed to pursue their own inquiries in the days following Fauci’s Fifth Amendment testimony — a meaningful legal distinction from the federal proceedings, since Biden’s pre-emptive pardon covers only federal exposure and leaves state prosecutors free to pursue Fauci on entirely separate legal grounds, regardless of what Congress or the Justice Department ultimately decide to do with the contempt referral. An eighty-five-year-old man who has already spent one nearly three-hour session declining to answer a single question now faces the prospect of doing so again, potentially in front of multiple state legislatures and attorneys general, for the indefinite future.

Underneath all of this sits a genuinely unresolved constitutional question, and it deserves more careful treatment than either side of the current fight has given it, because the honest answer is that serious legal scholars disagree about it in good faith. Jonathan Turley, a constitutional law professor at George Washington University, has described the interaction between a presidential pardon and Fifth Amendment eligibility as murky territory with strikingly little governing case law, and has said there are credible arguments on both sides of Fauci’s specific situation — including, notably, an argument Fauci’s own defenders should take seriously rather than dismiss: that his opening statement at the hearing, in which he explained at some length his reasoning for declining to answer before formally invoking the privilege, could itself be read by a court as a waiver of the very protection he then claimed for everything that followed. Kimberly Wehle, a law professor at the University of Baltimore who studies the pardon power specifically, reaches the opposite conclusion, arguing that Fauci’s invocation was not merely permissible but the obviously correct legal strategy given what she describes as the transparent intent behind the investigation itself — a chairman who has spent years publicly promising to see the witness imprisoned is not conducting the kind of good-faith oversight that would make immunized, cooperative testimony a safe option, which is precisely the scenario the Fifth Amendment exists to protect against.

There is a real, direct precedent bearing on exactly this dispute, and Senator Lankford — the same senator whose measured questioning distinguished him from his own party’s leadership in the previous essay in this series — raised it himself at the close of last week’s hearing. In 2013, an IRS official named Lois Lerner testified before a House committee investigating whether the agency had improperly targeted conservative political groups for additional scrutiny; when pressed on whether her earlier testimony had been misleading, she declined to answer further and invoked her Fifth Amendment rights. House Republicans, arguing that her initial statement had effectively waived that protection, voted along party lines to hold her in contempt in 2014. In 2015, federal prosecutors declined to bring charges, explicitly rejecting the waiver argument House Republicans had built their case on. If that precedent controls here — and Lankford’s decision to raise it publicly suggests at least one Senate Republican thinks it might — Fauci’s own opening statement is unlikely to strip him of Fifth Amendment protection for what followed, whatever his questioners argued to the contrary in the room.

A different, less directly applicable precedent is worth noting for contrast, because it illustrates how much Fauci’s approach actually diverged from the alternative playbook available to him. Two former Trump administration officials, Stephen Bannon and Peter Navarro, each served four months in federal prison in 2024 after being convicted of contempt of Congress for defying subpoenas connected to the investigation into the January 6, 2021 attack on the Capitol. Their situation differed from Fauci’s in a crucial respect: rather than appearing under subpoena and invoking a constitutional privilege on the record, as Fauci did, Bannon and Navarro simply refused to show up at all, offering no legal mechanism a court could weigh against the government’s authority to compel testimony. That distinction is precisely why legal scholars surveyed on Fauci’s situation have generally treated his position as considerably stronger than either man’s turned out to be — a pardoned witness who appears, states his reasoning on the record, and invokes a specific constitutional right is playing a fundamentally different legal game than two men who did not show up to play at all.

Representative Jamie Raskin of Maryland, a former constitutional law professor now serving as the senior Democrat on the House Judiciary Committee, raised a related but distinct objection worth sitting with on its own terms, separate from the technical waiver question. Raskin’s argument is essentially about legitimacy rather than doctrine: that a legislator who has spent years publicly declaring he wants a witness imprisoned has, by that declaration alone, compromised his own standing to then insist that same witness answer questions about the very conduct he has already characterized as criminal. It is not, in Raskin’s telling, that Paul lacks the legal authority to seek the testimony. It is that seeking it while simultaneously broadcasting the desired outcome turns the hearing into something closer to a trial conducted backward — verdict first, testimony second — which is not what congressional oversight is supposed to be, whatever its formal legal boundaries technically permit. This series has made a version of that same argument about the hearing itself in the previous essay; Raskin’s framing gives it a sharper constitutional vocabulary than this series’ own analysis had reached for on its own.

Where all of this actually lands is uncertain, and honesty requires saying so plainly rather than predicting a tidy resolution that the current legal record does not support. Most scholars surveyed on the question, including both Turley and Wehle despite their disagreement about the underlying reasoning, believe that if the dispute ever reaches a federal court — which would require either full Senate adoption of the contempt resolution or a direct Justice Department decision to prosecute, neither of which currently looks likely — the courts would probably side with Fauci’s right to have invoked the privilege. Turley, notably, has been careful to distinguish that legal prediction from a judgment about wisdom, observing that having the right to do something is not the same question as whether it was the right thing to do — a distinction this series has tried to hold onto throughout, in both directions, refusing to let Fauci’s obvious legal justification erase the genuine communication failures that preceded it, and refusing to let those same failures justify a process this openly designed to produce a predetermined outcome. Wehle, for her part, has warned that a contrary ruling — one holding that Fauci could not properly invoke the Fifth given his pardon — would represent a genuinely alarming inversion of the amendment’s basic purpose, effectively telling every future witness that claiming the privilege carries its own separate risk of prosecution, which is close to the exact opposite of what the Fifth Amendment was designed to guarantee. That inversion would not stay confined to Fauci’s case either; any future pardoned official, in any future administration, would inherit the same precedent, which is part of why several scholars surveyed on the question described the stakes here as considerably larger than one 85-year-old man’s legal jeopardy, however much the hearing itself was staged as though his jeopardy were the only thing in the room worth discussing.

What remains, after the hearing and the contempt vote that followed it — a vote almost certainly headed nowhere procedurally, whatever its political messaging accomplished in the meantime — is the question this series set out to answer at the start: not whether Fauci is a saint or a villain, since he is neither, but what an honest accounting of his actual fifty-year record — the vasculitis breakthroughs, the AIDS-era reversal, the twenty-five million lives PEPFAR is credited with saving, the seven presidents who trusted him, the vanity, the school-closure discrepancy, and now the silence and the vote that followed it — actually adds up to. That accounting is where this series concludes.
