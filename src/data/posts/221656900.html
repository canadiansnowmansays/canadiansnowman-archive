Part 97 of this series covered Amodei’s September essay as a single event, and Part 98 covered Jensen Huang’s case against taking any of it seriously. This entry pulls back to the full body of work Amodei’s essay belongs to — a half-dozen pieces written over three years that, read in sequence, tell a different and more useful story than any one of them does alone. It also covers something neither of those two entries had room for: the world’s actual heads of state now convening to discuss what to do about all of it, and the first piece of real evidence this series has encountered that the utopian case might rest on more than rhetoric.

A rhetorical record that bends with whoever’s in power

As The New York Times’ David Streitfeld reported on September 17, 2026, Amodei’s public essays — measured, footnoted, closer to engineering memos than manifestos — trace a position on regulation that has moved considerably more than their tone would suggest. In January’s 20,000-word “The Adolescence of Technology,” he warned that regulations “can potentially destroy economic value or coerce unwilling actors who are sceptical of these risks (and there is some chance they are right!)” — a wariness toward government intervention about as plain as a Silicon Valley executive commits to paper. By June, after the Trump administration asked AI companies to voluntarily submit new models for review, his tone had reversed entirely: he called himself “grateful,” comparing AI products to airplanes whose release “should be blocked or reversed as a threat to public safety if they do not meet high standards of safety.” By September — the essay Part 97 already covered in full — he was proposing the industry pace itself unilaterally. An Anthropic spokesman told Streitfeld the company remains “very much in favour of regulation” but is “immediately implementing self-regulation” in its absence, a formulation that manages to endorse government oversight and explain why the company isn’t currently waiting for any, in the same sentence.

The pattern isn’t limited to regulation. In 2016, before Anthropic existed, Amodei co-wrote a paper called “Concrete Problems in AI Safety” that illustrated misalignment risk with a cleaning robot that, encountering a pet it had never seen, “attempts to wash with soap, leading to predictably bad results” — a small, almost quaint failure mode next to what this series has spent twenty parts documenting since. By April 2025’s “The Urgency of Interpretability,” the metaphor had scaled up considerably: “We can’t stop the bus, but we can steer it.” By January’s “Adolescence of Technology,” the framing had escalated again, into language this series’ own account of tech messianism in Part 91 would recognise immediately: AI as “a rite of passage, both turbulent and inevitable, which will test who we are as a species.”

The essay that explains the utopia nobody’s asking who pays for

Streitfeld’s most useful find, by his own account, is Amodei’s October 2024 essay “Machines of Loving Grace” — some 14,000 words making the positive case for AI that this series’ own largely accountability-focused coverage has had less occasion to examine directly. Its title, unattributed anywhere in the text beyond a footnote link, comes from a 1967 Richard Brautigan poem imagining a “cybernetic ecology” where humans are “joined back to nature... all watched over by machines of loving grace” — a poem literary critics still debate as sincere or tongue-in-cheek, written by a man who also produced, during the same Caltech residency, a poem beginning “I don’t care how God-damn smart these guys are: I’m bored,” and who killed himself in 1984. Amodei’s own use of it is unambiguously sincere. He describes near-term AI as “a country of geniuses in a datacenter,” promises freedom from disease as merely a starting point, and reaches for what he calls “biological freedom” — control over weight, physical appearance, reproduction, and personality itself, curing PTSD, depression, schizophrenia, and addiction as a matter of course, with the frank observation that “everyone can get their brain to behave a bit better and have a more fulfilling day-to-day experience.”

What Streitfeld notes is conspicuously absent from that vision is nearly as telling as what's in it: politics as a mechanism for distributing any of this, and a corporate world organised around profit rather than gift-giving, don't meaningfully appear. Healthcare companies, in Amodei's telling, are simply benign actors who will cure ailments as a public service. The real-world data point Streitfeld sets against that framing is direct: A.I. is already a documented driver of rising individual healthcare costs in the United States. The clearest measure of it came from the insurers. As Anthony Ha reported for TechCrunch on September 26, 2026, an analysis by the Blue Cross Blue Shield Association found that hospitals’ A.I. tools, used as claims are submitted, added $942 million to healthcare spending over two years, driven by "a sharp increase in patients being documented as having complex conditions" with "no evidence of corresponding change in care delivered." The gap between A.I. curing disease and A.I. helping bill more thoroughly for treating it is exactly the kind of distinction "Machines of Loving Grace" doesn’t pause to draw.

Streitfeld closes with a historical parallel this series hasn't previously had reason to reach for: the April 2, 1965 Time cover story, "The Cybernated Generation," whose language — "swept forward by a great wave of technology, of which the computer is the ultimate expression, human society is surely headed for some deep-reaching changes" — reads, six decades later, almost indistinguishable from Amodei's own essays. That prediction, Streitfeld notes dryly, didn't happen, at least not on the timeline or in the form 1965 imagined. Whether this decade's version fares any differently is the question this entire series exists to keep asking rather than answer in advance.

The first evidence the utopian case might be more than rhetoric

Everything this essay has covered so far, and nearly everything this series has covered since Part 93, treats Amodei’s positive vision as a claim awaiting evidence. The New York Times’ Teddy Rosenbluth, reporting September 17, 2026, supplies the first genuinely concrete data point this series has encountered on that question — not a company’s own marketing, but a peer-reviewed result. James Zou’s lab at Stanford has built what he calls a “virtual lab”: teams of AI agents, organised by specialty (immunology, computational biology, machine learning), that debate research directions with each other and generate testable hypotheses with a human scientist reviewing before anything is synthesized in the real world. In 2024, when Zou’s team tasked a swarm of these agents with redesigning part of a Covid-19 vaccine, the bots produced dozens of novel candidate proteins within days; two, when tested, actually worked. John Pak, the human researcher who ran the experiment, admitted he’d expected the exercise mainly to reveal the models’ limitations. “It was shocking,” he said.

The more recent result, published in Science, is a sharper test of the same premise. Tens of thousands of Zou’s agents analysed a protein called B7-H3, a known lung-cancer drug target, and independently recommended the treatment approach most likely to succeed in clinical trials. Weeks later, a major pharmaceutical company announced promising trial results from a drug using that same approach — work the agents could not have known about, since the underlying model’s training data ended in January 2025 and the system had no internet access during the exercise. Zou called it “a very nice, independent corroboration.” The caveats are real and worth stating alongside the result rather than after it: Dr. Charles Rudin of Memorial Sloan Kettering notes there’s no way yet to confirm the agents’ chosen approach is actually the best one, since no rival drugs targeting the same protein have completed trials; the system still can’t run physical lab experiments; and it remains unproven on a target that hasn’t already been extensively studied by humans, which is precisely the kind of target where the positive case for AI matters most.

What makes this genuinely different from the marketing material this series has spent its Machines of Loving Grace coverage sceptical of is the process Zou describes building around the agents, not just their output. The bots, left to their own devices, were initially too polite to each other to reach useful conclusions — a failure mode Zou’s team corrected by deliberately making the AI personas more confrontational and installing dedicated “devil’s advocate” agents whose job is to find flaws and flag danger before anything moves toward synthesis. That is a small, concrete answer to a question this series has asked in the abstract since Part 94: what does actual internal safety culture look like, rather than a company’s stated commitment to one. The whole exercise, door to door, took less than a day and cost about $46 in compute — a number worth sitting next to the trillion-dollar valuations this series has spent Part 97 documenting, if only to note how little relationship currently exists between what AI costs to do something genuinely useful and what investors are paying to own the companies doing it.

Anthropic, whose chief executive wrote the utopia, has since begun testing it in a wet lab of its own. On September 18, Engadget, following Reuters, reported that the company had quietly set up a biology laboratory in the San Francisco Bay Area; Eric Kauderer-Abrams, its head of life sciences, said that “to do biology, the final test is still and will be for a while in real lab work.” Five days later, as Julie Bort reported for TechCrunch, Anthropic announced that Claude, running roughly 950 agents for 21 hours, had identified a previously unknown enzyme system in bacteriophage DNA with what the company called “properties reminiscent of CRISPR.” The same caveats apply as to Zou’s work, and Amodei volunteered some of them: a Stanford team had earlier found “a system that is in some ways similar,” and “all of the lab work is performed by human scientists.” The lab handles only low-risk organisms and no human pathogens. TechCrunch noted that the wider research community has yet to validate its significance. Outside specialists were less impressed. As Carl Zimmer reported for The New York Times on September 27, 2026, Mario Rodríguez Mestre of the University of Copenhagen said of the enzymes, “They are the same systems we have been studying for years,” and questioned whether Claude had reasoned its way to the result or been guided there partly by his own work. It is still the closest Anthropic has come to putting a date and a protocol on “Machines of Loving Grace.”

The king who convened the meeting nobody elected him to hold

King Charles had announced that he would personally convene AI executives to discuss safety. As Adam Satariano reported in The New York Times on September 17, 2026, that meeting took place at Dumfries House, the headquarters of the king’s charitable foundation in Ayrshire, Scotland — the first international gathering of major AI companies since Amodei’s essay and the chorus of endorsement and pushback this series has covered at length across Parts 97 and 98. Executives from Nvidia, Google DeepMind, OpenAI, and Anthropic attended, alongside Kanishka Narayan, Britain’s AI minister; Demis Hassabis; and Sarah Friar, OpenAI’s chief financial officer. Anthropic declined to disclose its own attendees to The Times; TechCrunch‘s Dominic-Madori Davis reported that the company was represented by Tino Cuéllar, and that Jensen Huang attended for Nvidia. The palace was explicit that no binding agreements were expected, and the King himself left after about twenty minutes, leaving the executives and officials to carry on without him.

The King’s own prepared remarks are worth recording in full because they’re a genuinely unusual register for this series to quote — not an executive managing investor expectations, not a senator managing a primary challenge, but a head of state with no regulatory authority over any company in the room, framing the stakes in explicitly moral rather than competitive terms: “The decisions taken at this formative time will shape the world inherited by future generations.” His call was for a set of principles treating AI “not merely as a driver of capability and efficiency, but as a tool that upholds human dignity,” closing with a line that reads, against everything else this series has documented about Washington’s paralysis and Beijing’s adversarial posture, almost plaintive: “Those in our world who value our humanity and its vital moral component are anxiously seeking your reassurance that we will not lose control of our destiny.” He was blunter still about the warnings coming from inside the industry, noting that “those who have created these technologies are now increasingly warning that AI risks developing darker capacities,” and asking the room: “Surely, we need sufficient means of control before it’s all too late?” That a monarch with zero enforcement power is now the one supplying the moral framing no elected government in this series has managed to articulate is its own quiet verdict on the twenty-plus parts that came before this one.
