There is a particular kind of catastrophe that only centralized power can produce.

A drought kills people. A flood kills people. A war kills people in numbers that defy comprehension. But these are catastrophes that arrive from outside the human will, or from the collision of human wills in open conflict. The Great Leap Forward was different. It was a catastrophe produced entirely by a government’s decisions, sustained by a political system that made it impossible to stop, and invisible to the world for years because the system that created it also controlled all information about it.

Between 1959 and 1961, somewhere between fifteen and forty-five million people died in China. The range is that wide because the Chinese Communist Party has never conducted a full accounting, the archives that would allow one remain partially closed, and the scholars who have attempted estimates have arrived at different conclusions using different methodologies and different political assumptions about what the evidence requires. The most careful work — Frank Dikötter’s research in provincial archives, Jung Chang and Jon Halliday’s biographical excavations, Judith Banister’s demographic analysis — converges on a figure of roughly thirty million excess deaths, which is the number I will use here while acknowledging that the honest answer is: we do not know exactly, and the party prefers it that way.

Thirty million. In peacetime. In a country at peace with every neighbor, with no drought or flood or epidemic sufficient to explain the deaths independently. Killed by a government policy based on ideological enthusiasm, falsified statistics, institutional terror, and the structural impossibility of telling Mao Zedong that his plan was failing.

The policy had a name that carried its own retrospective irony: the Great Leap Forward.

Announced in 1958, it was Mao’s attempt to accelerate China’s transformation from an agricultural society to an industrial one through a single, overwhelming mobilisation of mass human energy. The underlying theory was Maoist rather than Marxist in the strict sense: Mao believed that will and revolutionary consciousness could substitute for capital, technology, and expertise. China was poor in capital and technology but rich in people — 600 million of them, organized into communes, capable of being directed by the party toward any goal the party chose. If the goal was steel production, they would produce steel. If the goal was grain, they would produce grain. The numbers could be whatever the party needed them to be, because the party would mobilise the will to achieve them.

The backyard steel campaign is the detail most Western accounts reach for, because it is simultaneously the most visible and the most absurd. Mao set a national target to double steel production in a year. The target was impossible through conventional means — China’s industrial capacity could not achieve it. So the campaign reached into the villages: every commune was to build a backyard furnace and contribute to national steel output. Peasants melted down whatever metal was available — cooking pots, farming tools, door hinges, ploughs, woks, the iron fittings from agricultural machinery — and fed it into furnaces built from clay and brick by people who had never seen a steel mill.

The output was, in metallurgical terms, useless. The small-scale furnaces could not reach temperatures sufficient for quality steel. What they produced was pig iron of such low quality that it could not be used for any industrial purpose. Across China, hundreds of millions of people spent weeks and months producing worthless metal while the agricultural harvest rotted in the fields, because the labour that should have been gathering it was tending furnaces instead.

But the statistics reported upward were not worthless. They were magnificent. Local officials, under extreme pressure to demonstrate the success of the Great Leap, reported output far beyond what their furnaces could possibly have produced. Those reports flowed upward through the party hierarchy, accumulating enthusiasm as they went. By the time they reached Beijing, they described a miracle of socialist construction. Mao received the miracle reports and ordered exports — China sent grain to the Soviet Union to service debts and to other countries as foreign aid, because the harvest statistics showed abundance.

The harvest statistics showed nothing of the kind.

The agricultural commune system compounded the disaster.

Collective farming — in which peasants worked communal land rather than their own plots — had been introduced in the early 1950s and had produced mediocre but manageable results. The Great Leap pushed collectivisation to its extreme: enormous “people’s communes” of tens of thousands of households replaced the smaller cooperatives, communal kitchens replaced private cooking, and individual initiative in farming decisions was replaced by party directives issued by officials who often had no agricultural experience.

The communal kitchens were, in the first months, genuinely popular. After years of scarcity, the sight of communal tables loaded with food — drawn from grain stores that had been accumulated over previous years — felt like the promised abundance of socialism made real. Peasants ate freely. The stores were consumed. When the harvest of 1959 fell short — as it did, partly because of drought, partly because of mismanagement, partly because the peasants working communal land had less incentive than those working their own plots — there was nothing to fall back on.

The grain had already been eaten at the communal tables. Or it had been reported upward as production that justified further export. Or it had been hidden by local officials protecting their quotas. Or it had rotted in transit because the transport infrastructure was overwhelmed. The causes were multiple and mutually reinforcing, which is the nature of systemic failure: it is not one thing going wrong but everything going wrong simultaneously in ways that prevent any single intervention from working.

What followed was starvation. Not the starvation of a country that has been stripped bare by an external enemy but the starvation of a country in which food existed somewhere — was being exported, in fact, in some quantities — but could not reach the people who were dying for want of it, because the system that should have recognized the crisis and redirected resources was instead generating false data that concealed it.

The concealment is the part of the Great Leap story that most directly connects to the present.

Peng Dehuai, the Defense Minister and a military hero of the Korean War, wrote a private letter to Mao in July 1959 expressing concern about the Great Leap’s failures — the waste of the steel campaign, the exaggerated production reports, the signs of food shortage in the countryside he had observed on a visit home to Hunan. The letter was measured, respectful, and factually accurate.

Mao had it circulated to all senior party members as an example of “right opportunism” — the sin of insufficient faith in the revolution. Peng was stripped of his positions, denounced at the Lushan Conference in a session that lasted days, and eventually sent to internal exile. The message to every other official in the party was precise: if Peng Dehuai, a Marshal of the People’s Liberation Army and a veteran of the Long March, could be destroyed for telling Mao that the plan was failing, what would happen to them if they did the same?

The answer was that they reported success. They reported it enthusiastically and in escalating numbers, competing with each other to demonstrate the most impressive results, because the alternative was to be the next Peng Dehuai.

What this produced, at the village level, was a bureaucratic horror that Frank Dikötter’s archival research has documented in granular detail. Local cadres who knew the harvest had failed were nonetheless required to meet grain quotas based on the inflated production figures they had previously reported. Meeting those quotas meant taking grain that villages needed to survive. Cadres who refused to extract sufficient grain were accused of protecting class enemies or lacking revolutionary commitment. Some extracted the grain and watched their neighbours starve. Some falsified the extraction records and faced punishment when the discrepancy was discovered. A few hid grain in barns, wells, or fields and were beaten or killed when informants reported them. The system had constructed a situation in which the rational choice for a local official was to take food from starving people, because the punishment for not taking it was potentially fatal and the punishment for the starvation it caused was invisible — the deaths were happening in villages, not in Beijing, and the statistics flowing upward showed abundant harvests.

The famine deepened. The reports of abundance multiplied. The gap between what the statistics described and what the countryside was experiencing widened until people were dying in provinces that had officially produced record harvests.

The system could not self-correct because self-correction required someone to tell the emperor his plan was failing, and the emperor had demonstrated, with perfect clarity, what he did to people who tried.

The deaths were not evenly distributed.

The famine fell hardest on the provinces with the most aggressive local officials — those who had reported the highest production figures and were therefore required to surrender the most grain to meet their quotas, leaving the least for their own populations. Anhui province, which had some of the most zealous local reporting, lost a higher proportion of its population than almost anywhere else. Some villages lost a third of their inhabitants. Entire communities were erased from the landscape, not by war or disease but by the intersection of false statistics and extracted grain.

The peasantry died disproportionately relative to urban workers. Urban populations received rationed food allocations that, while reduced, provided a baseline that prevented mass starvation in the cities. The people dying in the countryside were largely invisible to the urban party members who were generating and receiving the production statistics. They were also invisible to the outside world — China’s borders were closed, foreign journalists were absent, and the party’s information control was sufficiently complete that the scale of what was happening did not become internationally known until years later.

Some people knew. Provincial officials knew their production figures were false. Some local cadres tried to protect their villages by hiding grain or underreporting production, and were punished when discovered. Peasants who tried to flee famine areas were prevented by internal passport controls — the hukou system, which tied citizens to their registered place of residence, was enforced partly to prevent the kind of mass movement that would have made the famine visible. People died where they were registered to live, which is to say: where the system had placed them and could not afford to acknowledge they were starving.

The Cultural Revolution that followed the Great Leap Forward is sometimes analyzed as a separate episode — Mao’s second great policy catastrophe, distinct in its targets and methods from the first. In terms of mechanism, however, it is continuous. The Great Leap demonstrated that the system could not correct itself when Mao was wrong. The Cultural Revolution demonstrated that the system could not protect anyone — not even senior party officials who had built the revolution — from Mao’s suspicion.

The Cultural Revolution began in 1966 and will receive its own full treatment in Part VII. But its origin requires one sentence here: Mao launched it because Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping had successfully cleaned up the mess the Great Leap made, and their success demonstrated that pragmatic management worked where revolutionary mobilisation had killed thirty million people, and that demonstration was, in Mao’s analysis, ideologically threatening.

He was not wrong about the threat. He was catastrophically wrong about the cure.

What does the Great Leap Forward mean for the Long Return?

Xi Jinping’s government does not discuss it. The famine is not in the school curriculum in any detail that conveys its scale. The archives that would allow full accounting remain partially restricted. Chinese scholars who have attempted serious research on the period have found the space for honest inquiry narrow and narrowing further. The official party assessment, issued in 1981 and not substantially revised since, describes the Great Leap as a “serious mistake” for which Mao bore “chief responsibility” while maintaining that his overall historical contribution was “primary” and his errors “secondary.” The formula — seventy percent correct, thirty percent mistaken — was Mao’s own assessment of Stalin, applied posthumously to himself.

The formula is politically necessary and historically inadequate. A leader who kills thirty million of his own citizens in peacetime through preventable policy cannot be adequately described by a ratio. But the party cannot say that, because the party’s legitimacy rests on the revolutionary generation of which Mao was the undisputed leader, and delegitimising Mao delegitimises the revolution, and delegitimising the revolution delegitimises everything built since.

The compromise is silence. The Great Leap is not celebrated. It is not examined. It exists in the official record as a mistake that was corrected, full stop, with no investigation of why the system produced it or whether the system has been structurally changed to prevent recurrence.

It has not been. The system that prevented Peng Dehuai from telling Mao the truth in 1959 — the system in which honest assessment of a leader’s failures is treated as a political crime — is recognisably present in the system that suppressed Are You Dead? in 2026. The mechanism is identical. Only the stakes have changed.

Xi Jinping’s consolidation of power — the abolition of term limits, the elimination of institutional rivals, the tightening of ideological conformity — has reconstructed, in updated form, the conditions under which the Great Leap happened: a system in which the leader receives the information he wants rather than the information he needs. Whether this produces another catastrophe depends on whether the decisions being made are correct. The 141 pages may be correct. The rare earth strategy may succeed. The quantum networks may be built on schedule.

But if they are not — if the plan fails in ways that require honest assessment to correct — the system currently has no Peng Dehuai mechanism. It has the memory of what happened to Peng Dehuai, which is not the same thing.
