BREAKING NEWS: December 11-17, 2025

As I finalize this series, Congress is failing in real time.

December 11: The Senate voted on healthcare. Two bills. Both failed, each falling short of the 60 votes needed (NPR, “Competing health care plans fail in the Senate as ACA premium hikes loom”, December 11, 2025).

The Republican bill: expand Health Savings Accounts, let the enhanced ACA subsidies expire. Rejected.

The Democratic bill: extend the enhanced ACA subsidies for three years. Rejected.

But Senator Hawley, you voted yes on the extension. You, Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski and Dan Sullivan were the four Republicans who voted with Democrats to extend the subsidies.

December 16: Speaker Mike Johnson refused to let House Republicans vote on subsidy extensions. The House Rules Committee rejected amendments from GOP centrists who had spent days crafting compromise proposals with income limits and reforms (Axios, “GOP centrists help Dems defy Mike Johnson on ACA in stunning revolt”, December 17, 2025).

December 17 (yesterday): Four House Republicans defied their own Speaker. Reps. Brian Fitzpatrick (R-PA), Mike Lawler (R-NY), Ryan Mackenzie (R-PA) and Rob Bresnahan Jr. (R-PA) signed a Democratic discharge petition to force a floor vote on a three-year extension, bringing it to the 218 signatures needed. Hours later, Johnson’s own healthcare bill, which does not extend the subsidies, passed the House 216–211. It faces long odds in the Senate.

Fitzpatrick’s argument was simple: the only thing worse than a clean extension is letting the subsidies expire with no bridge at all.

The discharge petition can force a vote in January. By then, the subsidies will have expired.

One week. Three failures. Open rebellion in the House.

Inside the Capitol: Rep. Chip Roy (R-TX) called his own party’s bill “milquetoast garbage.” Speaker Johnson said Democrats broke the system fifteen years ago. President Trump has said, aboard Air Force One in November, that he wants the money sent to people instead of to “big, fat, bloated insurance companies” (Mediaite, “Reporter Presses Trump on Logic of Healthcare Idea: ‘Won’t Americans Buy It From Insurance Companies?’”, November 14, 2025).

In 14 days, on January 1, 2026, the roughly 22 million Americans who receive enhanced subsidies will see them shrink or disappear. KFF estimates that what subsidized enrollees pay in premiums will more than double, rising 114% on average (KFF, “ACA Marketplace Premium Payments Would More than Double on Average Next Year if Enhanced Premium Tax Credits Expire”, September 30, 2025).

Senator Hawley, here’s my question:

You voted to extend subsidies. Four of your House colleagues just defied their own Speaker to force a vote. That takes political courage.

But extending subsidies for three years only delays the crisis.

Will you put your name to reform that goes further?

You voted the right way on December 11. Now finish the job.

Senator Hawley, we need to talk.

On December 3, two weeks ago, you proposed the “No Taxes on Healthcare Act,” allowing Americans to deduct up to $25,000 per person in out-of-pocket healthcare costs, whether or not they itemize. You were right about the problem: American families are crushed by medical costs.

But your solution treats a symptom, not the disease.

Tax relief doesn’t address the fundamental problem: American healthcare costs $14,885 per person (OECD, 2024), roughly double what comparable wealthy countries pay. A deduction is worth most to people with the highest incomes and the most taxable income to deduct it from. A family earning $50,000 with $15,000 in medical bills gets far less from it than the bills cost.

But you saw the problem. You proposed something. You tried.

That’s more than most of your colleagues have done.

For nine parts, I’ve documented America’s 80-year healthcare failure:

  • Part 1: Your proposal and 80 years of failed reform attempts

  • Part 2: Nine years of Republican “concepts of a plan” and little delivery

  • Part 3: Where the money goes: roughly $2.4 trillion a year more than comparable countries spend

  • Part 4: Five countries that solved this using different models

  • Part 5: The bipartisan compromise nobody wants to embrace

  • Part 6: More than $700 million a year in lobbying

  • Part 7: Medicare, the public program that tens of millions of Americans rely on

  • Part 8: Red states refusing Medicaid expansion while residents pay the price

  • Part 9: The 10-year implementation roadmap

Now, Part 10: the bill.

Not concepts. Not principles. Not tax relief that helps around the margins.

A bill, in outline, ready for legislative counsel to draft.

Senator Hawley, you saw the problem and tried to help. You crossed party lines when others wouldn’t.

Here is something that goes further.

Introduce it, or tell us what you would introduce instead.

THE AMERICAN HEALTH SECURITY ACT

The full bill, section by section, with its costs and sources, is in the Code Blue Ledger. Every section has its own link, so a reader, a reporter or a congressional staffer can point to exactly the clause in question.

What follows is the summary.

What it is: a scaled-back bill that takes the steps already supported by members of both parties, pays for them with savings the Congressional Budget Office has scored, and keeps universal coverage as the long-term goal. Five titles, twelve sections.

What it is not: a finished statute, or a scored one. I am not legislative counsel. Every cost below is an estimate drawn from CBO scores of similar provisions, and the ledger says which.

Title I · Keeping people covered

  • Restore the enhanced premium tax credits for three years (about +$80.6 billion over ten years; 3–4 million more people insured, using CBO’s estimate for the House’s three-year extension).

  • Restore the incentive for states that newly expand Medicaid, which the 2025 reconciliation law repealed (about +$14 billion).

  • Pre-filled marketplace applications, and a demonstration of automatic enrolment (not yet scored).

Title II · Honest prices

  • Write hospital and insurer price transparency into law, as the House Energy and Commerce Committee voted 45–0 to do in July 2026 (about −$2.2 billion).

  • Pay hospital outpatient departments the same as doctors’ offices for routine services Medicare covers (”site-neutral” payment), with rural hospitals exempt (up to −$156.9 billion, CBO).

  • End surprise bills from ground ambulances (not scored).

Title III · Drug prices

  • Ban “spread pricing” by pharmacy benefit managers in Medicaid (about −$1.1 billion).

  • Protect the existing Medicare drug negotiation schedule.

Title IV · Workforce

  • 14,000 new Medicare-funded residency positions, prioritising rural and primary care (roughly +$25 billion).

Title V · Accountability

  • Public reporting on coverage, prices and the 2025 Medicaid changes.

  • A pay-as-you-go safeguard: the new spending takes effect only if CBO certifies the savings cover it.

Indicative ten-year result: about $120 billion in costs against about $160 billion in savings. That is roughly deficit-neutral, before an official score.

I want to be plain about two things. First, almost all of the savings come from one provision, site-neutral payment, and hospitals will fight it hard. Second, this bill does not achieve universal coverage on its own. The bigger steps (a public option, Medicare at 60, a cap on out-of-pocket costs, Swiss-style regulated prices) are set out in the ledger under “Going further”, with what each would cost and what could pay for it.

THE CHALLENGE

Senator Hawley, this bill gives you what your own proposal reached for: lower costs for families, paid for by making the system charge less, not by borrowing more.

To Democrats: it gives you the subsidy extension your party has been fighting for, now with Republican votes behind it, and a renewed push on Medicaid expansion.

It does this while:

  • Keeping private insurance

  • Adding no new mandates

  • Paying for itself, by design, with savings CBO has already scored

This is the compromise described in Part 5, in its first, achievable form:

  • Republicans get: market-based price reforms, transparency, no new taxes

  • Democrats get: subsidies restored, Medicaid expansion encouraged, surprise bills ended

  • Americans get: lower premiums, honest prices, more doctors

Members who have championed parts of it: none of them has endorsed this bill, but each has publicly backed one of its pieces.

  • Premium tax credits: Sens. Lisa Blunt Rochester, Ron Wyden and Maria Cantwell; the four House Republicans who signed the discharge petition.

  • Price transparency: Reps. Brett Guthrie and Frank Pallone.

  • Site-neutral payment: Sens. Bill Cassidy and Maggie Hassan.

  • Residency positions: Sens. Raphael Warnock and John Boozman; Reps. Terri Sewell and Brian Fitzpatrick.

THE DARE

Senator Hawley, you proposed healthcare tax relief because you saw families crushed by medical costs.

Here is a bill. Twelve sections. Honestly costed. Paid for by design.

Now answer these questions:

1. Will you introduce it, or something like it?

If yes: you’ll be the Republican who moved healthcare forward instead of just promising to.

If no: why not? Each of its pieces has already won support from members of both parties.

2. Will you demand a vote?

If your leadership won’t schedule one, will you do what Fitzpatrick, Lawler, Mackenzie and Bresnahan did, and force it?

3. Will you campaign on this?

Will you hold town halls in Missouri and ask your constituents: “Should we keep paying $14,885 per person, when Switzerland pays about $9,963 and covers everyone?”

4. If you won’t introduce this bill, what’s your alternative?

“Concepts of a plan” isn’t an answer.

You proposed tax relief. Here is reform. Introduce it, or tell us what you would do instead.

THE FINAL WORD

Senator Hawley, you proposed tax relief because you saw the problem.

Congress has not fixed that problem, and the reason isn’t that it’s impossible. It isn’t untested, and it isn’t that Americans oppose it. Congress has lacked the courage.

Switzerland shows universal coverage can work with private insurance. Germany shows it can work with competing non-profit funds and regulated prices. Medicare shows a public program can cover tens of millions of Americans. Canada shows single-payer can work across a vast country. Singapore shows it can work with personal savings accounts.

The solutions exist. The models work, imperfectly. The bill is outlined.

The only question is whether anyone in Congress will introduce it.

You proposed tax relief, Senator Hawley. You started this conversation.

Now finish it.

Introduce the American Health Security Act. Force the vote. Make Congress choose on the record:

  • Lower premiums, or another expiry?

  • Honest prices, or the status quo?

  • A paid-for bill, or another round of “concepts”?

History is watching.

And so are 340 million Americans who deserve better than “concepts of a plan.”
