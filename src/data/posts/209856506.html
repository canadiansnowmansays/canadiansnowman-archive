Postmark: Toronto Western Hospital. An operating room, June 2026. A robotic arm, a membrane ten times thicker than the thread meant to cross it, and a patient controlling a cursor within the hour.

Part V of this series was called The Hands That Never Shake, and it ended at the edge of a frontier the da Vinci system and its rivals had not yet crossed: the human brain, still largely reserved for the steadiest human hands neurosurgery could produce, still requiring an incision measured in centimetres to reach tissue that is measured in millimetres of consequence. Part XV carried that frontier further, into an operating room in La Jolla where a teleoperated humanoid removed a gallbladder from a living patient with no human hands at the table. This postcard goes further still, into the one part of the body every surgical robot has approached with the most caution of all — and reports a change so specific, so narrow, and so consequential that it may turn out to be the single most important surgical robotics milestone this series has yet filed.

On June 30, at Toronto Western Hospital, a surgical robot pushed an electrode thread straight through a patient’s dura mater — the tough membrane that has protected the human brain from the moment surgeons first learned to open a skull — without removing so much as a sliver of it first. The patient was controlling a cursor with their thoughts within the hour. The company that built the robot is Neuralink. The procedure it eliminated, called a durectomy, has been one of the most delicate and dreaded steps in neurosurgery since the discipline began.

The armor and the needle

To understand why this matters, you have to understand what the dura actually is, and why surgeons have never had any choice but to cut it.

The dura mater is the brain’s outermost protective layer — a tough, fibrous membrane that Neuralink’s own engineers describe, without much exaggeration, as the brain’s armor. It is more than ten times thicker than the electrode threads the company’s implants use, threads so fine that each one is thinner than a single human hair. Beneath the dura, the brain itself is in constant motion, pulsing with every heartbeat and shifting with every breath, and the membrane conceals blood vessels that a misplaced needle could sever with consequences no surgeon wants to contemplate. For as long as anyone has performed brain surgery, the answer to this problem has been the same: cut the armor open, do the delicate work beneath it, close the armor back up. The durectomy is not a formality. It is one of the most demanding manual steps in the entire procedure, and every prior Neuralink implant, like every conventional neurosurgical intervention before it, required one.

As Neuralink’s engineers explained on June 30, achieving a transdural insertion — passing the electrode threads directly through the intact membrane rather than opening it — required extensive preclinical work developing new imaging and insertion techniques capable of guiding a needle thinner than a hair through armor ten times its thickness, without full vision of what lay on the other side. The company’s own internal description of the design philosophy behind this work is worth quoting because it is, in miniature, the entire argument of this postcard: the best step is no step. Every step you can remove from a surgical procedure is a step that cannot go wrong, cannot add time, cannot require a scalpel a patient’s body will need to heal around afterward. Eliminating the durectomy did not just make the procedure gentler. It made the procedure structurally different — shorter, with a smaller incision, and, critically, simple enough that automating it becomes a realistic engineering target rather than a science-fiction aspiration.

The Toronto connection

The hospital where this happened is not incidental to the story, and a Canadian readership should know its full shape. As University Health Network’s own announcement recorded, Toronto Western Hospital was selected in November 2024 as the exclusive Canadian site for Neuralink’s CAN-PRIME clinical trial, with Health Canada approval to recruit up to six participants living with cervical spinal cord injury or ALS. The hospital performed Canada’s first Neuralink implant surgeries in late August and early September of 2025, led by neurosurgeon Dr. Andres Lozano — and both of those first two Canadian patients, worth noting for anyone still picturing brain surgery as a multi-day hospital ordeal, were discharged the following morning. The June 30 transdural procedure was performed at the same site, with Dr. Lozano again at the table, guiding the same robotic system through a technique it had never before attempted in a living human being.

This is the same city, the same hospital lineage in spirit, that Part II of this series found building Canadarm — a country whose contribution to the most consequential engineering projects of a given era tends to arrive quietly, at the technical core of the thing rather than in the press conference announcing it. Canada did not build Neuralink’s robot. But the first human being anywhere to have an electrode threaded through an intact dura did so on a table in Toronto, under a Canadian neurosurgeon’s hands, inside a Health Canada-approved trial — a detail that belongs in the same file as the Hansen mission and the MDA clean room, filed under the same quiet national habit of being present at the technical centre of a story the world will remember by someone else’s name.

The robot doing the threading

The machine performing this work is Neuralink’s R1 surgical robot, and it is worth pausing on what it actually does, because the word “robot” undersells the precision involved. As one detailed technical account explains, the R1 uses machine vision to guide each electrode thread — one at a time, sixty-four threads per implant, distributing 1,024 electrodes across a coin-sized device roughly twenty-three millimetres across — while actively steering around blood vessels it can only partially see through the tissue above them. Transdural insertion makes the robot’s job harder in one specific sense: it must now thread a membrane it previously bypassed entirely, guided by imaging techniques built specifically to see through tissue no scalpel has opened. And it makes the job simpler in the sense that matters most for the industrial ambition behind it: the surgeon no longer performs the single most skill-dependent manual step in the whole procedure, which means a machine, rather than a specific surgeon’s specific years of training, is now responsible for the hardest part.

This is the through-line connecting this postcard to Part XV’s surgical humanoid and to every surgical robot this series has examined since Part V. The da Vinci system filtered a surgeon’s tremor but left every decision, every incision, entirely in human hands. The teleoperated humanoids of La Jolla put a robot’s body between the surgeon’s hands and the patient’s tissue, with a human still driving every motion. The R1, threading through an intact dura, is doing something the earlier machines were not yet asked to do: performing, with its own machine vision and its own steering judgment, the literal hardest step of an entire surgical category — while a human surgeon supervises rather than directly guides each millimetre.

The scaling ambition, stated plainly

Why does any of this matter beyond the individual patients it has helped? As Musk stated publicly on December 31, 2025, Neuralink’s explicit goal for 2026 was high-volume production of brain-computer interface devices paired with what he called an almost entirely automated surgical procedure — and the transdural technique is, by the company’s own account, the change that makes that ambition structurally possible rather than merely rhetorical. The previous surgical workflow required a scalp incision, a craniectomy to remove a disc of skull, a durectomy to expose the cortex, robotic thread insertion, and closure — a sequence with at least two steps, the craniectomy and the durectomy, that depend on a human surgeon’s judgment and cannot easily be reduced to a repeatable, machine-executable protocol. Eliminating the durectomy does not merely shorten that list. It removes precisely the step that stood between a bespoke neurosurgical procedure and a manufacturable, scalable clinical product — which is the difference, in Neuralink’s own stated arithmetic, between the dozens of patients implanted so far and the thousands Musk has floated as a production target.

The company’s next-generation robot, announced separately in May 2026, can now place electrode threads into virtually any region of the brain — a capability that moves the technology’s ambition well beyond restoring motor function in paralysis and ALS, toward Parkinson’s disease, treatment-resistant epilepsy, and depression that has not responded to any other intervention. Devices have now been implanted in patients across the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and the UAE. None of this is approved for anything beyond investigational use; the company is candid on this point, and so should this postcard be.

The company that is not alone

Neuralink’s transdural milestone should not be read as a story about a single company solving brain surgery unaided, because it is entering a competitive field, and the shape of that competition tells its own story about where this technology is actually headed.

Synchron, a rival brain-computer interface company, has pursued an entirely different surgical philosophy: rather than opening any part of the skull at all, its Stentrode device is threaded through the patient’s blood vessels — inserted via a catheter in the neck, the same minimally invasive approach cardiologists use to place a coronary stent, and guided up into a vein that runs alongside the brain’s motor cortex. Synchron’s method avoids brain surgery in the conventional sense entirely, at the cost of a real trade-off this postcard should state plainly: reading neural signals through a blood vessel wall captures a coarser, lower-resolution picture of brain activity than an electrode planted directly in cortical tissue. Neuralink’s transdural technique sits, quite literally, in the middle of the two approaches other companies have taken — more invasive than Synchron’s vascular route, but now meaningfully less invasive than the direct-implant methods every brain-computer interface company, including Neuralink itself, used until this year. Three different philosophies, three different bets on where the line between safety and signal quality should sit, all racing toward the same eventual market: restoring capability to people whose bodies no longer follow their intentions.

The honest limits

No dispatch in this series has earned the right to report only the triumph, and this one is no exception. As the same reporting on the June 30 procedure noted, Neuralink has been candid about earlier setbacks, including thread-retraction issues that emerged in its first American patient — a reminder that an electrode thinner than a hair, anchored in tissue that moves with every heartbeat, is not a permanently solved engineering problem merely because a milestone has been achieved. Dural surgery carries well-documented risks, including cerebrospinal fluid leakage and, in more severe cases, herniation — precisely the failure modes the transdural technique is designed to reduce rather than eliminate outright, and long-term outcomes for the participants in this newest procedure remain, honestly, still to be seen. A single successful transdural insertion is a genuine and significant milestone. It is not yet evidence that the technique is reliable across the scale of thousands of patients the company has set as its goal, any more than one gallbladder removed from one pig in La Jolla proved that teleoperated humanoids are ready for a human operating room. The pattern holds across every surgical robotics postcard this series has filed: the demonstration is real, and the distance from demonstration to routine, reliable, at-scale practice is measured in years of unglamorous validation work, not headlines.

What the intact membrane means

From the balcony, this postcard belongs to the deepest and most literal version of the question this series has been asking since Part V: what does it mean for a machine to work this closely with the human body, and what do we owe the people who let it?

There is something almost paradoxical in the achievement this postcard describes. The advance is not that the robot did more. It is that the robot, and the surgeon supervising it, did less — one fewer incision, one fewer manual step, one fewer places for something to go wrong. Every other postcard in this series’ Theatre territory has been a story about a machine adding capability: the da Vinci’s tremor filtering, the humanoid’s teleoperated hands reaching into an operating room a specialist could not otherwise attend. This is a story about a machine subtracting risk — proving that the bravest thing a surgical robot can sometimes do is find a way not to cut at all.

A Canadian reads this postcard with a particular attentiveness, because the patients who benefit most immediately from this technology — people living with ALS or cervical spinal cord injury, for whom a working cursor restored within an hour of surgery is not a curiosity but the return of a capacity for communication and control that illness had taken away — are exactly the population this series’ companion coverage of elder care and rehabilitation has kept returning to. The needle that doesn’t cut arrived, this time, in a hospital a short drive from where Canadarm was first designed, threaded by a Canadian neurosurgeon’s hands through a membrane that had never before been asked to stay whole.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one arrived without leaving a scar.
