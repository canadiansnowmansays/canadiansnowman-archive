On the morning of August 6, 1945, the United States dropped an atomic bomb on the Japanese city of Hiroshima, killing between 70,000 and 80,000 people instantly. Three days later it dropped a second bomb on Nagasaki, killing perhaps 40,000 more. On September 2, 1945, Japan surrendered unconditionally aboard the USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay, and the most destructive war in human history was over.

The United States emerged from that war in a position of dominance so extraordinary that it had no historical precedent and no ideological framework adequate to describe it. It had not sought this position. It had not planned for it. And many of its most senior leaders were genuinely uncertain what to do with it.

This is the story of how America accidentally inherited the world — and what it did with that inheritance during the brief, remarkable decades when it got the answer mostly right.

The Numbers That Made a Superpower

To understand 1945, you have to understand the numbers.

The United States entered the Second World War with a GDP of approximately $1.1 trillion in 2020 dollars. It ended the war with a GDP of roughly $2.2 trillion — its economy had doubled in four years while fighting the most expensive war in human history. Every other major combatant nation had been economically devastated. The Soviet Union had lost 27 million people and vast swaths of its industrial capacity. Britain was technically victorious but financially ruined — it would spend the next decade rationing food and dismantling its empire because it could no longer afford either. France, Germany, Japan, Italy: rubble, occupation, reconstruction. China: civil war. The colonial world: the beginning of the end of European empire.

Only the United States had emerged from the war economically stronger than it entered. And not marginally stronger — transformatively stronger. By 1945, the United States accounted for approximately half of the world’s entire economic output. It held two-thirds of the world’s gold reserves. Its manufacturing capacity had been so dramatically expanded by wartime production that it was producing more steel, more aluminum, more aircraft, more ships, more vehicles than the rest of the world combined.

It also had the bomb. The only bomb. No other nation would test a nuclear weapon until the Soviet Union in 1949. For four years, the United States held a monopoly on the most destructive force in human history.

This was not, to use the term loosely, a modest position.

The Reluctance Was Real

But here is what the mythology of American power consistently obscures: the United States did not want this.

Not in the sense that American leaders were self-effacing or indifferent to power. Roosevelt was a supremely confident leader who understood American strength and was entirely willing to use it. Truman was a more modest man but no less decisive. The generals and admirals and industrial titans who had prosecuted the war were not shrinking from their country’s new position.

What they were reluctant about was the specific form of international responsibility that the moment demanded — the long-term, institutionalized, rule-bound engagement with world affairs that would require America to subordinate at least some of its freedom of action to collective frameworks. The America that emerged from the Second World War was deeply ambivalent about entanglement. It had fought two world wars in thirty years from which it had expected to remain aloof. The isolationist tradition — the belief that America’s geographic position and democratic character made it essentially different from the European powers and their perpetual conflicts — was not dead in 1945. It was wounded, discredited by Pearl Harbor and the Holocaust, but it retained significant political force.

Senator Arthur Vandenberg of Michigan — the most important Republican internationalist of the era — had been an isolationist until December 7, 1941. His conversion to internationalism was genuine but conditional: he supported American engagement with the world so long as that engagement was structured through multilateral institutions that would give Congress and the American public some confidence that the United States was not simply becoming another imperial power. Without that framework, the isolationists would return.

This political constraint shaped everything the Truman administration built in the late 1940s. The United Nations, NATO, the Marshall Plan, the Bretton Woods institutions — all of them had to be designed in ways that were defensible to a skeptical American public and a Congress that had no intention of writing blank checks for global empire. They had to look, and to some significant extent actually be, something other than American imperialism wearing multilateral clothing.

The Bretton Woods Conference, July 1944 — the gathering at the Mount Washington Hotel in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire

That constraint, imposed by domestic politics, turned out to be the most important design feature of the post-war order. The institutions that resulted were not perfect instruments of American power. They were messy compromises that genuinely constrained American freedom of action while distributing enough benefits to other nations that those nations had a stake in maintaining them. The imperfections were features, not bugs. They were what made the order legitimate.

What Harry Truman Understood

Harry Truman was not an intellectual. He had no graduate degree, had not traveled widely before entering politics, and was widely regarded — including by himself, initially — as inadequate to the office he inherited when Roosevelt died in April 1945. He was a machine politician from Missouri, a failed haberdasher who had clawed his way up through the Democratic Party of one of America’s most corrupt urban political organizations.

He was also, as it turned out, exactly the kind of president that 1945 required.

Truman understood power practically rather than theoretically. He understood that the Soviet Union was going to be a serious long-term problem — he arrived at this conclusion faster than many of his more sophisticated advisers. He understood that a devastated Europe was a vulnerable Europe, and that a vulnerable Europe was a Europe susceptible to communist movements that the Soviet Union would be delighted to sponsor. He understood that American security could not be guaranteed by American isolation — that the world was now too interconnected, American economic interests too widely distributed, for the United States to simply withdraw behind its oceans.

But he also understood something more subtle and more important: that American power would only be sustainable if other nations chose to align with it rather than resist it. The United States could not police the entire world by force. It could not afford to. It could not sustain the domestic political support for such an enterprise. And even if it could, the attempt would produce exactly the kind of resentment and resistance that would make American primacy fragile and temporary.

What it needed was a different kind of primacy — one that other nations supported because it served their interests, one that was embedded in institutions and norms that they had helped design and that constrained American behavior along with everyone else’s. Primacy through architecture rather than primacy through force.

This was not altruism. It was the most sophisticated form of self-interest: the recognition that durable power requires legitimacy, and legitimacy requires consent.

Dean Acheson — Truman’s Secretary of State and perhaps the most consequential architect of the post-war order — expressed this with characteristic directness in his memoir. The United States had to be, he said, “present at the creation” of a new international system. Not because American values demanded it. Because American interests required it. A world of stable, prosperous democracies — even if those democracies sometimes disagreed with American policy — was infinitely preferable to a world of unstable, impoverished states susceptible to Soviet influence.

The Marshall Plan, announced in June 1947, was the purest expression of this logic. $13 billion in American economic assistance to the devastated economies of Western Europe — approximately $150 billion in 2024 dollars. Not charity. Investment. Investment in the customers who would buy American goods, the allies who would share the costs of containment, the democracies who would legitimize American leadership by choosing to follow it.

US aid to Greece under the Marshall Plan

It was the most effective strategic investment in American history. The countries that received Marshall Plan aid became, within a generation, the prosperous democracies that constituted the core of the Western alliance. West Germany — occupied enemy in 1945 — became America’s most important continental ally within fifteen years. Japan — object of nuclear devastation in August 1945 — became the anchor of American strategy in Asia within a decade. These transformations did not happen because of American military power alone. They happened because American economic and institutional investment made prosperity possible and aligned the interests of the reconstructed nations with the continuation of American-led order.

The Canadian View From the Table

Hugh Keenleyside watched all of this from a particular vantage point — close enough to understand it in detail, independent enough to see it clearly.

Canada in 1945 was the third-largest allied power, a status it had earned through extraordinary wartime contribution and would rapidly lose as American and Soviet dominance reasserted itself in the peace. It had mobilized over a million men and women for military service — remarkable for a country of 12 million — and its industrial production had been critical to allied success. It had also developed, through the wartime experience of intimate cooperation with both the United States and Britain, a sophisticated understanding of how great power relationships actually worked.

Canadian diplomats of Keenleyside’s generation occupied a peculiar but valuable position in the emerging order. They were close allies of the United States — geographically inseparable, economically intertwined, culturally overlapping — but they were not Americans. They could engage with American power from the inside without being of it. They could advocate for multilateral constraints on that power without being dismissed as adversaries. And they had a specific interest in doing so: a Canada that simply deferred to American power was a Canada that had no independent foreign policy, no independent voice, and no protection against the inevitable moments when American interests and Canadian interests diverged.

The Canadian formula — developed in the late 1940s and refined through Keenleyside’s generation and those that followed — was to support American leadership while insisting on the multilateral framework that gave that leadership legitimacy. Not because Canada was idealistic about multilateralism. Because a world organized around American power alone, without institutional constraints, was a world in which Canada had no leverage. The United Nations, NATO, the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade — these were not just good ideas. They were the mechanisms through which Canada could influence a superpower it could not otherwise constrain.

This is the Canadian insight that is most relevant to the world of 2026: that small and medium powers have an overwhelming interest in maintaining the rules-based international order, because that order is the only thing that gives them protection against the arbitrary exercise of great power. When the United States abandons the order it built, it is not just engaging in strategic miscalculation. It is destroying the framework that protects its smaller allies — including Canada — from the kind of power politics that great powers always win.

Trump’s tariffs on Canada. His suggestion that Canada might become the 51st state. His casual willingness to threaten allies and reward adversaries. These are not aberrations from the American-led order. They are what the American-led order was specifically designed to prevent — the return of great power coercion as the organizing principle of international relations.

Keenleyside understood in the 1940s why that mattered. His successors are relearning it in the hardest possible way.

The Architecture Begins

Harry Truman signing the North Atlantic Treaty, April 4, 1949 — the moment the United States committed itself permanently to European collective defense

Between 1944 and 1949, the United States presided over the construction of an international order of extraordinary ambition and surprising durability. The Bretton Woods Conference of July 1944 — held in a hotel in the New Hampshire mountains while the war was still being fought — created the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank, establishing the dollar as the world’s reserve currency and creating institutional frameworks for international financial cooperation. The San Francisco Conference of April-June 1945 produced the United Nations Charter — signed by 50 nations, the largest gathering of sovereign states in history to that point. The Marshall Plan of 1947-1951 rebuilt Western European economies and created the material foundation for the Western alliance. NATO, signed in April 1949, committed the United States to the collective defense of Western Europe for the first time in its history.

Each of these achievements required the United States to accept constraints it would not otherwise have chosen. The IMF and World Bank operated under governance structures that gave other nations formal voice in decisions. The United Nations Security Council gave veto power to four other nations — including the Soviet Union, America’s primary adversary. NATO committed the United States to go to war if Belgium or Luxembourg was attacked — an extraordinary limitation on American freedom of action, accepted because the strategic benefits of the commitment outweighed the costs of the constraint.

These were not small concessions. They reflected a genuine strategic judgment — shared by Truman, Acheson, Marshall, Vandenberg, and the architects of the order — that durable American leadership required institutional legitimacy, and institutional legitimacy required genuine constraints on American power.

This judgment was correct. It produced the longest period of great-power peace in recorded history. It produced the most rapid and widespread improvement in human living standards in recorded history. It produced, for all its failures and contradictions, something that humanity had never previously managed: an international order that other nations defended as their own rather than resisted as an imposition.

It also, as we are now discovering, could be dismantled by a single American administration that simply decided the constraints were inconvenient.

What 1945 Tells Us About 2026

The men and women who built the post-war order were not naive. They had lived through the consequences of the alternative. They had watched the 1930s — American isolationism, the failure of collective security, the appeasement of aggressors — produce the worst catastrophe in human history. They built the post-war order with that catastrophe in their living memory, as a direct response to it.

Their insight was simple and profound: unconstrained great power competition leads to war. The only alternative is a system of rules, institutions, and norms that gives great powers incentives to cooperate rather than conflict — incentives powerful enough to survive the inevitable moments of friction and mistrust.

The American-led order they built was not that system in its ideal form. It was that system as it could actually be constructed, by fallible human beings working under political constraints, in the specific historical conditions of 1945. It had failures, hypocrisies, and contradictions. It excluded the Soviet Union and China from its core architecture, making them adversaries rather than stakeholders. It tolerated colonialism and supported authoritarian regimes when anti-communism demanded it. It was, in many ways, a system designed to benefit the United States and its closest allies at the expense of the Global South.

But it was also, genuinely and consequentially, better than what came before it and better than what is replacing it. The absence of great-power war since 1945 is not coincidental. The extraordinary expansion of global trade and prosperity since 1945 is not coincidental. The spread of democratic governance from a handful of countries in 1945 to a majority of the world’s nations by the end of the twentieth century is not coincidental. These achievements happened within the framework of the American-led order, and they are now at risk as that framework collapses.

Pete Hegseth told reporters on March 31, 2026 that America negotiates with bombs. He said it as if it were a statement of strength. Hugh Keenleyside’s generation would have recognized it as a confession of failure — the admission that the most powerful nation in history had abandoned the tools of legitimate leadership and retreated to the only instrument that does not require allies, does not require consent, and does not require the hard, patient work of institution-building.

Force. The oldest and least durable form of power.

The world that Keenleyside helped build in 1945 was built precisely to supersede that logic. We are now watching what happens when it returns.
