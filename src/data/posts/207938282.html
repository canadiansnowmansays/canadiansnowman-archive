Stephen Gordon Jul 28, 2026

Eighteen months ago, Canada faced a 25 per cent tariff and answered with a speech people still remember. This week, Canada faced a 50 per cent tariff and answered with silence that lasted a day. I don’t think that comparison is an accident of personality. I think it’s the whole story of this trade war, told through two men who never once had to face the other’s version of the fight.

I. February 2025 — The Opening Shot

The first round came fast and blunt: 25 per cent on Canadian exports, 10 per cent on energy, effective within days. Trudeau, weeks from resigning and with nothing left to spend politically, met it with the loudest response this series will probably ever quote. He spoke straight to Americans — Normandy, Kandahar, Katrina, September 11th, the JFK line about geography making neighbors and necessity making allies — and then turned to Canadians with a mobilization order: check the labels, buy Canadian, skip the Florida orange juice, “opt for Canadian rye over Kentucky bourbon.” Canada answered dollar-for-dollar, $155 billion in retaliatory tariffs staged over three weeks, framed explicitly as “Team Canada at its best.” It worked, in the sense that matters least and most at once — Canadians rallied hard, boycotts stuck, and Trudeau’s Liberals, who’d been heading for electoral wipeout, handed the job to someone else within weeks anyway.

II. March–June 2025 — The Banker Learns to Fold Fast

Carney inherited the file almost immediately, campaigning explicitly on crisis-management credentials rather than rhetoric — two G7 central banks, no elected experience, a pitch built entirely on temperament. His first real test came fast: Ontario’s one-day electricity surcharge, which rattled Washington within hours and was suspended just as fast, in exchange for what Ford called an “olive branch” phone call. This series has already made the case, in Part XVIII, that standing that lever down was the wrong call.

It wasn’t the only fast retreat that spring. In late June, with Canada’s digital services tax — a 3 per cent levy on revenue tech giants like Google, Meta and Amazon earned from Canadian users, retroactive to 2022 — set to begin collecting payments the next morning, Trump called it “a direct and blatant attack on our Country” and announced he was terminating all trade talks with Canada over the weekend. Canada rescinded the tax less than forty-eight hours later, one day before the first payments were due. The White House’s own read on it afterward wasn’t subtle: officials said Canada had “caved.”

Two retreats in four months, both under direct, sharp, public pressure, both reversed inside a day or two. Where Trudeau’s instinct was to escalate loudly and let Canadians feel the fight, Carney’s instinct, from his very first months, was to de-escalate quickly whenever Washington applied a hard enough shove — and hope the calm bought something later.

III. Through 2025 — Warmth, and a Widening Gap Between Tone and Terms

What followed for the rest of the year was, by every account, a genuinely warmer relationship at the top. Carney told Trump directly that Canada was “not for sale,” standing beside him, both men complimenting each other minutes earlier — the line delivered as a fact of real estate, not a rebuke. Researchers started calling him a “Trump whisperer.”

The liquor ban stayed in place, quietly doing more economic damage than any speech, while Carney avoided anything resembling Trudeau’s fire. A former Trudeau advisor offered the honest caveat that undercuts an easy reading of all this warmth: relationship quality is “frankly not the most important” factor in how a trade dispute resolves, and Trump was still criticizing Canada’s negotiating strategy in nearly identical terms to how he’d criticized Trudeau and Chrystia Freeland. He’d just started liking the man doing the negotiating.

IV. The One Time He Wasn’t Calm

It’s worth noting Carney did have one moment that actually looked like Trudeau’s register. At Davos on January 20, 2026, Carney delivered a real broadside: without naming Trump, he warned that the post-Cold War order had suffered “a rupture, not a transition,” and told the room that middle powers “are not at the table, you are on the menu” unless they band together. He got a standing ovation. Commentators reached for Churchill. It was, by any measure, fire.

Trump’s retaliation was real and fast: he called the speech “ungrateful,” revoked Canada’s invitation to his so-called Board of Peace, and threatened a 100 per cent tariff if Ottawa concluded a trade deal with China. Six days later, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told Fox News that Carney had gotten Trump on the phone and “aggressively” walked the Davos comments back.

Carney denied it flatly, and in public, the very next morning. “To be absolutely clear, and I said this to the president, I meant what I said in Davos,” he told reporters on Parliament Hill — and when asked directly whether he’d walked anything back, he said simply, “No.” He also didn’t let Bessent’s dig about technocrats-turned-politicians pass unanswered.

That complicates the tidy version of this comparison I initially reached for. Davos wasn’t a case of Carney’s one lapse into fire getting punished into a retraction — it was Washington claiming a retraction that Carney refused to give, even after real retaliation landed on the invitation list and the tariff threat sheet. Compare that to the Oval Office three months earlier, where Carney was just as firm on substance — “not for sale,” the 51st-state talk called “not useful” — delivered, as the Washington Post put it, with “a mixture of flattery and firmness,” and Trump’s reply was a shrug rather than a rebuke. Two different moments, two different kinds of firm: one that needed to be defended publicly against a hostile characterization, one that never drew a characterization worth disputing at all.

V. July 2026 — The Tariff That Doubled

Which brings this series back to where Parts XVII and XVIII left it. This week’s order — 50 per cent on more than 500 goods worth roughly $20 billion, about 2 per cent of the full $720-billion trading relationship, effective August 19, no USMCA exemption — is double the rate Trudeau faced in February 2025. Carney, true to the pattern this series has tracked since Part XV, took a full day before his office issued anything. Ford, not Carney, produced the fastest reaction: tariff for tariff, dollar for dollar. When Carney did speak, on Tuesday, it was in his now-familiar verbal register: he’d spoken with Trump, the two had agreed to “deepen and speed up” talks, but he was only interested in a “comprehensive agreement,” not a partial one. On the alcohol file specifically — the one this series has tracked since Part XVI — Carney made the provinces’ position explicit: restocking American liquor “should only be taken, in my judgment, as part of an overall agreement,” not as a concession offered up front.

But the written statement his office released was sharper than the verbal one, and worth reading in full rather than paraphrasing. It invoked “threats to Canadian sovereignty” directly, framed Canada’s retaliation as lawful rather than escalatory — “as is its right, has merely matched those measures” — and flipped the pain narrative back onto Washington, noting the dispute “has raised costs for families, particularly in the U.S.” It closed with language that reads less like Oval Office flattery-and-firmness than like something closer to Trudeau’s register: Canada would “work relentlessly and take any measures necessary.” The full statement:

VI. The Lever Ottawa Says It Won’t Pull

Part XVIII made the case that Ontario’s electricity surcharge was the one measure that ever rattled Washington fast, and argued it should come back. It’s worth engaging honestly with why it hasn’t — and the reasoning turns out to be more considered than mere timidity. Reporting on this file has surfaced the government’s actual rationale directly: Ottawa has discussed cutting electricity exports, along with export tariffs on oil and potash, but has deliberately avoided it because it doesn’t want to escalate, and specifically doesn’t want to hurt ordinary Americans in states that depend on Canadian hydropower. That’s a real, defensible position, not an oversight — Carney’s whole approach has been to negotiate without escalating, on the theory that punishing American households is a different act than punishing American policy.

Whether that theory is working is genuinely contested at home. Pierre Poilievre, the Conservative leader, has been blunt about it: “Mark Carney was wrong to surrender Canada’s leverage for over a year and a half, without actually working to secure the deal he promised.” That’s close to Part XVIII’s own argument, delivered by the leader of the opposition rather than this series.

But the polling cuts the other way. Abacus Data found that while six in ten Canadians consider a deal urgent, only one in five want Canada making compromises to get there faster — and pollster David Coletto reads that as Carney having “a lot of room to maneuver” rather than pressure to fold. Canadians appear to have moved from wanting speed to distrusting any quick deal as likely to reward bad behavior. If that holds, that’s public backing for exactly the kind of slow, disciplined posture this series has been describing: hold the rhetorical line, don’t escalate materially, wait out the clock. Whether hydro exports ever factor into it may depend less on economic logic than on whether the government believes Canadians want that kind of escalation at all — and right now, the polling suggests they might not.

VII. What the Comparison Actually Shows

Here’s where I land, for whatever it’s worth, though I’ll admit plenty of reasonable people will land elsewhere. Line up the confirmed record and the pattern is narrower than I first thought, and more interesting for it: Carney has folded fast, twice, when the pressure was concrete and economic — the electricity surcharge gone in a day, the digital services tax rescinded in under forty-eight hours with the White House calling it a cave. But when the pressure was rhetorical and reputational rather than economic — Davos, with a revoked invitation and a tariff threat as the price of standing firm — Carney held, publicly, on the record, against a sitting U.S. Treasury Secretary’s contrary account. That’s a real distinction: this isn’t a man who folds at any pressure. It’s a man who folds specifically when the cost is measured in dollars and holds when the cost is measured in words — with one exception worth naming: the electricity lever, which he’s declined to use not because it would cost Canada, but because of the cost to ordinary Americans on the other end of the wire. The tariff rate itself is the evidence that holding on rhetoric hasn’t been enough on its own: it’s only gone up on Carney’s watch, doubling from Trudeau’s 25 per cent to this week’s 50.

VIII. The Open Question

So I’ll leave you with the version of this I can’t fully resolve myself. Is picking your battles this precisely — hold on principle, fold on the balance sheet, spare the lever that would hurt ordinary Americans — the more mature strategy, the kind that spends political capital only where it can’t be measured in dollars, and that Canadians themselves, by Abacus’s numbers, appear to be backing? Or has something been lost in a pattern where the concrete, material fights are the ones Canada keeps losing fast, while the rhetorical wins pile up in transcripts nobody in Washington has to act on? I know which moment moved me more when I watched it. I’m considerably less sure which prime minister is actually winning — and I suspect that’s a question this series’ readers will answer differently depending on what they think winning even looks like here.

But there’s a version of this that doesn’t require picking a side between fire and calm at all, and it’s the one this series made the case for last week. Tone was never really the variable that moved Washington. Leverage was — and this series has only ever found one measure that rattled the White House inside a single day: Ontario’s electricity surcharge, suspended in March 2025 and never reinstated, deliberately, out of a stated wish not to hurt ordinary Americans rather than any failure to notice the tool was sitting there. Trudeau had fire without that leverage. Carney has calm, and a clear reason for not using it. Neither has stopped the number from climbing. If there’s a lesson sitting underneath this whole comparison, it might be that the fight was never really about which prime minister has the better voice. It’s about which one is finally willing to put the meter back on — and whether Canadians, watching the numbers keep going up anyway, decide they want that lever pulled after all.
