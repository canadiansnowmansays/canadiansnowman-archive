Part II of this series left Solomon’s kingdom fractured after his death, its united monarchy split permanently between a northern Israel and a southern Judah within a single generation of his own reign. What Part II didn’t have room to trace is where the Solomonic legend itself went afterward, because it did not stay contained within the borders of ancient Israel and Judah at all. In the highlands of what is now Ethiopia, a dynasty arose that claimed direct, unbroken descent from Solomon himself, through a son born, according to national legend, of Solomon’s meeting with the Queen of Sheba — the very meeting Part II mentioned briefly as evidence of Solomon’s international reputation, but which Ethiopian tradition remembers as considerably more consequential than a diplomatic visit. That claim of continuity, arguably the single longest and most audacious dynastic lineage claim examined anywhere in this series, survived for the better part of three thousand years, weathered foreign invasion and colonial occupation, and was finally extinguished not by a rival claimant to the same throne, but by a Marxist military committee that had no interest in claiming any throne at all.

The foundational text of this claim is a work called the Kebra Nagast, the Glory of Kings, compiled in its surviving form sometime around the fourteenth century CE though drawing on considerably older oral and written traditions, which tells the story of Makeda, the Queen of Sheba, traveling to Jerusalem to test Solomon’s legendary wisdom, exactly as the biblical account in Part II described. Ethiopian tradition extends this encounter considerably further than the biblical text does, recounting that Solomon, through a mixture of hospitality and deliberate stratagem, ensured that Makeda would spend a night in his palace and share his bed, and that she returned to her own kingdom pregnant with a son, Menelik, who would later travel to Jerusalem himself as a young man to meet his father and, according to the most theologically consequential part of the legend, return to Ethiopia carrying the Ark of the Covenant itself — the same Ark this series noted in Part II as the object David moved to Jerusalem to bind religious and political authority to his new capital. Ethiopian tradition holds that this original Ark still rests today in a guarded chapel in the town of Axum, watched over by a single dedicated guardian monk who, according to that same tradition, will not permit any other living person to see it, a claim that has made verification of any kind impossible and, whether by design or accident, has only deepened the legend’s mystique across the centuries since.

Whatever the Kebra Nagast’s basis in verifiable history — and this series notes the same caution here that any serious historian would apply to a foundational royal legend serving an obvious and enormously convenient political purpose — its political function is unmistakable and directly comparable to the legitimating stories examined throughout this series. A dynasty claiming descent from Solomon himself was claiming, in effect, the same divine covenant Part II traced through Nathan’s promise to David: an eternal throne, underwritten not by any local conquest or administrative achievement but by a bloodline running directly back to the single most legendarily wise and divinely favored king in the entire biblical tradition. This gave Ethiopia’s rulers something almost no other monarchy examined in this series could claim quite so specifically — legitimacy imported wholesale from an entirely separate civilization’s most celebrated royal lineage, adapted and localized so completely that it became, over the following centuries, as central to Ethiopian national identity as any homegrown legitimating story this series has examined anywhere else.

The dynasty’s actual historical continuity is considerably messier than the unbroken three-thousand-year narrative suggests, and it’s worth being honest about the gap, because it mirrors a pattern this series has already seen in China’s dynastic cycle: legitimacy claims surviving political rupture by reasserting themselves on the other side of it. Between roughly the tenth and thirteenth centuries CE, the Solomonic line was actually displaced by a separate dynasty, the Zagwe, whose own most celebrated ruler, Lalibela, is remembered today primarily for commissioning a set of extraordinary churches carved directly downward out of solid volcanic rock rather than built upward from a foundation — structures that remain, to this day, among the most remarkable pieces of religious architecture examined anywhere in this series. The Zagwe dynasty was itself eventually overthrown in 1270 by a claimant, Yekuno Amlak, who asserted a restored connection to the original Solomonic bloodline, and Ethiopian court chroniclers, in a maneuver this series has now seen repeated across multiple civilizations in slightly different costume, simply folded the Zagwe interregnum into a larger narrative of the Solomonic line’s temporary eclipse and eventual restoration, rather than treating it as a genuine break in dynastic continuity. From 1270 onward, the restored Solomonic dynasty held the Ethiopian throne, with only intermittent interruption, for nearly seven more centuries.

The dynasty’s international standing by the early twentieth century owed a great deal to an earlier ruler’s military achievement that this series should not pass over, because it explains why Ethiopia entered the era of European colonial partition from a position of strength almost no other African kingdom could match. In 1896, Emperor Menelik II — who took his regnal name deliberately to invoke the legendary son of Solomon and Sheba, reasserting the Solomonic claim at a moment of genuine national crisis — led Ethiopian forces to a decisive victory over an invading Italian colonial army at the Battle of Adwa, inflicting a defeat so complete that Italy was forced to formally recognize Ethiopian independence in the treaty that followed. Adwa made Ethiopia the only African state to successfully repel a full-scale European colonial conquest attempt during the continent-wide partition of the late nineteenth century, a fact that gave the Solomonic dynasty a unique claim to legitimacy grounded not just in ancient legend but in a very recent and thoroughly documented military triumph, and explains why the Italian invasion this series will discuss shortly, some four decades later under Mussolini, carried such a specific weight of revenge for Rome as well as raw colonial ambition.

The dynasty’s final and most globally famous ruler, Haile Selassie, ascended to the regency in 1916 and was formally crowned emperor in 1930 under the full title Negusa Nagast, King of Kings, and Conquering Lion of the Tribe of Judah — a title that made the dynasty’s Solomonic and biblical claims as explicit as any monarchy examined in this entire series has ever stated them in a formal coronation. Haile Selassie proved a genuinely consequential modernizing ruler by the standards of early twentieth-century African monarchy, introducing Ethiopia’s first written constitution in 1931 — a document that, while still concentrating substantial authority in the emperor’s own hands, formally established a bicameral parliament and a written framework of government law where none had existed before — expanding secular education, abolishing the legal status of slavery that had persisted in parts of the country, and positioning Ethiopia, one of only two African nations never formally colonized by a European power, as an increasingly prominent voice on the international stage, including founding membership in both the League of Nations and, decades later, the United Nations and the Organization of African Unity, the latter of which he helped establish and host in Addis Ababa in 1963. His most memorable moment on that stage came in 1936, following Italy’s invasion and brief occupation of Ethiopia under Mussolini’s fascist government, when Haile Selassie traveled personally to Geneva to address the League of Nations, delivering a speech warning that the international community’s failure to meaningfully sanction Italian aggression against a fellow member state would be remembered as a fatal precedent for exactly the kind of unchecked territorial conquest that would engulf the wider world within a few years — a warning that proved, in retrospect, considerably more prescient than the League’s own members were prepared to act on at the time.

It is worth pausing here on an entirely unexpected afterlife Haile Selassie’s reign produced, one with no real precedent anywhere else in this series: in Jamaica, in the years following his 1930 coronation, a new religious movement arose that identified Haile Selassie not merely as a legitimate earthly monarch but as the literal return of Jesus Christ, drawing on his coronation title as Conquering Lion of the Tribe of Judah and on a specific reading of biblical prophecy that anticipated a Black African king’s return as a messianic figure. This movement, which became known as Rastafari, spread from Jamaica across the Caribbean and eventually much of the world, its adherents regarding Haile Selassie throughout his life as a divine figure entirely independent of, and considerably more theologically elevated than, his own actual political self-presentation as a devout member of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church, which regarded any claim to his personal divinity as doctrinally mistaken. Haile Selassie’s own recorded reactions to the movement that had deified him were reportedly bemused rather than encouraging, and his state visit to Jamaica on the twenty-first of April, 1966, during which enormous and ecstatic Rastafarian crowds reportedly numbering over a hundred thousand people greeted him at the airport in weather so charged with anticipation that a sudden rainstorm broke the moment his plane touched down — an event Rastafarian tradition still commemorates annually as Grounation Day — remains one of the more genuinely singular moments in this series’ entire survey of monarchy. It is worth being clear about what actually happened next: accounts describe the emperor initially reluctant even to disembark given the sheer size and fervor of the crowd, eventually persuaded to appear by a respected Rastafarian elder invited aboard the aircraft to reassure him, and the visit closing without Haile Selassie ever personally endorsing the theological claims being made about him. It stands nonetheless as a reigning king confronted, in real time and in a country he did not himself rule, with a mass religious movement that had already decided, independent of his own wishes on the matter, that he was considerably more than merely a king.

The end of Haile Selassie’s reign, and with it the end of a claimed three-thousand-year dynastic line, arrived with a speed and a bleakness that stands in sharp contrast to the centuries of legend that preceded it. By the early 1970s, Haile Selassie, by then in his eighties, presided over a government increasingly disconnected from the realities facing ordinary Ethiopians, most catastrophically during a severe famine that struck the northern Wollo province beginning in 1972 and killed an estimated one hundred thousand people or more over the following two years, a crisis the imperial government’s own censorship and denial did enormous additional damage by concealing from both the Ethiopian public and the international community for as long as it possibly could. When the scale of the famine and the government’s concealment of it finally became public knowledge, combined with rising military discontent over pay and conditions and broader dissatisfaction with the aging emperor’s increasingly disconnected rule, a coordinating committee of junior military officers, which came to be known as the Derg — simply the Amharic word for committee — began systematically stripping Haile Selassie of his practical authority through 1974, arresting his ministers one by one and gradually assuming control of the machinery of government while the emperor himself remained, for months, formally on the throne but functionally powerless.

The Derg formally deposed Haile Selassie on the twelfth of September, 1974, ending, in a single bloodless afternoon, a dynastic claim its own adherents had traced back three millennia to a meeting between an Israelite king and a Sheban queen. Haile Selassie was placed under house arrest, and this series’ account of what happened to him afterward reflects the genuine uncertainty that has surrounded his death ever since: the Derg initially announced, in August of 1975, that the eighty-three-year-old former emperor had died of natural causes related to complications from a prostate surgery, but persistent testimony from former Derg officials in later years, including an account from a former palace guard alleging Haile Selassie was smothered on the personal order of the regime’s eventual strongman, Mengistu Haile Mariam, has left the precise circumstances of his death formally unresolved even decades later; his remains were not given a proper burial until 2000, when they were discovered beneath a palace latrine and finally reinterred with full royal and religious honors following the fall of the Marxist regime that had deposed him.

The Derg’s own subsequent rule, a brutal Marxist-Leninist military dictatorship responsible for a campaign of political terror against its own population known as the Red Terror, along with further catastrophic famines during the 1980s that drew unprecedented international humanitarian attention, offers this series one final, bitterly ironic data point on the question of legitimacy this entire project keeps returning to: a regime that overthrew an ancient monarchy in the explicit name of delivering the Ethiopian people from feudal exploitation and toward a more just and equal society went on to preside over human suffering, on a raw numerical scale, considerably worse than anything documented under the dynasty it replaced — a reminder that the story a new regime tells about why it deserves to rule, however sincerely believed by its own adherents at the moment of revolution, offers no actual guarantee whatsoever about what that regime will do with the power it has just seized.

What Ethiopia’s Solomonic dynasty leaves this series with, more than any other case examined so far, is the clearest possible demonstration of a legitimating story’s outer limit: a bloodline claim imported from an entirely separate civilization’s most celebrated king, maintained with genuine sincerity and real political effectiveness across nearly three thousand years of recorded and legendary history, surviving foreign invasion, a two-century dynastic interregnum, and colonial occupation — and still, in the end, proving no match whatsoever for a famine its own government tried to hide, and a committee of junior officers who had stopped believing the story entirely. Ethiopia’s story stands as this series’ longest-running legitimacy claim and one of its shortest final collapses, the entire three-thousand-year edifice brought down not by a rival king with a competing claim to the same sacred lineage, but by men who had simply decided the lineage no longer mattered at all.

Part XII turns from a single royal bloodline’s claim of biblical descent to an entirely different model of African legitimacy altogether — kingdoms whose authority rested not on imported scripture but on physical, tangible objects believed to carry the sanctioned spirit of the state itself, a golden stool among the Ashanti, a royal spear among the Zulu, and bronze plaques recording centuries of royal history in a kingdom the Portuguese, on first encountering it, could hardly believe was not itself a match for any court in Europe.
