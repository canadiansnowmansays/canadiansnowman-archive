The four erasures before this one were of things — a memorial, a museum’s exhibits, the signs on the trails, the records on the servers. This one is older and, in Orwell’s scheme, more fundamental. It is the erasure of a person: the deliberate removal of an individual from the official account, so that the record no longer shows they were there.

Orwell had a word for it. To be unpersoned was to be not merely punished but unmade — your name struck from the rolls, your photograph cut from the archive, your existence quietly subtracted from the past until no document admitted you had ever held your post. Winston Smith unpersoned people for a living. A man named Comrade Withers, once a Party dignitary, is mentioned in the novel as having become “an unperson”: he “did not exist: he had never existed.” Winston’s task was to rewrite an old speech so that the vanished official was replaced by an invented hero, and the substitution left seamless.

I want to show you the American version, because it happened on the first day, in the first hours, and it came with a detail so exact that it could have been lifted from the book: after they took the man down, they patched the wall.

Two Hours

On January 10, 2025, in an ornate Pentagon corridor lined with the portraits of every chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff going back to Omar Bradley, the newest painting was unveiled. It was the official portrait of General Mark Milley, who had served as chairman under both Trump and Biden, at a ceremony attended by Milley himself and the sitting Secretary of Defense. Nineteen predecessors on the wall; Milley the twentieth, the latest link in an unbroken line the corridor existed to display.

The portrait hung for ten days.

On January 20, President Trump was inaugurated at noon. By two o’clock that afternoon — within two hours of the oath — reporters inside the Pentagon noticed that Milley’s portrait was gone from the wall. Not relocated with ceremony, not sent to a museum with a plaque. Simply removed, on orders from the White House, its whereabouts, one official later said, unknown. The New York Times called the move unprecedented. The line of chairmen now had a gap in it, at the most recent link.

And then came the detail that makes this a Ministry of Memory story and not merely a story about a grudge. The next day, reporters watched workers patching the holes and repainting the wall where the portrait had hung.

Consider what that second act accomplishes, above and beyond the first. Removing the portrait subtracts the man. Spackling the nail holes and repainting the plaster subtracts the removal — it erases the erasing. A visitor walking that corridor a week later would not see a conspicuous blank where a portrait had been hastily pulled; they would see an unbroken wall, smooth and freshly painted, as though the twentieth chairman had never been hung there at all. This is the difference between throwing a book on the fire in the town square and quietly returning the shelf to a state in which the book was never catalogued. The first is punishment. The second is unpersoning. Milley did not exist on that wall; he had never existed there.

Not One Man

If it were only Milley, it would be a vendetta, and this series would leave it to others. Trump’s resentment of Milley was long and public — he had called him a “moron,” had suggested on social media that the general’s contacts with China amounted to treason, had floated, in 2023, that the punishment for such treason was death. Biden, in his last hours in office, issued Milley a preemptive pardon precisely because he expected retaliation. A single erased portrait, in that light, is a personal score settled with government property.

But it was not only Milley. In the same stretch of days, the Pentagon removed the official portrait of former Defense Secretary Mark Esper as well — another official who had broken with Trump. Defense Secretary Hegseth stripped Milley of his security clearance and his protective detail, and convened a review board to consider demoting him in retirement, which would cut his rank and his pension — reducing not just his image on the wall but his official standing in the record, retroactively lowering what the man had been. A congresswoman, watching, called it what it plainly was: the president’s petty grievances, dressed as military housekeeping.

That phrase — the grievance dressed as housekeeping — is the through-line of this entire series, and here it reaches the individual. At the Smithsonian the housekeeping was “restoring the exhibit to 2008.” In the parks it was flagging signs as “concerning.” In the data it was “compliance.” Here it is a review board and a maintenance crew. The euphemism scales down to fit a single human being: we are not erasing a general who crossed the president; we are conducting a routine review, and repainting a wall.

Why the Person Is the Deepest Cut

Each installment of this series has removed a different layer of memory, and there is a reason I have saved the person for last. A memorial, a museum, a sign, a dataset — these are the records of what happened. A person is a witness to it. When you unperson someone, you are not only editing the account; you are removing one of the people who could contradict the edited account, and demonstrating to everyone still on the wall what happens to those who become inconvenient.

The Pentagon corridor is, itself, a kind of memory device — a physical argument that the office of chairman is continuous, apolitical, larger than any president, an unbroken line from Bradley to the present. Every portrait on that wall says: this institution remembers its own, regardless of who occupies the White House. To reach in and pull the newest portrait two hours into a new administration is to break that argument on purpose. It announces a new rule: you hang on this wall at the president’s pleasure, and the line of history can be edited, in an afternoon, by the man currently in power. The other nineteen chairmen are, in that instant, put on notice retroactively — their permanence was never permanence. It was tolerance, and tolerance can be withdrawn.

This is why the unperson is the oldest and most feared of the Ministry’s operations. The pulled sign and the deleted dataset teach the public what it may know. The unpersoned official teaches every insider what will happen to them if they, too, become a name the president would prefer the wall did not carry. It is memory-erasure and intimidation in a single stroke: the record is corrected, and everyone still in the frame understands that they are next, should they ever be moronic enough, or treasonous enough, or merely inconvenient enough, to warrant a freshly painted wall.

The Whereabouts Unknown

Let me end on the smallest, strangest fact in the account, because it is the one that lingers.

When officials were asked what had become of the Milley portrait, the answer was that its whereabouts were unknown. A large official oil painting, unveiled at a formal ceremony ten days earlier, belonging to the Defense Department — simply gone, location unstated. Perhaps it sits in a basement. Perhaps in a crate. Perhaps it was destroyed; no one has said. The not-saying is itself part of the technique. An unperson’s portrait does not get a dignified retirement with a press release; it goes to an unspecified elsewhere, and the vagueness is the point. You are not meant to know where it went. You are meant, eventually, to stop asking, and then to forget there had been anything to ask about, because the wall is smooth and freshly painted and shows no seam.

That is the whole ambition of a Ministry of Memory, distilled into one corridor: not a blank where a portrait was torn down, which would at least testify that something had been removed, but a clean, continuous, plausible surface on which the inconvenient man never hung. Comrade Withers did not exist; he had never existed. General Milley’s portrait’s whereabouts are unknown, and the wall has been repainted, and the line of chairmen runs smooth from Bradley to the present with one link quietly missing — unless you happened to be standing in that hallway on the tenth of January, and remember, against the fresh paint, that it was there.

We still know what was hung. For now, in this series, that has had to be enough.

This is Part 5 of The Ministry of Memory, a series on the erasure of shared public memory. The earlier essays removed records — a memorial, a museum, the signs on the trails, the data on the servers. This one removes a person, in the oldest Orwellian tradition: the portrait pulled two hours into a new presidency, the wall patched and repainted the next morning so that no seam remained. It is memory-erasure and warning in one act — the record corrected, and everyone still on the wall put on notice. The series continues; the erasing has not stopped, and neither, for now, have the people who refuse to forget what was there.
