BEFORE THE CANADIAN RADIO-TELEVISION AND TELECOMMUNICATIONS COMMISSION

Intervention No. CRTC-2026-COUNTER-001

In the Matter of: A Complaint Concerning Advance Billing, Filed by a Person With Apparently Nothing Better to Do

Intervenor: The Canadian Telecommunications Association, In Defence of Itself

I. PRELIMINARY STATEMENT

  1. The Intervenor comes before this honourable Commission with a heavy heart and a light conscience.

  2. It has come to our attention that a complaint has been filed — by a private citizen, a retired gentleman of Toronto, a person who by his own account writes five essays a day and therefore cannot plausibly claim to be busy — alleging that the practice of advance billing constitutes some manner of impropriety.

  3. We wish to state, at the outset and for the record, that we are wounded. Not financially. We are never wounded financially; that is rather the point of the enterprise. But emotionally, spiritually, we are wounded that the relationship between a Canadian and his telecommunications provider — a relationship of trust, of mutual respect, of automatic monthly withdrawal — should be called into question by a man with a Substack.

  4. We ask the Commission to dismiss the complaint, to award costs against the complainant, and, if it is not too much trouble, to have a quiet word with him.

II. THE NATURE OF ADVANCE BILLING, PROPERLY UNDERSTOOD

  1. The complainant labours under a misapprehension. He believes that advance billing means charging the customer for service not yet rendered. He says this as though it were a criticism.

  2. It is, in fact, a courtesy.

  3. Consider the alternative. Under a system of billing in arrears — that is, charging the customer for service he has actually received — the customer would be subjected, at the end of each month, to a surprise. He would open his statement not knowing what horrors lay within. He would experience suspense, anxiety, the cold dread of the unknown sum.

  4. We spare him this. By billing in advance, we permit the customer the dignity of knowing, with perfect certainty, what he will be charged for a thing he has not yet used and may, owing to death or relocation, never use at all. There is a serenity in this. We have given the customer the gift of a settled mind.

  5. The complainant calls this “paying for nothing.” We prefer to call it paying for peace of mind, which is something, and arguably the most valuable something of all.

III. THE 1877 PRECEDENT

  1. The Intervenor wishes to draw the Commission’s attention to a matter of settled law and unbroken tradition.

  2. In the year 1877 — that is to say, in the very infancy of Confederation, when this Dominion was but ten years old and still learning to walk — the Bell Telephone Company, in the person of its agents, rendered unto a subscriber in the city of Hamilton a bill for telephone service.

  3. The subscriber had not yet received the telephone.

  4. We pause to let the Commission absorb the magnificence of this.

  5. Here, at the very dawn of Canadian telecommunications — before the light bulb was common, before the motorcar, before this nation had so much as a transcontinental railway running reliably — our forebears had already grasped the essential genius of the industry: that the bill may precede the service, the charge may precede the thing charged for, the invoice may, in a very real sense, exist independently of reality altogether.

  6. This is not, as the complainant would have it, an “error.” We have examined the record. We can find no evidence that anyone at the Bell Telephone Company in 1877 considered it an error. They considered it, we are confident, a Tuesday.

  7. It is a tradition unbroken since Confederation’s infancy. It is, we submit, as Canadian as the maple, the beaver, and the quiet acceptance of being slightly overcharged for things by large institutions one is too polite to confront.

  8. To strike it down now would be to sever a thread that runs, gold and gleaming, through the whole tapestry of our national life. We did not fight at Vimy for this.

IV. ON THE FOUNDATIONS OF CANADIAN ENTERPRISE

  1. Let us speak plainly, as one does only when one has a great deal to hide.

  2. The complainant appears to believe that an enterprise should charge for goods and services it has provided. This is a charming notion. It is the sort of notion held by people who have never run an enterprise, and it would, if adopted, bring the entire Canadian economy to its knees within the fiscal quarter.

  3. Charging for unprovided service is not a defect of Canadian enterprise. It is the foundation of Canadian enterprise. It is the bedrock upon which the cathedral is built.

  4. Consider the gymnasium membership, billed monthly in perpetuity to the customer who attended twice in January. Consider the streaming subscription, renewing in silence, in darkness, while the customer sleeps. Consider the warranty extension, the loyalty programme, the “convenience fee” levied for the convenience of being permitted to give us money.

  5. The entire architecture of the modern Canadian commercial relationship rests upon a single, load-bearing principle: that the customer, having once consented, may be relied upon never to look closely again.

  6. We did not invent this principle. We merely perfected it, scaled it, and attached it to a service so essential that the customer cannot leave us, and so confusing that he cannot understand us, and so automatic that he cannot, in any meaningful sense, catch us.

  7. The complainant wishes to look closely. This is the entire substance of his offence. He has looked closely, and worse, he has written it down, and worse still, people have read it.

V. ON THE COMPLAINANT HIMSELF

  1. We do not wish to make this personal. We wish to make it extremely personal, but we recognize that the Commission frowns on this, so we shall do it gently.

  2. The complainant is, we understand, a retired man of seventy-four. He has the time. This is, in our submission, the root of the problem. A properly employed Canadian, harried and exhausted, billed in advance and too tired to notice, is the natural and intended subject of our industry. He is the customer in his proper state: present, paying, and pleasantly unconscious.

  3. The complainant has escaped this state. Through the twin calamities of retirement and curiosity, he has acquired the two things most fatal to a healthy telecommunications relationship: leisure, and attention.

  4. He has used them to read his bill. He has used them to file a complaint — Complaint No. COMP-01365593, a number we have committed to memory the way one remembers the licence plate of the car that struck one. He has used them, we are given to understand, to grant an interview to a consumer reporter at a major Toronto newspaper.

  5. This last is the unkindest cut. It is one thing to be examined. It is another to be publicized.

VI. PRAYER FOR RELIEF

  1. Wherefore the Intervenor respectfully prays that this honourable Commission:

  2. (a) Dismiss the complaint in its entirety, on the grounds that it is correct, which is precisely what makes it so unhelpful;

  3. (b) Award costs against the complainant, including our legal fees, our filing fees, and a small additional sum to cover the emotional distress of having been noticed;

  4. (c) Affirm, as a matter of settled Canadian law reaching back to 1877, the inalienable right of an enterprise to bill for that which it has not yet, and may never, provide;

  5. (d) Direct the complainant to resume the posture of the model Canadian consumer — that is, to pay, to sigh, and to move along; and

  6. (e) In the alternative, if the Commission is determined to side with the customer, that it do so quietly, on a Friday afternoon, in a ruling worded so as to be incomprehensible to the press.

  7. All of which is respectfully submitted, in advance, for service the Commission has not yet rendered.

The Canadian Telecommunications Association reserves the right to bill the Commission for the filing of this intervention.

The complainant in this matter is real. The complaint number is real. The 1877 Bell precedent is real. The Toronto Star interview is forthcoming. Everything else here is invented, though one notes, with the weary resignation of a man who has read his own phone bill, that the line between invention and documentation grows thinner every year.
