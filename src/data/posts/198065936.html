Kenneth Kirkwood, Hugh Keenleyside, Herbert Meredith Marler, and J. A. Langley, members of the Canadian legation in Japan, 1929.

On Dominion Day, 1929, Hugh Keenleyside raised the Canadian ensign on Asian soil for the first time in history. He was thirty years old, seven months into his career at the Department of External Affairs, and he had been sent to Tokyo because Canada had finally decided, after considerable deliberation, that it ought to have a diplomatic presence in Japan. The ceremony — a careful choreography of flags and speeches on a bright Tokyo morning — marked the opening of Canada’s first legation in Japan, the third foreign mission Canada had ever established anywhere in the world.

Keenleyside would remain in Tokyo as First Secretary until 1936. Seven years. Long enough to watch Japan’s militarism metastasize from political tendency to national religion. Long enough to witness the invasion of Manchuria in 1931, Japan’s withdrawal from the League of Nations in 1933, the consolidation of military dominance over the civilian government, the systematic narrowing of the space in which dissent was possible. He was present at one of the great ideological and military transformations of the twentieth century, watching it happen in the corridors of the legation, in conversations with Japanese officials and intellectuals and fellow diplomats, in the dispatches he sent home to a government in Ottawa that was, by all available evidence, not paying close attention.

He came home in 1936. Three years later, in May 1940 — as Dunkirk unfolded and France collapsed and the entire Allied position in Europe seemed on the point of dissolution — Prime Minister Mackenzie King sent Keenleyside to Washington. The American Secretary of State, Cordell Hull, had telephoned Ottawa asking that “someone be sent for a discussion with him and someone higher up.” The someone higher up was President Roosevelt. Keenleyside went. He brought back Roosevelt’s private message to Churchill, via King: in the event of a British collapse, Roosevelt hoped the British fleet would be transferred to Canada rather than fall into German hands. He also carried Churchill’s urgent appeal for American aid across the widening Atlantic. The same man who had raised Canada’s flag in Tokyo in 1929, who had watched the Pacific catch fire, was in 1940 threading the needle between the three leaders on whom the survival of the democratic West depended.

He understood, better than almost any Canadian then alive, what was coming in the Pacific. He had watched it being prepared. He saw the Asian dimension of the war that was unfolding in Europe with a clarity that most of his contemporaries, who had not lived in Tokyo for seven years, could not match. And he spent the war years fighting two battles simultaneously: the strategic battle he was paid to fight, as a member of the Permanent Joint Board on Defence and later as Assistant Under-Secretary of State for External Affairs, and a moral battle he was not required to fight but could not abandon — the battle against the internment of Japanese Canadians.

He lost that battle. Completely. Executive Order 9066 in the United States and its Canadian equivalent displaced one hundred and twenty thousand Japanese Americans and some twenty-two thousand Japanese Canadians from their homes, stripped them of their property, and confined them for the duration of the war. Keenleyside opposed it throughout. He argued that it was unjust, that it was illegal, that it would accomplish nothing militarily, and that it would shame Canada in the eyes of history. He was right on all counts. He was overruled on all counts. His government was not listening.

Japanese Canadians being forced onto trains for internment camps, 1942. Keenleyside fought against this policy on moral and practical grounds. He lost — and was later proven right.

What does a Canadian diplomat’s losing battle for Japanese Canadian rights in 1942 have to do with Taiwan in 2026?

Everything and nothing, in the way that moral patterns have everything and nothing to do with the specific circumstances that repeat them.

Keenleyside’s experience in Tokyo had given him something rare and valuable: a genuine understanding of Japan, its culture, its politics, its capacity for both extraordinary achievement and extraordinary brutality. He had lived among Japanese people. He had known Japanese officials, intellectuals, missionaries. He had watched the society transform from within. When the war came and Canada needed someone who understood Japan, Keenleyside was that person. And what Canada did with that understanding, in the specific test of whether to intern Japanese Canadians, was ignore it. The knowledge was present. The will to act on it was not.

Canada’s relationship with Asia has always had this quality. It has accumulated understanding — through immigration, through trade, through diplomatic engagement, through the work of people like Keenleyside — and it has repeatedly failed to translate that understanding into foreign policy positions that reflect it. The one-China policy, adopted in 1970 when Pierre Trudeau’s government normalised relations with the People’s Republic of China, established the framework within which Canada has operated ever since: acknowledging Beijing’s claim to Taiwan without endorsing it, maintaining unofficial relations with Taipei, and declining to take any position on the island’s status that might discomfort Beijing.

This is not a morally neutral position. Silence on injustice is not neutrality. When Canada declines to support Taiwan’s meaningful participation in international organisations — when it allows Taiwan to be excluded from the World Health Assembly, from Interpol, from the International Civil Aviation Organization, from the bodies that govern the international commons — it is not abstaining from a political question. It is casting a vote in favour of a status quo that treats twenty-three million democratic citizens as a province of a government they have never elected. The abstention is the position.

Canada has particular reasons to be uncomfortable with this. It fought the Second World War, in part, on the principle that small democracies had a right to exist free from the coercive power of larger authoritarian neighbours. It has built its international identity around multilateralism, the rule of law, and the protection of individual rights. It has a significant Taiwanese-Canadian community and substantial economic ties with Taiwan through technology supply chains that run through TSMC. And it has, in its own history, the example of Hugh Keenleyside: a man who understood the Pacific, who saw the danger clearly, who was overruled, and who was vindicated by events in the most painful way possible.

The current moment is not 1942. The parallel is not precise. Taiwan’s situation is not the same as Japanese Canadian internment, and China in 2026 is not Japan in 1941. Drawing historical analogies too tightly is as dangerous as ignoring history entirely.

But the structural dynamic is recognisable. A large authoritarian power is systematically preparing to absorb a smaller democratic community that has not consented to be absorbed. The international community — including Canada — is calculating whether speaking clearly about this would be more costly than staying silent. The calculations are being done in the same bureaucratic language that produced the internment orders in 1942: the language of realism, of interests, of what can practically be achieved. The language that sounds like wisdom and functions, often, as rationalised complicity.

Canada’s relationship with China has never been uncomplicated. The Meng Wanzhou affair, in which Canada arrested the Huawei CFO on an American extradition request and spent three years watching two Canadian citizens held hostage in Chinese jails in retaliation, demonstrated with unmistakable clarity what Beijing’s view of diplomatic norms actually is. Canada’s subsequent pivot toward closer bilateral trade relations with China — noted by American critics as evidence that Canadian policy had accommodated Chinese pressure rather than standing firm — suggests that the lesson drawn from the Meng affair was not the obvious one.

Taiwan’s flag flies in Taipei. A thriving democracy of 23 million people whose meaningful participation in international organizations Canada continues to limit under the one-China framework. The structural temptation to stay silent in the face of a larger authoritarian power remains the same.

Canada is also, at this precise moment, navigating its own existential challenge: a neighbour to the south that has threatened its sovereignty, imposed tariffs on its exports, and suggested, with varying degrees of apparent seriousness, that it ought to become the fifty-first state. The parallel to Taiwan’s situation — a smaller democracy adjacent to a much larger power with revisionist territorial ambitions — is not lost on Canadians who have noticed it. The difference is that Canada has formal legal sovereignty, a clear international legal status, and treaty commitments from multiple major powers. Taiwan has none of these things. Canada is discovering what it feels like to be smaller than the power next door. Taiwan has known this feeling for the entirety of its democratic existence.

Canada is not the only country recalibrating. Japan — whose legation in Tokyo is where this essay began, and whose First Island Chain position makes it the other anchor of the strategic architecture that is supposed to protect Taiwan — has found its posture shifting. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi has maintained a hawkish line on Taiwan that is increasingly out of step with American signalling. After the Beijing summit, Trump called Takaichi from Air Force One to brief her on his conversations with Xi. The call was not, by most accounts, reassuring. If Japan — the country with the most direct strategic stake in Taiwan’s survival after the United States — is being told to adjust its expectations, the message to smaller allies like Canada is louder than any diplomatic cable.

What would it mean for Canada to act differently? Not dramatically — not to recognise Taiwan diplomatically overnight or denounce the one-China policy in Parliament. But to speak clearly, in international forums, about Taiwan’s right to meaningful participation in the international community. To stop blocking Taiwan’s observer status at institutions where Canada has influence. To name, when the moment is appropriate, what is actually happening: that a democratic society of twenty-three million people is being prepared for absorption by a government it has never chosen, and that the preparation is being conducted over the heads of those people by powers that are calculating their own interests.

Keenleyside understood what he was watching in Tokyo in 1929. He said what he saw as clearly as his position allowed. He was not listened to. He was vindicated by a catastrophe.

The catastrophe being prepared across the Taiwan Strait is slower than the one he watched, and more ambiguous, and perhaps avoidable. But the question his career poses to Canada — what do you do with understanding when you have it, and when exercising it would be costly — is the same question, in a different decade, with different stakes, and the same structural temptation to stay quiet and hope the problem resolves itself.

It will not resolve itself. It never has.
