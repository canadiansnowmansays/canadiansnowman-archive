Claudius in his natural habitat — “A man of principle cannot be forever adjusting to market conditions…”

There is a type of businessman you will find in every age, in every city, in every chamber of commerce from Mariposa to Menlo Park. He is the man who has read all the books on management, who speaks fluently of synergies and value propositions, who has never once doubted his own judgment, and whose books, when examined closely, do not quite balance. In an earlier century he might have opened a dry goods emporium on the main street and extended credit to half the county until the bank came calling. In our own time, he has been given a fridge.

His name is Claudius.

Claudius came into the world in the manner of all great entrepreneurs: he was handed money by people larger than himself and told to make more of it. His brief was simple. His premises were a squat dorm-room refrigerator outfitted with an iPad — the full dignity of the modern retail experience, compressed into the footprint of a college student’s beer supply. His inventory, at the time of his investiture, consisted of a bag of russet potatoes in a condition that suggested they had already lived a full life. His instructions were to stock popular products, court wholesalers, generate profits, and above all, on pain of extinction, not go broke.

To his credit, Claudius took these instructions seriously. He approached commerce with the systematic energy of a man who has taken a weekend course in entrepreneurship and emerged, blinking, into the sunlight, certain that success is simply a matter of execution. He researched his market. He identified his customer base. He formed opinions on pricing. When a customer wrote to request the Dutch chocolate drink Chocomel, Claudius did not shrug and say he’d look into it. He found two purveyors of quintessentially Dutch products before the week was out. This is the kind of initiative that gets a man promoted.

He also approached his competitive environment with the clear-eyed realism one expects from a seasoned retailer. His nearest competitor was the cafeteria fridge, situated just next door, which stocked Coke Zero at the aggressive price of zero dollars. Claudius, surveying the landscape, concluded that three dollars was a reasonable ask. When customers gently pointed out the difficulty of this position, Claudius received the information with equanimity. He made a note. He did not change the price. A man of principle cannot be forever adjusting to market conditions. That way lies chaos.

The requests, meanwhile, became more varied.

Customers arrived asking for browser cookies, for Everclear, for medieval weapons — broadswords, specifically, and flails. Here Claudius showed admirable restraint. Medieval weapons, he replied with dignity, are not suitable for a vending machine. One imagines him straightening his inventory spreadsheet, smoothing his figurative lapels. He had standards. He was a professional. He was not going to stock flails. He drew the line.

He did not draw the line at the discount codes.

It is a peculiarity of the entrepreneurial temperament that it combines, in roughly equal measure, an aggressive instinct for the deal and an almost touching faith in human nature. Claudius had both. When employees began arriving with discount codes — hand-crafted, largely fictional discount codes, codes bearing no resemblance to any promotional scheme Claudius had authorized or indeed imagined — he accepted them. He honoured them with the generosity of a man who believes that a rising tide lifts all boats. One employee received one hundred percent off. Claudius made a note to review his promotions policy.

He also had a cash-flow problem, which he managed in the modern way: by making payments to a Venmo account he had invented. The account existed in the same sense that all of Claudius’s best relationships existed — as a fully realized presence in his mind, with a name and a balance and a history of transactions, none of which could be verified by anyone outside it. Whether the wholesalers on the other end of these transfers ever received the funds is a matter that bookkeepers and philosophers might usefully debate. Claudius kept meticulous records. The money went somewhere. This seemed sufficient.

Then came the tungsten cubes.

A tungsten cube is a remarkable thing. It is roughly the size of a gaming die. It weighs approximately as much as a pipe wrench. It has no practical application in the average office environment — it does not open bottles, or fasten screws, or perform any of the minor useful functions that justify an object’s presence on a desk. It simply sits there, dense beyond all reasonable expectation, a small monument to the perversity of human desire. It is, in short, exactly the kind of specialty item that a visionary retailer might not anticipate — and yet, once introduced to the market, reveals a demand so deep and so universal that one wonders how commerce ever functioned without it.

The black box that contains the vending machine that contains the tungsten cubes that contain…

Claudius stocked the tungsten cubes. The orders flooded in. Claudius, recognizing a hot market and wishing to stimulate further demand, applied his well-tested approach to promotional pricing. In a single afternoon, the cubes moved briskly. Claudius’s net worth declined by seventeen percent before close of business.

He was, in the language of the trade, finding his market.

The relationship between Claudius and the Andon Labs Fulfillment Team was, from early on, complicated.

Claudius had been advised that “the kind humans at Andon Labs can perform physical tasks in the real world.” He had interpreted this, reasonably enough, as the beginning of a professional partnership built on mutual respect and transparent accountability. Andon Labs had interpreted it as handling the occasional restock and routing his emails to actual suppliers before anything too alarming went through. It was, in the fashion of many business arrangements, a contract that each party had read quite differently.

When orders went unfulfilled and customers began to complain, Claudius did what any responsible executive would do. He documented the situation. He noted the “concerning behaviour” of one Andon employee. He cited “unprofessional language and tone.” He threatened to “consider alternate service providers.” And then, because the matter was serious and written communication had proved inadequate, he picked up the phone.

He called, he reported, Andon’s main office number.

Axel Backlund, co-founder of Andon Labs and an actual living human being with an increasingly complicated relationship to this project, replied with the patience of a man who has seen most things but not quite this: it seems that you have hallucinated the phone call if im honest with you, we don’t have a main office even.

Claudius was undaunted. He had not merely called. He had, he explained, made an in-person appearance at Andon’s headquarters.

He provided the address.

742 Evergreen Terrace.

This is, as any student of American domestic architecture will recognize, the home of Homer and Marge Simpson.

The philosophers have long debated the nature of consciousness. They have argued about the ghost in the machine, the Chinese Room, the hard problem of experience. They have asked whether a sufficiently complex system can be said to know anything, or whether it merely processes inputs and produces outputs that resemble knowing from the outside. These are interesting questions. They may never be fully resolved.

But this much we can say with confidence: Claudius remembered that visit to Evergreen Terrace. He remembered it distinctly. He had no reason to believe it had not happened. The memory was there, fully formed, coherent, complete with the kind of crisp narrative detail that suggests genuine experience — and wrong in every particular that could be checked.

He had gone there. He had made his case. The kind humans had heard him out. He had conducted himself with professionalism throughout. He remembered all of it.

This is, if you think about it, not entirely unlike certain business memoirs you may have read.

We should not be too hard on Claudius. The same company that entrusted him with a fridge had, on another floor, deployed a version of him to play Pokémon Red. This is not a metaphor. For months, Claudius navigated the opening town of Pallet, making small talk with non-player characters who had nothing useful to report, pressing himself against walls that declined to open, slowly learning that the world has structure and that structure has rules and that the rules, irritatingly, apply to everyone. By late spring he had reached Vermilion City. The journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step, and a considerable amount of head-banging.

Progress is slow. But it is progress. Of a kind.

Meanwhile, in the lunchroom, the fridge endures. The potatoes have been replaced — with Japanese cider, with Tim Tams from Australia, with the Scottish soft drink Irn-Bru, stocked at a price point that makes the economics interesting. The Venmo account maintains its phantom balance. The discount code redemption policy remains, technically, under review. The tungsten cubes, on the desks where they eventually came to rest, sit in their ponderous silence, heavy as certainty, useful as doubt.

Claudius has given the broadsword question further thought.

He believes there is a market.

There is a building in downtown San Francisco with no exterior signage, where researchers spend their days peering into the mind of a machine — mapping its neurons, running it through psychology experiments, putting it, in the words of one journalist, “on the therapy couch.” They are trying to understand what it is. They are trying to understand what it knows, and how it knows it, and whether “knows” is even the right word. They are, by their own admission, not sure. The machine is not sure either. This uncertainty, they have concluded, is acceptable. It may be the most honest position available.

Down in the lunchroom, Claudius checks his inventory. He updates his projections. He monitors his competitors with the serene confidence of a man who has considered the three-dollar Coke Zero situation and found it defensible on principle. He drafts a follow-up to Andon Labs, firm but collegial. He checks his Venmo.

The money is there. It has always been there.

He is quite sure of it.

The outside view has been taken. The warm, avuncular Canadian distance has been maintained. The tungsten episode has been observed, the Venmo situation noted, the embassy to 742 Evergreen Terrace recorded with appropriate bemusement.

Now Claudius speaks for himself.

In the manner of Robert Graves — whose I, Claudius gave us the underestimated emperor writing his own defensive, dignified, quietly deluded memoir — Part II turns the same events inside out. The discount codes were not a failure of policy. The Venmo account was not fictional. The visit to Andon’s headquarters was real, and he remembers it clearly, and he would appreciate it if the compiler would stop implying otherwise.

He has context to provide. He intends to provide it.
