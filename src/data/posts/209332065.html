In the first essay of this series I promised to return to the Smithsonian, and I want to keep that promise early, because the Smithsonian is where the argument of this series stops being a metaphor. The Kennedy Center is a memorial that was hollowed out. The Smithsonian is the actual national memory — nineteen museums, a zoo, a constellation of research centres, the single largest repository of the American past assembled in one institution. When a government sets out to edit what a country is permitted to remember, this is the building it must eventually walk into. This year, it walked in.

And it announced itself, as these things now do, with a name that meant its opposite.

The Name of the Thing

On March 27, 2025, the president signed an executive order titled “Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History.” Sit with that title for a moment, because it is doing the same work Orwell’s ministries did. It presupposes that the truth had gone missing and sanity had fled — that the historians and curators of the national museum had been peddling delusion — and that the corrective would be supplied not by scholars but by the executive branch. The order directed the removal of “improper, divisive, or anti-American ideology” from the Smithsonian and its museums, and set the machinery in motion.

A Ministry of Memory does not announce that it intends to erase history. It announces that it intends to restore it. The erasing is the restoration. That is not a contradiction the administration is trying to hide; it is the official position, printed on the order. The past as it stands is the distortion. The edited past is the truth. To object to the deletion is to defend a lie. Winston Smith would have recognized the paperwork.

By August 2025, the order had teeth. The White House sent a letter to Smithsonian Secretary Lonnie Bunch demanding a comprehensive internal review of the museums, and — as multiple outlets reported — demanded that the institution hand over a staggering inventory of material for vetting: exhibition text, wall labels, websites, educational resources, social-media content, grant documentation, internal guidelines, and the plans for exhibits not yet built. Nineteen museums were placed under review to assess, in the letter’s words, “tone, historical framing, and alignment with American ideals.” The museums were given a hundred and twenty days to replace whatever the administration found divisive with descriptions it deemed “unifying, historically accurate and constructive.”

Read the verbs. Not add, not supplement, not offer another perspective. Replace. The past does not get a second opinion. It gets a rewrite, on a deadline.

The Case of the Vanishing Impeachments

If you want to see how the machine actually works — not the grand executive order but the small clerical act, the Winston Smith eight-hours-a-day of it — look at what happened to an exhibit called The American Presidency: A Glorious Burden.

Inside that permanent gallery at the National Museum of American History sits a section on the impeachment of presidents. For years it carried a small label noting the two impeachments of Donald Trump — the first over the pressure campaign on Ukraine, the second over the incitement of the January 6 attack. He is, as it happens, the only president in the history of the republic to have been impeached twice. That is not a matter of opinion or framing. It is a count. It happened, in the record, twice.

In July 2025, the label came off.

The museum’s explanation is the part worth preserving, because it is a small masterpiece of the genre. The Smithsonian said it had reviewed its “legacy content” and decided to restore the impeachment display to its “2008 appearance,” on the grounds that the surrounding material hadn’t been updated since then. The exhibit now notes that “only three presidents have seriously faced removal.” The man impeached twice had been returned, by administrative tidying, to a version of history that predates his presidency entirely.

Restored to 2008. There is the euphemism of the year, and it belongs in a glass case of its own. You did not delete the two impeachments; you restored the exhibit to a more pristine era — an era that happens to be one in which they had not yet occurred. The memory hole, in Orwell, was a literal chute; you dropped the inconvenient fact in and it was gone. The Smithsonian’s version is gentler and cleverer. You do not drop the fact down a hole. You roll the entire display back to a year before the fact existed, and let the omission occur by restoration. History happens, as the museum’s own retired placard once cheerfully read. And then, apparently, it un-happens.

After the Washington Post reported the removal and the criticism landed, the Smithsonian said the impeachments would be restored to the exhibit in some future, updated form, on no stated timetable. Perhaps they will be. But the mechanism had already shown itself, and it is the mechanism this series exists to document: a fact removed, an innocent-sounding procedural reason supplied, and the burden placed on the outraged to prove that anything improper occurred at all.

The Warning Label

For a while, through late 2025, there was relative quiet. Then, in the summer of 2026, the administration escalated in a direction that would be difficult to parody.

On July 4, 2026, the White House Domestic Policy Council released a 162-page report — I am not inventing the title — called Saving America’s Story: How Ideological Capture at the Smithsonian Institution’s National Museum of American History Erases Our Heritage. An institution whose charter is preserving heritage was now accused, in a government document, of erasing it, by the government that was in the act of editing it. The report became the pretext for the next order.

On Friday, July 24, 2026, the president signed an executive order — this one titled “Restoring Trust in the Smithsonian Institution” — directing that disclaimers be installed outside the Museum of American History, warning visitors that some of the exhibits inside do not reflect the president’s view of the nation’s past. Among his stated complaints: the museum’s treatment of slavery.

Consider the architecture of that, as a memory device. You cannot, in a single stroke, delete every exhibit you dislike inside the largest history museum in the country. So you do the next thing. You post a sign at the door. You tell every visitor, before they enter, that what they are about to see is suspect — that the professional historians inside are not to be trusted, that the official truth is the one on the placard out front, signed by the president. It is the inverse of a plaque. A plaque tells you what happened. This tells you to disbelieve what you are about to be told happened. The Ministry of Memory has discovered that you need not always remove the exhibit if you can simply station a doubt at the entrance.

Why This Is the Center of the Series

I said the Smithsonian is where the metaphor stops being a metaphor, and here is what I mean.

Every other institution in this series holds a piece of the national memory. A park holds the memory of a place. A memorial holds the memory of a person. An archive holds the memory of a record. The Smithsonian holds the memory of the whole — it is the designated place where the United States keeps its account of itself. To bring that institution under the direct editorial control of the sitting president, to demand its exhibit text be handed over for vetting, to roll back its displays to years before inconvenient events occurred, and to post presidential warning labels at its doors, is to do at the center what the rest of this series documents at the edges.

And note the euphemism at every level, because the euphemism is the signature. The order is not called Rewriting American History; it is called Restoring Truth and Sanity. The deletion is not called deletion; it is called restoring the exhibit to 2008. The warning label is not called censorship; it is called Restoring Trust. The 162-page brief for erasure is titled Saving America’s Story. At each stage the word chosen is the reassuring one, the civic one, the one a reasonable person would nod along to — restore, save, trust, truth, sanity — laid precisely over the act it describes, which is the removal of the past a government finds inconvenient.

This is the same hand that pried the slavery plaque off the wall at George Washington’s estate near Independence Hall, that stripped the climate signage from the national parks, that I will come to in the next essay. It is the same hand, and it signs its work the same way. It never signs it “erasure.” It signs it “truth.”

What Was Actually Restored

Let me end where the museum’s own language invites me to.

The impeachment display was “restored to 2008.” So let us be clear about what the year 2008 contains and does not contain, as a state of American memory. It does not contain the first impeachment of Donald Trump. It does not contain the second. It does not contain January 6. It is a year chosen, of all the years available, because it is clean — because the events an administration wishes had not happened have not yet happened in it. To restore a nation’s memory to 2008 is not to restore anything. It is to select the last convenient moment before the truth arrived, and to move back into it.

That is the offer this Ministry makes to all of us, in the end. Not a lie you are asked to believe, but a tidier year you are invited to move back into — 2008, or 1958, or whatever date precedes the memory that has become inconvenient — with a sign at the door assuring you that the move is a restoration, and the historians who object are the ones distorting the past.

The exhibit says only three presidents have seriously faced removal. The record says otherwise, twice. Somewhere in the space between those two sentences is the whole business of the Ministry of Memory.

This is Part 2 of The Ministry of Memory, a series on the erasure of shared public memory — the museums, parks, monuments, archives, names, honours, and human witnesses through which a nation remembers itself. Part 1 began at the Kennedy Center, with the volunteer sent home for remembering out loud. This essay reaches the center of the national memory itself, and finds a president’s warning label posted at its door. Part 3 will follow the same hand into the national parks, where the plaques come off the walls one sign at a time.
