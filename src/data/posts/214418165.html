Stephen Gordon September 7, 2026

The women had arrived with henna on their hands. Children were playing in the courtyard. In a room draped with carpets and pillows, a meal was about to be served for a bride and groom in Kuhestak — a coastal town of about three thousand people in Sirik County, Hormozgan Province, near the Strait of Hormuz, and predominantly Baloch.

At around half past nine on the night of Tuesday, September 1, a bomb brought the roof down.

As Christiaan Triebert, Malachy Browne, Farnaz Fassihi and Erika Solomon reported in a visual investigation for the New York Times, five wedding guests were killed and at least sixty-seven wounded. Among the dead was Amir-Mohammad Karimi, six years old, who had been so excited about the wedding that he went on ahead with his fourteen-year-old sister, Kiana. When his mother heard the explosions she ran into the street and found her children covered in dust and shrapnel. Mohammad Malahi, sixteen, was so badly disfigured that his father and brother identified him by the mobile phone found on his body. Kolsoum Malahi Najandnia was forty-three. Zarkhatoun Taheri was fifty.

The bride and groom survived.

I. The Bomb Had a Name

What distinguishes this from every other contested casualty claim of the past six months is that the munition was recovered and identified.

The Times collected videos of weapons remnants filmed at the scene by a local teacher, Hamid Kasiri, and by a reporter for Iran’s state broadcaster, and gave them to Trevor Ball, a former U.S. Army explosive ordnance disposal technician now with Armament Research Services. Ball identified the fragments as components of an American glide bomb — a Joint Standoff Weapon, or JSOW. The recovered tail fin matches JSOW fins recovered after strikes in Yemen and Venezuela and catalogued on the Open Source Munitions Portal. The casing dimensions and the distinctive spacing of its bolt holes match a reference JSOW.

And a JSOW appears loaded beneath the wings of an American fighter jet in a video that Central Command itself published on Tuesday, alongside its announcement that it had struck targets in Iran.

Asked about the wedding, Captain Tim Hawkins of Central Command said this: they are aware of reports, which originated from Iranian state media, and the U.S. military never targets civilians, unlike the IRGC.

This diary has now recorded that sentence three times. It was the answer in July when a two-thousand-pound bomb killed a taxi driver, his wife and their two-year-old son on Qeshm Island. It was the answer on Tuesday. It is the answer now, to physical evidence rather than to a claim — and it remains not a denial but a comparison. An institution that cannot say we did not do this says instead that somebody else is worse.

Adil Haque, a professor of international law at Rutgers, put the obligation plainly: the American military must address the attack, because on its face it seems inexplicable and indefensible. There was no indication of any military target around the building.

II. The Fourth Time

The investigation contains a paragraph that should be read slowly, because it assembles something nobody had assembled before. Kuhestak is the fourth occasion on which the Times has documented strikes hitting structures with no military use and killing civilians.

On February 28, the first day of the war, an American Tomahawk destroyed the Shajareh Tayebeh primary school in Minab. Iranian authorities put the dead at at least 175, most of them schoolchildren. Hours later, several PrSM missiles struck near a residential area in Lamerd, killing twenty-one. In July, a two-thousand-pound bomb hit a house on Qeshm Island, killing three. On Tuesday, a glide bomb hit a wedding.

The wounded from Kuhestak were taken to Hazrat Abolfazl Hospital in Minab — the same hospital that received the schoolchildren on the first morning of the war.

Six months, one province, the same emergency room.

Iranian officials have called the strike a war crime, and the head of Iran’s Red Crescent Society has asked the prosecutor of the International Criminal Court to investigate. Ahmad Nafisi, the deputy governor of Hormozgan, said the United States has once again committed a war crime in his province.

Kian, a local photographer who was five hundred yards away, said he heard a strange sound before the explosion — almost like a whistle, coming from the direction of the sea. The electricity failed and the phones went down. The air smelled of gunpowder. People carried small children toward the hospital in pickup trucks and whatever else they could find. The bomb, he said, struck the part of the wedding set aside for women, which is why most of the wounded are women and children.

One guest, a woman who has not given her surname, had posted a photograph of her henna the night before, delighted, promising more stories to come. Within an hour of the strike she posted again in the same lilac headscarf, her face marked, and wrote that a night that was supposed to be love and laughter had turned into mourning. Later she added an observation that requires no assistance from me: they were not enriching uranium at the wedding, and the wedding was not near a military area.

III. Neither Closed Nor Open

Why was an American aircraft over Hormozgan on Tuesday night at all? Because of the waterway sixty miles away, and because after six months neither side can resolve it.

Peter Eavis and Jenny Gross reported for the Times on Sunday the clearest account yet of what the Strait of Hormuz has become. Eugene Gholz of Notre Dame gave them the sentence that describes the entire war: the strait is neither fully closed nor fully opened. Iran cannot close it completely, and the United States cannot open it completely.

The numbers underneath that are worth setting down.

Over the seven days to Thursday, an average of 6.7 million barrels a day transited the strait — nearly sixty percent below pre-war levels. Adding the pipelines that bypass it entirely, the Gulf is exporting about two-thirds of what it did before February. At least twenty-three ships were hit in the strait during July and August. Iran struck twelve vessels in August and eleven in July, which is to say that for all the drama of the past fortnight, the campaign against shipping has been running at a constant rate all summer.

August was the deadliest month for merchant seamen in the strait since March.

Central Command says its protection operation, begun in mid-May, has helped roughly 1,600 commercial vessels and 800 million barrels of oil through the strait. Captain Hawkins says commercial traffic continues despite Iranian aggression and that momentum is clearly building. He declined to say how many Iranian attacks there were in July and August.

A command that publishes its vessel count and withholds its attack count is telling you which number it believes.

IV. Ten Million Dollars a Crossing

The most revealing figure in the reporting is not military at all.

Insuring a tanker through the strait has cost, over the past fortnight, an additional four to seven percent of the value of the ship and its cargo — which David Smith of the broker McGill and Partners puts at over ten million dollars per tanker, with rebates available for voyages that pass without incident. In the calm following the June ceasefire the same cover fell to one or two percent. Rates change by the hour. In forty years in the business, Smith says, it remains the most dynamic shipping insurance market he has seen.

That is the blockade the American Navy cannot lift. It can escort a ship; it cannot underwrite one.

And so the trade has changed hands. Private shipping companies have largely not come back. Executives told the Times they will not risk their crews, or lose a vessel for weeks of repairs — and Iran mostly damages rather than destroys, which is its own form of deterrence. One executive, speaking anonymously so his company would not become a target, said Iran appears to be aiming at engine rooms, leaving ships stranded rather than sunk.

What moves instead are state fleets. The Gulf governments send their own tankers, transponders switched off, which is why every count of traffic and every count of attacks is a floor rather than a total. The Sidr, the Saudi vessel struck last week, belongs to the state shipping company Bahri and was carrying around two million barrels, transiting in the dead of night. Emirati tankers have gone through and several have been hit; two Kuwaiti tankers were struck in late August.

Samir Madani of TankerTrackers put the position as briskly as anyone has: Iran can try to stop the flow of oil from the Gulf, but that will not stop the Arab nations from exporting.

Noam Raydan of the Washington Institute explains why Iran keeps firing anyway. Tehran wants to demonstrate that even if its neighbours find ways around it, it will hit back. The object is durable leverage and a stronger negotiating hand. This, she says, is why she does not see them letting go.

V. The Oil Fleet

On Saturday the war acquired a new category of target.

Yeganeh Torbati and Leily Nikounazar reported for the Times that American forces struck three Iranian crude carriers — permanently disabling one off Kharg Island, Iran’s principal export terminal, disabling a second near Jask, and completely destroying a third, empty, in the Gulf of Oman after directing its crew to abandon ship. Iranian state media confirmed the strikes. No casualties were reported.

Central Command described the vessels as part of a multibillion-dollar shadow network funding the Revolutionary Guards and their allied groups abroad, and said the strikes answered Iranian attempts to hit two American warships — a carrier and a guided-missile destroyer — with ballistic missiles. Both evaded; no American personnel were harmed. The timing of those alleged attacks was not specified, and Iran did not acknowledge them, warning only that its strikes on American military vessels would grow more severe if Washington kept attacking Iranian ships and maintaining the blockade.

Admiral Brad Cooper said the United States would impose an even higher economic cost if Iran shoots at American ships, and would, if necessary, destroy Iran’s limited and exposed oil fleet.

Iran answered the same day. The Revolutionary Guards said on Saturday that they had attacked several vessels, including ships they described as linked to the United States, in retaliation for the strikes on the Iranian tankers. Which is the cycle in a single afternoon: America destroys Iranian tankers to punish attacks on shipping, and Iran attacks shipping to punish the destruction of its tankers.

Note what that does to a distinction the administration has drawn since the beginning of August. The pivot from military campaign to economic campaign was the entire premise of Operation Economic Outcast — sanctions instead of bombs, asphyxiation instead of force. On Saturday the economic campaign was delivered by aircraft. Bessent’s sanctions and Cooper’s munitions now aim at the same thing: Iran’s ability to sell oil.

And on Friday, in the Oval Office, the president said the United States may strike Pickaxe Mountain, the suspected nuclear site, very soon — if activity is detected there. He also offered an accounting of the campaign: they are five months in, and what they did in five months is ensure Iran will not have a nuclear weapon.

It has been six months. And in July he called Pickaxe a possible target for a big fat shot in the front door; six days after that he said he did not need Netanyahu to tell him about Pickaxe, and that it was not a big problem. It is a problem again.

VI. The Empty Tanker

I want to place two events from the same week beside one another, and then stop.

In the Gulf of Oman, the United States destroyed a tanker carrying no oil, having first directed its crew to leave the ship. Nobody died. The care taken was real, and Central Command filmed it and released the footage.

In Kuhestak, sixty miles from the strait, an American glide bomb was released over a residential neighbourhood where a wedding was being held, and five people died, and sixty-seven were injured, and the youngest of the dead was six years old and starting kindergarten. There is no footage of that from Central Command. The evidence was assembled by a local teacher with a phone, a state television reporter, a munitions database maintained by volunteers, and an American ordnance technician working from photographs.

The first proves the capability exists to give warning. The second is what happened when nobody did.

VII. By the Numbers

  • 5 killed and at least 67 wounded at the Kuhestak wedding; the youngest of the dead was 6.

  • 4 documented episodes in six months of American strikes hitting structures with no military use and killing civilians — Minab, Lamerd, Qeshm Island, Kuhestak.

  • 175 killed at the Minab primary school on the war’s first day, most of them children; the Kuhestak wounded went to the same hospital.

  • 6.7 million barrels a day through the strait last week, nearly 60% below pre-war.

  • 23 ships hit in the strait in July and August; 12 in August, 11 in July.

  • 1,600 vessels escorted by the U.S. Navy since mid-May, per Central Command, which declines to give an attack count.

  • $10 million+ to insure a single tanker crossing; 1–2% of ship and cargo value in the June calm, 4–7% now.

  • $95.50 Brent crude, over 30 percent above pre-war; diesel at a record $5.85 a gallon in the United States.

  • 19+ sailors killed since the war began, including two Filipino crew aboard the Sidr.

  • 3,000+ Iranians killed since February 28, according to Iranian authorities, including more than 50 during the sixty-day truce.

  • Narges Mohammadi remains in Evin Prison, in a province where one hospital has now received the casualties of the war’s first day and of its one hundred and eighty-sixth.

VIII.

Gholz’s formulation is the truest thing said about this war by anyone: Iran cannot close the strait and America cannot open it. Six months of bombing, a memorandum signed at Versailles and abandoned, two carrier strike groups, a sanctions operation with a codename, and the result is a waterway running at forty percent of normal, insured at ten million dollars a crossing, with twenty-three ships hit in two months and the tally of dead sailors climbing by twos.

Neither closed nor open is not a stalemate in the usual sense. It is a condition being actively maintained, at enormous cost, by two governments that have each concluded the other will tire first. Every week that neither does, the bill is presented to somebody who has no say in it — a Filipino seafarer on a Saudi tanker, a teacher in Bandar Abbas whose salary runs out before the month does, a six-year-old who wanted to see a wedding.

They were not enriching uranium at the wedding. That is the whole of it, and it was said by a woman in a lilac headscarf an hour after the roof came down, which is sooner than anyone in Washington has managed to say anything about it at all.
