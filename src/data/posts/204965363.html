by Stephen Gordon

I count my essays. The number climbs, it lands on a year that already means something, and I stop and tell you what. This is essay 1,378, and after the plague — which I could not make funny, and did not try — the count has handed me something that is, I am relieved to report, almost entirely absurd.

In 1378, the Catholic Church, an institution whose entire operating premise is that there is exactly one of certain things, briefly found itself with two of the most important one.

Two popes.

Not in sequence. Not one dying and another elected. At the same time. Simultaneously. Two men, each in full regalia, each seated on what he insisted was the only real throne, each pointing across Christendom at the other and declaring him a fraud, an imposter, and — this is the good part — excommunicated. Two popes, each excommunicating the other, which raises a theological question so delicious I have been turning it over for days: if the pope excommunicates you, but you are also the pope, and you have excommunicated him first, who exactly is going to hell, and who gets to decide?

Let me explain how the Church acquired a surplus, because the mechanism is a small masterpiece of institutional self-sabotage.

For most of the fourteenth century the popes had not even been in Rome. They had been in Avignon, in the south of France, for about seventy years — a period the Italians bitterly called the “Babylonian Captivity,” because a French-dominated papacy sitting comfortably in France looked, to Roman eyes, less like the seat of universal Christianity and more like a subsidiary of the French crown. Finally, in 1377, the pope moved back to Rome, and everyone breathed a sigh of relief, and then he promptly died, which is when the trouble started.

The cardinals gathered in Rome to elect a successor. A Roman mob, terrified the cardinals would pick another Frenchman and scurry back to Avignon, surrounded the conclave and made its preferences known in the direct manner of mobs. Under this considerable pressure, the cardinals elected an Italian, who became Pope Urban VI. So far, so orderly.

Except Urban VI turned out to be — the historical consensus is unusually blunt here — difficult. Possibly unstable. He alienated the very cardinals who had just elected him, with a speed and thoroughness that suggested real talent. And those cardinals, a few months later, decided they’d made a terrible mistake, declared that their election had been invalid because the mob had coerced them, and elected a different pope, a Frenchman, who took the name Clement VII and set up shop back in Avignon.

Now. You will notice the problem. They had not waited for the first pope to die or resign. Urban VI was still very much in Rome, still very much wearing the hat, still very much of the opinion that he was the pope — because he was the pope, he’d been properly elected. But the cardinals now said Clement was the pope. And Clement agreed with them. So did France. So did Scotland, and Naples, and a good part of the map. Meanwhile Rome, England, the Holy Roman Empire, and the rest lined up behind Urban.

Christendom, the single unified body of Western Christianity, the institution that existed to be the one true church, had split cleanly down the middle into two true churches, each with its own pope, its own cardinals, its own claim to be the sole channel between humanity and God, and its own firm conviction that everyone on the other side was damned.

Here is what I find genuinely fascinating about this, underneath the comedy, and it is very much a Numbers Game observation.

The entire authority of the medieval papacy rested on singularity. There is one God, one truth, one church, and one man who holds the keys — Peter’s keys, handed down in an unbroken line, the single point where the infinite touches the earthly. The whole spiritual architecture depends on the number being one. One pope. Not the best pope, not the most popular pope, not the pope with the strongest army — the pope, the only one, by definition.

And the moment there were two, the number itself became the crisis. Because two infallible popes is not twice as much authority. It is no authority, instantly, for everyone. If there can be two, there can be none, because the entire claim was that there could only ever be one. The second pope didn’t divide the Church’s power in half. He exposed that the power had never been about being right — it had been about being alone. The pope’s authority was the authority of the only number in the room. Put a second number beside it and the magic doesn’t split. It evaporates.

They could not agree on who the real pope was because there was no test. That was the whole point of the office — you didn’t need a test, there was only ever one candidate, the singularity was the proof. Remove the singularity and there was nothing underneath it to appeal to. No tiebreaker. No higher court, because the pope was the higher court. Two popes meant the highest authority in the Western world had, at a stroke, no way to settle the single most important question it had ever faced, which was: which of us is you?

It got worse, which in this series you have learned to expect.

The schism lasted not months but decades — nearly forty years. Popes died and were replaced, on both sides, so that the split perpetuated itself, each line electing its own successors, each generation inheriting the standoff. And then, in 1409, a church council met at Pisa to fix the whole thing, and did so with the special genius available only to committees: they deposed both existing popes and elected a new one to replace them both.

You will have already guessed the outcome. The two existing popes declined to be deposed. They kept being popes. So the council’s brand-new unifying pope did not replace the two — he simply joined them. The council convened to solve the problem of two popes and solved it by producing a third.

Three popes. Three simultaneous Vicars of Christ, each excommunicating the other two, each the sole voice of God on earth, in triplicate. It took another church council, at Constance, running from 1414 to 1418, to finally clean it up — deposing or accepting the resignation of all the various claimants and electing a single agreed pope at last, ending forty years in which the most singular office in the Western world had been, at various times, doubled and then tripled.

So this is essay 1,378, and it is the year the number broke.

I have spent this series watching people treat numbers as feelings — inflating them, rounding them, branding them. Here is the rarest case of all: an institution whose entire power came from a number being one, discovering the hard way that you cannot have a spare. That singularity is not a quantity you can increase. That the whole edifice of sole authority rests on there being, precisely and only, one — and that the instant a second appears, you do not have more of the thing. You have none of it, and forty years of councils ahead of you, and three popes before you’re done.

There is a lesson in there for anyone whose authority depends on being the only one in the room. The claim to be the sole voice, the single truth, the one channel — it is only ever as strong as its monopoly. It does not survive a second claimant. It cannot, because it was never really about being right. It was about being alone.

I will see you at the next one, where ordinary people, taxed past endurance in the long shadow of the plague, decide to march on London and ask their own inconvenient question about who, exactly, gets to be in charge.

Two popes. Then three. Zero authority. The number, it turns out, was load-bearing.
