The sign has been there since 1987. The second line was added last Tuesday. The swans have not been informed. They have not asked.

There is a sign at the edge of Elsinore that reads, in the manner of all such signs, Welcome to Elsinore — A Community That Cares. The sign has been there since 1987 and has survived two repainting controversies, one minor flood, and a prolonged dispute about whether cares implied financial liability. It has, until recently, been considered sufficient.

Last Tuesday, Deputy Reeve Reg Plimpton announced that Elsinore would be adding a second line.

Welcome to Elsinore — A Smart Community.

“We’re not just a community that cares anymore,” Reg told the assembled council, setting down a document that had clearly been printed that morning and not, as some suspected, written that morning, since writing implies effort of a particular kind Reg has historically delegated. “We’re a community that thinks.”

Councillor Don Farnsworth asked what this meant in practice.

“It means,” said Reg, consulting the document, “that we will leverage AI-enabled civic infrastructure to deliver proactive, data-driven community outcomes aligned with resident expectations.”

Farnsworth wrote this down. Then he crossed it out. Then he wrote it down again, because the alternative was to understand it, and there wasn’t time for that before lunch.

Norman Stalker, who was covering the meeting for the Beacon from his customary seat at the back, wrote down three words in his notebook, underlined them once, and did not write anything else for the remainder of the meeting. The three words were: whose expectations, exactly. He had been asking this question in Elsinore for twenty-three years in various forms and had not yet received a satisfactory answer, which had not discouraged him from continuing to ask it.

After the meeting he went back to the Beacon office and spent twenty minutes with the archive, which is a thing Norman does when he senses that history is about to repeat itself and wants to be precise about which history. He found what he was looking for in the spring edition of 2019: Elsinore Embraces Digital Transformation, above a photograph of Reg Plimpton holding a tablet computer with the expression of a man who had been handed it thirty seconds before the photographer arrived. The initiative had produced a municipal app that crashed on launch, a Smart Parking pilot that confused the Legion parking lot with the library, and a line item in the following year’s budget marked technology transition costs that Norman had asked about three times without receiving a response he could print.

He clipped the 2019 piece, placed it in a folder he labelled Smart Community, and put it in the filing cabinet where he kept things that would be relevant later. In twenty-three years the cabinet had never been wrong about what would be relevant later.

Constance Hay, who attended all council meetings in her capacity as someone who had been in Elsinore longer than the current building and whose opinion, once formed, carried the specific gravity of geological fact, listened to Reg’s presentation with the expression she reserved for ideas that would either improve or embarrass the town, and had not yet decided which.

“Smart,” she said afterward, to no one in particular, in the tone of someone testing a word for structural integrity. She did not say anything further. She didn’t need to.

On the Kemp Road, where the county concession meets the back forty of the Haliwell farm, a man who has been working that land since before Reg Plimpton discovered the word leverage was mending a fence post when his neighbour stopped to mention the Smart Community Initiative.

The farmer listened. He had the particular quality of stillness that comes from spending decades in the company of weather, which does not negotiate and does not rebrand.

“AI,” said his neighbour. “For the whole town. Apparently.”

The farmer considered this for a moment. He looked at his barn. His barn contained four cats who had been conducting rodent management operations since before the term existed, without a report, without a protocol, and without a budget line.

“Hm,” said the farmer.

He returned to the fence post. His neighbour, who had been hoping for more, waited a respectable interval and then drove on.

This was, as it turned out, the most searching critique of the Smart Community Initiative that anyone in Elsinore would produce. Norman Stalker later tried to interview the farmer for the Beacon. The farmer said he didn’t have much to add. Norman printed that too.

The first practical consequence of Elsinore’s pivot to intelligence arrived at the veterinary clinic on a Wednesday morning.

The patient was a tabby cat named, according to his owner, Chairman Meow. The owner — I will call him Gerald, not because that is his name — Gerald Fitch, who is the Gerald most people in Elsinore think of when the name is spoken, has a cat named Hansard, who is nineteen and constitutionally opposed to examination — but because this man has the specific quality of a Gerald: the settled conviction that he has already done most of the relevant work before arriving.

“I’ve diagnosed him,” said Gerald.

“Have you,” said Maggie.

“I used the AI. It was very thorough.” He produced his phone. “I have a seventeen-point differential.”

Maggie was not looking at the phone. She was looking at Chairman Meow, who had communicated in the thirty seconds since entering the room — through posture, coat condition, the angle of one ear, and a specific quality of stillness that is different from the stillness of a cat that is merely offended by Wednesdays — rather more than Gerald’s AI had managed in seventeen questions.

“Point one,” said Gerald, “is a rare endocrine condition generally found in —”

“Gerald.”

“— Burmese cats specifically, but the AI said —”

“Gerald. When did he last eat?”

Gerald looked up. “Tuesday, I think. He seemed —”

“And is he drinking?”

“Less than usual, maybe —”

“Right,” said Maggie, with the calm efficiency of someone who has spent years translating between species, which is to say between animals who cannot speak and owners who cannot stop. She examined Chairman Meow with the particular attention of someone who has learned to listen to what is not being said, which in veterinary medicine is most of the relevant information.

The diagnosis took four minutes. It was not on Gerald’s list.

At the door, Gerald paused. He had clearly been saving this since he read about Elsinore’s Smart Community Initiative in the Beacon.

“Are you worried?” he asked. “About being replaced? By AI?”

Maggie looked at Chairman Meow, who looked back at her with the expression cats reserve for questions they have already answered.

“Gerald,” she said, “your AI asked Chairman Meow seventeen questions. Chairman Meow answered none of them. I asked him one question and he told me everything.” She handed back the carrier. “The day an AI can do that, you let me know.”

Gerald left looking chastened. Chairman Meow left looking like a cat who has made his position clear and considers the matter closed.

Gerald’s AI produced a seventeen-point differential. Maggie asked the cat one question — when did he last eat — and he told her everything. The day an AI can do that, she said, you let me know.

That evening, Maggie’s brother Jim was at his kitchen table with a cup of tea and his laptop, which is where Jim could be found most evenings after the dogwalking duties were done. Jim walked dogs by day — deliberately, professionally, with a patience that the dogs appreciated and their owners paid for — and in the evenings he played online poker with the focused calm of a man who understood probability and had made his peace with variance.

He had not, lately, made his peace with variance.

“Something’s off,” he said, when Maggie stopped by and found him staring at the screen with the expression of a man doing mathematics he doesn’t like the answer to.

“Off how?” said Maggie, pouring herself the last of the tea.

“The patterns.” He turned the laptop toward her. “Online poker. I’ve been playing the same stakes, same sites, same general strategy for three years. The edges I used to have — they’re closing. Some of these players, the way they calculate pot odds in real time, the way they adjust —” He shook his head. “It’s too fast. Too precise.”

Maggie looked at the screen. “You think they’re using AI.”

“I think some of them aren’t exactly not using AI.”

Maggie considered this, the way she considered most things, by looking at it steadily until it told her something. “So the table’s changed and you didn’t get the memo.”

“The table’s changed and nobody sent a memo. That’s rather the point.” He closed the laptop. “The AI doesn’t get tired at eleven o’clock. It doesn’t go on tilt when it takes a bad beat. It doesn’t get impatient in the fourth hour.” He paused. “All the things that used to make me better than average are the things that make me worse than a program.”

“Meanwhile,” said Maggie, “I spent this morning watching a man show me a seventeen-point AI diagnosis of his cat.”

“Was it right?”

“It wasn’t even wrong in an interesting way.”

Jim nodded slowly, in the manner of someone assembling a theory. “So in your situation the AI was confidently useless and a human was better. In my situation the AI is quietly competent and a human is worse.”

“Seems that way.”

“And the difference is —”

“Chairman Meow,” said Maggie, “cannot tell an AI whether he’s been eating. A poker hand can.” She set down her cup. “You’re playing a game that produces data. The AI is very good with data. I’m doing a job that requires reading something that doesn’t produce data. The AI finds that considerably harder.”

Jim was quiet for a moment. Outside, his last dog of the day — a large and philosophical golden retriever named after a former prime minister — could be heard settling heavily onto the porch.

“So I should walk more dogs,” said Jim.

“Or find a poker table where the cards are dealt by someone who doesn’t own a subscription,” said Maggie.

“Those tables exist?”

“There’s one at the Legion on Thursdays. Cash game. Reg Plimpton plays.”

Jim brightened fractionally. “Reg plays poker?”

“He calls it strategic community relationship building.”

“Does he win?”

“He thinks he does,” said Maggie. “Which is its own kind of answer.”

The swans were not consulted.

This is not unusual. The swans are never consulted. They have been on the Avon for longer than the town has had opinions about them, and they have developed, over the centuries, the refined indifference of those who have outlasted every administrative structure that has ever tried to manage them.

The AI Swan Management Optimization Report — commissioned by the municipal Smart Community Initiative at a cost Norman Stalker later published in the Beacon under the headline Your Tax Dollars, Apparently — arrived in twelve pages of confident specificity. It recommended adjusted feeding schedules, modified nesting zone parameters, and a “visitor interaction protocol” designed to reduce what the report called “adversarial swan engagement incidents.”

The Swan Warden, who has been managing the swans since before the current council was born and who has the particular patience of someone who has been hissed at by experts for thirty years, read the report with care.

“It says here,” she told Norman, “that swans respond well to positive reinforcement.”

“And?”

“I showed it to Gertrude.” Gertrude is the large female who runs the east bank. “She bit the report.”

“Did she.”

“Twice. Very deliberately.” She folded the paper. “I thought that was probably a data point.”

Norman printed it. Constance Hay cut it out and put it on her refrigerator, where it remained until she could find a better refrigerator for it.

Constance had, in seventy-eight years of Elsinore, seen the town pivot to many things. She had seen it pivot to tourism, to heritage designation, to a brief and ill-advised period of rebranding itself as a Festival Destination, and to a municipal wellness strategy that had lasted one budget cycle and produced a single unused bicycle rack outside the Legion. Each pivot had arrived with a document, a sign, and a Reg Plimpton who was not yet the current Reg Plimpton but was recognizably of the same genus.

“The town,” she told Reverend Faye, when they met for their customary Tuesday tea, “has always been smart. It simply didn’t require a sign.”

Reverend Faye agreed that this was probably true.

“The Haliwell farm has been feeding this county since 1889,” said Constance, “without a single optimization report. The swans have been on the Avon since before the theatre. Maggie —” she meant the vet assistant at the clinic “— can tell you what’s wrong with an animal before it’s settled into the chair.” She set down her cup with the finality of someone delivering a verdict that does not require an appeal. “That is intelligence. It doesn’t need to be artificial.”

Reverend Faye said nothing, which was her way of agreeing completely.

Reverend Faye preached that Sunday on the parable of the talents.

She did not mention the Smart Community Initiative. She did not mention the Swan Management Report, the seventeen-point differential, the second line on the welcome sign, or the Thursday poker game at the Legion. She did not need to. She has a gift for finding the parable in the local situation without naming the local situation, and the congregation left feeling that something had been addressed, though they could not have said precisely what.

The parable, for those who need the reminder, concerns a man who gives his servants money to tend while he is away. Two of them put it to work and return it multiplied. The third, frightened of losing what he had been given, buries it in the ground and returns it unchanged. He considers this prudent. His employer considers it something else entirely.

Reverend Faye did not dwell on the application. She never did. She trusted Elsinore to do its own arithmetic.

Alistair, who had come despite not being strictly a churchgoing man, lingered afterward.

“Good sermon,” he said.

“Thank you,” said Reverend Faye.

“The bit about burying the talent in the ground rather than risking it.” He paused. “Very topical.”

“I thought so,” said Reverend Faye.

They stood for a moment in the comfortable silence of two people who understand what a stage is for, and what it isn’t for, and do not need to discuss this further.

The sign at the edge of town still says A Smart Community.

The swans remain largely unconvinced.

On the Kemp Road, the fence is mended. The barn cats have submitted no report.

And Maggie, who has not yet been replaced, was seen on Tuesday morning walking to the clinic with the particular stride of someone who speaks fluent cat, and knows it, and finds the comparison to a chatbot less flattering to the chatbot than Gerald intended.

Her brother Jim, for his part, showed up at the Legion on Thursday. He did not mention AI. He did not need to. He won three hands before Reg Plimpton arrived and lost two of them back again afterward, which is the sort of variance a human can live with, and did.
