San Francisco Federal Courthouse, Courtroom 12, 19th Floor. Seventy-five minutes. Seven objectors. Two minutes each.

On the afternoon of May 14, 2026, in Courtroom 12 on the nineteenth floor of the San Francisco Federal Courthouse, Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín convened the fairness hearing in Bartz v. Anthropic — the $1.5 billion class action settlement that is, depending on who you ask, either the most important copyright ruling in the history of artificial intelligence, or the most expensive capitulation in the history of publishing.

The hearing lasted seventy-five minutes.

Seven people were given two minutes each to object.

The courtroom itself had only a handful of people in attendance, and the Zoom feed — which was the principal way for class members and the public to follow the hearing — was sparsely populated as well. The objectors who appeared did so almost entirely by Zoom. Only one attorney appeared in the courtroom in person.

This is what the first serious public accountability moment in AI copyright law looked like: a nearly empty courtroom, seven people on a Zoom call, two minutes apiece, and a settlement that had already been accepted by 91.3 percent of eligible works before a single objector spoke.

The judge took the matter under submission. She did not rule from the bench. Most observers expect she will approve the settlement. The question before the court, technically, is whether the settlement is fair, reasonable, and adequate. The question before history is something else entirely.

What the Settlement Actually Settled

Before turning to what happened in Courtroom 12, it is worth being precise about what Bartz v. Anthropic resolved — and what it did not — because the press coverage has been, as one legal analyst observed, widely confused on this point.

Bartz is not really a settlement about AI training. In June 2025, Judge William Alsup ruled that Anthropic’s use of books to train its large language models was fair use, and he also concluded that Anthropic was entitled to scan books it had lawfully acquired. Nothing about this settlement disturbs those rulings.

What the settlement resolves is narrower and more specific: Anthropic’s potential liability for downloading and storage of files from two shadow libraries — Library Genesis (LibGen) and Pirate Library Mirror. Seven million books, downloaded from pirate websites, stored in what internal documents described as a permanent central library of “all the books in the world.” That is what the $1.5 billion covers.

Seven million books. Downloaded from pirate websites. Stored in what Anthropic called "a permanent central library of all the books in the world.

The training-on-legally-purchased-books question — the fair use ruling that the AI industry desperately needed and received from Judge Alsup — is untouched. It remains the law. The settlement money is not compensation for training. It is compensation for piracy. That distinction matters enormously, and it has been almost universally elided in the coverage.

What this means, practically: the $1.5 billion does not establish a precedent that AI companies owe authors money for training on their work. It establishes that AI companies owe authors money for stealing their work. These are different propositions with different legal implications, and the AI industry’s relief at the settlement reflects, in part, relief that the line was drawn where it was.

The Arithmetic of Justice

The claims rate at the hearing had been updated to 92.77 percent — 447,576 of 482,460 works on the Works List — and roughly 53 objections had been logged.

The claims rate is striking. The 91.3 percent figure represents a claims rate ten times the typical class action average. Authors knew their books had been taken. They filed claims in numbers that suggest the notice reached people who understood what they were being asked to sign. The Authors Alliance noted, however, that the headline percentage is somewhat misleading: because large publishers hold rights in tens of thousands of works and presumably filed claims for all of them, the actual percentage of individual human authors who responded is almost certainly lower.

The payout per work is approximately $3,000. The statutory ceiling per work is $150,000. The gap between those two numbers — $147,000, per work, per author — is the number that defines the settlement’s critics.

Attorney James Bartolomei, appearing on behalf of some objectors, put it directly to the court: “I’m not here to ask this court to stop this settlement from ever being approved. My clients want what every class member deserves, which is sufficient information to make an informed, knowing, and intelligent choice as to whether $3,000 is worth releasing a claim for books pirated by Anthropic that are worth up to $150,000 per work.”

That is the fairness hearing’s central exchange, compressed to one sentence. The objectors were not arguing that the settlement was wrong. They were arguing that authors who accepted $3,000 may not have understood they were releasing a claim worth fifty times more.

Robert Jackson, one of two objectors who raised broader questions about the settlement’s terms, argued that approximately $3,000 per work was only about “2% of the $150,000 statutory ceiling available” and asked the court to weigh whether that figure was “fair, reasonable, and adequate” — the legal standard for approval.

Randy Hoopman raised a different objection, one that goes to the heart of what a one-time settlement can and cannot resolve: “I don’t really understand how it can be just if Anthropic is continuing to profit from essentially stealing our work even after the settlement is complete. A one-time payment doesn’t really seem like justice.”

Hoopman’s objection is not, strictly speaking, a legal argument. It is a moral one. The court cannot adjudicate it. But it names the problem that no settlement can solve: Anthropic built its models on those seven million pirated books, the models will continue generating revenue for years, and the authors who wrote the books will receive $3,000 and release all further claims. The books are inside the machine now. The machine is not going back.

The Group Registration Problem

Among the more technically specific objections was one that reveals how settlement design can create arbitrary inequities that no one planned but no one fixed.

Victoria Pinder, author of more than forty independently published novels, registered her books under a single lawful group copyright registration. Under the settlement’s terms, each copyright registration number counts as one “claimable work.” An author who registered forty books individually would receive forty shares. Pinder receives one — for the same number of distinct books, each separately used by Anthropic, each with its own ISBN or ASIN.

She reports that when she raised this with Class Counsel, she was told that counting her individually published books separately could constitute a “windfall.”

A windfall. Forty books, forty separate works pirated by Anthropic, forty separate ISBN numbers — and being paid for forty of them rather than one would constitute a windfall. The logic of large-scale settlement arithmetic produces outcomes that, viewed from the position of a single author, look less like justice and more like arithmetic applied without judgment.

The court was not moved to address this on the merits. The supplemental briefing ordered after the hearing concerned late opt-outs, not group registrations. Pinder’s forty books will likely be counted as one.

The Notice Problem

One objector, described in filings, reported that the settlement notice was not received until approximately March 3, 2026 — almost a full month after the deadline to opt out of the class settlement had expired.

This is the late opt-out question that generated the judge’s supplemental briefing order. Following the hearing, Judge Martínez-Olguín filed an order stating that Anthropic has until May 21 to file a supplemental brief of no more than two pages addressing why late opt-outs should not be honoured in the lawsuit. She also wrote that she did not require anything more from the objectors nor will she consider any further submissions from them.

The supplemental brief order is the hearing’s most significant procedural outcome — the one signal that the judge was not simply rubber-stamping the settlement as presented. An author who received notice after the opt-out deadline had already passed could not meaningfully exercise the right to opt out. If the notice was defective — either through failure of delivery or inadequate distribution — the settlement’s fairness is compromised at the procedural level before the substantive $3,000-versus-$150,000 question is even reached.

Anthropic filed its supplemental brief on May 21. The court has not yet ruled. Final approval is pending.

What Happened in the Meantime

Dave Eggers, one of 28 authors who opted out and filed Cruz v. Anthropic. They want a jury trial. And a permanent injunction.

While the fairness hearing was pending and the settlement was under submission, the opt-out authors were not waiting.

Twenty-eight authors and rights holders who opted out of the Bartz settlement — including Angie Cruz, Dave Eggers, and Vendela Vida — filed a non-class action in the Northern District of California demanding a jury trial on statutory damages plus a permanent injunction against continued Claude training and deployment on their works. A follow-on suit, Kwon v. Anthropic, was filed two days later by 34 additional authors.

The permanent injunction demand is worth pausing on. Most copyright plaintiffs seek money. These plaintiffs are seeking to stop the models from using their work — not a payment for past piracy, but a court order preventing future deployment of AI trained on their books. Whether such an injunction is legally available, given Judge Alsup’s fair use ruling on training, is a question the opt-out suits will eventually force the court to answer.

The six-defendant structure of the opt-out suits — Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, xAI, and Perplexity all named simultaneously — is itself a statement. This is not a targeted complaint about one company’s shadow library downloads. This is an industry-wide claim about the systematic appropriation of literary work without permission or payment, brought by authors who have calculated that $3,000 per book, per company, across six companies, is a better outcome than accepting $3,000 once and walking away.

They may be right. The math requires the claims to succeed. That is a different question.

The Canadian Dimension

One detail in the settlement’s geographic scope deserves attention from this particular vantage point.

Author George Tombs objected to the exclusion of many works, including foreign works, that were denied participation in the settlement because they lacked a U.S. copyright registration.

Canadian authors whose books were taken — and there are many, given the shadow libraries’ indifference to national borders — may be excluded from the settlement entirely if their works were not registered with the U.S. Copyright Office. Canadian copyright law does not require registration. American settlement mechanics do. The result is that a Canadian author whose book was downloaded from LibGen and used to train Claude may have no claim in the Bartz settlement and no obvious path to a separate remedy, while an American author in identical circumstances receives $3,000.

This is not the settlement’s most pressing inequity. It is, however, one that a Canadian outsider watching these proceedings from Toronto finds worth noting.

What Comes Next

Final approval of the Bartz settlement is expected, possibly within weeks of this dispatch. When it arrives, it will resolve the shadow library question for the class members who filed claims, and it will protect Anthropic from further litigation over LibGen and Pirate Library Mirror.

It will not resolve anything else.

The opt-out suits — Cruz, Kwon, and the Carreyrou group — will continue. The New York Times litigation against OpenAI will continue. The music publisher claims will continue. The question of whether scraped website content qualifies as fair use has not been litigated to judgment. The question of whether AI-generated output qualifies for Section 230 protection has not been resolved. The question of whether Congress will legislate on any of this remains, as it has been for three years, unanswered.

The Bartz settlement is the Copyright Wars’ first major battle. The hearing lasted seventy-five minutes. Seven people got two minutes each.

The war has not ended. It has found its footing.
