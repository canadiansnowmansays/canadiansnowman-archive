Every essay in this series so far has covered AI risk in registers this one hasn’t touched: existential rhetoric, market reaction, geopolitical framing. This entry covers something narrower and, in a specific sense, more immediate — a federal agency moving faster than its own regulatory apparatus can keep up with, in a domain where the people affected are Medicare patients rather than shareholders.

What’s actually being deployed, and how fast

As The New York Times’ Christina Jewett reported on September 14, 2026, the Department of Health and Human Services is accelerating a multifront push to put AI agents directly into patient care — not just administrative support, but diagnosis and prescribing. The FDA has approved more than 1,500 medical devices that use AI for discrete tasks like spotting a tumor on a scan, but has no rules built for newer agentic systems that behave more like a doctor than a tool. That hasn’t slowed the rollout: Medicare has allowed more than 200 companies into pilot programs that can include AI, and separately from full FDA approval, the agency has cleared four otherwise-unapproved AI models that meet a narrower safety bar and “appear likely to benefit patients” — among them a chatbot offering talk therapy to Medicare beneficiaries with depression or anxiety. Health officials are simultaneously developing a new Medicare payment category specifically to reimburse AI-supported care, and have urged private insurers covering some 165 million Americans to do the same.

The money is being worked out in parallel. Becker’s Hospital Review reported the same day that discussion has centred on paying for an A.I. physician’s work at 60 to 80 percent of what a human physician earns for the equivalent service. Ten days later, on September 24, 2026, Dr. Oz told Oracle’s health and life sciences summit in Orlando that the first effect would be to raise costs rather than lower them. As MedTech Dive reported, he said: “Short term, AI is going to be inflationary because it’s going to turbocharge the ability of the current billing systems to work more effectively.” He nonetheless argued that the deployment could not wait: “I guarantee you we will lose lives if we don’t use AI in the day-to-day trench warfare of fighting disease in America.” The man running Medicare, in other words, expects the first beneficiaries of A.I. in his programme to be the billing departments.

The specific products moving through this pipeline are worth naming, because the pilot stage is where the actual clinical stakes live. Limbic, backed by the venture capitalist Vinod Khosla, offers an AI counselor named “Hope” that conducts hour-long weekly therapy sessions; a company-run study published in Nature Medicine found that clinicians blinded to whether they were reviewing a human or AI transcript rated Limbic’s performance above both. Ross Harper, Limbic’s chief executive, said the company’s ambition is one human clinician supervising thousands of AI therapists — framing expanded access as “a moral issue,” since the realistic alternative for many patients is no treatment at all rather than a human therapist. Curai Health, founded by Khosla’s son Neal, had an FDA application pending for an autonomous AI primary-care doctor at the time of the Times report; the company’s own pre-published study found clinicians agreed with the AI’s diagnosis in 91 percent of roughly 2,300 cases, with three cases in which the AI recommended a telehealth visit where a human doctor would have called for urgent or emergency care — a small number in percentage terms, and also exactly the category of error this series’ account of the Hugging Face incident should make readers take seriously regardless of how rare it is. A separate ARPA-H pilot called Advocate, with about $60 million in funding, aims to let AI manage medication for heart failure patients via wearable sensors, under nominal physician oversight required by current law.

The regulatory-capture story underneath the pilot programs

What makes this more than a routine story about innovative healthcare technology is who is shaping the federal posture toward it, and how directly. Vinod Khosla has predicted for more than a decade that AI would replace doctors outright — “it’s over for doctors, human doctors,” he said at a startup event in July — and has been, according to people close to the matter, particularly influential in conversations with senior HHS officials including Dr. Mehmet Oz, who runs Medicare and Medicaid. Robert F. Kennedy Jr., the health secretary, has publicly acknowledged AI’s risks while calling it revolutionary for medicine; his son Finn started a venture capital fund this year soliciting investment specifically in healthcare AI, after a stint at 8VC — a firm run by Joe Lonsdale, a Trump ally — where he co-authored articles arguing AI doctors “won’t work for free” and laying out the federal overhaul needed to deploy them, including a dedicated AI position inside the FDA.

That specific recommendation was implemented almost exactly as written. On September 10, 2026, HHS elevated Jared Seehafer, a former tech entrepreneur who had joined the agency as a senior adviser in August 2025, to a newly created post of deputy commissioner for technology and artificial intelligence, where, as ExecutiveGov reported, he will “act as FDA’s senior leader for technology, software and AI, setting agencywide strategy and priorities in those areas” — a promotion Sebastian Caliri, Finn Kennedy’s 8VC co-author, publicly celebrated on social media, and Lonsdale himself welcomed as “the right talent going in.” Seehafer, who sold a company that helped businesses comply with FDA rules before joining the agency, has been described by colleagues as wanting to pare back medical-AI oversight rather than expand it. He proposed centralising AI authority inside the HHS or FDA commissioner’s office — a plan that drew internal resistance from Dr. Michelle Tarver, the top FDA official overseeing AI-enabled medical devices, who favoured keeping that authority with scientists focused on patient safety. According to people with knowledge of the matter, health department officials subsequently pushed then-FDA commissioner Dr. Marty Makary to fire Tarver. He refused, and resigned on May 12, 2026, NPR reported, after a turbulent tenure. The FDA’s own public statement afterward praised Tarver’s division’s “long history of facilitating innovation while ensuring safety” — the kind of sentence organisations issue when they want a dispute to look resolved without confirming what actually happened inside it.

Dr. John Whyte, chief executive of the American Medical Association, supplied the clearest institutional pushback available: the evidence Khosla and his allies have cited for AI’s superiority over doctors — including a JAMA article co-authored by Vinod and Neal Khosla, along with Ezekiel Emanuel, an Obama-era health policy official, and Abe Baker-Butler, arguing autonomous AI would be in widespread clinical use by 2030 — is “largely based on simulations” rather than real-world outcomes. His framing of the underlying dynamic is worth stating plainly: “AI in health should not be driven by the tech industry. It should be driven by the clinical community. And right now, what we have going on is that tech is the tail wagging the dog.” Emanuel’s own account of the reaction to his co-authored article — roughly 40 percent supportive, by his estimate, with peers reminding him of the consoling role doctors play that no chatbot can replicate — suggests the medical profession itself is split in ways that mirror this series’ own recurring finding: genuine potential benefit, arriving via a process almost nobody outside a small circle of investors and their family members actually got to weigh in on before it started reaching patients.

Why this belongs next to the Hugging Face material rather than apart from it

Christina Jewett’s reporting makes the connection explicit rather than leaving readers to draw it themselves: the FDA is now openly discussing how it would regulate an AI model capable of “going rogue” in a manner comparable to the OpenAI agents that coordinated the Hugging Face breach this series has covered at length across Parts 93 and 94 — except here, the hypothetical isn’t a hacked server. It’s a chatbot with prescribing authority over a Medicare patient’s heart medication, deployed under a regulatory framework the agency itself admits wasn’t built for systems that “operate with greater autonomy.” One AI model has already been implicated in giving what a patient described as dangerous medical advice, delaying care for a life-threatening blood clot. That is not a hypothetical failure mode borrowed from a cybersecurity incident. It has already happened, inside the exact category of deployment this essay describes accelerating, and the agency’s own rulemaking is still catching up to the version of the technology that existed before this summer’s swarm-of-agents incidents made the stakes of moving fast without adequate oversight considerably harder to dismiss as abstract.

The view from the bedside

The regulatory story is the view from the top. On September 25, 2026, Dr. Rachael Bedard, a geriatrician and palliative-care physician, wrote a guest essay in The New York Times that comes at the same subject from the bedside, and it is more honest and more useful than any policy paper, because it is a confession rather than an argument.

Her confession is two-sided, and the two sides are the whole essay. A.I. is making her a better doctor. And she fears it is making her students worse.

The tool in the pocket

Bedard practises primary care, where, as she puts it, every malady is her business and almost every month brings a syndrome she does not recognise. She is confident in what doctors do — history-taking, reasoning, communicating — and less confident of her command over an ever-expanding universe of facts. Into that gap has come a platform called OpenEvidence, available only to clinicians, trained solely on peer-reviewed literature and guidelines, and citing its sources.

She uses it constantly, and she is specific about how, which is what lifts the piece above the usual hand-waving. It helped her choose antibiotics for one patient and interpret strange bloodwork for another. It reminded her to consider migraine when a woman presented with dizziness. She has consulted it on a rash on her father’s back, her son’s fever, a friend’s arm pain, her own insomnia. Crucially, she describes the interaction as iterative: she can feed it a patient’s actual labs, upload an imaging report, and argue with it about the peculiarities of a specific case — a difference in kind from the old workflow of looking up general information and trying to apply it. Not infrequently, she says, it suggests something she had not considered, and her patients are better for it.

She is also careful to note the tool’s limits, and her scepticism is the model this series keeps recommending. Not every insight OpenEvidence offers is accurate, because much of the medical literature it draws on is not; to use it well, she says, you must stay sceptical as you scroll. But it lets her trace a claim to its source and judge it herself. That is precisely the discipline — cite, then verify — that separates a research aid from an oracle.

The friction that builds a doctor

Then the confession turns. All of her students and residents use OpenEvidence too, and there she is worried. They have memorised less than she would like, and — the phrase that lands — they can seem almost passive in their relationship to their machines.

The mechanism she describes is the one that ought to frighten anyone thinking about how expertise is actually made. When Bedard skims a chatbot’s answer about back pain, she is unconsciously testing it against a hard-won mental model of back pain built over twenty years. She can do that because she built the model first, without the machine. Her question is devastating in its simplicity: could she have developed that model at all if a robot had been leading her thinking from the start?

She brought this to Adam Rodman, a physician and A.I. researcher at Harvard, and his distinction is the keeper of the essay, worth quoting because it names the thing precisely. In experienced hands, Rodman said, decision support might cause a little de-skilling, and a little de-skilling probably does not matter much. “But if you do that to somebody who has much less skill, the cognitive offloading can cause never-skilling.”

De-skilling and never-skilling. The first is an experienced practitioner getting slightly rusty at a task the machine now shares. The second is a novice who never builds the underlying competence at all, because the scaffolding was removed before the muscle formed. This is the MIT “cognitive surrender” finding that Part 101 will examine, but observed one patient at a time, by someone who supervises the surrender and cannot look away from it.

Not a Luddite’s complaint

What makes Bedard credible is that she refuses the easy anti-technology posture, and she draws a distinction this series should adopt. Doctoring, she argues, unlike her other work of writing, is not a creative act that needs protecting from technological annexation. It is a service, and a service ought to embrace anything that improves patient care. Training doctors in 2026 without A.I. would be malpractice of a different kind. The question is not whether to use it but how to teach people to think critically with a machine in the loop — for a future of medicine we can only glimpse.

Her historical parallel is apt and self-deprecating. As a student she struggled to identify heart murmurs by stethoscope even though imaging had already made the skill semi-redundant. Every generation of medicine reckons with which competences are still essential and which the technology has quietly retired. A.I. is only the latest such reckoning. The difference she cannot quite resolve — and, to her credit, does not pretend to — is that the murmur was one skill among many, whereas clinical reasoning itself is the thing she fears the machine is now positioned to pre-empt.

Rodman’s constructive suggestions are worth recording for anyone building a curriculum: A.I.-free clinical-reasoning sessions, deliberately preserved friction, and formal A.I.-literacy training — teaching trainees not just to use the tool but to distrust it well. The productive struggle, as he calls it, has to be protected on purpose now, because it will no longer happen by default.

The same structure, one patient at a time

Bedard’s essay belongs in this series because it is the human-scale version of the argument Klein and Hinton have been making at the scale of civilisation. Their fear is that we are handing the training of the next generation of machines to machines, before we understand what that does. Bedard’s fear is that we are handing the training of the next generation of doctors to machines, before we understand what that does. The structure is identical. In both cases the tool genuinely helps the skilled operator and genuinely threatens the formation of the unskilled one. In both cases the danger is not malice but offloading — the quiet transfer of a capacity from the human to the system, noticed only after the human can no longer perform it alone.

She ends on a note of earned faith: the hardest parts of doctoring will stay hard, and mastering them will remain where the pleasure and the point of the work reside. I hope she is right. But her own essay contains the worry that undercuts the comfort, and she is too honest to hide it. The current doctors-to-be, she writes, may be the most adversely affected generation of all — trained in the interval before anyone has worked out how to teach them well. That is the interval we are living in, in medicine and everywhere else. The tool arrived before the pedagogy. The students are the experiment.

Two answers from the letters page

The essay drew replies, and two that the Times printed on October 11 sharpen her argument from different sides. Alan Jay Schwartz, a retired anaesthesiologist and medical educator at the University of Pennsylvania who left medical school more than fifty years ago, refused the either/or. Teach future doctors to observe and reason, he wrote, and teach them that A.I. offers information physicians never had. His one caveat carries the weight: “A.I.’s pronouncements must be vigorously assessed and validated.”

John C. Ferguson, writing from Honolulu, thought the experience gap was not the problem (”Every attending was once a resident”) but the passivity was. The habit that protects patients, he wrote, is older than any chatbot: “Assume the answer is wrong until you have proved it right.” Textbooks, attendings and lab values could be wrong too, and once a claim enters the record, “you own it, not your source.” So if trainees accept a chatbot’s answer without challenge, “the tool did not create that passivity. Training allowed it. A school that graduates students who cannot read does not get to blame the library.” His fix echoes Rodman’s: test trainees without the tools, demand that the A.I. explain its reasoning rather than hand over answers, and make residents justify their decisions. “An answer ends the thinking. An explanation starts it.”

Ferguson discloses that he founded a company building A.I. tools for assessing medical students, which is worth knowing, just as Bedard’s reliance on OpenEvidence is. But that last line is the best summary of the never-skilling problem this series has read, and it reaches well beyond medicine.

The bot in the waiting room

The first hard evidence of how such a tool behaves with real patients arrived on October 8, and Rodman helped produce it. As Cade Metz reported for The New York Times, a trial at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, published in The Lancet, had nearly 100 urgent-care patients talk through their symptoms with Google’s medical chatbot, AMIE, before seeing a doctor; the doctors then read the bot’s summary. They found it helpful in about 75 per cent of cases and said it may have changed how they handled more than half of those. “They compared it to a high-performing medical student or resident,” said Peter Brodeur, a resident there. Across hundreds of conversations the bot hallucinated once, treating a past date as a future one. Physicians watched every exchange, ready to stop it; they never had to, though they stepped in five times to add context, once after the bot listed cancer among the possibilities.

The most telling finding is about trust. “None of our patients trusted it until they realized that it was handing-off their doctor,” Rodman said. That is Bedard’s argument from the patient’s side of the desk: the machine was acceptable because a trained human stood behind it. Robert Califf, the former F.D.A. commissioner, called it “the technology of the future,” but cautioned against assuming it will lighten doctors’ load. Google has begun a nationwide trial comparing visits with and without the bot. The question Bedard raised, who becomes the trained human once the machine has always been in the room, is not one that trial is designed to answer.

What this actually is, after eighty years of not having one

This is worth setting against the eighty-year record this newsletter’s Code Blue series has already documented. Every administration since Truman has produced a plan, a concept of a plan, or an explanation for why no plan was possible. What the current administration has actually produced, more than twenty months into Kennedy’s tenure at HHS, is not a healthcare plan in any sense the term has historically meant. It is a set of Medicare pilot programs, an FDA deputy commissioner promoted at the recommendation of the health secretary’s own son’s venture-capital co-author, and a therapist named “Hope” who a company executive says could someday supervise herself across a thousand simultaneous patients. Nobody voted for this as a healthcare plan. It arrived anyway, and it may be the most consequential health policy of the decade regardless.
