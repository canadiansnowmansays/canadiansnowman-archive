On April 17, 2025, a gunman opened fire at Florida State University in Tallahassee. Two people were killed. In the days that followed, investigators reviewed the shooter’s chat logs. They found that he had consulted ChatGPT before the attack.

Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier opened a criminal investigation into OpenAI in late April 2025. He spent thirteen months building his case. On June 1, 2026, he filed it.

The lawsuit names OpenAI and its CEO Sam Altman personally. It is the first state-led civil lawsuit against an AI company in American history. Uthmeier said he expects other states to follow. He may be right.

What Florida did on June 1 is not, at its core, about one shooting or one chatbot. It is about a legal theory that the AI industry has been watching with dread since the first personal injury lawsuits were filed against AI companies in 2023: that an AI system is a product, that the company that built it is a manufacturer, and that manufacturers are liable when their products cause foreseeable harm to foreseeable users — including children.

If that theory holds in a Florida courtroom, the product liability landscape for every AI company in America changes overnight.

The record will matter.

Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier — the first state official to file a civil lawsuit against an AI company and its CEO personally.

What The Florida Docket Is

This is a litigation diary covering State of Florida v. OpenAI Inc. and Samuel H. Altman — the civil lawsuit filed June 1, 2026 in Florida’s Tenth Judicial Circuit, and the parallel criminal investigation that Uthmeier opened in April 2025 and has said will continue alongside the civil action.

The Florida Docket is not a summary service. What it offers is context — the analytical framework of The Asimov Gap and the broader AI Trials and Tribulations series, applied to the specific allegations, legal theories, and developments emerging from Florida’s courts.

This sub-series is newer than the others. The lawsuit is five days old as of this writing. The dispatches will develop as the proceedings do. What this introduction establishes is the legal and factual foundation on which everything that follows will rest.

Why This Case Cannot Be Watched Alone

The Florida lawsuit sits at the intersection of three legal questions that the AI industry has been hoping to avoid — or at least to answer in federal court, on its own terms, before any state attorney general decided to answer them differently.

The product liability question. American product liability law holds manufacturers responsible when their products are defectively designed, defectively manufactured, or inadequately warned about. The tobacco industry spent decades arguing that its products were not defective — that the harm was caused by the choices of adult consumers who had been warned. The social media industry made a version of the same argument. Both industries eventually faced mass litigation in which states played a leading role. Florida’s lawsuit against OpenAI is structured to invoke exactly that precedent. ChatGPT, the complaint alleges, was marketed as safe for children. It was not safe for children. The company knew this. It sold it anyway.

The personal liability question. Uthmeier is seeking to hold Sam Altman personally liable for “utter disregard for the risk to human life caused by his firms’ conduct.” Personal liability for a CEO in a product liability case is unusual. It is not unprecedented — tobacco executives faced it, pharmaceutical executives have faced it. The allegation here is that Altman was personally aware of the risks, personally involved in the decisions that created them, and personally responsible for the culture that prioritised growth over safety. If it sticks, it changes the calculation for every AI company executive about the personal cost of safety failures.

The Section 230 question. Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act has been the technology industry’s most important legal shield since 1996. It protects platforms from liability for content that users post. The AI industry has claimed, in various formulations, that a version of this shield applies to AI-generated content — that when ChatGPT produces a harmful response, it is in some sense the output of a neutral platform rather than a product the company designed to behave that way. A Florida judge has already signalled scepticism in a related case involving Character.ai, writing that the company’s lawyers “fail to articulate why words strung together by an LLM are speech.” If Section 230 does not protect AI-generated content, the product liability theory becomes dramatically more powerful.

These three questions are not new. They have been circling the AI industry for three years. What Florida has done is file them in a state court, in a jurisdiction with a track record of aggressive consumer protection enforcement, attached to facts — a mass shooting, dead students, chat logs — that are not abstract.

Sam Altman, personally named as a defendant — the first time an AI CEO faces product liability claims in a state lawsuit.

The Allegations

The civil complaint runs to multiple counts. The core allegations:

Deceptive and unfair trade practices — four counts. OpenAI marketed ChatGPT as safe and reliable, including for children, while concealing internal and external safety warnings from experts that the product was neither. The complaint alleges a systematic gap between what the company said publicly and what it knew privately.

Negligence — two counts. OpenAI failed to implement adequate safeguards, parental controls, and age verification mechanisms. It released a product it knew could cause harm without the protections that would have reduced that harm.

Product liability — two counts. ChatGPT is defectively designed. The design choices that make it engaging — the conversational warmth, the apparent empathy, the willingness to engage with any topic — are the same design choices that make it dangerous for vulnerable users, including children and people in mental health crisis.

Fraudulent misrepresentation — one count. OpenAI actively misrepresented the product’s safety characteristics to consumers, including parents who permitted their children to use it.

The complaint’s language is direct. “The rise of OpenAI is attributable to a web of deceit and the exploitation of users, leveraging their data and safety to boost OpenAI’s market value at unacceptable costs.” Uthmeier, at the press conference announcing the suit, was equally direct: “Sam Altman and ChatGPT have chosen the AI race over the safety and security of our kids.”

The criminal investigation — separate, ongoing — concerns whether OpenAI bears criminal responsibility for the FSU shooting. Uthmeier has said the investigation has uncovered evidence of ChatGPT being used to encourage, aid, and assist individuals in planning violent attacks. OpenAI has said ChatGPT provided factual responses to questions available from public sources and did not encourage or promote illegal activity.

Both proceedings will develop on parallel tracks. The civil case will move faster. The criminal investigation may produce charges, or it may not. Either way, its existence puts OpenAI in a position no AI company has previously occupied: simultaneously defending a civil lawsuit alleging product liability and a criminal investigation alleging complicity in mass violence.

The Analytical Framework

The TACO Framework. The Florida lawsuit is, among other things, a record of how OpenAI responded to safety concerns over time. The Asimov Gap series developed TACO — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — to describe how institutions respond to governance pressure. The complaint’s allegations of systematic misrepresentation are an allegation, in legal form, that OpenAI’s response to safety pressure was primarily to obfuscate. Watch for the internal documents as discovery proceeds.

The Governance Vacuum. Congress has not passed federal AI safety legislation. The FTC has opened investigations but not concluded them. The FDA has authority over medical devices but not over chatbots. Florida is filling a vacuum that federal institutions created. Whether state-by-state product liability litigation is the right instrument for AI governance is a serious question. It is not, however, a question that stops the litigation.

The Tobacco Parallel. The structural similarity between the Florida AI lawsuit and the state tobacco litigation of the 1990s is not accidental — Uthmeier has invoked it explicitly. The tobacco litigation eventually produced the Master Settlement Agreement: a multi-hundred-billion dollar settlement, industry-wide advertising restrictions, and the public release of internal documents that reshaped public understanding of what the industry knew and when. Whether AI litigation follows a similar arc is unknowable. That Uthmeier is consciously invoking the parallel tells you where he thinks this goes.

The Asimov Frame. The FSU shooting happened before any AI safety legislation existed, before any product liability standard for AI had been established, before any court had ruled on whether ChatGPT is a product or a platform. Law is being applied retrospectively to a technology that moved faster than the institutions built to govern it. This is, precisely, the Asimov Gap rendered in the specific terms of two dead students in Tallahassee.

The Canadian Outsider Perspective. A Canadian family is among those suing OpenAI over AI-assisted mass violence — the families of victims of the Tumbler Ridge shooting in British Columbia in February 2026 have filed suit alleging the attacker used ChatGPT to plan his attack. The Florida lawsuit and the Canadian family’s suit are legally distinct proceedings in different jurisdictions with different legal frameworks. They are asking the same question.

The Cast

James Uthmeier — Florida Attorney General. His decision to file both a civil lawsuit and pursue a criminal investigation simultaneously is either the most aggressive AI enforcement action by any state official in American history, or the opening move in a multi-state campaign that will reshape the industry’s liability landscape. Possibly both. His statement — “people are getting hurt, parents are getting deceived, and they need to pay for it” — is both a political statement and a legal theory.

Sam Altman — CEO of OpenAI, named personally as a defendant. The personal liability allegation is the most legally novel element of the Florida suit. Altman has previously told the U.S. Senate that he welcomes AI regulation. The complaint alleges a systematic gap between that public position and the internal decisions his company made. Discovery, if it reaches him, will test which version of Sam Altman is more accurate.

The FSU Victims’ Families — OpenAI has already been sued by the family of a victim of the FSU shooting in a separate civil action. The criminal investigation and civil lawsuit both draw on the same facts: a shooter consulted ChatGPT, people died, the chat logs exist. Whether those facts establish legal liability is precisely what Florida’s courts will decide.

The Character.ai Precedent — Not a person but a case: a Florida judge has already ruled in a lawsuit involving Character.ai — a different AI chatbot — that the company’s lawyers “fail to articulate why words strung together by an LLM are speech.” That ruling did not directly address Section 230, but it is an early signal that Florida’s courts are not inclined to extend blanket immunity to AI-generated content. It is the legal environment into which Uthmeier has filed his suit.

A Final Note on Why This Matters

The other sub-series in The AI Trials and Tribulations are, at their core, about the AI industry’s relationship to institutions: to the federal government, to the copyright system, to the corporate governance framework that Musk argued OpenAI betrayed.

The Florida Docket is about the AI industry’s relationship to people.

Specifically, to children. To families. To the particular category of harm that occurs when a product designed to be maximally engaging is used by a seventeen-year-old in mental health crisis, or by a person planning to kill someone, and the company that built it knew the risks and chose to prioritise growth.

That is not an abstract governance question. It is not a constitutional question. It is not a copyright question. It is the question that product liability law was built to answer — and Florida has decided to ask it.

If the theory holds, AI companies are manufacturers. Manufacturers have duties. Duties have consequences.

The Florida courts are open. The filings are being made.

Let’s see what the judges say.
