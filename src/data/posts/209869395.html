Nobody owns the sky, technically. Ask any government and they’ll tell you, with a straight face, that the airspace above your house belongs to the nation, right up until it doesn’t — right up until a satellite passes overhead at eight kilometres a second, a guest nobody invited and nobody can really evict.

But somewhere between the ground and orbit, there’s a layer that absolutely is owned, fenced, licensed, and fought over with the same seriousness nations reserve for oil fields and shipping lanes. It’s called spectrum — the radio frequencies that carry every phone call, every text message, every video buffering on a bus. You cannot see it, weigh it, or hold it in your hand. And yet a government can sell a slice of it for billions of dollars, and a company can spend a decade in court trying to get a slice back.

This series is about what happens when the company best equipped to ignore that fact tries to sell you a phone plan anyway.

On August 4, 2026, SpaceX filed its first-ever earnings report as a public company, and buried in the usual avalanche of numbers was a sentence that ought to have every wireless carrier on the planet checking its rear-view mirror. President Gwynne Shotwell described a new 65-megahertz spectrum acquisition as giving Starlink Mobile “a massive increase in capability.” CFO Bret Johnsen laid out a timeline: commercial mobile service by the end of 2027. Global direct-to-device coverage by 2028.

Read that plan next to how telecommunications actually works, and something doesn’t add up. Spectrum licensing is one of the slowest-moving processes in modern government — auctions that take years to design, consultations that take years to run, incumbents who’ve spent decades locking up whatever’s left. SpaceX is promising to do all of that, in every country that matters, inside about eighteen months.

It can’t. Not by owning spectrum outright, anyway. Which is the real story here — not a rocket company selling phone plans, but a rocket company solving, market by market, the oldest problem in telecommunications: how do you get on the air in a country that doesn’t want to sell you the air?

The answer, it turns out, already exists, quietly, in places like Canada, where my own household sits — by accident — on opposite sides of a race most Canadians don’t know is happening. Rogers signed with SpaceX years ago and now offers satellite texting nationwide. My own carrier, Telus (by way of its Koodo brand), bet on the other horse entirely — a rival satellite operator with a tenth of a percent of Starlink’s constellation. Two Canadian companies, two incompatible bets, and somewhere in the middle of it, ordinary customers who mostly just want their phone to work and their bill to stay the same.

That last part matters more than the satellites do, and it’s the thread that will run under everything else in this series. I’ve spent the better part of a year, in another series on this Substack, documenting how Canadian telecom billing quietly extracts money from people who aren’t paying attention. The lesson from that fight applies here too, maybe more so: the deciding factor in whether Starlink Mobile succeeds in a country like Canada will not be whether its satellites are better. It will be whether its price beats what a determined customer can already negotiate out of a retention desk over the phone, for free, in fifteen minutes. Superior technology has lost to a cheaper status quo before. It will happen again here unless SpaceX reckons with it directly.

Over the parts that follow, I’ll trace the actual shape of this expansion: who SpaceX has already signed and who it’s still courting, why its strategy inside the United States looks nothing like its strategy everywhere else, what gets disrupted along the way, and whether the 2028 promise survives contact with a hundred and ninety different governments, each one absolutely certain that the air above its territory belongs to nobody but itself.

Who owns the air? For the moment, the honest answer is: it depends which country you ask, and how long you’re willing to wait for the answer to change.
