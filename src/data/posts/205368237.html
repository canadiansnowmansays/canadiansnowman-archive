It has been nine days since the last installment of this series posted, and I want to name that rather than pretend the gap didn’t happen. Nine days is not long in a war now well into its fifth month. But it’s long enough to say plainly: I am tired of writing about this war, and I suspect many of you are tired of reading about it. This dispatch alone covers a funeral, a fee dispute, a bombing campaign, a retaliatory strike on two countries that had nothing to do with any of it, and a president declaring the whole peace process dead from a podium in Turkey. That is not a week’s worth of news. It’s what used to be a year’s, compressed into the time it took one man’s body to travel from Tehran to Najaf. If you want to look away, you’re not wrong to feel that pull. The people actually living inside it don’t get the option.

There is a kind of theatre that only autocracies know how to stage, and Iran staged it this week with real virtuosity. On July 4, 2026 — while Americans set off fireworks for the 250th anniversary of their declaration of independence — tens of thousands of Iranians filed into Tehran’s Grand Mosalla mosque at dawn to view the flag-draped casket of Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, dead now for over four months, killed on the opening morning of a war that has yet to properly end.

The scheduling was not an accident, though which side intended the irony is hard to say. Khamenei was assassinated on February 28, the first casualty — spiritually if not chronologically — of Operation Epic Fury. It has taken Iran a full four months to bury him, a delay long enough that his casket now serves two purposes at once: a genuine object of Shiite religious mourning, and a five-city, two-country demonstration that the Islamic Republic, minus its supreme leader for nearly half a year, is still standing. By Tuesday, that demonstration would be sharing headline space with something the framework peace agreement was specifically written to prevent: American fighter jets, over Iran, again.

Six Days, Five Cities, One Very Careful Absence

The funeral itself is a logistical marvel. Khamenei’s remains, along with those of his daughter, son-in-law, and two grandchildren killed in the same strike, began their journey in Tehran on Saturday and travelled next to Qom, before crossing into Karbala and Najaf in Iraq, ahead of a final burial in Mashhad — his birthplace — on Thursday. Qom officials reportedly stockpiled 20 million bottles of water and 1.5 million sandwiches for the crowds. Some mourners, determined not to miss the viewing, slept in their cars overnight.

The one person conspicuously absent from all of it, at least so far, is the man Khamenei’s death made supreme leader: his son Mojtaba. Injured in the same strike that killed his father, Mojtaba has not been seen in public or heard on tape since taking the position in March. Iranian officials say his security team has vetoed his attendance at most of the ceremonies out of fear that Israel is still hunting him — though, as the New York Times reported from the funeral proceedings on Saturday, two IRGC officials say he has asked to appear at the burial in Mashhad and to personally recite the prayer of the dead over his father. Three senior ayatollahs will lead prayers in his stead. It is a strange kind of succession: a son so thoroughly hidden that his own father’s mourners carry photographs of him instead, hoping for a glimpse of a man who may not come.

Also missing, and more tellingly so: former presidents Mohammad Khatami, Hassan Rouhani, and Mahmoud Ahmadinejad, who appear not to have been invited to Friday’s private viewing for senior officials and foreign dignitaries. As the same New York Times funeral coverage noted, a former vice president, Mohammad Ali Abtahi, remarked dryly that the funeral “would have been a good opportunity to project unity.” Instead it has become, like so much of Iranian public life under this regime, an occasion for the government to decide in advance who counts as unified enough to be seen.

Qom, and the Ghost of a Funeral That Became a Protest

By Tuesday, the procession had reached Qom, the seat of Iran’s Shiite religious education and, not coincidentally, the city where Khamenei himself was elevated from a mid-ranking cleric to ayatollah on the eve of taking power in 1989. As the New York Times reported from the Qom ceremony, the prayer service at Jamkaran Mosque was led by Ayatollah Abdullah Javadi Amoli, one of the last living clerics from the generation that made the 1979 revolution — a choice of officiant meant to lend Khamenei a legitimacy his own religious credentials, thin at the time of his appointment, never entirely supplied.

Qom carries its own warning for a government trying this hard to stage-manage grief. The same reporting on the Qom ceremony recalled that Khamenei spent much of his rule tightening the state’s hand over the city’s clerical finances and education, and that he placed one of his most serious religious critics, Grand Ayatollah Hossein Ali Montazeri — once considered his own likely successor as supreme leader — under house arrest for years after Montazeri condemned the execution of political prisoners in the 1980s and later backed the 2009 Green Movement protesters. When Montazeri died in 2009, hundreds of thousands turned out for his funeral in Qom, and it became, by the same account, an opposition protest in all but name. Iran’s government is evidently aware that funerals in this country do not always stay funerals. It has built this one, five cities and six days of it, specifically so that it doesn’t.

A City Performing Its Grief, and Meaning Some of It

What makes this week’s coverage worth your time — beyond the obvious spectacle of hundreds of thousands beating their chests under a 90-degree sun — is what the New York Times found in its own Tehran dispatch, filed by correspondents who were, for the first time since before the war, allowed into the country itself. It came with strings: a government translator, a government guide, restricted movement. The reporters were candid about this, which is more than the arrangement deserves credit for, but also exactly the kind of caveat a Canadian reader should keep in mind before taking any single quote gathered under those conditions as the last word on what Iranians actually feel.

Still, what came through was a country in visible contradiction with itself. According to that same Tehran dispatch, at Enghelab Square, women in denim skirts and uncovered hair smoked cigarettes a few metres from women in black chadors praying on the pavement, all of it beneath a mourning billboard the size of a building. One woman, asked how she felt, told the paper’s correspondents, “I am afraid I won’t be able to say what’s in my heart.” That single sentence tells you more about the state of the Islamic Republic in July 2026 than the entire choreographed processional.

Because underneath the flags and the sandwiches and the misting stations, Tehran is a city running on fumes. The Times’ Tehran dispatch found that war damage still scars major roads, that Golestan Palace, a historic site, sits partly wrapped in plastic sheeting where it was hit, and that water shortages and electricity blackouts remain part of daily life. And this is a population that, six months before the war began, was already in the streets demanding the regime’s end — demonstrations the government crushed by killing thousands, according to human rights groups. Some of those same Iranians, by their own account, celebrated when they learned Khamenei had been killed.

So when the crowd at Grand Mosalla erupts into chants of “Death to America” and, repeatedly, the name of Donald Trump — as the New York Times’ funeral coverage documented on Saturday — it would be a mistake, the kind of mistake a lazier newsroom might make, to read that as evidence of a nation speaking with one voice. Some of the anger is real and organic. Some of it is a 37-year investment in anti-American ritual paying its dividends on cue. And some of the loudest voices in the crowd, per that same coverage, are hard-liners furious that the regime signed a cease-fire at all — mourners carrying banners demanding to know “what happened to revenge for the blood of our martyred Imam.” Grief, capitulation, and defiance are all being performed on the same square, sometimes by the same people, within the same hour.

Meanwhile, Somebody Still Has to Run the Country

The government’s use of scale to answer uncertainty — five cities, two countries, a week of ceremony — is not subtle, and as one Tehran historian told the New York Times in its funeral coverage, put it about as plainly as an academic ever does: “That’s the purpose of the entire exercise.” It is worth remembering that Iran has been here before. The same reporting recalled that when Ayatollah Khomeini died in 1989, mourners tore his body from its casket and shredded his burial shroud; eight people were trampled to death. The regime called it proof of devotion. This week’s version has been engineered, with considerable success, to avoid a repeat — a controlled, choreographed grief in place of the chaotic kind. Whether that control extends to the country’s politics once the cameras leave Mashhad is the real question, and nobody serious is claiming to know the answer yet.

Certainly not the negotiators. While Khamenei’s coffin has been on the road, the U.S.-Iran peace process has been, as the New York Times’ funeral coverage put it, making “little progress.” The framework agreement signed last month — the Islamabad memorandum, dated June 18 — promised a 60-day window to work out the details, chief among them the fate of the Strait of Hormuz, through which more than a fifth of the world’s oil normally passes and through which, before February, ships sailed free of charge. That promise had already collapsed in practice well before Tuesday. As the New York Times reported on the Oman fee proposal, Iran and Oman are pushing forward a fee arrangement for the strait, dressed up as “voluntary” and modeled, Oman says, on a private foundation that manages contributions in the Straits of Malacca and Singapore.

I want to be direct about why that comparison offends me, because it should offend anyone paying attention. Malacca’s arrangement grew up organically among parties who never fought a war over the passage and who all still treat transit itself as free underneath the voluntary extras. Hormuz’s “voluntary” fee is being proposed in the direct aftermath of a war in which Iran mined the strait, attacked cargo ships, and used the resulting chaos as leverage — with Oman, a country that never wanted any of this, left to draft the fig leaf that might let the arrangement survive an International Maritime Organization legality test. Calling something voluntary does not change what the alternative to paying it currently is: mines, attacked vessels, and a naval power that has explicitly said, through its own foreign minister, that the strait will not return to its prewar status. The same New York Times reporting on the Oman proposal notes that Secretary of State Marco Rubio has called any version of this “unacceptable,” in public, while an unnamed American official has told the paper, in private, that Washington is confident it can resolve the dispute “at the technical level.” I will let readers decide for themselves how much daylight belongs between those two sentences.

The Ceasefire Breaks Again, Right on Schedule

That daylight closed rather suddenly on Tuesday. Three commercial vessels were attacked transiting the strait — a Saudi oil tanker, a Qatari liquefied natural gas carrier called the Al Rekayyat, and a third ship struck by drone — in waters off the Omani coast. As the New York Times reported in its coverage of the U.S. response, Washington did not wait for Tehran to claim responsibility, which it hadn’t, before acting on two fronts at once: the Treasury Department revoked the sanctions waiver that had allowed Iran to sell oil on international markets for the previous 60 days, and U.S. Central Command launched what it called “a series of powerful strikes against Iran to impose heavy costs” for the ship attacks. Explosions were reported before dawn Wednesday in Bandar Abbas, Qeshm, and Sirik — the same stretch of Hormozgan province the U.S. had already struck once, on June 26, in response to an earlier Iranian attack on a container ship.

Iran’s government, mid-funeral, called it exactly what it was: a violation. As the New York Times’ live coverage of the sanctions and strikes noted, Iran’s deputy foreign minister said on social media that the airstrikes broke the Islamabad memorandum’s war-ending provisions, while the foreign ministry separately said the sanctions reversal breached the agreement’s terms on oil relief. Both complaints are, on the available facts, accurate. Whether that will matter to anyone is a separate question — because Iran did not stop at complaining.

Overnight Tuesday into Wednesday, Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps said it had struck 85 U.S. military sites across Bahrain and Kuwait, as the New York Times’ Wednesday live coverage reported, naming the Ali Al Salem Air Base in Kuwait and the U.S. Fifth Fleet’s naval base and Khalifa Bin Salman Port in Bahrain among the targets, and claiming to have shot down an American MQ-9 drone in the process. Kuwait’s military said it intercepted two ballistic missiles and thirteen drones with no casualties, though some power lines were damaged by falling shrapnel. Iran’s central command called the earlier American strikes an “overt act of aggression” and promised “a crushing response,” which is, credit where due, exactly what it delivered. Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf — Iran’s chief negotiator, the same man who nearly died twice in this war and once made an eight-hour overland detour home rather than risk an Israeli strike on his plane — posted his own verdict on the American violations: “The era of bullying and extortion is over. It leads nowhere. We don’t fold.”

By Wednesday morning, standing at the NATO summit in Ankara, Mr. Trump gave up the pretense of the process entirely. As the New York Times’ live coverage from Ankara quoted him, asked directly about the state of the ceasefire, he said: “To me, I think it’s over.” He went further, calling Iran’s leadership “cuckoo” and complaining, “We make a deal, and everyone’s agreed. No nuclear weapons. We make a deal. They go outside, talk to the press, they say we never even talked about it. There’s something wrong with them.” He said, in the same breath, that he’d still let his negotiating team keep talking — which is either a genuine hedge or simply a president who has learned that walking away from a deal and continuing to negotiate it are no longer mutually exclusive activities in this administration. Under the memorandum signed last month, Washington and Tehran were supposed to use their 60 days to reach a comprehensive agreement including Iran’s nuclear program. That timeline, three weeks in, now looks like the least of the document’s problems.

Even Washington’s allies split on how to read it. The same live coverage noted that NATO’s secretary general, Mark Rutte, called the American strikes “absolutely necessary” and said it was “totally crucial that the U.S. forcefully react” — a level of enthusiasm the European Union’s own foreign affairs chief, Kaja Kallas, pointedly did not share. Kallas condemned Iran’s strikes on Bahrain and Kuwait and accused Tehran of breaching the ceasefire, but stopped well short of endorsing the American bombing campaign that preceded them, warning instead that “the exchanges of fire between the US and Iran further complicate already fraught talks to end the war.” NATO’s chief and the EU’s chief, standing more or less in the same building complex, could not agree on which side started the latest round — which tells you something about how thoroughly this conflict has scrambled the usual transatlantic script.

It is worth sitting with what that oil waiver represented before it was revoked, because the logic behind it was almost elegant. The same coverage explained that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent had described the sanctions relief as “a very conditional performance-based negotiation” — carrots for Iranian factions who wanted the deal to hold, sticks for the military leadership that never did. The idea was to let ordinary economic self-interest inside Iran do some of Washington’s diplomatic work. That theory lasted almost exactly as long as the funeral did.

A Coffin Crosses a Border, and So Does the War’s Symbolism

While all of this was unfolding, Khamenei’s coffin kept moving, because a state funeral, once scheduled, apparently answers to nobody. On Wednesday it reached Najaf, Iraq, where, as the New York Times reported from the procession there, hundreds of thousands of mourners — Iraqi, not Iranian — filled the streets in 106-degree heat, pressing against the truck carrying his casket. One woman who had travelled from Baghdad at 3 a.m. told the paper, “He was our guardian and protector, and we are here today to return the favor.” An Iraqi Shiite militia fighter put the political subtext into words nobody needed translated: “The message here is that we defied the world: America, Israel, and the great global powers. We are with justice, and we are with Iran.” The same reporting quoted an Iran historian noting that the spectacle of one nation holding a state funeral for another country’s leader was itself nearly without precedent — evidence, in his words, that Tehran is working to position Khamenei as belonging to the entire Shiite world, not just to Iran, at the exact moment its regional militia network is being tested by a war it has not conclusively won.

The procession’s next and final stop, Mashhad, offered its own piece of choreography, and its most disquieting image of the week. As the New York Times reported from Mashhad, volunteers greeted arriving mourners at the airport with walnut cakes and banners reading “We Must Rise,” alongside two large screens playing an AI-generated animation of Khamenei — dead since February — depicted walking. A few metres away, a separate memorial had been built from dozens of children’s backpacks, mostly pink and blue, each paired with a rose and a pair of small shoes, commemorating the more than 175 people, most of them children, killed when an American strike hit an elementary school in the town of Minab early in the war. The same dispatch noted that U.S. military officials have privately acknowledged the strike as their own, and have called it an intelligence failure. Iran’s government, in other words, chose to place a synthetic resurrection of its dead leader beside a genuine memorial to dead schoolchildren, in the same terminal, on the same day it was firing missiles at American bases two countries away. If you are looking for a single image that captures how this regime manages grief, atrocity, and propaganda as three tools in the same kit, you will not find a better one than a hallway in Mashhad airport this week.

The Market Notices What the Diplomats Couldn’t Fix

The financial numbers, for once, tell a cleaner story than the politics. As the New York Times’ markets coverage reported, Brent crude jumped 6 percent on Wednesday to above $78 a barrel — its highest level in more than two weeks, and now comfortably above the roughly $72 prewar price it had only recently clawed back down to. West Texas Intermediate followed it past $74. American drivers are paying $3.80 a gallon on average, 27 percent above pre-war prices, with analysts noting gas stations have been quietly widening their margins through the volatility. U.S. stock futures fell a percentage point on the news, and European and Asian markets slid further as the overnight exchange of fire continued — though, tellingly, the same coverage found, most market strategists remain more preoccupied with whether AI infrastructure spending will pay off than with whether this war reignites in earnest. One analyst called the ceasefire’s collapse a risk that “further complicates” already fraught talks; another called the whole situation, with a straight face, a potential “catalyst for a meaningful relief rally” once it resolves. Global markets, it turns out, are pricing in a return to peace with more confidence than the actual negotiators are managing to project.

The shipping picture is grimmer still. The same reporting noted that the International Maritime Organization’s secretary-general urged shipowners on Wednesday to avoid the strait entirely, warning that continued transit could expose the nearly 6,000 seafarers still stranded in the Persian Gulf to “unnecessary danger.” Only two of the 41 ships that transited on Tuesday took the Omani route the U.S. Navy has been shepherding; the rest used the Iranian-mandated corridor, the only one Tehran now claims is safe. Before the war, more than 130 ships a day made this passage. Qatar, meanwhile, demanded Iran halt what it called “dangerous practices” after its own LNG carrier was hit, and reserved, in its own words, “its full rights to take whatever measures it deems appropriate” — a warning from a country that has spent the entire war trying to mediate, not escalate.

The Envoys Who Can Only Be in One War at a Time

There is a wider cost to all of this that deserves its own paragraph, because it rarely makes the front page next to a funeral procession or a fresh round of airstrikes. President Trump’s entire diplomatic apparatus for both Iran and Ukraine currently runs through two men: Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner. The post of U.S. ambassador to Moscow has sat empty for more than a year, the acting ambassador to Kyiv resigned in April, and Secretary Rubio has played only a limited role on Ukraine. Which means that while Witkoff and Kushner were occupied with Hormuz, Russia launched what a former State Department official called an “escalatory spiral” of strikes against Kyiv that killed at least 21 people in a single night, and Ukraine’s president told CBS he was still waiting for either man to visit his country at all. “Unfortunately, we are in this queue of these wars,” Volodymyr Zelensky said — a sentence that will likely outlive the news cycle it was spoken in.

President Putin, for his part, appears entirely content to wait. The same New York Times reporting on Witkoff and Kushner notes he has told American officials he expects the pair back in Moscow once the “hot phase” on Iran concludes — which is as close as a Russian president will ever come to admitting, on the record, that every day Washington spends managing a funeral, a fee dispute, and now a fresh bombing campaign in the Gulf is a day of reduced pressure on him. Mr. Trump, asked previously about the stakes of the Ukraine war for the United States, said it has “no impact on us, other than we sell weapons.” Analysts at RAND and Carnegie disagree, and so, it is worth noting, does simple arithmetic: a nuclear power’s war does not stop mattering to Washington just because Washington has decided not to look at it this month — and neither, it turns out, does a second Middle Eastern bombing campaign make Eastern Europe wait any less.

What the Funeral Cannot Bury

Buried underneath all of this — literally, in some cases, and figuratively in the coverage — is a second story Iran would very much like the funeral to distract from. As the New York Times reported in its funeral coverage, the same week Tehran urged Iranians “of every ethnicity, religion, preference and political tendency” to attend a display of national unity, its security services arrested two well-known environmentalists, Houman Jokar and Sepideh Kashani, along with Ms. Kashani’s sister, who has multiple sclerosis. It is at least the second time Mr. Jokar and Ms. Kashani have been arrested by this government; they spent over six years in Evin Prison the first time, on spying charges they denied, before their release in 2024. Amnesty International puts the number of arrests since the war began at more than 6,000. Siamak Namazi, who spent eight years imprisoned in Iran himself, asked the obvious question on social media: how does dragging away “two of Iran’s finest people... without charges, without transparency, and without due process” square with a government talking, in the same week, about “national reconciliation.”

It doesn’t, of course. It was never going to. The funeral this week is many things at once — a genuine outpouring of religious grief for millions of Iranians and Iraqis both, a carefully staged assertion of continuity by a government that lost its supreme leader and much of its senior leadership on a single February morning, a distraction from a peace process that has now been pronounced dead by the American president himself, and a domestic crackdown that never stopped. It has crossed two borders, filled five cities, employed an AI resurrection and a memorial to real dead children within a hundred metres of each other, and outlasted, if barely, a ceasefire that took three weeks to unravel. Iran’s government has always been good at holding contradictory things in one hand. This week it managed to hold all of it — grief, propaganda, war, and a body still travelling toward its grave — at the same time, and mostly without dropping any of it.

POSTSCRIPT — One thing worth adding after the fact, because it reframes how this week’s Oman fee proposal and the Hormuz strikes should be read together, rather than as two separate stories.

Since the June 17 memorandum was signed, Iranian strikes on commercial shipping in the strait have not been random. On more than one occasion now, the ships hit have been using the traditional middle route through Hormuz — the route international shipping has defaulted to for decades — rather than the coastal lane along Iran’s own shore that Tehran has repeatedly said it expects traffic to use instead. That is a pattern, not noise. Strike the ships that ignore your preferred route often enough, and you are not really violating a ceasefire. You are establishing a norm, one tanker at a time, in advance of the very negotiation the memorandum already promises Iran a formal seat at: who gets to define the strait’s future administration once the sixty-day window closes.

Read that way, the Oman “voluntary fee” proposal this diary has already covered and the ongoing strikes are not two separate items on the ledger. They are the same negotiation, conducted through two different instruments — one diplomatic, one explosive. Washington and Tehran do not appear to agree on what Hormuz’s management should even look like once the funeral proceedings end and talks resume, and that unresolved disagreement, not any single missile, is the actual source of the grief and destruction still being inflicted on ships that thought the war was over. Ironing out that one question — whose rules govern passage, and at what price — may matter more to the region’s actual peace than anything else left on the sixty-day clock.
