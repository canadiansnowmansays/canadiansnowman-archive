Across twenty-one parts of this series, I have documented a consistent pattern: a target is identified, a pretext is constructed, prosecutorial machinery is deployed, and the institution — the court, the grand jury, the judge — resists. The cases collapsed because the facts were weak. The facts were weak because the investigations were pretextual. The investigations were pretextual because the goal was political, not legal.

That pattern assumed something important: that the Justice Department, however corrupted its leadership, was still attempting to win in court. Winning required some relationship with the law. The law, even stretched, imposed limits.

That assumption no longer holds.

What Todd Blanche’s Justice Department is now doing — documented this week by Quinta Jurecic in The Atlantic, and visible in the accumulating wreckage of this past week’s legal filings — represents a qualitative shift in the enterprise. The old DOJ stretched the facts to fit the law. The new DOJ is distorting the law itself. And when even that fails, it submits court filings that read like Truth Social posts and calls that a legal strategy.

The only thing that matters, at this point, is whether the president is satisfied before lunch.

The SPLC: Charging Someone for Doing Legal Things

The indictment of the Southern Poverty Law Center — the left-leaning civil rights organization that has spent decades tracking extremist groups — heralded the new era. Attorney General Blanche stood at a podium flanked by two posters and FBI Director Kash Patel and alleged that the SPLC had been “manufacturing the extremism it purports to oppose by paying sources to stoke racial hatred.”

The allegation is inflammatory. The indictment, examined closely, is something else.

What DOJ actually charges is this: the SPLC paid informants within extremist groups, and used shell companies to conceal the source of those payments — presumably so that the informants’ cover would not be blown. The wire fraud allegation rests on the theory that donors were somehow deceived. The bank statute charge doesn’t explain what the SPLC was lying about, or what it was trying to persuade a bank to do.

Here is the problem. Paying informants is not a crime. The government has provided no evidence that donors were deceived about a practice the SPLC did not publicize — for the obvious reason that publicizing your informant program gets your informants killed. And the indictment was written, in the words of former DOJ Civil Rights Division prosecutor Kyle Boynton, in a way that “almost encourages the reader to find more in it than DOJ states outright.”

There is more. Court filings submitted this week by the SPLC state that the organization used its informant program to gather detailed intelligence about the risk of violence at the 2017 Charlottesville rally — and shared that intelligence with the FBI and other law enforcement agencies in advance. In at least one case, the SPLC’s informant network may have helped avert a white-supremacist terrorist attack in Las Vegas.

None of this appears in the indictment. Presumably because it would ruin the story the administration wants to tell.

Blanche claimed in a Fox News interview that the SPLC had never shared what it learned with law enforcement. The SPLC’s own court filing directly contradicts this claim. The indictment omits exculpatory material. The public allegation contradicts the record.

This is no longer stretching facts. This is manufacturing a case from the outside in — deciding the verdict first, then constructing the charge to support it, then omitting the evidence that would undermine it.

The Ballroom: A Motion That Reads Like a Tweet

While the SPLC indictment was being filed, the Justice Department submitted a motion in the ballroom litigation that deserves its own place in the historical record.

Some context: Trump has been pursuing the construction of what he calls a “Militarily Top Secret Ballroom” in place of the demolished East Wing of the White House. A federal judge, Richard Leon, issued an injunction halting the project. DOJ filed a motion demanding he dissolve it.

The motion does not read like a government legal filing. It reads like a Truth Social post that someone attached a few stray citations to. The plaintiffs, DOJ lawyers write, “are very bad for our Country” and “suffer from Trump Derangement Syndrome.” The ballroom will be built “FREE OF CHARGE AS A GIFT TO THE COUNTRY!”

The Justice Department of the United States. In a federal court. Filed under penalty of perjury.

The department has traditionally prided itself on what insiders call “government gray” — professional, careful, scrupulously un-emotive legal work. That standard, maintained across administrations of both parties, existed for a reason: courts extend credibility to lawyers who behave like lawyers. When DOJ speaks, judges listen, because DOJ has historically been trustworthy.

Blanche’s DOJ is spending that credibility as fast as it can. The ballroom motion is not designed to win before Judge Leon — the lawyers who drafted it know Leon has already ruled against the government and is not going to be moved by the phrase “Trump Derangement Syndrome.” The motion is designed to make the boss happy. The performance is for an audience of one, and that audience does not sit on the federal bench.

Comey, Again: The Seashells Indictment

And then there is James Comey.

Last May, Comey posted a photo on social media of shells on a beach arranged to spell “86 47.” He captioned it “Cool shell formation on my beach walk.” He deleted it the same day, writing that he hadn’t realized some people associated those numbers with violence. The post was gone within hours of its publication.

On Tuesday, a federal grand jury in the Eastern District of North Carolina — where Comey has a beach house — returned an indictment charging him with threatening the president.

This is the second indictment of James Comey. The first — charging him with lying to Congress about a 2020 investigation — was dismissed when a judge found the prosecutor was illegally appointed. The administration did not accept the dismissal as a verdict. It accepted it as a setback, and went looking for a new case. It found one in a year-old Instagram post about shells.

The legal threshold for a threats prosecution is not low. The Supreme Court established in 2023 that a true threats case requires showing not only that a reasonable person would read the statement as threatening, but that the defendant himself understood it would be received that way — or recklessly disregarded the possibility. Comey said he didn’t realize the numbers had that connotation. He deleted the post immediately. He apologized.

The courts have previously found constitutional protection for statements considerably more explicit: a Vietnam War protester who said he wanted to get a rifle and put a sitting president in his sights; a message-board post suggesting a former president “will have a 50 cal in the head soon.” If those statements are protected, seashells photographed by a man on a beach walk face a formidable First Amendment obstacle.

Boynton, the former DOJ prosecutor, put it with precision: “A Justice Department that is willing to stretch both the facts and the law to mislead a grand jury into returning an indictment could literally charge any American with a crime.”

Read that sentence again. Not any political enemy. Any American.

The Coda That Changes Everything

On the same Tuesday that the seashells indictment was unsealed, Manhattan federal judge Jesse Furman ruled that Maurene Comey — James Comey’s daughter, a former federal prosecutor with more than 200 convictions across eleven criminal trials, including cases against Jeffrey Epstein, Ghislaine Maxwell, and Sean Combs — may proceed with her lawsuit challenging her firing by the Trump administration.

She was fired in July 2025. Her lawsuit contends there was no plausible explanation for her dismissal other than her father’s identity or her perceived political beliefs. The judge agreed she had a claim worth hearing.

“No president can ignore the Constitution, the Bill of Rights and federal law and fire a career federal employee based solely on her last name,” her lawyers said.

The father was indicted over seashells. The daughter won a ruling in federal court. On the same afternoon.

The enemies list, as I wrote in Part XXI, runs in both directions. Tuesday confirmed it runs in both directions simultaneously.

What the Shift Means

Let me be precise about what has changed, because the distinction matters.

The cases documented in Parts I through XX of this series shared a common structure: the administration had a political target, constructed a legal pretext, deployed prosecutorial machinery, and the courts found the pretext inadequate. James Comey’s first indictment: illegal prosecutor. Letitia James: two grand jury rejections. The six lawmakers: zero votes. The Federal Reserve subpoenas: “zero evidence of a crime other than displeasing the president.”

The system held because the cases, however politically motivated, were still being constructed within some relationship to the law. The facts were bad. The theories were thin. But there were facts, and there were theories, and judges could examine them and find them wanting.

What Jurecic documents in The Atlantic this week is something different. The SPLC indictment charges conduct that is not illegal, omits material that contradicts its claims, and was written to imply more than it states so that readers — and jurors — will fill in the gap with their own assumptions. The second Comey indictment rests on a legal theory the Supreme Court has made nearly impossible to satisfy, applied to a post that was deleted within hours by a man who said he didn’t understand its implication.

These cases are not designed to win. They are designed to punish. The indictment is the sentence. The years of litigation, the legal fees, the reputational exposure, the constant shadow of federal prosecution — that is what the administration is imposing, deliberately, on people it has decided to target.

And because an indictment requires a grand jury, and grand juries hear only the government’s evidence, and the government controls what the grand jury hears — the administration now has a mechanism for imposing that punishment on almost anyone it chooses.

Boynton is right. Not any political enemy. Any American.

The Career Prosecutor They Removed

One detail from this week’s reporting that has not received the attention it deserves.

The career prosecutor leading the Brennan inquiry — the investigation into the former CIA director who oversaw the investigation of Russian interference in the 2016 election — was removed from the case after raising concerns about its validity. He was replaced by Joseph diGenova, a 81-year-old Trump ally and long-time conservative media presence who had recently told Newsmax that Pam Bondi was “ineffective.”

A lawyer who said the case wasn’t legally sound was taken off the case. A loyalist who wanted the case pursued was put in charge of it.

This is what the internal resistance looks like now, and what happens to it. The career prosecutors who maintained professional standards under Bondi — who counselled against the Letitia James indictment, who raised concerns about the Comey arrest, who told their supervisors when the evidence wasn’t there — are being replaced, one by one, with people whose primary qualification is the willingness to proceed anyway.

When the institution loses the people who say no, it loses the capacity to say no. That capacity, once lost, is not quickly rebuilt.

What Comes Next

The SPLC case will likely be challenged on the grounds that the indictment fails to allege conduct that is actually illegal. The seashells case will face a First Amendment motion that, based on existing Supreme Court precedent, the government will struggle to answer. Both cases may eventually collapse, as the first Comey indictment did, as the James charges did, as the Powell subpoenas did.

But the administration has already demonstrated that collapse is not deterrent. The first Comey case collapsed; the second Comey case followed. The answer to judicial rejection is not revision. It is escalation.

And the escalation now includes not just new targets but a new method: cases constructed not from evidence but from the accusation itself, written to imply what cannot be charged, designed to punish through process rather than conviction.

The only meaningful check remaining is the judiciary — and the judiciary’s credibility depends, in part, on a Justice Department that treats its representations to courts as binding. When DOJ submits motions that read like social media posts, when DOJ omits exculpatory evidence from indictments, when DOJ removes the prosecutors who raise concerns and replaces them with loyalists — the entire system of mutual institutional trust that makes American law function begins to corrode.

Not for the targets. For everyone.
