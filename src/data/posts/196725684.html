I. The Word He Chose

Howard Lutnick sat before the House Oversight Committee on Wednesday and, according to people familiar with his testimony, called his 2012 visit to Jeffrey Epstein’s private island inexplicable.

He chose that word himself. His lawyers presumably approved it. It was, in the clinical vocabulary of congressional self-preservation, the best available option — because “inexplicable” acknowledges the problem while refusing to explain it. It says: I know this looks bad. I have no account of it that would satisfy you. Let us therefore agree that it is beyond reckoning and move on.

The committee, to its credit, did not move on.

II. Three Meetings Over Ten Years

In his opening statement, Lutnick told the committee he had met Epstein exactly three times.

Once, for coffee and a tour of Epstein’s home on the Upper East Side of Manhattan — their first meeting, after they became next-door neighbours — at which Lutnick said he saw a massage table surrounded by candles, asked a question about it, received an answer that made his skin crawl, and departed. “My wife and I decided that I will never be in the room with that disgusting person ever again,” he said on the Pod Force One podcast last October. “So I was never in the room with him socially, for business or even philanthropy.”

Once, when the Lutnick family — wife, four children, nannies, and another family along for the voyage — sailed to Epstein’s private Caribbean island, Little St. James, for lunch in 2012.

Once, to discuss a construction project on Epstein’s New York home that might have affected Lutnick’s property.

Three meetings. Ten years as neighbours. “Meaningless and inconsequential,” he told Democrats who pressed him on the timeline.

There is a word for this kind of testimony. It is not “forthcoming,” though that is the word committee chairman James Comer, Republican of Kentucky, chose after Republicans finished their questioning. The word is “calibrated.” Every syllable of Lutnick’s account was designed to contain the damage without admitting the conduct.

III. What the Files Say

The Justice Department’s release of Epstein files told a different story than “three meetings.” Lutnick’s name appeared in more than 250 documents, a review by The New York Times found.

The documents suggested a 2011 encounter at Epstein’s New York home — years after Lutnick said he had cut contact following the 2005 tour. They showed that Epstein had invested in a privately held company alongside Lutnick. They documented neighbourhood and philanthropic dealings between the two men. They showed Epstein receiving the résumé of Lutnick’s nanny in 2013 — a year after the island visit that Lutnick himself calls inexplicable — and an apparent interest in meeting her. Whether she and Epstein ever met, Lutnick told lawmakers in February, he didn’t know. He also said he did not know whether she was one of the nannies he’d brought to the island.

Seven months after the 2012 island visit, an aide to then-Prince Andrew wrote to Epstein asking for his assessment of Lutnick. “What is your view on Howard Lutnick? Just met him with PA.” Epstein’s circle was evaluating Lutnick as a person of influence. This is not the footprint of a man who was never in the room.

IV. The Podcast and the Perjury Gap

In October 2025, Lutnick sat down for a podcast interview and explained, with evident satisfaction, how he had recognized Epstein’s depravity at their very first meeting and never looked back. He was clear. He was unequivocal. He was, as he put it, “never in the room with him socially, for business or even philanthropy.”

Four months later, in a Senate appropriations hearing, after the DOJ released the files and his name appeared in 250 documents, Lutnick admitted to the island visit. He confirmed he had also met Epstein about the construction project. The podcast version evaporated.

Before Wednesday’s closed-door session began, Comer acknowledged — with the particular care of a man who has decided to land the plane — that Lutnick “wasn’t 100 percent truthful” about whether or not he had been on the island. After Republicans finished questioning him, Comer declared himself satisfied that Lutnick was being “forthcoming.” He declined to directly address the discrepancy between the podcast and the Senate testimony.

Representative Yassamin Ansari, Democrat of Arizona, was more direct. She said Lutnick had not admitted to misleading Americans. She said she was not satisfied with his explanation for the island visit. She said he called it inexplicable, and left it at that.

Lutnick is the first sitting Trump cabinet secretary to testify before the panel. He did so voluntarily — after it became clear that Democrats and some Republicans were prepared to subpoena him. The committee did not record the session on video, a decision that Democrats criticized and that Comer defended on the grounds that Lutnick had come in willingly. The transcript will be released.

The Commerce Department, in a statement issued before the hearing, said Lutnick looked forward to “putting to rest the inaccurate and baseless claims in the media.”

No claims appear to have been put to rest.

V. Meanwhile, a Dead Man’s Note

At 6:04 p.m. on the same Wednesday that Howard Lutnick sat in a Capitol Hill corridor explaining himself to reporters, a federal judge released a suicide note purportedly written by Jeffrey Epstein.

It had been sealed for years as part of the criminal case of Epstein’s former cellmate, Nicholas Tartaglione, a former police officer convicted of quadruple murder. Tartaglione said he found the note in July 2019, tucked inside a graphic novel, after Epstein was found unresponsive with a strip of cloth around his neck — the apparent suicide attempt that preceded his death by three weeks.

The note, written on yellow legal paper, begins: “They investigated me for months — FOUND NOTHING!!!”

It continues: “It is a treat to be able to choose one’s time to say goodbye.”

It concludes: “Watcha want me to do — Bust out cryin!! NO FUN. NOT WORTH IT!!” The final three words are underlined.

The New York Times, which petitioned the court last Thursday for the note’s release, has not authenticated whether Epstein wrote it. The DOJ said it had never seen it. A chronology filed in the case said Tartaglione’s lawyers had authenticated the note, without explaining how.

Judge Kenneth M. Karas of Federal District Court in White Plains, who oversaw the cellmate’s case, unsealed it after the U.S. Attorney’s office in Manhattan — which prosecuted Tartaglione — wrote to the court that “there appears to be a strong public interest in the circumstances surrounding Epstein’s death.”

The note does not resolve anything. It answers no questions about the 2.5 million pages of Epstein files the Justice Department has withheld. It does not name anyone. It does not explain anything we did not already know about the arc of Epstein’s life. It is the last recorded transmission from a man who spent decades cultivating powerful friends, running what appeared to be a blackmail operation of breathtaking scope, avoiding prosecution through a plea deal his own victims were never told about, and dying in federal custody under circumstances that no official account has fully explained.

NO FUN. NOT WORTH IT!!

Even in the grave, the exclamation points.

VI. The Convergence

Consider what happened on a single Wednesday in May 2026.

The first sitting member of the Trump cabinet testified — voluntarily, unrecorded, behind closed doors — before a congressional committee investigating a convicted sex offender. He said he met the man three times. He called one of those meetings inexplicable. He did not, according to Democrats on the committee, admit to misleading anyone. He was declared forthcoming by the Republican chairman, who had earlier acknowledged he “wasn’t 100 percent truthful.”

On the same day, a federal judge released a purported suicide note from the man at the centre of all of it — a document that had been sealed for nearly seven years, that had survived Epstein’s death, Tartaglione’s murder conviction, and the release of 3.5 million pages of federal records, and that surfaced on the precise afternoon when the Commerce Secretary of the United States was in a Capitol hallway explaining why he took his children to the island.

Also on Wednesday: Tova Noel, the prison guard on duty the night Epstein died — the woman who Googled "latest on Epstein in jail" forty minutes before his body was found, who the FBI flagged for twelve suspicious cash deposits she was never asked about, who was leasing a $60,000 Land Rover at the time — had been scheduled to testify before this same committee on March 26. She did not appear. Scheduling issues, the committee said. She has not rescheduled.

These things are not connected by conspiracy. They are connected by the machinery of a story that refuses to close — a story in which the files are released but the questions multiply, in which the testimony is heard but the record is disputed, in which the powerful offer calibrated accounts and the committee accepts them, and in which the dead man’s last note turns out to be sealed in a graphic novel in a murder case in White Plains.

Watcha want me to do.

VII. What Has Not Changed

The Epstein Files series has now tracked this story across thirty-four essays. The contours are familiar.

Documents are released. Names appear. The named explain. The explanations are accepted by those with the power to reject them and rejected by those without the power to compel anything further. Transcripts are promised. No video is recorded. The chairman finds the witness forthcoming. The Democrats find the witness unsatisfying. The next hearing is scheduled. The survivors wait.

Pam Bondi — who was subpoenaed by this same committee before Trump fired her as attorney general — is scheduled to appear on May 29. She was fired before her deposition. Whether that deposition now proceeds is a question no one in the administration has answered.

Howard Lutnick remains Commerce Secretary. Karoline Leavitt said last February that “the president fully supports the secretary.” Nothing Wednesday suggested otherwise.

The 250 documents bearing Lutnick’s name are part of the public record. The nanny’s résumé is in the files. The Prince Andrew aide’s inquiry is in the files. The 2011 New York encounter is in the files. The private company investment is in the files.

The word Lutnick chose for the island visit is in the transcript.

Inexplicable.

It is, at minimum, the most honest word spoken in a closed room on Capitol Hill this week.
