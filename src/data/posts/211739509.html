A memorandum of understanding between Iran and the United States expired on 17 August 2026, unmourned by either side. As David A. Graham reported for The Atlantic, it had been signed in June to buy breathing room in an already-faltering war; the cease-fire it was meant to stabilize had collapsed within three weeks, and the Strait of Hormuz — the entire practical stake of the conflict by this point — remained exactly as closed as Iran had made it at the war’s outset. The memorandum’s expiry changed nothing, because there had been nothing left standing for it to hold up.

That is worth sitting with for a moment. Diplomatic memoranda are not usually allowed to expire so quietly. Ordinarily, an agreement nearing its deadline generates last-minute shuttle diplomacy, a flurry of calls, some public signal that both sides at least wanted to be seen trying. Here, the expiry passed without ceremony because neither government appears to have believed the paper was accomplishing anything by the end. A cease-fire that could not survive three weeks, followed by two more months of a memorandum nobody bothered to renegotiate seriously, is not a diplomatic process running its course. It is a diplomatic process that stopped being real sometime in June and simply hadn’t been declared dead yet.

What the days surrounding that expiry revealed is more instructive than the memorandum itself: an administration that cannot agree, even internally, on what the war is for.

Two goals, one president

Asked to state the purpose of continuing military pressure on Iran, Trump has been unambiguous, if inconsistent with his own government’s findings: “the number one Goal is, and always will be, that Iran cannot have, in any way, shape, or form, a Nuclear Weapon.” This is a striking claim to anchor a war on, given that his own administration assessed before the fighting began that Iran was not actively building a weapon — and given that a war fought to prevent proliferation is, if anything, likely to make an Iranian regime that has just been attacked more determined to acquire one, not less.

Vice President Vance, asked the same question, gave a different answer. Lowering fuel prices, he said, is now “goal No. 1” of the war. Not deterrence. Not nonproliferation. Fuel prices — the domestic, kitchen-table cost of a Middle Eastern conflict that has driven gasoline prices up and that Trump himself has asked Americans to simply accept as the price of the fight he has chosen to wage.

A war can survive a difference of emphasis between a president and his vice president. It cannot easily survive two officials, both nominally in charge of explaining it, naming two entirely different objectives with no visible coordination between them. That is not a communications lapse. It is the absence of an actual, agreed war aim — the thing a theory of victory is supposed to supply, and the thing this administration has never produced.

A strait that cannot be taken by declaration

The one goal that would actually resolve the war’s central practical problem — reopening Hormuz to shipping, after Iran seized effective control of it early in the conflict — has no visible strategy behind it either. Trump’s basic choice, as it has been for weeks, is between a much larger military operation, potentially including ground troops, or accepting that Iran has permanently altered the strait’s status. He has chosen neither. Instead, at a Friday speech, he told the assembled crowd: “pretty soon, I’ll be declaring the Hormuz Strait a territory of the United States.”

There is no framework, legal or military, under which a presidential declaration converts an international shipping lane between Iran and Oman into sovereign American territory. The claim also directly contradicts Secretary of State Marco Rubio’s own public insistence on the strait’s neutrality, and it sits oddly beside Trump’s repeated claims that the United States should not act as the world’s policeman. Whether the statement reflects genuine confusion about what a president can unilaterally declare, deliberate bluster aimed at Tehran, or simple frustration finding its way into a microphone, the practical result is the same: the United States has announced an ambition it has no means of executing, in front of the one audience — Iran, and the shipping companies deciding whether to test the strait — that needed a credible plan rather than a boast.

The sailors paying for the incoherence

While the war’s stated purpose shifts between briefings, the people bearing its most immediate cost are the roughly five thousand sailors aboard the USS Abraham Lincoln, deployed to the Gulf for nearly nine months now with no clear end point. Reports from the carrier describe shortages of basic supplies, malfunctioning shipboard systems, and — perhaps most tellingly — faltering mental health among a crew that has had no announced return date since the deployment began. When asked on Friday about military families who have publicly voiced concern for their deployed relatives, Trump’s response was flat denial: “No, they’re not,” he said, adding that the deployment had not gone on “nearly long enough” yet.

That denial did not survive the weekend. The admiral overseeing the broader war effort visited the Abraham Lincoln within days and, unlike the president, acknowledged the crew’s mental-health issues directly — an implicit correction delivered not through a press statement contradicting the commander-in-chief, but through the much quieter act of a senior officer treating a documented problem as real while the president had just told the public it wasn’t. The gap between a flat presidential denial and an admiral’s on-site acknowledgment, arriving within the same week, is its own small measure of how disconnected the war’s public narrative has become from the war’s lived reality for the people fighting it.

The allies who are done pretending

The incoherence has not stayed contained to Iran. With Washington unable to break the deadlock at Hormuz, Oman — a longstanding regional partner of the United States — began its own negotiations with Iran toward a toll system that would let shipping resume without American involvement. Trump’s response, delivered to Fox News, was a threat: “If Oman gets in the way, we’ll bomb the shit out of them.” Frustration with a partner quietly solving a problem the administration cannot is understandable. Threatening an ally with bombing, on live television, is not a minor rhetorical excess; it is the kind of statement that tells every other regional government exactly how much their cooperation is worth to Washington.

Other allies have reached similar conclusions on their own. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu — who reportedly helped talk Trump into the Iran war in the first place, and who has long been among his closest foreign partners — flatly rejected the administration’s latest Gaza disarmament framework, a plan being pushed not through the State Department but through Jared Kushner, Trump’s son-in-law, who holds no official government position and met with Hamas officials over the weekend regardless. The optics alone are worth pausing on: a private citizen, however closely related to the president, conducting freelance negotiations with a designated terrorist organization on behalf of an administration whose own secretary of state is simultaneously trying to hold a coherent public line on the Strait of Hormuz a few thousand miles away. That the plan is struggling should surprise no one — not because disarming Hamas and rebuilding Gaza are unreasonable goals, but because a negotiation run outside ordinary diplomatic channels, by a family member with no formal accountability, is a hard way to convince even a friendly government like Netanyahu’s to take the terms seriously.

South Korea, like nearly every other major American ally, has declined to involve itself in the Iran conflict at all — a decision Trump answered by curtailing joint military exercises with Seoul, citing not the exercises’ military value but “my very good relationship with Kim Jong Un, of North Korea.” That relationship has produced one photographed summit in 2018 and a correspondence Trump took with him from the White House after losing the 2020 election; it has not produced a single concession on North Korea’s nuclear program, which continues uninterrupted while the administration wages a war elsewhere justified explicitly by nonproliferation.

Bad-faith negotiators, previously known

None of this is happening in a vacuum of surprise. Trump continues to complain publicly that Iranian negotiators are acting in bad faith — an assessment that is very likely true, and that was equally true before the war began, when the same negotiators, the same regime, and the same incentives were all already on record. A war launched partly to change an adversary’s behaviour should, at minimum, produce some evidence that the behaviour has changed. Iran’s negotiating posture at Hormuz has not shifted in any direction favourable to Washington; if anything, an Iran that has weathered months of conflict without losing control of the strait has less reason than before the war to treat American demands as urgent. Complaining about bad faith after months of fighting failed to alter it is not a strategic observation. It is an admission, phrased as a grievance, that the war has not worked.

No doctrine to find

Analysts have spent both of Trump’s terms searching for the through-line in his foreign policy — a doctrine, a consistent theory of American interest, something that would let allies and adversaries alike predict what Washington wants and plan accordingly. The week surrounding the Iran memorandum’s expiry is a fair test of whether such a doctrine exists, and it does not survive the test. A president and vice president naming different war aims. A territorial claim over water that cannot be legally or militarily sustained. A threat to bomb a partner nation for the crime of solving a problem Washington could not. A reward extended to North Korea for offering nothing, timed to coincide with a war fought in the name of stopping exactly the kind of weapons program Pyongyang already has.

Administration officials reportedly described their own foreign-affairs philosophy earlier this year as “f*** around and find out” — meant as a warning to other countries about the costs of testing American resolve. Iran, Oman, Israel, and South Korea appear to have already run that experiment and drawn their own conclusions. What the events of this week suggest is that the administration is now finding out something too: that a war without an agreed purpose, waged alongside allies treated as afterthoughts or targets, does not become coherent simply because it continues.

There is a version of American foreign policy — transactional, blunt, willing to alarm adversaries into concessions — that has a real intellectual lineage and real defenders, some of whom would argue that unpredictability itself is a form of leverage, and that a rival like Iran gains nothing from being able to forecast Washington’s next move. That argument requires, at minimum, that the unpredictability be aimed outward, at the adversary the strategy is meant to unsettle. What the Iran war has instead produced is unpredictability aimed inward and sideways as often as at Tehran: a vice president publicly naming a different war goal than his president, a secretary of state’s declared position on the strait’s legal status contradicted by his own boss days later, a family member running parallel diplomacy with a terrorist organization, and a treaty ally threatened with bombing on cable news for the offense of trying to solve a problem Washington could not. An adversary confused about American intentions might be useful. An administration confused about its own is not a strategy. It is simply what a war without a theory of victory looks like from the inside, one contradictory briefing at a time.
