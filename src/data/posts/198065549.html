Donald Trump speaks during his second term. His transactional approach has turned longstanding U.S. commitments to Taiwan into negotiating variables — from defense spending demands to arms sales pauses and public shrugs about the island’s future.

Donald Trump has said more revealing things about Taiwan in casual conversation than most presidents say in formal policy documents. During his second term, he has told an interviewer that Xi Jinping “considers Taiwan to be part of China, and that’s up to him, what he’s going to do.” He has suggested that Taiwan “stole” its semiconductor industry from the United States. He has demanded that Taiwan raise its defence spending to ten percent of its gross domestic product. He has paused military aid to Taiwan during trade negotiations with China. He has denied Taiwan’s president a transit stop in New York City. He has said that if Xi attempts to seize Taiwan, Trump “would be very unhappy” — a formulation that has the diplomatic weight of a shrug.

TSMC semiconductor facility in Taiwan. The island’s advanced chip industry — once praised as a “Silicon Shield” — became another bargaining chip. Trump accused Taiwan of “stealing” the industry while demanding investment and tariff concessions in exchange for continued security ties.

And then, on May 15, 2026, flying home from a two-day summit with Xi Jinping in Beijing, he said several more things.

He said he is “not looking to have somebody go independent, and you know, we’re supposed to travel 9,500 miles to fight a war. I’m not looking for that.”

He said he might or might not approve a fourteen-billion-dollar arms package for Taiwan that his own administration has held up for months. “I’ll be making decisions,” he told reporters. “But, you know, I think the last thing we need right now is a war that’s 9,500 miles away.”

He dismissed the 1982 Six Assurances — the policy under which the United States pledged not to consult with Beijing about Taiwan arms sales — as outdated. “What am I going to do, say I don’t want to talk to you about it because I have an agreement wrote in 1982?”

He called Taiwan “a place, because nobody knows how to define it.”

Asked whether the Taiwanese people should feel more or less secure after the summit, he said: “Neutral.”

He speculated that China would not attack while he is president, but added, with the candour that the island’s twenty-three million residents have learned to dread: “When I’m not here, I think they might, to be honest with you.”

Trump’s May 2026 summit with Xi Jinping in Beijing. The president returned signaling that Taiwan’s security guarantee, arms packages, and even its legal status were all on the table. “That’s up to him,” he said of Xi — five words that opened the auction.

Every administration since Carter’s has maintained strategic ambiguity on Taiwan: the calculated refusal to say definitively whether the United States would militarily defend the island in the event of a Chinese attack. The doctrine has worked, after a fashion, for four decades. But there is strategic ambiguity and there is Trumpian ambiguity, and they are not the same thing. Strategic ambiguity is a deliberate policy choice, maintained with care, designed to deter both Chinese aggression and Taiwanese unilateralism by keeping both sides uncertain about American intentions. Trumpian ambiguity is something else: it is the natural expression of a worldview in which all commitments are provisional, all alliances are transactions, and the question of whether the United States would defend any particular democracy is answered, implicitly, by asking what that democracy has done for America lately.

Taiwan has done quite a lot. The question is whether it is enough, and whether it will remain enough as the strategic calculation shifts.

The Trump administration’s Taiwan policy, viewed as a whole, has three discernible threads.

The first is extraction. Taiwan has been required to pay — in investment commitments, in tariff negotiations, in defense spending increases — for the security relationship that previous administrations maintained as a matter of shared strategic interest. The five hundred billion dollar investment package concluded in January 2026 is the most visible example, but it is part of a broader pattern in which American allies and partners across the Indo-Pacific have been told that the American security umbrella is now metered, not flat-rate. Taiwan has responded with the compliance of a country that has no alternative: it has paid, it has invested, it has raised its defense budget, it has bought American weapons. Its legislature approved a special defence budget of twenty-five billion dollars earlier this month — funding the very arms package Trump is now publicly wavering over. It has done everything asked of it. Whether this compliance provides durable security or merely defers the next demand is no longer an open question. The summit answered it.

The second thread is deliberate silence. The National Defense Strategy released in January 2026 outlines “denial defense along the First Island Chain” — the strategic architecture that only makes sense if Taiwan is at its centre — without once naming Taiwan. The island that the entire document is designed to protect is too politically sensitive to mention in the document. This silence was replicated at the summit: the White House readout of the Trump-Xi meeting did not mention Taiwan at all. Wang Yi, China’s foreign minister, told state media afterward that Beijing “sensed” that Trump “understands China’s position” and “does not support or accept Taiwan moving toward independence.” The American readout’s silence and China’s readout’s confidence are not contradictory. They are complementary. When you have not said you will defend something, you have not lied when you fail to defend it.

Chinese naval forces in the Taiwan Strait and military exercise map. Beijing is advancing its long game — increasing pressure through large-scale exercises while calculating that Trump’s transactional approach creates a strategic window to erode Taiwan’s security without full-scale war.

The third thread is the summit itself — not as an event but as a structural feature of the relationship. Xi Jinping will visit the United States in the autumn of 2026. The auction is not a single transaction. It is a series of rounds, each one establishing a new baseline from which the next round begins. In the first round, Trump signalled that Taiwan’s arms sales are negotiable, that America’s legal commitments are antiquated, and that the island’s status is undefined. In the next round, in Washington, the baseline will have moved. The question will no longer be whether America’s commitments are firm. It will be what can be obtained in exchange for their further erosion.

What would a deal look like? No one outside the principals knows. But the architecture is visible. Taiwan’s current status — functional independence backed by an implicit American security guarantee — is a variable that Trump could trade for Chinese concessions on trade, on fentanyl, on the Strait of Hormuz, on other issues that the administration regards as higher priorities. He would not describe it that way. He would describe it as a historic achievement, a deal that only he could make, a resolution of a problem that had defeated every previous president. And he might not be wrong that it would represent a historic achievement, in the same way that a fire sale is a historic achievement in the art of disposing of assets below their value.

It would be unfair to suggest that Trump is the only American president who has treated Taiwan as a bargaining chip. Nixon used it in 1972. Carter used it in 1979 when he switched recognition. Every administration has balanced Taiwan’s interests against the requirements of the broader US-China relationship and found some point at which Taiwan’s interests gave way. The difference is that previous administrations maintained, alongside their various accommodations of Beijing, a genuine commitment to Taiwan’s security that derived from something other than pure transaction — from shared democratic values, from the logic of liberal internationalism, from the belief that the United States had obligations to countries that had staked their futures on American commitments.

The Trump administration has no evident commitment to liberal internationalism. It has no particular investment in shared democratic values as a basis for foreign policy. What it has is a theory of transactions: that every relationship can be reduced to a deal, that every deal should benefit the United States materially, and that any commitment not currently generating material benefit is a commitment that should be renegotiated. This theory is not irrational on its own terms. It is simply incompatible with the kind of deterrence that has kept Taiwan safe, because deterrence requires adversaries to believe that commitments will be honoured even when honouring them is costly. A president who has made clear that no commitment is unconditional has, by definition, weakened the credibility of all his commitments.

Beijing understands this. Its behaviour since Trump’s return to office — the military exercises, the invitation to Cheng Li-wun, the diplomatic positioning, the summit itself — confirms that it is calculating how to advance its Taiwan objectives in the window of opportunity that Trump’s transactional presidency provides. It is not planning an imminent military assault. Bonnie Glaser, writing in Foreign Affairs days before the summit, argued that Beijing is playing a long game — that the massive purges in the PLA suggest Xi is not prepared to use force in the near future, and that Beijing believes time and growing power will eventually deliver Taiwan without the catastrophic costs of invasion. The 2028 Taiwanese presidential election is the next inflection point. A negotiated outcome that achieved Beijing’s strategic objectives — formal acknowledgment of Chinese sovereignty over Taiwan, removal of the American security guarantee, reduction of Taiwan’s international space — without requiring military action would be vastly preferable. And the Trump administration, uniquely among American governments of the past fifty years, might be willing to provide one.

The Senate has been watching. Congress has passed an unprecedented number of Taiwan-related bills in the current legislative session, reinforcing American commitments in statutory language that would complicate any executive decision to trade Taiwan away. The Taiwan Assurance Implementation Act. The PORCUPINE Act. Various measures requiring arms sales, military aid, and strengthened security cooperation. Twenty-six senators wrote to Trump before the summit making it explicit: “You should make clear to Beijing that as you seek to level the economic playing field, American support for Taiwan is not up for negotiation.” Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, who has maintained a hawkish line on Taiwan, found herself increasingly out of step with the American posture after the summit and received a call from Trump on Air Force One briefing her on what he and Xi discussed. The call was not, by most accounts, reassuring.

Taiwan’s flag over Taipei. Twenty-three million people watch as their de facto independence and security guarantee are treated as tradable assets. Congress has passed protective legislation, but Taiwan’s fate increasingly rests on one man’s personal calculations.

Whether Congressional constraints will bind a president who has shown considerable facility in finding workarounds to legislative intent is not clear. What is clear is that Taiwan’s security now depends, more than at any point in the past fifty years, on the personal calculations of a single man whose approach to strategic commitment is transactional, whose attention is dispersed across multiple crises simultaneously, and who has returned from Beijing having publicly called the island “a place,” having wavered on its arms sales, having dismissed its legal protections as antique, and having offered the Taiwanese people, when asked what they should feel, a single word: “Neutral.”

“That’s up to him, what he’s going to do.” Five words that contained, when Trump first said them, the outline of an auction.

The auction has now opened. The lot is beautiful. The bidding has begun. The people who live on it were not informed that their home was for sale.

The auction is open. Taiwan’s functional independence and America’s implicit security commitment have become negotiating variables in Trump’s deal-making with Xi. What was once a cornerstone of deterrence is now a lot on the block.
