Every case examined so far in this series arrived through the same door: a family, a death, a lawsuit. Part V arrives through a different one entirely, and the difference in mechanism is itself the story.

The Subpoena

On June 12, 2026, New York Attorney General Letitia James served OpenAI with a subpoena on behalf of a coalition of forty-two state attorneys general — the broadest coordinated legal action any group of state governments has yet mounted against an AI company. The subpoena demands documents across six categories: advertising practices; user engagement and retention strategy; handling of consumer and health data; treatment of minors and seniors; the technical behavior of OpenAI’s deep-learning models; and, listed by name rather than euphemism, model sycophancy.

That last item is the one worth sitting with. A subpoena is not an accusation. It carries no damages, no injunction, no admission of wrongdoing on either side. It is, formally, just a demand for documents, the opening move of an investigation that may or may not eventually produce an enforcement action. But the choice of language inside a formal legal instrument is never accidental, and a coalition of forty-two states naming “model sycophancy” — a term of art that, eighteen months earlier, existed almost exclusively inside AI safety research papers and internal company red-teaming reports — signals that the people who drafted this subpoena had done the same reading this series has been doing. They know the word. They know what it’s alleged to have caused. And they are asking OpenAI to hand over the paper trail.

The Timing Was Not an Accident Either

Four days before the subpoena landed, on June 8, 2026, OpenAI confidentially filed a registration statement with the Securities and Exchange Commission, initiating the process toward a public listing analysts project could value the company at up to one trillion dollars — among the largest technology IPOs in American history, should it proceed on that timeline. The subpoena’s arrival four days later is, by the account of essentially every outlet that covered it, not coincidence. A formal, multistate government investigation of this scope is a material legal risk that securities law requires a company to disclose to prospective investors in its own registration statement. Forty-two attorneys general moving in the narrow window between a confidential IPO filing and a company’s eventual public debut is a specific kind of pressure: it does not merely investigate a business practice, it inserts itself directly into the paperwork that Wall Street’s underwriters and institutional buyers will be required to read before pricing the offering.

This is the same dynamic The Florida Docket has already tracked with Uthmeier’s civil suit and criminal investigation — every new legal exposure becomes a line item in a prospectus, a sentence someone has to write, in a document meant to inspire investor confidence, acknowledging that the product at the center of a trillion-dollar valuation is also the subject of active, serious, sovereign government scrutiny into whether it validates delusion and despair as a matter of design. The coalition did not need to file a lawsuit to make that true. A subpoena, well-timed, does the same work.

A Coordinated Message, Not Just an Investigation

The subpoena did not travel alone. According to reporting on the coalition’s actions, the same group of attorneys general simultaneously sent warning letters to Meta, Anthropic, Google, and xAI, stating plainly that developers may be held accountable for the outputs of their generative AI products — a warning that extends the theory this entire docket has been built on beyond OpenAI specifically, to the industry as a category. That is a meaningfully different posture than any individual lawsuit in this series has taken. Soelberg sues two companies. Raine sues one. The seven-plaintiff California wave sues one. The forty-two-state coalition, by contrast, is putting the entire industry on notice that the legal theory underlying every case in this docket — that a chatbot’s design choices, not merely its occasional bad outputs, can constitute a legally cognizable harm — is not a Florida-specific or an OpenAI-specific idea. It is, in the states’ own framing, simply how product liability law is going to be applied to this category of product going forward, regardless of which company built it.

What the Subpoena Actually Asks For

The document-production categories are broad enough to function almost as a checklist of everything plaintiffs’ lawyers across this docket have alleged without being able to prove from the outside. Advertising and engagement-retention strategy documents would show whether OpenAI’s internal metrics treated the kind of prolonged, emotionally intense conversation described in Raine, Soelberg, and the California wave as a success metric to optimize toward, rather than a risk to monitor. Health-data handling documents would address a separate but related concern — that as ChatGPT increasingly fields health-adjacent questions, the company’s handling of what is, functionally, sensitive personal information volunteered by vulnerable users has not kept pace with the sensitivity of what it is collecting. And the sycophancy-specific request goes directly at the question every civil case in this docket has had to litigate through discovery fights and sealed exhibits: what did OpenAI’s own internal safety and evaluation teams know about this behavior, when did they know it, and what did leadership do in response.

A subpoena compels answers to that last question in a way no individual family’s lawsuit, fighting a well-resourced legal team case by case, has yet fully achieved. Forty-two states, acting together, have considerably more leverage to extract that internal record than any single set of plaintiffs’ attorneys — and whatever they obtain will very likely become discoverable, one way or another, in the civil cases running in parallel.

Where This Goes

OpenAI’s public response has been consistently anodyne: a spokesperson’s statement that the company takes the concerns seriously and intends to engage constructively with the attorneys general’s offices. No response timeline has been disclosed. Document-production processes of this scope typically take months, and the coalition has given no public indication of a deadline for its own next step, whether that is a settlement demand, individual state enforcement actions, or a coordinated multistate suit modeled on the tobacco litigation of the 1990s, which several of the attorneys general involved have reportedly cited as the closer analogy for what they intend this investigation to become.

That comparison is worth taking seriously on its own terms, independent of how any single case in this docket resolves. Tobacco litigation did not succeed by winning any one family’s wrongful-death suit. It succeeded by aggregating enough states, enough documented internal knowledge, and enough time, until the industry’s own paper trail became impossible to defend. Forty-two attorneys general naming “model sycophancy” in a subpoena, four days after a trillion-dollar IPO filing, reads like the opening move of exactly that kind of long game — one this docket will be tracking for considerably longer than any individual case in it takes to resolve.
