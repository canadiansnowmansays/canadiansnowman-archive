In 2023, India simultaneously hosted the G20 summit, purchased discounted Russian oil in defiance of Western sanctions, conducted joint military exercises with the United States, maintained a long-standing defence relationship with Moscow, refused to condemn the invasion of Ukraine, deepened strategic cooperation with Israel, cultivated diplomatic ties with Iran, joined the Quad alongside the US, Japan, and Australia, and maintained membership in the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation alongside China and Russia.

If you find this confusing, the confusion is the point.

India is the world’s swing state — the one major power that refuses to be categorized, that maintains relationships with all sides simultaneously, and that treats the expectation of choosing a side as an affront to its sovereignty. This is not a new posture. It is the oldest continuous foreign policy tradition in the post-colonial world, dating to Nehru’s non-alignment, rooted in India’s civilizational self-image, and turbocharged by the strategic realities of a multipolar twenty-first century in which India’s 1.4 billion people, nuclear arsenal, and economic trajectory make it too important for anyone to ignore and too independent for anyone to control.

I. The Nehruvian Inheritance

India’s foreign policy begins with non-alignment, and non-alignment begins with a premise the West has never fully accepted: that the newly independent nations of Asia and Africa owed no allegiance to either Cold War bloc, and that their foreign policies should serve their own interests rather than the strategic agendas of Washington or Moscow.

Nehru’s non-alignment was not neutrality. India recognized China early, criticized the Soviet invasion of Hungary, criticized the Anglo-French-Israeli invasion of Suez, and voted independently at the United Nations on issues where the superpowers expected compliance. India also signed a friendship treaty with the Soviet Union in 1971 — a strategic relationship that provided military hardware, diplomatic support, and a counterweight to the American tilt toward Pakistan.

The American tilt toward Pakistan is one of the great strategic errors of the Cold War, and its consequences are still playing out. Washington chose Pakistan as its South Asian ally for the simplest of Cold War reasons: Pakistan was willing to join anti-Soviet alliance systems (SEATO, CENTO), and India was not. The choice sent India toward Moscow, armed Pakistan with American weapons that were used against India (not the Soviet Union), and created a dynamic of mutual suspicion between Washington and New Delhi that persisted for decades.

The end of the Cold War dissolved the framework but not the habits. India emerged from the 1990s as a rising power in a unipolar world, and it navigated that world with the same strategic autonomy Nehru had practiced — updated for new circumstances, but fundamentally unchanged in principle. India would be on India’s side.

II. The China Problem

The most consequential bilateral relationship in twenty-first-century Asia is not US-China. It is India-China. And it is worse than most Western observers understand.

India and China share a 3,488-kilometre border that has never been formally demarcated. The two countries fought a war in 1962 that India lost badly and has never forgotten. They have maintained competing territorial claims in Aksai Chin (controlled by China, claimed by India) and Arunachal Pradesh (controlled by India, claimed by China) for over sixty years. In June 2020, Indian and Chinese soldiers fought hand-to-hand in the Galwan Valley — using rocks, clubs, and fists, because agreements prohibit firearms along the Line of Actual Control — and at least twenty Indian soldiers and an undisclosed number of Chinese soldiers were killed.

The Galwan clash changed the temperature. Before 2020, Indian strategic thinking held open the possibility of managing the China relationship through economic engagement and diplomatic accommodation. After Galwan, the Indian establishment — military, diplomatic, and political — shifted toward a harder line. India banned Chinese apps, including TikTok. It restricted Chinese investment. It deepened the Quad partnership. It conducted joint military exercises with the United States, Japan, and Australia that were transparently aimed at Chinese containment.

But India has not joined an alliance against China. It has not signed a mutual defence treaty with the United States. It has not abandoned the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation. It continues to trade with China — bilateral trade exceeded $100 billion in recent years, with a massive deficit in China’s favour. India’s strategic posture toward China is confrontation without alliance — the maintenance of independent deterrence, the diversification of partnerships, and the refusal to become anyone’s junior partner in a containment strategy.

The parallel to my Middle Kingdom series is direct. In The Quiet War and Formosa: The Beautiful Problem, I examined China’s strategic behaviour through the lens of a civilization that thinks in centuries, not election cycles. India does the same. The India-China rivalry is not a Cold War proxy contest. It is a civilizational competition between the two oldest continuous cultures on earth, both of which regard themselves as the natural centre of the Asian order, and both of which have populations, economies, and ambitions that the existing international architecture was not designed to accommodate.

III. The Russia Relationship

India’s refusal to condemn Russia’s invasion of Ukraine has bewildered and infuriated Western capitals. It should not have.

India’s relationship with Russia is not ideological. It is structural. Russia has been India’s primary arms supplier for decades. Approximately 60 to 70 percent of India’s military equipment is of Russian origin. The BrahMos missile — India’s most advanced cruise missile — is a joint Indian-Russian project. The S-400 air defence system, which India purchased despite the threat of American sanctions under CAATSA, is Russian. The nuclear submarine that India leases is Russian.

You do not condemn the country that makes your weapons. Not because you approve of its wars, but because you cannot afford to.

India has also maintained a diplomatic tradition of non-interference in the internal affairs and external conflicts of other states — a principle that serves India’s own interests, given its sensitivities about Kashmir, the Northeast insurgencies, and the general principle that no foreign power should have a say in India’s domestic affairs. If India condemns Russia for invading Ukraine on the principle of territorial sovereignty, it establishes a precedent that others might invoke regarding India’s own contested territories.

The Western expectation that India should sacrifice a sixty-year strategic relationship, jeopardize its military readiness, and abandon its foreign policy tradition to align with the Western position on Ukraine reflects a persistent misunderstanding of Indian strategic culture. India’s answer to “whose side are you on?” has not changed since 1947: our own.

IV. The American Courtship

The United States has been courting India with increasing urgency since the early 2000s, and the relationship has deepened dramatically under both Obama and Modi. The US-India Civil Nuclear Agreement of 2008, negotiated by the Bush administration, was the breakthrough — it effectively recognized India as a legitimate nuclear power despite India’s refusal to sign the Non-Proliferation Treaty, and it ended decades of nuclear sanctions.

Since then, the relationship has expanded across every dimension: defence cooperation (India now conducts more military exercises with the US than with any other country), technology partnerships (semiconductor manufacturing, AI, space), intelligence sharing, and strategic alignment on China. The Quad — the US, India, Japan, and Australia — is the closest thing to a formal alliance structure that India has ever joined, though India insists it is not an alliance and not directed at any specific country, a formulation that convinces no one but preserves India’s diplomatic flexibility.

The courtship is genuine but asymmetric. The United States wants India as a counterweight to China. India wants American technology, investment, and diplomatic support — but on its own terms, without the obligations of alliance, without the expectation of exclusivity, and without the assumption that American interests and Indian interests are identical.

They are not. The United States supports Pakistan’s territorial integrity. India does not forget the decades of American arms supplies to its adversary. The United States wants India to cut ties with Russia. India cannot and will not. The United States preaches democratic values. India, under Modi, practices democratic backsliding that the US State Department periodically criticizes and the US government periodically ignores because the strategic relationship is too valuable to jeopardize.

The TACO framework applies to India’s diplomatic style as much as it applies to Modi’s domestic politics. India threatens — nuclear tests in 1998, military mobilizations on the Pakistan border, the abrogation of Article 370. India accommodates — strategic partnerships with the US, the Quad, participation in multilateral institutions. India rarely capitulates — the exceptions are notable precisely because they are rare. And India obfuscates — maintaining strategic ambiguity on everything from nuclear doctrine to its position on Taiwan to the precise nature of its relationship with Russia.

V. The Pakistan Wound

No account of India’s foreign policy is complete without Pakistan, and no account of India-Pakistan relations is complete without acknowledging that the relationship is, at its root, a family quarrel — a dispute between siblings who were separated at birth and have spent seventy-nine years alternating between hostility and heartbreak.

Four wars. Multiple nuclear crises. The ongoing insurgency in Kashmir. The 2008 Mumbai attacks, conducted by Pakistani-based militants. The 2019 Balakot airstrikes, in which India bombed Pakistani territory for the first time since 1971. The relationship is defined by military confrontation, intelligence warfare, and a mutual suspicion so deep that even routine diplomatic courtesies are treated as strategic signals.

And yet: the cultural connections are real. The languages are mutually intelligible — Hindi and Urdu are essentially the same spoken language written in different scripts. The food, the music, the cricket, the family connections that predate Partition — all of these persist despite everything the two states have done to suppress them. When India and Pakistan play cricket, a billion people on both sides of the border stop what they are doing and watch. The intensity is not merely sporting. It is existential.

The tragedy of the India-Pakistan relationship is that it was not inevitable. Partition created it. The geopolitics of the Cold War calcified it. The nuclear weapons both countries now possess make it permanent. And the domestic politics of both countries — Hindu nationalism in India, military-Islamist dynamics in Pakistan — make resolution impossible for the foreseeable future. The wound Mountbatten and Radcliffe inflicted in 1947 is self-perpetuating. It is, in a very real sense, the last colonial legacy — the gift the British Empire left behind, ticking, on the doorstep of the subcontinent.

VI. The Canadian Angle

I cannot write about India’s place in the world without writing about India’s place in Canada, because the two are now inextricable.

Canada has managed to embarrass itself in the India relationship twice in five years, in opposite directions.

The first was sartorial. In February 2018, Prime Minister Justin Trudeau visited India with his family and dressed for nearly every occasion in elaborate Indian formal wear — ornate sherwanis, embroidered kurtas, a Bollywood-worthy ensemble at a reception in Mumbai. The Indian press was bemused. The Canadian press was savage. Modi, in a gesture of diplomatic precision that Canadians should have read more carefully, did not greet Trudeau at the airport — a break from the standard courtesy extended to visiting heads of state. The message was clear to anyone who understands Indian diplomatic protocol: India was not impressed. Trudeau had confused costume with connection. He had tried too hard, understood too little, and performed a version of cultural respect that Indians experienced as cultural tourism. The episode was minor in isolation but symptomatic of a broader Canadian failure: the assumption that enthusiasm is a substitute for understanding.

The second was lethal. The Nijjar affair — the assassination of Hardeep Singh Nijjar, a Canadian citizen and Khalistan activist, in Surrey, British Columbia, in June 2023, and the subsequent allegations by the Canadian government that Indian intelligence agents were involved — has produced the most serious diplomatic crisis in the history of Canada-India relations. Diplomats have been expelled. Intelligence-sharing has been disrupted. The relationship between the two governments has been, at times, effectively frozen.

The affair sits at the intersection of everything this series has examined: Partition’s unresolved traumas, the Sikh experience of state violence, the persistence of diaspora politics, India’s assertive nationalism under Modi, and Canada’s complicated self-image as a multicultural democracy that is now being tested by the political violence of other countries playing out on its soil.

The relationship is now rebuilding — tentatively, asymmetrically. This week, Indian Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal led a 100-member trade delegation to Canada, the largest India has sent abroad, for meetings with officials, traders, and investors. Goyal met with Prime Minister Mark Carney to discuss a future trade agreement. The diplomatic language has softened. But the wound has not healed.

I live in this intersection. My roommate Gurneet is Sikh. He lives in a country — Canada — that has accused another country — India — of assassinating a member of his community. He also lives in a house — mine — with a Hindu man from Gujarat. The geopolitics of the subcontinent does not stop at the arrivals gate of Pearson International Airport. It walks through my front door every evening.

VII. The Rubio Visit

As I write this essay, U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio has just concluded a four-day visit to India — his first — spanning Kolkata, Jaipur, the Taj Mahal, and New Delhi. The trip was framed as a repair mission. U.S.-India relations have fallen to their lowest point in over two decades, strained by Trump’s tariff policies, Washington’s deepening engagement with Pakistan as an interlocutor in the Iran crisis, and the growing sense in New Delhi that the current American administration treats India as a market to be bullied rather than a partner to be respected.

The diplomatic choreography told the story more precisely than any joint statement. When Rubio landed in New Delhi, he was greeted not by Jaishankar or another senior figure, but by a mid-level bureaucrat. In India’s diplomatic language, this is not an oversight. It is a sentence. It says: we received you because protocol requires it, and we assigned you the rank your government’s behaviour has earned.

Rubio met Modi. He described an Indian pledge to purchase $500 billion in American goods over the next five years. He participated in a Quad foreign ministers meeting. He visited the Taj Mahal. He described India as being “at the cornerstone of how the United States approaches the Indo-Pacific.” And India listened politely, dispatched its commerce minister to Canada with a hundred-member trade delegation at the same moment, and continued doing what it has done since Nehru: playing every side simultaneously, committing to none, and reminding anyone who confuses accommodation with alignment that India is on India’s side.

The TACO framework could not be more visible. India accommodated Rubio — the meetings happened, the statements were issued, the Quad convened. India simultaneously obfuscated — the $500 billion pledge is aspirational, not contractual, and the details are conspicuously absent. India has not capitulated on Russian oil, on its own tariff structure, or on its refusal to take sides in the Iran conflict. And the threat — subtle, structural, unmistakable — is the message India sends by courting Canada, maintaining Russian ties, and deepening engagement with the Gulf states at the very moment Washington expects gratitude for a secretary of state’s visit.

This is not comfortable. It is not supposed to be. The impossible democracy is not just India’s project. It is, increasingly, ours.
