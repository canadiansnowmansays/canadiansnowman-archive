Fifth of five essays Stephen Gordon Sept 11, 2026

Four essays ago, this series made a commitment worth restating before it is honored: if the record showed Donald Trump applying a consistent standard to election administration across 2020 and 2026, this series would say so, using the word it held back rather than the word its readers might have expected from the start. The record is now assembled. Part I catalogued the specific, falsifiable claims Trump made about the election he lost. Part II catalogued the mechanisms his administration has built for the election he has not yet lost. Part III catalogued something neither category quite anticipated: federal power spent not on the next election but on reopening the last one, and on dismantling the bodies built to referee both. What remains is the thing this series postponed three times — laying the claims and the mechanisms side by side, pair by pair, and asking what connects them.

Start with the machines, because Part I ended there and Part III returned to them. In 2020, Trump and his allies claimed Dominion’s voting machines had switched votes against him — a claim every court that heard it rejected, a claim Fox News paid $787.5 million to settle rather than defend, a claim Sidney Powell pleaded guilty to related conduct over rather than contest at trial. In 2026, the federal body responsible for certifying that voting machines meet national standards — the Election Assistance Commission — has no sitting commissioners at all, as Alan Feuer, Nick Corasaniti, and Alexandra Berzon reported for The New York Times, emptied by the same president in July, months before ballots are cast on the machines it is supposed to certify. That does not prove Trump still believes, in 2026, that the 2020 machines were rigged; nothing in the public record suggests he does. It proves something narrower and, in its own way, more telling: that whatever he now believes about the last election, he is not willing to leave a bipartisan body standing that could rule on the next one either way. The claim in 2020 was that the machines could not be trusted. The action in 2026 is not to make them more trustworthy. It is to remove the only body positioned to certify, one way or the other, whether they are.

Take the rolls next, because they carry the same shape and offer the hardest numbers in this series. In 2020, Trump’s campaign alleged thousands of dead voters had cast ballots in Georgia; the actual number, once checked against death records, ran to dozens. In 2026, Trump named Nevada by name in a prime-time address as one of four states harboring thousands of noncitizen voters — as Nick Corasaniti and Alan Feuer reported for The New York Times, 15,903 in Nevada specifically, part of a 278,000 figure across the four states. In a closed-door meeting, his own Homeland Security officials admitted they had so far substantiated 185 of those Nevada cases, calling even that number “the ceiling.” An Idaho review told the same story at smaller scale: 760 initial flags, roughly three dozen that survived investigation. The conservative Heritage Foundation, checking a quarter-century of records, found about 100 cases of noncitizen voting nationwide. Nationally, a ProPublica analysis of Justice Department data found that between January 2025 and May 2026, fewer than 150 alleged noncitizen voters were referred for prosecution and only 41 were actually charged, against an electorate of more than 150 million. The evidentiary ratio has not meaningfully changed in six years — the fraud, checked against the record, keeps turning out to be a rounding error, whoever checks it. What changed is which lever gets pulled in response to that rounding error. In 2020, Trump filed a lawsuit over it and lost. In 2026, his Justice Department threatens prison time over it, has sued thirty states over their voter rolls directly, and has lost twenty-three of those thirty suits outright — a fact this series recorded in Part III precisely because a series testing consistency does not get to report only the wins.

The institutional-verification pair is the sharpest. In 2020, Attorney General Barr told the Associated Press there was no fraud that would have changed the outcome, and resigned weeks later once he and the president could no longer occupy the same page. In 2020, CISA Director Chris Krebs told the country the election was the most secure in American history, and was fired by tweet for it. Both men were Trump’s own appointees. Both were removed, in effect or in fact, for reporting a finding that did not favor him. In 2026, that pattern has not softened with time; it has hardened into policy. Krebs’s security clearance was revoked and the Attorney General was directed to investigate him — not for anything new, but for the same November 2020 statement that got him fired the first time. The agency he once ran has had its entire election-security function eliminated in the pending federal budget, its staff cut by roughly a third, its information-sharing hub with state election officials shut down. Barr’s old department, meanwhile, is the one now sending the prosecution letters. The Federal Election Commission — which was, in February 2025, actively reviewing complaints against Trump personally — had its chair targeted for removal in the same month that review was ongoing. None of these four institutions changed what they do. What changed is that the one making the findings no longer has the standing, the staff, the clearance, or in the EAC’s case the personnel at all, to make them. The reverse case makes the same point from the other direction. Heather Honey spent 2022 building the case that a bipartisan, multistate voter-roll consortium called ERIC could not be trusted — a theory nine states acted on and election officials of both parties have since said was baseless. In 2026, rather than being investigated for it the way Krebs was investigated for telling the truth, Honey is a senior Department of Homeland Security official, and it is her department now asking the Justice Department to open a fresh investigation into the same consortium she once helped drive states out of. Being wrong in Trump’s favor and being right against him have not, on this record, drawn the same consequence from the same administration once.

Fulton County is where the pattern stops being an inference and becomes a single, continuous thread running through all three body essays. In January 2021, Trump personally telephoned Georgia’s secretary of state and asked him to find 11,780 votes in that county’s tally — a request Raffensperger refused, after a hand recount had already confirmed the result. In January 2026, federal agents did not ask. They executed a search warrant on the same county’s election hub and removed roughly seven hundred boxes of the same 2020 ballots, four years after three separate counts, including that hand recount, had already settled the question the warrant was nominally investigating. A judge let the Justice Department keep the ballots while noting the seizure was “certainly not perfect.” A second judge quashed a follow-up subpoena for the names of the county’s 2020 election workers as “staggering” in scope. This is not two different disputes about Fulton County four years apart. It is one dispute that could not be won by asking, and was pursued instead through the machinery of federal law enforcement once that machinery answered to the person who had done the asking. One fact in this thread cuts against a simple capture story, and it is worth sitting with rather than passing over: the judge who let the Justice Department keep the ballots was a Trump appointee who also wrote, in his own opinion, that the seizure was “certainly not perfect.” A fully captured judiciary does not produce that sentence. What it produces instead is a judge willing to criticize the government’s conduct in writing while still declining, on narrower legal grounds, to reverse it — which suggests the pattern this series has documented is real but not total, a distinction that makes the finding more credible, not less.

The redistricting fight and the fake-elector scheme are the pair least alike on the surface and most alike underneath. The fake electors were an attempt to override a certified count after the fact, through paperwork built to hand Mike Pence a pretext he was never going to accept. Redistricting reshapes the board before a single vote is counted, so that the outcome depends less on persuading anyone and more on which seats exist to be won. Different mechanism, same objective: decouple the result from the count. That structural resemblance is real, and it is also the least extraordinary pairing in this essay. Redistricting, including aggressive mid-decade redistricting, is a legal and long-practiced tool of American politics; the fake-elector scheme was a criminal conspiracy that has already produced indictments in four states. Placing them in the same paragraph illustrates a shared objective. It does not equate their severity, and this essay does not intend it to. It is also worth repeating, because the series’ own fairness commitment requires it, that California’s Democratic countermap shows this particular game is not being played by only one side — but nonpartisan trackers agree the current national redistricting race began with Trump’s own request to Texas in the summer of 2025, which means the countermaps are a response to a race he started, not evidence the race was already underway before he started it.

Then there is the claim that has not changed shape at all — and unlike the last several pairings, this one no longer needs a caveat about weak evidence, because the same speech that revived it also produced its own documentary refutation. In 2020, the theory was that Dominion’s voting machines carried out a vote-switching scheme with ties to the Venezuelan government — a claim no court accepted and Fox paid three-quarters of a billion dollars to have made about it stop being repeated on air. In July 2026, in the same prime-time address that pitched the SAVE America Act on unsubstantiated Chinese-interference claims, Trump revived the Venezuela theory nearly verbatim, calling barcode and QR-code voting systems “vulnerable” and “easily compromised” and citing newly declassified intelligence about Smartmatic — the company at the center of the original 2020 theory, whose old rival Dominion now trades under the name Liberty Vote. The declassified document he pointed to does not say what he claimed it says. A CIA analysis in that same release states that the Venezuelan government had “some capability in manipulating electronic voting systems” but that neither Venezuela nor Smartmatic could “manipulate the outcome of an election outside Venezuela.” His own government’s own selectively released evidence forecloses the claim he built on it — the same shape as Barr’s December 2020 finding, except this time the record contradicting the president is a document his own administration chose to declassify and hand to him. Two claims, one theory, six years apart, and on both occasions the paper trail that was supposed to prove it instead disproved it.

And there is the pair this series hoped it would not have to make, because it is the one where the stakes stop being institutional and become physical — and the two halves are not equivalent and should not be written as though they are. On January 6, 2021, several thousand people marched on the Capitol because the president had told them, for weeks, that Congress needed to count only the electors “lawfully slated” — his own fake-elector scheme’s language, handed back to a crowd as if it described the real count rather than the fraudulent one. Five people died in connection with that day. In 2026, the open question is whether the government itself might appear at polling places in a uniform. Trump has said he would “do anything necessary” to secure the midterms; in a January 2026 interview with The New York Times, he said he regretted not having ordered the National Guard to seize voting machines after his 2020 loss; and he has left open the possibility of a national emergency declaration to gain control over November’s election specifically. None of that has happened. This essay draws no equivalence between a riot that occurred and a deployment that, as of this writing, has not — that would be a different and unsupported claim, and this series will not make it to complete a symmetry the record does not offer. What the record does support is narrower: a president who did not rule out force over an unfavorable count in 2020 has also not ruled it out over one in 2026, in his own words, on both occasions. The direction of the threat reversed, from a crowd outside government aimed at Congress to officers potentially inside government aimed at voters. The willingness behind it, on the president’s own statements four to six years apart, did not.

This is the point at which the Introduction’s test comes due. A consistent standard, honestly applied, would look like this: skepticism of centralized federal power over elections in 2020, and the same skepticism in 2026 once that power sat in friendly hands; or trust in state-run administration in both years, win or lose. Nothing in the assembled record produces either pattern. What it produces instead is a standard that has been consistent the entire time, just never the one stated out loud. Verification is treated as legitimate exactly when it confirms the outcome Trump wants, and as illegitimate, corrupt, or in need of federal correction exactly when it does not — and the tool available to enforce that standard has simply scaled with the power available to him. Lacking the presidency’s machinery in December 2020, the tool was lawsuits, a phone call, and finally a crowd. Holding that machinery in 2026, the tool is redistricting maps, prosecution letters, a database, a seized county’s ballots, an emptied commission, and an agency director’s revoked clearance four years after the fact. The claim changed direction. The standard underneath it — my win is proof the system worked; my loss, or the prospect of one, is proof the system needs to answer to me — has not moved an inch. This decision rule, not any single pair above it, is the actual finding of this series, and it stands on its own before any single word is reached for to describe it.

The word this series settled on in the Introduction, and has withheld since, is hypocrisy — used in its precise, technical sense and no other: not a change of mind, not an error later corrected, but one rule applied to a circumstance that disadvantaged him and a different, more convenient rule applied to the identical circumstance once it did not, without the standard’s shift ever being acknowledged as a shift at all. The word names the pattern. The decision rule described above is the pattern. A reader is free to keep the rule and set the word aside; the record does not depend on the word to make its case, only on the rule the record actually shows.

Some of these pairs are more than inconsistency, and the distinction is worth drawing precisely rather than folding it into the same word. Hypocrisy is applying a different standard once it suits you. Projection is narrower and, in its way, more revealing: accusing someone else of the specific fault you are committing, or about to commit, yourself — which only works because the accuser knows exactly what the fault looks like from the inside. In 2020, the claim was that the system had been rigged to produce a predetermined outcome against him. In 2026, by his own word, choice, and repetition, the SAVE America Act exists to “guarantee” the midterms — not secure them, not protect them from fraud, guarantee them. He did not adopt a different standard for what elections should do. He described, accurately, the thing he once called illegitimate, and then built it. That is the clean case. Fulton County is related but weaker, and worth keeping separate rather than folding into the same claim: in 2020, the accusation was that the county’s election administration could not be trusted; in 2026, a court found the defect in the seizer, not the county. That is not the accusation changing hands. It is a different fault, in a different place, discovered by the same process this series has tracked throughout. Hypocrisy explains why the standard moved. Projection, in the one case where it actually holds, explains where the accusation landed.

It is also, on the record assembled here, categorically different from the objection this series raised against itself in the Introduction — that every losing side complains about the process that beat it. The test is simple to state: did the losing side accept the courts and legislatures as the forum where the argument would be settled, win or lose, or did it reach for a remedy outside that forum entirely? Al Gore’s five-week challenge and Stacey Abrams’s advocacy for structural reform both pass that test. Nothing in the record above does. Sixty-plus lawsuits alleging an invented conspiracy, a call asking an official to manufacture votes, a scheme to hand the vice presidency a false slate of electors, and a crowd sent to the Capitol on the strength of it are not what accepting the forum looks like. Neither is emptying the commission built to certify the next election’s machines, nor seizing that same county’s already-triple-counted ballots by the truckload, nor revoking the clearance of the man who said the count was clean. Both records fail the same test the Gore and Abrams comparisons pass. That is the difference scale was always meant to mark, and the record has now marked it twice.

Say plainly, then, what four essays were built to earn the standing to say: Donald Trump is the Hypocrite-in-Chief of his own stated cause. He did not become one between 2020 and 2026. The record suggests he already was one in December of 2020, filing sixty lawsuits against a count his own Justice Department and his own cybersecurity chief had already told him was clean. What changed is that he now holds the office, and the office, it turns out, was always the only thing missing. A grievance machine, once it is finally handed the levers of government, does not stop being a grievance machine. It simply stops needing to ask.
