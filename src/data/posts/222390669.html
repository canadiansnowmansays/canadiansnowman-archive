Most of what this series has covered happens in the open. A lawsuit is filed, a judge rules, a thumb drive goes unopened, a clerk retrains her poll workers. The friction is visible, and so, eventually, is the cost.

There is one form of election management that does its work quietly and in advance, and is finished long before anyone votes. It is the map.

On October 26, 2026, Eve Washington, Nate Cohn and Rumsey Taylor published in The New York Times a ranking of every state with more than one congressional district, from the most gerrymandered to the least. Their method was not to look at the shapes and wince. They took 5,000 computer-drawn maps for each state, 220,000 in all, built from Harvard’s ALARM redistricting project. Every map was drawn to be compact, and none took any account of party or race. Then they compared each state’s real map with that crowd of party-blind alternatives. Two numbers came out. The first is how many seats the real map hands one party beyond what the simulations would give. The second, scored out of 99, is aggressiveness: how close the map comes to the most extreme gerrymander the mapmakers could have assembled.

The result is the most useful single picture of the 2026 House map anyone has produced. It also overturns a fair amount of received wisdom, on both sides. I have built a companion interactive map from the ranking, where each state can be clicked for its numbers. What follows is what the numbers say.

The arms race, scored

The top of the list is the story of the last eighteen months, and readers of this series will know it.

Texas is first. Its mid-decade map, drawn in 2025 at the President’s urging, gives Republicans about six more seats than a party-blind map would. It splits the north side of Austin among five districts within a short drive, and stretches Dallas suburbs into districts that reach hundreds of miles west. The simulations suggest that a neutral map would produce around a dozen more districts that are competitive or lean Democratic. Texas would become the country’s main congressional battleground instead of a Republican fortress.

California is second, and deliberately so. Its 2025 map was drawn to cancel Texas out, and on the numbers it nearly does: five seats to Democrats against Texas’s six to Republicans, with an identical aggressiveness score of 73. On the new California map, Kamala Harris would have carried 48 of 52 districts. The reason California ranks just below Texas is a quirk of political geography the Times explains well. About four in five Californians live in places that vote Democratic, so even a neutral map would hand Democrats something like 43 seats. Texas has no such cushion. Nearly half of its people live in areas that voted for Harris, so a neutral Texas map would cost Republicans heavily.

Put plainly, Texas started the war and California answered it, and the net effect between the two is about one seat. Months of special sessions, ballot measures and court fights produced roughly a draw. Gavin Newsom’s argument was that unilateral disarmament against Texas would have been worse. On the arithmetic alone, the ranking bears him out, whatever one thinks of the principle.

Then come the states that followed Texas and had no California to answer them. Florida is third, with nearly four extra Republican seats, under a state constitution that bans partisan gerrymandering. North Carolina is fourth, and earns the second-highest aggressiveness score on the list, 88: its map squeezes out almost every seat a Republican map could take without spreading the party’s voters too thin. Tennessee is fifth, with an aggressiveness score of 99 out of 99, the maximum. It did not need strange shapes. It simply divided Memphis and Nashville among several Republican districts, an outcome the simulations almost never produce, since they nearly always give each city its own seat.

Ohio, seventh, adds about a seat and a half for Republicans under a map passed unanimously by a commission that voters created to stop exactly this.

The tally

If you add up the seats gained in the Times’s table by party, the rough totals come to about 18.6 seats tilted toward Republicans and about 11.2 toward Democrats, a net Republican advantage of a little over seven seats. The Times does not present it this way, and the two measures behind its numbers are not built to be summed. Treat it as a rough indication, not a forecast. But the direction is not in doubt. Of the seven states with the largest tilts, four were drawn by Republicans, two by Democrats (California, as a reply, and Illinois) and one, Ohio, by a commission that produced a Republican-leaning map anyway.

Seven seats is not a decisive margin in a good Democratic year. It is a decisive margin in a close one. That is the point of a gerrymander. It does not decide the election. It decides how big a wave the other side needs to win it.

The gerrymanders that aren’t

The more surprising part of the ranking is the bottom half, where some of the most notorious maps in America turn out to be less guilty than their reputations.

Illinois is the Republican exhibit A, and its 13th District, which winds from East St. Louis through Springfield to Champaign, may be the ugliest line on any congressional map. The simulations rank Illinois sixth, a serious score but not a catastrophic one. Its mapmakers went to great lengths for about two seats, and a neutral map might still leave Democrats competitive in the 13th’s replacement. Tennessee, with clean lines, does something more consequential: it turns safely Democratic seats into safely Republican ones.

Massachusetts gives Democrats all nine of its seats in a state where Republicans win about two votes in five. The simulations say a neutral map would almost always do the same, because nearly every town in the state voted for Harris. Connecticut’s five-for-five Democratic sweep is the same story. Hawaii is the extreme case: the Times says it could not find a way to draw a Republican district there at all.

And Wisconsin, which for a decade has been the textbook example of gerrymandering, is ranked thirty-eighth and labelled “fine.” The state splits close to fifty-fifty, but its map gives Republicans six of eight seats. The simulations produce the same result, because Democratic voters are concentrated in Milwaukee and Madison. Once each city has its own district, as it should, there is no natural place to draw a third Democratic seat. To get one, a mapmaker would have to break those cities up, which would itself be a gerrymander.

This is the part of the ranking partisans on both sides will like least, and it is the part worth taking most seriously. A map can be unfair to a party’s voters without anyone having rigged it. Geography does some of the work on its own. The honest question about a gerrymander is not whether one party wins more seats than its vote share. It is whether it wins more than a neutral map would give it.

Where this series has been

Three entries in the ranking close loops this series opened.

Missouri ranks thirty-fourth, with zero seats gained and an aggressiveness score of 1. That number is the end of the fight Parts L, LV and LVI followed through August and September: the referendum petition, the contradictory same-day court orders, the Eighth Circuit and the Supreme Court. The Republican plan to eliminate the Kansas City Democratic seat was derailed, and the map Missouri will use on November 3 is, according to the simulations, about as neutral as a Missouri map gets. The clerks who spent September three weeks behind will at least be running an election on fair lines. Missouri voters will also decide Proposition A, which asks whether to remove the referendum mechanism that saved that map. The ranking is, in effect, a reminder of what the referendum was worth.

Utah ranks twenty-fifth and is the only state that went from a partisan gerrymander to a neutral map before the midterms, after its courts struck down the Republican plan. Had the old map survived, Utah would have been in the top ten. Instead, Salt Lake City gets a Democratic-leaning district, just as nearly every simulation draws one. Utah’s lieutenant governor, Deidre Henderson, appeared in Part LIX planning for federal interference. Her state is also the only one where a court made the map fairer this cycle.

Virginia ranks twenty-sixth, but the Times notes that if Virginia Democrats had succeeded with their proposed 10-1 map, the state would have been roughly tied with North Carolina for fourth place. Virginia’s Supreme Court struck it down. The ranking is a useful corrective for anyone, this series included, tempted to treat gerrymandering as a one-party sin. It was Democrats, this time, whom a court stopped.

The next war is already drawn

The Times labels several states “Watching”: the places where redistricting fights are likely in 2028. Most are in the Deep South, and the reason is a Supreme Court decision.

In Louisiana v. Callais, the Court cleared the way for states to dismantle majority-Black districts that the Voting Rights Act had previously required. The Times removed race-based requirements from its simulations to reflect that ruling, and the consequences show up in its southern entries. Louisiana has already redrawn. Alabama dropped its second majority-Black district. Mississippi has not redrawn yet, but its Republicans are reported to be considering a four-to-nothing map for 2028, which would eliminate the state’s only Democratic seat.

Here the simulations make an uncomfortable point. In several of these states, a race-blind map is not the same thing as a fair one. In Mississippi and Alabama, the simulations score the current maps as slightly favourable to Democrats, because a race-blind algorithm would not draw a majority-Black district at all. It would produce one or two competitive seats in its place. A future legislature that wants to remove those seats entirely will be able to point to the simulations and call its work neutral.

That is the friction strategy in its longest form. Every tactic this series has tracked, from the voter-roll suits to the thumb drive to the threat of prosecution, works on the weeks before a vote. A map works on the decade.

What a ranking cannot do

The Times is candid about its method’s limits, and so should I be. A simulation measures maps against a neutral baseline, not against fairness, and the two are not always the same. Pennsylvania and Michigan both produce maps somewhat better for Democrats than the simulations, but the Times rates them “balanced,” because the result is closer to the state’s actual vote. Reasonable people can disagree about whether a mapmaker should ignore party or deliberately balance it. The Times accepts both, which is generous and probably right.

What the ranking does show, beyond reasonable dispute, is where the real damage sits. It is not in the squiggly lines that get the attention. It is in a handful of large states, mostly drawn in the last eighteen months, where a legislature decided before anyone voted how big a wave the other party would need.

A map is also a forecast, and forecasts can be overrun. On October 30, the Cook Political Report said Democrats could gain between 12 and 25 House seats, and it moved three Republican-leaning districts to toss-ups, among them South Carolina’s First, which had been redrawn to favour Republicans further. The Times’s own ranking notes that North Carolina’s mapmakers stopped where they did because reaching for one more seat would have spread their voters too thin. In a large enough wave, even a map drawn to the limit can discover where the limit was. Part LXII looks at the district where that test is sharpest.

Part LIX ended with clerks writing a new chapter in the binder, for a federal government they never expected to plan against. The map is the chapter nobody gets to write. It was finished in Austin, Sacramento, Tallahassee, Raleigh and Nashville long before November 3, and on Election Day it will do its work without a single agent, monitor or thumb drive.

There is something faintly Leacockian about the result, too. After a year of the fiercest redistricting war in modern American history, Texas and California have fought each other almost exactly to a draw. The rest of the country has been left to absorb the difference.
