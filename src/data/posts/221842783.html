Every essay in this series so far has tracked AI risk through incidents, rhetoric, or regulatory capture — events with a clear before and after. This entry tracks something that doesn’t announce itself as an event at all: a labour-market shift visible only in aggregate wage and hiring data, unfolding slowly enough that the unemployment rate, the one number most people actually watch, has moved in the opposite direction.

The prediction that didn’t come true, on its own terms

In May 2025, Dario Amodei warned that AI could eliminate half of entry-level white-collar jobs within five years and push unemployment above 10 percent. Jim VandeHei and Mike Allen of Axios, who interviewed him, summarised the warning as AI that could “wipe out half of all entry-level white-collar jobs — and spike unemployment to 10-20% in the next one to five years.” As The New York Times’ Talmon Joseph Smith reported on September 16, 2026, that hasn’t happened — unemployment fell to 4.1 percent in August, even as the same models Amodei was warning about have grown considerably more capable since he made the prediction. The next report did not change the picture much. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on October 2, 2026 that employers added just 29,000 jobs in September and that the unemployment rate was 4.2 percent, with July revised from a gain of 21,000 jobs to a loss of 10,000. A labour market adding almost no jobs while unemployment stays near 4 percent is a market where few people are being let go and few are being taken on. Read narrowly, this looks like a clean falsification of the doomsaying this series has catalogued at length across Parts 94 through 101. Read against the research Smith actually reports, it looks more like the prediction measured the wrong variable.

Where the damage is actually showing up

Sania Edlich, a Princeton researcher, and Torsten Slok, chief economist at Apollo Global Management, reviewed data across 321 occupations and found that companies are capturing AI’s productivity gains by suppressing wage growth rather than cutting headcount. Occupations highly exposed to AI have seen real wages decline 6.7 percent since 2023, with the effect landing hardest on the people least equipped to absorb it: service and administrative workers down 24 percent, the bottom quarter of earners down 11 percent, and top earners barely touched at all. Slok’s own framing draws the exposure line plainly: “If you are a hairdresser, if you are a massage therapist, if you are a doorman, of course your occupation is not threatened by A.I., whereas if you are a journalist or an economist, there is just more pressure on wages because now the work that we do can be replaced, at least in some ways, by A.I.” His summary of where to actually look for the impact is the sentence this essay is built around: “The wage that you’re getting paid is where we’re seeing all the action.”

Melissa Krut, a senior vice president at the Virginia software firm Sogolytics, gave Smith the view from inside a company living this pattern in real time: three years of rising productivity, client growth, and revenue, without a proportional increase in headcount across sales, account management, or support. Her own read on what that means for anyone seeking a raise or a new job is blunt: “If your job could be done by A.I., yeah, you better have something else on top. What is the cherry on top that you’re bringing to that situation?” That is a genuinely different labour market than the one AI-doom rhetoric usually describes — not workers replaced outright, but workers required to justify, individually and continuously, why a machine hasn’t already absorbed their job description.

The generation absorbing this first

Liminal Capital, an investment firm, estimated in August that roughly a third of jobs are more exposed to outright substitution than to augmentation by AI. Inside that group, new hires aged 22 to 25 have fallen by a third since 2021, even as firing rates have barely moved — meaning the adjustment isn’t happening through people losing jobs they already have, but through people never getting hired into the first rung at all. Ben Verschuere, one of the paper’s authors, points to a specific mechanism: young workers are, definitionally, the ones entering the labour force right now, which makes them the group most exposed to employers who have quietly raised the bar for a first hire. The retirement of the baby boomer generation, which should in theory be opening space and upward pressure on wages for the cohorts below them, hasn’t produced that effect either. Loujaina Abdelwahed of the labour-data firm Revelio Labs gives this dynamic its clearest possible summary: “We could be living in a world where unemployment is low while job quality, composition and wages deteriorate” — a sentence that describes an economy performing well on the one metric most commonly cited as proof AI hasn’t yet caused serious harm, while quietly failing on nearly every metric underneath it.

The aggregate wage data bears this out at the national level. Average hourly earnings growth for rank-and-file workers peaked above 6 percent in 2022 and had fallen to near 3 percent by August — a decline that runs directly against the usual pattern, since wages typically rise as unemployment falls, which it has done all year. Verschuere’s own phrase for the anomaly is precise: “Wage growth has been diverging from productivity.” The labour share of national income — compensation measured against corporate earnings — has fallen roughly ten percentage points from its late-1990s peak, meaning the gains from that diverging productivity are accruing overwhelmingly to capital rather than to the workers producing it. The September figures sharpened the point: the bureau’s October 2 release put the year-on-year rise in average hourly earnings at 3.0 percent, below the 3.4 percent annual rise in consumer prices it had reported for August. On those numbers, the typical paycheque was losing ground to prices.

The most detailed academic tracking points the same way. Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen of Stanford’s Digital Economy Lab, whose payroll-data study “Canaries in the Coal Mine?” was revised on August 12, 2026, found that employment of workers aged 22 to 25 in A.I.-exposed occupations “now stands 19% below where it would be,” with the gap widening steadily since August 2025. They found no evidence of widespread displacement across the economy, and they located the damage in the same place Liminal did: in reduced hiring rather than in separations, and in jobs where A.I. substitutes for workers rather than complementing them. A year earlier the picture looked calmer. In October 2025, researchers at Yale’s Budget Lab concluded that 33 months after ChatGPT’s release “the broader labor market has not experienced a discernible disruption.” Both findings can hold at once. The aggregate stays quiet while one cohort’s first rung quietly disappears.

Two employers, two versions of the same story

Two specific companies in Smith’s reporting illustrate the mechanism at ground level better than any aggregate statistic. Ethan Giffin, chief executive of the B2B eCommerce Agency, has largely stopped hiring technical writers: work that once took a year or more to complete for a 5,000-to-10,000-product catalogue now takes about three months with AI assistance, a cost saving he measures directly against the three or four salaries it used to require. Ryan Close, founder of the home-cocktail-machine company Bartesian, offers the more complicated case — his company’s revenue grew from $50 million to $80 million since 2022, largely on the back of AI tools that draft budgets, generate retail reports, and identify potential brand ambassadors, and he’s actually hiring more midlevel staff to focus on strategy rather than repetitive tasks. But the pay offers have fallen alongside the productivity gains: candidates two years out of college who commanded above $100,000 for sales and marketing roles four years ago are now, in Close’s own words, doing work he considers “commodifiable,” and he expects them to settle for around $80,000. “I think it’s just coming back down to earth,” he said. Two companies, two different hiring trajectories, and the identical underlying story: whatever AI is doing to the price of labour, it is doing it downward.

The work the machines create

There is a complication, and it cuts against the simplest version of both the boosters’ story and the doomers’. On October 10, Sarah Kessler reported in The New York Times‘s DealBook newsletter that the promised productivity gains are proving hard to find. In a survey of nearly 6,000 senior executives by researchers from the Atlanta Fed, Stanford, the Bank of England and the Autonomous Technological Institute of Mexico, about 90 per cent said A.I. had had no impact on either employment or productivity, even though 69 per cent said their firms used it. “The productivity payoff from A.I. remains a forecast rather than an observation,” wrote Torsten Slok, the same Apollo economist whose wage study anchors this essay.

Part of the reason is a new kind of human work: checking the machine. Kessler calls it the “verification tax.” A Workday survey of 3,200 employees and business leaders found they spent about 37 per cent of the time A.I. saved them correcting, clarifying or rewriting its output. Google economists found that nearly 90 per cent of scientists who saved time with A.I. spent a tenth of it checking the results, and 46 per cent spent more than a quarter. A study by M.I.T. and Wharton researchers of 500,000 GitHub developers found the gains “shrink sharply as work moves from writing code to shipping software.” “The binding constraint on growth is no longer intelligence,” the M.I.T. Sloan researcher Christian Catalini wrote. “It is human verification bandwidth.” Using one A.I. to check another does not escape the problem, he warned, because “all the blind spots will be completely correlated.”

For workers, the verdict is mixed. Alexandra Mousavizadeh of Evident Insights said the banks that had invested most in A.I. were the ones hiring most aggressively, and that their bankers were working longer hours than ever. Verification is real work, and someone has to be paid to do it. But it is a different job from the one being automated: the reviewer rather than the author, the “botsitter” rather than the analyst. “You cannot verify if you do not understand the problem,” said Ion Stoica of Berkeley. That raises the question the young workers in this essay face most sharply. If the entry-level tasks that taught people to understand the problem are the ones being automated, where will the next generation of verifiers learn?

The jobs the money creates

There is one more effect that rarely enters the argument, and Annie Lowrey made the case for it in The Atlantic on October 15. In the Bay Area, the fastest-growing job listings are for machine-learning engineers and cloud architects, as everyone would expect. But demand is also surging, Laura Ullrich of the Indeed Hiring Lab told her, for estheticians, Pilates instructors, massage therapists, psychotherapists and interior designers. Those are A.I. jobs too, Lowrey argues: someone earning seven figures at Anthropic wants a person to untie the knots in their back and another for the knots in their psyche.

Economists, she explains, think about a general-purpose technology’s effect on work in three layers. The first is direct: interpreters and call-centre staff lose work, while electricians building data centres gain it. The second is new kinds of job that are “impossible” to imagine in advance, as María Jesús Ramírez of the McKinsey Global Institute put it, the way the combustion engine eventually produced airline pilots; McKinsey forecasts 500,000 to 2 million such roles, from robot trainers to deepfake investigators. The third is what Lowrey calls “wealth jobs”: the services people buy once they are richer, mostly personal and face to face. Martha Gimbel of the Yale Budget Lab described them as the things you tell your parents you paid for, who answer that it was a waste of money, then ask, “Was it great?”

Rogé Karma, also in The Atlantic, in June, set out three questions that explain why that first layer is harder to predict than the forecasts suggest. Is the job a “strong bundle,” in the economist Luis Garicano’s phrase, where the routine tasks and the messy human ones are so bound together that handing off the first weakens the second, as with a trial lawyer who must do her own preparation to cross-examine well? If the product got cheaper, would people want much more of it, as with cars after the assembly line or bank tellers after the ATM, the pattern economists call the Jevons paradox? And does the technology enhance the worker’s expertise or commodify it? MIT’s David Autor and Neil Thompson found that computers left accounting clerks fewer but better paid and inventory clerks more numerous but worse paid, a pattern that has held across more than 300 occupations. Radiology, the profession Geoffrey Hinton said in 2016 should stop training people, passes all three tests: there are 17 per cent more radiologists than there were then, and average pay has risen from about $350,000 to $570,000. Karma’s own caution is the right one. The bank teller survived the ATM and was then undone by the iPhone, which nobody saw coming.

Lowrey’s own last paragraph supplies the condition that ties her argument back to this one. Wealth jobs appear only if A.I. actually produces wealth, and only if that wealth reaches ordinary households. If the gains stay with the people at the top, as the wage evidence in this part suggests they so far have, the third layer will be mostly cold-plunge installers for the few. “Pretty much everyone I know would love to hire a house manager,” she writes. “Pretty much nobody I know can afford to hire one, yet.”

The sectors where even the softer version of the story is failing

Some sectors have skipped the wage-suppression phase entirely and gone straight to headcount reduction. The US tech sector lost roughly 7,000 jobs in August and 48,000 over the preceding year, according to the economic analyst Joey Politano. Financial services — long one of the more reliable on-ramps into the upper middle class for new graduates — had shed 100,000 jobs between January and August, and the bureau’s September report put employment in financial activities 129,000 below its May 2025 peak, with most of the loss, 90,000 jobs, at insurance carriers and related firms. The share of US adults employed in financial firms has fallen to its lowest level since 1981, even as the number of applications to banks keeps rising by the hundreds of thousands. That combination — rising applicant pools, flat or falling hiring — is the labour-market signature of a sector where the wage-suppression Edlich and Slok describe has already given way to the more familiar and more visible version of the same story this series’ other essays have spent more time on: not fewer raises, but fewer jobs.

That distinction is the one worth carrying out of this essay. Every other part of this series has covered AI risk as something that announces itself — an incident, a resignation, a Truth Social post, a Senate hearing. This is the version that doesn’t announce itself at all. It shows up, if it shows up anywhere a casual observer would notice, as a slightly worse offer, a slightly longer job search, a raise that doesn’t quite keep pace with how much more the same job now produces. Unemployment, the number Amodei’s own prediction staked its credibility on, was simply never going to be the place this particular harm showed up first.
