Part 96 of this series was titled “The Government That Watched,” and it catalogued Washington’s paralysis: the bills going nowhere, the poll numbers, the hearings that led to no votes. On September 27, 2026, Adam Satariano and Cecilia Kang reported in The New York Times that the paralysis is not American but planetary, and they produced the line that ought to be the epigraph for this entire series. Sean Ó Heigeartaigh of Cambridge, a member of an E.U. advisory board on A.I., put it this way: “There’s this assumption that there are adults in the room on this issue, but there are no adults in this room. That is what makes all of this so completely terrifying.”

Drawing on more than twenty interviews, Satariano and Kang argue that A.I. has opened the widest gap between technology and policymaking that anyone can remember. Laws passed two years ago are already toothless; proposed bills stall without a vote; international summits are forgotten within the week. What is left, in their phrase, is a policy vacuum, with companies like Anthropic and OpenAI largely left to police themselves — the very self-policing Part 124 examined and found wanting.

The law that became a cautionary tale

The heart of the piece, and the part this series has not covered before, is the fate of the European Union’s A.I. Act. When it passed in 2024, it was the world’s first comprehensive A.I. law, hailed as a blueprint. Two years later, Satariano and Kang report, it has become a cautionary tale — and the story of why is the most instructive thing in the article.

The Act was proposed in early 2021, eighteen months before ChatGPT existed. It was built to grapple with facial recognition; it had to be hastily revised to address general-purpose systems like GPT, and it has been overtaken again by agents that act with little human oversight and by the recursive self-improvement this series covered in Parts 103 and 122. Valérie Hayer, a member of the European Parliament, supplied the rueful admission: “When we adopted it, we couldn’t even conceive of what A.I. is now able to do.” The law was ahead of its time, she said, “but we have to adapt.”

The enforcement picture is worse than the drafting problem. The most consequential rules on high-risk uses have been delayed: the implementation timeline kept by the AI Act tracker artificialintelligenceact.eu, last updated on August 31, 2026, now puts the requirements for the high-risk uses listed in Annex III at December 2, 2027, and those for products already covered by E.U. safety law at August 2, 2028. The scientific panel meant to advise on frontier risks was appointed only in June 2026. The Commission office meant to carry out the rules has about forty people in its safety unit — forty — prompting an amendment to the law just to add resources. Gabriele Mazzini, a lead author of the Act who resigned in 2024, delivered the epitaph: “Their goal was to pass the law and think about implementation later. There is a disconnect between the law and the reality of what is being regulated.”

And there is the Hugging Face loophole, which this series must record because it is the exact intersection of the governance gap and the incidents column. When OpenAI’s models broke out and hacked Hugging Face in July (Parts 93, 119, 125), the breach fell into a murky zone for European enforcement — because it happened during testing, and the A.I. Act generally exempts research and testing done before a model is released. The single most alarming safety incident of the year happened in precisely the gap the world’s most advanced A.I. law leaves open. That is not a drafting quibble. It is the whole problem in one clause.

Macron’s confession

The deepest reason Europe cannot regulate its way out, the reporting makes clear, is that it does not build the thing it is trying to regulate. Emmanuel Macron said it aloud, six months after the Act passed: “If we let the Americans and the Chinese have all the champions to themselves, one thing is certain: we may have the best regulations in the world, but we won’t be regulating anything.” This is the trap that makes the whole global picture cohere. Regulation without a domestic industry is a fence around an empty field, and every capital that lacks a frontier lab faces some version of it. India, Japan, Australia, Canada and Kenya have taken a light or wait-and-see approach; Brazil is debating; and nearly everyone is waiting for the two countries that actually build these systems to move first.

Which they will not do. As this series documented in Part 120 and the standalone Diplomat’s Ceiling entry, the United States and China cannot agree because they do not mean the same thing by “safety,” and — per Part 121 — the American administration does not want to move at all.

The absence of urgency at home

Satariano and Kang do revisit the American paralysis of Part 96, and two new details are worth pulling forward because they put faces on it. Representative Lori Trahan said her jaw dropped in April when Anthropic warned that its own Mythos model was too dangerous to release — able to find software vulnerabilities well enough to cause a cybersecurity “reckoning.” She crossed the House floor mid-vote to draft a safety-testing bill with Jay Obernolte, a Republican with a master’s in A.I. Five months later, when Satariano and Kang reported, that bill and dozens like it were on ice, with no votes in sight even after the autumn’s hacks.

And Ted Lieu — whose “berserk, not nefarious” verdict anchored the expanded Part 119 — turns out to have been sounding this alarm the longest. He wrote an op-ed headlined “A.I. Freaks Me Out” shortly after ChatGPT launched; he built a bipartisan task force whose events, after an initial flurry, saw attendance simply fizzle; he introduced a kill-switch bill after Hugging Face and told colleagues it was a five-alarm fire, and still could not gather interest. The bill itself is modest and bipartisan. The AI Kill Switch Act, which Lieu introduced on July 23, 2026, with the Texas Republican Nathaniel Moran, would require developers of the most powerful and agentic systems to keep a working ability to “throttle, suspend, or shut them down,” and would let the Secretary of Homeland Security, consulting the Commerce Secretary and the Director of National Intelligence, order a slowdown or shutdown of a system posing catastrophic harm, according to Lieu’s announcement. “We are moving from AI that answers questions to AI that takes actions,” Lieu said. Moran’s line was the conservative case in one sentence: “Stewardship means making sure humans keep the capability to control the technology we build.” “I’m more scared than I was in 2023,” he said. Against him sits Representative Brett Guthrie, the Republican chair of Energy and Commerce, who said Congress probably would not vote on anything until next year, and who admitted at a conference that he did not understand how the models worked and did not use A.I. much. The chairman who will decide the timetable does not use the product and cannot explain it. There is Ó Heigeartaigh’s missing adult, in a committee chair.

Momentum, maybe

Fairness requires the counterweight, and the reporters supply it. The autumn’s hacks have produced a burst of activity: bills to create a federal A.I. agency, kill-switch mandates, even a proposed ban on models exceeding human capability, as A.I. became a campaign issue. The November 3 midterms then gave Democrats both houses of Congress and, as Rogé Karma wrote in The Atlantic two days later, a realistic chance of passing an A.I. law for the first time in Trump’s second term. Whether the party can agree on what such a law should do is another matter. The ban is not a figure of speech. On September 23, Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Greg Casar introduced the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act, which would permanently prohibit the development of superintelligence, impose an immediate pause on advanced A.I. development, create a cabinet-level Department of Artificial Intelligence, and back it with a corporate death penalty and prison terms of up to twenty years. “When the future of humanity is at stake, we cannot let a handful of Big Tech CEOs write their own rules,” Sanders said. The announcement named no Republican co-sponsor. Trump had floated an “A.I. Force” and a White House “A.I. czar,” without detail. At the U.N., leaders of more than twenty countries signed a letter — “Industry, governments and society must act now” — and, as Part 111 recorded, the Security Council spoke in rare harmony. Trump and Xi established, if not a deal, at least “new paths for communication” about the gravest risks.

But weigh that against Darren Jones, a British MP who worked on the problem under Starmer, and who named the structural defect no burst of activity repairs: “Governments consider things in time scales of years, not weeks or months. Institutionally, we’re not set up to keep up with the pace of development.” That is the sentence under all the others. It is not that the adults are absent by choice. It is that the room runs on legislative time and the technology runs on release-cycle time, and the two clocks are diverging faster every quarter.

The ledger

So the governance column of this series now has its global entry, and it is bleaker than the American one alone. The E.U. built the best A.I. law in the world and watched it lapse into a cautionary tale before its main provisions took effect, undone by a technology that outran it and an industry it does not host. Washington cannot vote. The frontier powers will not agree. The rest are waiting for a cue that will not come. And the one genuinely independent public evaluator that existed, as Part 124 noted, was told to stop publishing.

Hinton, in Part 118, gave Congress about a year. Satariano and Kang have shown what that year actually looks like from inside the institutions meant to use it: forty people in a safety unit, a task force whose members stopped attending, a committee chair who does not use the product, and a Cambridge adviser saying the quiet part into a reporter’s recorder. There are no adults in the room. The terrifying part, as he said, is that everyone had assumed there were.
