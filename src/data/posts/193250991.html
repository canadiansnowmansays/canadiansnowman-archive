It was Wayne who raised it at the April meeting of the Elsinore Tourism and Culture Committee, with the calm confidence of a man who has done his research and trusts the numbers will speak for themselves.

He proposed a civic statue.

Not just any civic statue. Elsinore’s most famous native son. A permanent installation on the riverfront near the pedestrian bridge, where foot traffic was strong, tour bus access on Huron Street was manageable, and the sightlines from the water were, in his assessment, excellent.

He had brought comparables.

Graceland received six hundred thousand visitors a year. The Elvis Presley birthplace in Tupelo, Mississippi: approximately one hundred thousand. The John Lennon statue in Liverpool: a significant draw in its own right, quite apart from the broader Beatles tourism economy, which Wayne had researched and set aside in the interest of keeping the presentation to a manageable length.

He did not say that Justin Bieber was better than Shakespeare. He was careful about this. He simply noted that Elsinore had produced a figure of international cultural significance, that international cultural significance was a tourism asset, and that the riverfront location he had identified was currently occupied by three municipal benches and a recycling receptacle that had been out of service since November.

He sat down. The committee considered this.

The Swan Warden noted that the swans use that bank in April. She handed back the map. She sat down.

Alistair heard about it on a Tuesday.

By Thursday he had opinions.

He delivered them in the Festival Theatre parking lot, to Wayne, who had stopped by to drop off the latest Gold Level booking figures and had not anticipated staying for seventeen minutes. Alistair was not opposed, in principle, to recognising Elsinore’s cultural contributions across a range of disciplines. He was not making an argument about artistic merit.

He was making an argument about the riverfront.

The riverfront, Alistair said, belonged to the water. It belonged to the Avon and the Elizabethan Gardens and the swans and the particular quality of light on the water in the early morning that had been informing theatrical imagination in this city for seventy years. It was not an installation site. It was not Graceland. It was not, with respect to Wayne’s research, Tupelo, Mississippi.

He said this with the passion of a man defending something he loves, which he was. Wayne listened with the patience of a man who has heard passion before and knows it has a natural duration. He waited until Alistair was done. “I’ll put it on the Tourism and Culture Committee agenda,” he said, and went back to his office.

The committee met the following Tuesday. Mayor Doug wore his very good coat. It was the one his wife had told him made him look like he meant it. He meant the statue question.

He thanked Wayne for the thorough proposal and thanked Alistair for his — he paused briefly — passion for Elsinore’s cultural identity in all its dimensions. He said both perspectives reflected the remarkable richness of this community. He said he was proud to serve a city that could have this conversation. He did not indicate which side of the conversation he was on. This was intentional.

Constance Hay sat in the back row. She sat in the back row of every significant Elsinore meeting, and had done so since approximately 1974, and she had the quality of someone who had been attending meetings for long enough to know that the back row is where the useful observations are made and the front row is where they are presented. She wore a pale blue cardigan. Her white hair was arranged precisely. She had a small notebook of her own — not the Swan Warden’s dark green hardcover, but a narrow spiral-bound one, personal rather than institutional — which she opened and did not immediately write in, which was how she began every meeting she attended.

Bradley Fenn, present in his planning committee observer capacity, requested the floor. His shoes were Italian and recently resoled. He said he supported the principle of civic commemoration. He said Elsinore should celebrate its cultural contributions. He said, however, that the committee should consider what message a statue sent about the city’s identity and whether that identity was determined by a single individual or by a broader community process.

He produced a slide. It showed four photographs of statues in American cities and the controversies that had surrounded them.

Alistair looked at the slide. He said there was a difference between controversies arising from statues associated with slavery or colonial violence and a proposed statue of a recording artist from southwestern Ontario primarily known for pop songs and a widely reported incident involving a limousine.

Bradley said he wasn’t drawing a direct comparison, simply noting the principle.

Alistair said the principle was not the same.

Mayor Doug thanked both of them.

Constance wrote one thing in her notebook. Nobody saw what it was. She did not look up from it immediately, which was how she always finished writing something.

The Swan Warden was consulted about the riverfront site. She looked at Wayne’s proposal map for a moment. The installation point was marked with a small star, approximately eight metres from the water’s edge, between the pedestrian bridge and the second bench.

“The swans use that bank in April,” she said.

She handed the map back. She sat down.

Wayne noted that the installation would be permanent and the April usage pattern could be accommodated in the design phase. The Swan Warden did not respond. She had said what she had to say.

Gerald Fitch noted that a permanent installation of the proposed dimensions would require a foundation permit from the municipality, that foundation permits in the riverfront conservation zone were subject to a heritage impact assessment, and that the last heritage impact assessment in that area had taken, he believed, fourteen months, though he would confirm the exact figure and circulate it. He said this without implication. It simply took fourteen months.

Carly covered the committee meeting for the Beacon. She asked Wayne after the meeting how many South Korean tourists visited Elsinore annually. Wayne gave her the figures without hesitation, broken down by quarter, with a note about the post-paddle-boat-photograph increase. She asked Alistair about the riverfront light he had mentioned. He gave her three minutes in the parking lot, which produced one quotable sentence about the relationship between a landscape and the imagination it produces, which Norman ran in the piece without editing because it was already the right length. She asked Bradley Fenn about the slide. He said he stood by the principle.

Norman ran the story on page one, below the fold, with a photograph of the riverfront benches and the out-of-service recycling receptacle. The headline read: Statue Proposal Divides Committee; Swans Unavailable for Comment. Norman wrote the headline himself. This was unusual. It meant he was pleased.

Jim walked past the proposed installation site on a Wednesday morning with the labrador and the vizsla. He looked at the riverfront. He looked at the three benches. He considered the situation in the way he considers most situations: noting what was present, noting what was absent.

He mentioned it to Maggie at Sunday dinner. Maggie had heard from both sides — Wayne’s ferret was in for its quarterly check, and the rescue greyhound’s owner had come for a follow-up — and said from where she sat both of them were right about different things.

Jim said that was about right. He also said the subcommittee would meet for a long time.

Maggie asked how long.

He said longer than the heritage assessment.

The boys ate their dinner.

The Perth County farmer drove into Elsinore on a Saturday for feed supplies and passed the riverfront on his way back through. He slowed, briefly, near the pedestrian bridge. He looked at the three benches. He looked at the water.

He was asked, at the feed store before he left, what he thought about the statue question.

“Both of them,” he said. “Put up both of them.”

He paid for his feed. He drove home.

Nobody did this. The subcommittee had not considered it as an option. The farmer’s suggestion sits in the minutes, unexamined — possibly the most democratic proposal on the table, certainly the most efficient.

After the meeting, as the committee members were leaving, Constance Hay paused near the door and said to no one in particular that she remembered when the riverfront benches were installed. She said there had been a committee about those too. She said the committee had met for four months. She said the benches were very good. She put on her coat and walked out, back toward Wellington Street, at her own pace, which has never been hurried by anything external and does not intend to start.

Nobody was entirely sure whether this was a comment about the benches, the committee, or the statue. In Elsinore, when Constance speaks, people have learned to hold all available interpretations until the situation clarifies, which it usually does in its own time.

This is also the season, it should be noted, in which Alistair programmed Something Rotten! — a riotous musical comedy about two struggling playwrights in Elizabethan England who hope to outshine Shakespeare by inventing an entirely new form of entertainment. It is being performed at the Shakespeare Festival. Wayne noticed this in February and has said nothing, on the grounds that Alistair will arrive at the irony himself eventually and that the parking lot conversation will keep. Alistair has not yet arrived at it. Gerald has filed the production schedule in the appropriate folder. He has not been asked for his thoughts.

A Note for the Record

In Elsinore, the question of the statue remains open, pending the heritage impact assessment and the Swan Warden’s April survey report. The subcommittee meets on the second Tuesday. Nobody has proposed that the statue be gold. Nobody has suggested specifying a government-mandated aesthetic style. Nobody has proposed conducting the subcommittee meetings in Wayne’s Bieber Heritage Lounge, though Wayne has not ruled this out.

To the south, a man who sells gold-plated statues of himself at one hundred dollars per unit at political fundraising events has commissioned a National Garden of American Heroes. The statues must be “traditional and realistic” in style — which is to say neoclassical — which is to say the style associated with certain European regimes whose names one need not say for the association to be understood. The government is thus deciding not merely who deserves to be commemorated but what the bronze must look like and whose visual vocabulary it must borrow.

The farmer’s suggestion — both of them, put up both of them — remains in the minutes, unexamined. It may be the most democratic proposal currently available in any jurisdiction.

The subcommittee meets Tuesday. The Swan Warden will be there. Constance Hay will be in the back row with her narrow notebook. The swans will be on the river, proceeding with their business, indifferent to the question of their own commemoration, which they regard — insofar as swans regard anything — as entirely beside the point.

The Moonbeam Column appears in the Elsinore Beacon every Sunday morning. Justin Bieber is from Stratford, Ontario. The National Garden of American Heroes is from Washington. The swans are non-partisan.
