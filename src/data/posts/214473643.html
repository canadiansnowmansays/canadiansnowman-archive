Stephen Gordon Sep 14, 2026

By Wednesday afternoon, September 9, 2026, when the Trump administration’s third emergency application to the Supreme Court sat unanswered — Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson having called for responses from the challenging states by 4 p.m. but having made no ruling on the administration’s request for immediate relief — it became clear that the mail-in voting restriction project had entered a new phase. Not because anything legal had fundamentally changed. Not because the Court had issued a ruling that resolved anything. But because the Court’s silence, day after day, had become as consequential as any ruling could be.

When a Supreme Court does not grant emergency relief that a president has urgently requested, the refusal itself is news. It tells you that five justices — the number required to grant a stay pending appeal — did not believe the emergency was urgent enough, or the legal case strong enough, or the president’s position defensible enough, to intervene in what lower courts had already decided. It does not tell you that the Court thinks the administration is wrong on the law. A denial of emergency relief is not a ruling on the merits. It is a statement that the emergency is not emergency enough, and that is a more eloquent rebuke than any written opinion could be.

By Monday, September 14, 2026 — fewer than fifty days before the November 3rd election — the Court still had not ruled, though a decision was widely expected within the week. The U.S. Postal Service, according to its own filings, said it was running out of time to carry out the changes the president was demanding. More than twenty states had either already begun mailing ballots or were scheduled to within days, with five additional states beginning the mailing process the week of September 13 alone. The portal that the administration had been telling the Court it needed emergency relief to implement was still, weeks after the rule’s publication, nowhere near operational. And the Court, having received three separate emergency applications from the administration in the span of ten days, had let all three sit rather than rush to make a fourth application unnecessary.

This is what happens when the law, the calendar, the technology, and the judiciary all move at cross purposes with a policy before it can be implemented. It is not a vindication that the policy was wrong — courts do not need to make that determination when the simple passage of time accomplishes much of the same result. It is a recognition that we are now inside the last seven weeks before an election, the mail is already going out in most states, and remaking the entire federal voting infrastructure in that window is not a thing that is going to happen, no matter how many times the administration asks — and no matter which way the Court eventually rules.

The Friction Strategy Survives the Law

Here is what has already survived, regardless of how the pending applications are resolved: the ambient uncertainty that the rule was proposed to create in the first place. A voter in Pennsylvania, reading three months of litigation about mail-ballot barcodes and federal portal approvals, does not need a final Supreme Court ruling to understand that the federal government has decided mail voting is a target. A county clerk in Michigan, having spent September watching injunctions get issued and appeals filed and Supreme Court applications mount up, does not need to know the legal outcome to understand that there is now a cost to sending ballots in the mail that did not exist in 2024. An election official in Arizona, having fielded thousands of calls from voters asking whether their ballots will be counted, has already delivered the message the rule was designed to deliver, whether or not the rule itself ever takes effect.

This is the deepest sophistication of the friction strategy, and it is the thing that no court order can fully undo: the mere fact that the question was asked, over and over again, by the federal government, is enough to change the behaviour of millions of voters who have never been told by a court that the rule is not allowed. The injunctions can block the rule. The Supreme Court can decline to grant emergency relief on any given application. But neither can prevent a voter from spending September thinking, “Maybe I should just vote in person to be safe,” on the basis of nothing but weeks of headlines saying the federal government is trying to restrict mail voting.

The evidence of this working is already present. Exit polling from a handful of early voting states shows mail-ballot requests down significantly from 2024 — not because the rule is in effect, but because voters are uncertain whether to trust it. That is the point made in Part XLVI’s polling data: an electorate moving toward the opposition, but moving less confidently, and with millions of voters expending cognitive energy on questions that no election official should be asking them to solve. The vote margin may shift. But the turnout, and the composition of turnout, may shift more — because uncertainty depresses participation, especially among voters who are already uncertain: younger voters, newly naturalized voters, voters in communities that have historically faced barriers to participation.

The administration has not won the mail-voting case. But it has won the mail-voting conversation, in the sense that the conversation now exists at all, and it exists in precisely the form it was designed to exist: as a source of ambient uncertainty that no judicial ruling, however it eventually lands, can fully dispel this close to an election.

A Second Front Opens

If anything, the picture darkened for the administration rather than clarified over the weekend. Late on Sunday, September 13, 2026, as the Associated Press reported, U.S. District Judge Carl J. Nichols in Washington, D.C. — a Trump appointee — issued his own order barring the Postal Service from implementing the rule, in a separate case from the one Judge Talwani has been overseeing in Boston. That a judge appointed by the president himself reached substantially the same conclusion as Talwani is difficult to square with the administration’s argument that the rule is a modest, uncontroversial housekeeping measure. It suggests the objection running through the courts is not ideological but structural: two judges, appointed by different presidents, looking at the same timeline and the same evidence, and arriving at the same place. The administration now has not one but two adverse district-court rulings to dislodge, in two different circuits, with the Supreme Court still weighing whether to intervene in either.

The Absence of Victory

What is remarkable about the Court’s non-action, even now, is how little it surprises anyone who has been paying attention. After Judge Talwani found that the rule violated the Elections Clause, that it stripped states of their constitutional authority to run their own elections, that the administration had tried to “avoid” the separation-of-powers issue by “denying that the final rule is an election rule” — a denial she wrote “rings hollow” — it was difficult to imagine a scenario in which five justices would reverse her on an emergency basis and say, “Yes, go ahead, implement this in the seven weeks before the election.” Judge Nichols’ independent order only reinforces that difficulty.

The 6-3 conservative majority on the Supreme Court has been reliable allies to the Trump administration on voting questions. But even that majority has limits, and those limits appear to be under real strain here. A rule that the President’s own Postal Service cannot implement in time, that states cannot comply with without remaking their ballots, that has no evidentiary foundation in documented mail-ballot fraud, and that has now drawn injunctions from judges of two different judicial temperaments — these are not the ingredients of a straightforward emergency application, even to a Court that has shown willingness to see emergency where others see none.

Even if the Court were to grant relief, as Eileen Sullivan and Adam Sella reported for The Times, the implementation would have been built for failure. The process required three sequential steps, each dependent on the previous one being completed perfectly: states would have to upload voter lists to a portal still under construction; the Postal Service would then check each ballot envelope in bulk mailings for compliance with new barcode and design standards; and if even a single envelope in a mailing of tens of thousands was non-compliant, the entire batch would be rejected and returned to election officials. For a rural county in Wisconsin or Arizona without the staff to hand-add barcodes to every envelope, this was not a regulation. It was a logistical trap.

The concrete reality of this impossibility was on display well before the weekend. As Amy Schoenfeld Walker and Simon Ducroquet reported for The Times, election officials across the country were rushing to determine whether they could even meet the design requirements — and most were finding they could not. King County, Washington, one of the few that had already complied with the rules, paid an additional $70,000 to add voter-specific barcodes. Most states had already printed and paid for midterm envelopes and could not afford to toss them out and start over. The Postal Service had only 40 design analysts nationwide to approve designs from thousands of jurisdictions — an obvious bottleneck with fewer than fifty days remaining. Mark Earley, the elections supervisor in Leon County, Florida, had his envelope design rejected for placing “Return Service Requested” too close to a return address — the same successful envelopes he had used in 2024 and 2026, sent to tens of thousands of voters. “These are the exact same envelopes we used successfully in 2024 and 2026,” Earley said. “Designing, submitting for approval, manufacturing and receiving new ballot mail envelopes is not a trivial process.” By September 10th, Alabama, Delaware, and North Carolina had already begun mailing ballots. The rule was not just constitutionally contested. It was logistically undoable even before the second judicial rebuke arrived.

But here is where the analysis deepens into something more sinister. As Quinta Jurecic wrote for The Atlantic, the impossibility might not be a bug — it might be the feature. A Postal Service whistleblower had filed a disclosure warning that USPS was planning to operate the system with a “zero percent failure rate”: the agency would scan ballots in large batches, and if a single ballot failed to match the uploaded voter list, none of the ballots in the batch would be mailed. State election officials had flooded the Court dockets with declarations of specific harms. Hawaii’s chief election officer worried that if new envelopes were needed, they might not arrive from the continental U.S. in time. Rhode Island’s laws against dual barcodes created a direct conflict with USPS requirements. Oregon, which conducts all elections entirely by mail, worried its aging computer systems could not upload 3 million voters’ data; “If the system fails,” the director wrote, “Oregon may be unable to mail ballots to voters at all.” Nevada’s secretary of state was soliciting quotes from UPS as a backup — at $8 per ballot, versus $5 for USPS, a $6.3 million expense for the state.

Jurecic identified a political paradox at the heart of the scheme: if implemented, the rule would fall hardest on elderly, rural, and disabled voters — traditionally Republican constituencies. In a year when GOP enthusiasm was already lagging, disenfranchising your own base made no tactical sense. Unless, as Jurecic suggested, the point was not partisan advantage but chaos itself. “A group of election officials, including several Republican secretaries of state, warned in an amicus brief before the Supreme Court that ‘a chaotic election erodes confidence in the result,’” Jurecic wrote. “For a president long obsessed with undermining Americans’ trust in elections, perhaps that’s victory enough.”

Chief Justice John Roberts, no ally to voting-rights advocates, has also been notably absent from the Trump administration’s most sweeping victories this year. His jurisprudence has consistently held that if a voting rule cannot be implemented in time for an election, then asking courts to implement it anyway is asking them to take on more responsibility for the election than the law permits. The pending emergency applications fit exactly the scenario Roberts has repeatedly warned against: a rushed implementation, a short timeline, enormous stakes, and the assumption that the courts will step in to make the mechanics work even if the law does not.

What matters, as of Monday, September 14, 2026, is not whether the Supreme Court has formally decided against the rule — it has not — but that the practical window in which the rule could still govern the 2026 midterms is closing at the same rate whether the Court rules this week or lets the applications lapse into irrelevance. Whether the eventual ruling states that the rule is unconstitutional, or merely that it cannot be implemented on this timeline, is a distinction that matters in the law reviews. It matters not at all to a voter or an election official trying to figure out what the rules are fewer than fifty days before the election.

What Now

The mail is already going out. North Carolina has already sent ballots. Alabama and Delaware have followed. Pennsylvania and others are close behind. By the end of September, most of the ballots that will be cast by mail in the 2026 midterms will be in voters’ hands or on their way, without barcodes, without portal approvals, and without the federal government having exercised the unprecedented role in ballot distribution that the executive order envisioned. The rule that was supposed to govern their delivery remains blocked in two separate federal courts. The rule that was supposed to govern their eligibility remains enjoined. Whatever the Supreme Court eventually decides, it is deciding it against a backdrop in which the ballots have, for the most part, already gone out the door.

And yet the administration has not moved on. Having filed three emergency applications in ten days, having watched two different district judges — appointed by two different presidents — reach the same conclusion, the administration has continued to signal that it intends to pursue the mail-voting restrictions through the merits process regardless of what happens with the emergency applications — asking the Court to rule, eventually, on whether the executive order itself is constitutional, on whether the USPS has the authority it claims, on whether the Elections Clause means what the Democratic-led states argue it means. That process will unfold over weeks, likely stretching past the election itself. That is not a way of saying it will become irrelevant. That is a way of saying it will become something else: a question about whether the federal government’s authority over elections can be fundamentally remade, even if the remake arrives too late to matter for 2026.

The Pattern Completes

Three months ago, Part XLV of this series introduced a framework called TACO: Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate. The threat was the March executive order. The accommodation was the argument that yes, the law supports this, the administration knows what it is doing. The capitulation was supposed to be accepting the district court ruling. The obfuscation has been the past several days — filing and re-filing emergency applications not because anyone is confident they will be granted, but because filing them keeps the question alive, keeps the uncertainty present, keeps the friction machinery running even as the legal doors narrow one by one.

What happens when obfuscation runs out of procedural moves? When the Supreme Court finally rules, when two lower courts have already issued injunctions from judges of different political pedigree, when the calendar has already defeated much of the logistics regardless of the outcome? That is what we are about to find out, likely within days. The administration will most probably pivot to the merits case, the long-term question of constitutional authority, whatever the near-term ruling says. That is the fourth act of TACO: having threatened, accommodated, capitulated in part, and obfuscated past the rest, the final move is to find a new battlefield on which to fight the same fight.

But for this election, for these ballots, for these voters, the question is being settled less by judicial pronouncement than by time. Fewer than fifty days remain. The infrastructure cannot be remade at this stage regardless of what the Court says next. The uncertainty the rule was designed to create has already worked — it is now woven into the minds of millions of voters who have spent three months reading about whether mail voting is safe — even as the rule itself remains blocked from taking effect in time to enforce that uncertainty directly.

That is a kind of victory for the courts and for democracy, though it is a victory that will not look like one by November. The mail-in ballots will be counted. The voters who wanted to vote by mail will have had to think harder about it than they should have needed to. The election officials who wanted to send ballots out on schedule will have spent weeks in legal limbo, and some of them, like King County, will have spent tens of thousands of dollars they did not need to spend. And the administration, whatever the Supreme Court ultimately decides, will have kept the fight alive, preserved the question for another day, and planted the seeds for the next emergency application, the next ruling, the next attempt to remake the architecture of federal elections.

This is not how democracy is supposed to work. But it is how democracy works when one branch of government decides that the only limit on its power is the question of whether the courts will say stop, and when the courts, saying stop through two separate district judges while the Supreme Court weighs whether to say it more loudly still, discover that stop is not the same thing as go — and that sometimes all you can do is let time run out and hope that the damage created in the process is damage that can be repaired.

Postscript, September 14, 2026: This piece was written under the assumption that the Supreme Court's silence was the only verdict this fight was going to get before the ballots went out. That assumption held for exactly as long as it took the Court to finish deliberating. Tonight, the justices blocked the Trump administration's plan outright, ruling in an unsigned order that the government was "unlikely to succeed on the merits." Only Justices Alito and Thomas dissented. Justice Kavanaugh, joining the majority, wrote separately to note there was "at least a fair prospect" that the Postal Service has the underlying legal authority it claims — but that applying the rule to this election would be "arbitrary and capricious," since state and local officials simply do not have sufficient time to implement it before November.
