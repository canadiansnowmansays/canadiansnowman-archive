The man the United States and Israel chose to lead the new Iran was Mahmoud Ahmadinejad.

Read that sentence as many times as you need to.

Mahmoud Ahmadinejad — the president who called for wiping Israel off the map. The Holocaust denier. The man who told the United Nations there was not a single gay person in Iran. The patron of accelerated uranium enrichment. The crusher of the Green Movement. The keynote speaker at a conference called “A World Without Zionism.” The subject of Saturday Night Live parodies, which is how most Americans encountered him, because satire was the only frame that could contain the absurdity. That man. Him.

He was their choice New York Times

The plan, developed by Israel and briefed to American officials, was a multistage regime change operation. Phase one: massive U.S. and Israeli air assault, killing the supreme leader. Phase two: mobilise Kurdish forces. Phase three: influence campaigns to destabilise the regime. Phase four: political collapse under the weight of infrastructure damage. Phase five: install an “alternative government.”

The alternative was Ahmadinejad.

On the first day of the war, an Israeli airstrike hit his home in the Narmak district of eastern Tehran. The strike was not designed to kill him. It was designed to kill the IRGC guards holding him under house arrest — a jailbreak operation, carried out by fighter jet. The security outpost at the entrance to his dead-end street was destroyed. Satellite imagery confirms the damage. His “bodyguards” — in reality, his jailers — were killed.

The jailbreak worked. Ahmadinejad was freed.

But the strike also injured him. And after surviving it, he became disillusioned with the plan. He has not been seen publicly since February 28th. His current whereabouts and condition are unknown.

The regime change plan failed at every subsequent phase. The Kurdish mobilisation did not produce the expected instability. The influence campaigns did not trigger political collapse. Approximately 50 senior political and military leaders were killed in the opening strikes — and the government did not fall. Three months later, Iran is still governed, still fighting, still holding the Strait of Hormuz closed, and still negotiating from a position of defiance.

The White House, asked to comment, said: “The United States military met or exceeded all of its objectives.”

That sentence is now the most Orwellian utterance of the war. The objective, it turns out, was regime change. The regime did not change. The objective was met or exceeded.

Trump thought he could replicate Venezuela. He told the press in the war’s opening days that it would be best if “someone from within” took over Iran, and he was riding the high of the Maduro capture — the special operations raid that American intelligence agencies said neither Russia nor China could have executed. An associate of Ahmadinejad confirmed to the Times that the Americans saw their man as a figure like Delcy Rodríguez, who took power in Caracas after the seizure and has since worked closely with Washington.

The analogy is breathtaking in what it reveals about the planning — or the absence of it. Venezuela is a country of 28 million with a hollowed-out military and no nuclear programme. Iran is a civilisational state of 88 million with a battle-hardened revolutionary guard, proxy forces across four countries, and a nuclear enrichment capability that has survived decades of sabotage. The idea that you could airlift a leadership change into Tehran the way you extracted Maduro from a compound in Caracas tells you everything you need to know about the strategic imagination behind this war.

Even some of Trump’s own aides found the plan implausible. American officials were skeptical in particular about the viability of putting Ahmadinejad back into power. And Mossad’s chief, David Barnea, told associates he still believed the plan could have succeeded “had it received approval to move forward” — a sentence that contains its own refutation, since the man at the centre of it had already abandoned the project after being hit by the strike meant to save him.

Why didn’t the regime collapse? Because the men now running Iran were built for exactly this.

The New York Times published a profile this week of the power structure that survived the war’s opening salvo. What it describes is not a government in the conventional sense. It is a brotherhood — a small elite of current and former senior IRGC commanders whose formative experience was the eight-year war with Iraq, a conflict in which the West supported Iran’s enemy and convinced them, permanently, that Iran had to forge its own way regardless of cost.

Mojtaba Khamenei, 56, succeeded his father as supreme leader. But decision-making extends beyond one man. The inner circle includes Ahmad Vahidi, 67, the new IRGC commander — the man who created the Quds Force in 1988, who is suspected of embedding terrorism into Hezbollah’s DNA, and under whose watch the 1994 Buenos Aires bombing killed 85 people and the 1996 Dhahran truck bomb killed 19 American servicemen. Mohammad Ali Jafari, 68, who commanded the Guards for twelve years and developed the “mosaic strategy” of decentralised command that has kept Iranian forces functional even as commanders were killed. Mohammad Bagher Zolghadr, 72, the new secretary of the Supreme National Security Council, ensuring that the political, military, security, and judicial apparatus operates in tandem. Gholam-Hossein Mohseni-Ejei, 69, the head of the judiciary, a hanging judge whose torrent of executions continues even now. Hossein Taeb, 63, the intelligence operative believed close to Mojtaba Khamenei from their shared service in the Habib Battalion during the Iran-Iraq war.

These men have survived purges, protests, assassinations, and now a combined U.S.-Israeli air campaign that killed the supreme leader himself. Their shared backgrounds, careers, and ideological outlook are, as the Times notes, “one reason the war has neither collapsed the government nor paralysed it.”

Jafari’s mosaic strategy is the key. Decentralised command means that killing the head does not kill the body. The Americans and Israelis built a war plan around decapitation. The Iranians built a command structure designed to survive it.

“They survey, they control, they spy on each other,” said Saeid Golkar, a Guards expert at the University of Tennessee. “Because of that dominance over intelligence, they gradually became dominant in almost any aspect of politics in Iran.”

The jailbreak plan assumed that the regime was fragile — that killing the supreme leader and freeing a dissident ex-president would create a vacuum into which a new government could be inserted. The brotherhood assumed the opposite: that the regime was the revolution, and the revolution was designed to be unkillable.

The brotherhood was right.

And now the endgame. Chip Somodevilla / Getty

Robert Kagan, writing in The Atlantic on Wednesday, named what the administration will not. Trump called Netanyahu this week to explain that the United States was negotiating a “letter of intent” with Iran — a document that would formally end the war and launch a 30-day period of negotiations over the nuclear programme and the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz.

Kagan’s verdict was two words: endgame is a euphemism for surrender.

The war, Kagan argues, effectively ended on March 18, when Israel struck the Pars gas field, Iran retaliated against Qatar’s gas infrastructure, and Trump called for a halt to targeting energy facilities. Every threat since has been a bluff. Tehran calculated two months ago that Trump would not resume full-scale operations, and for that reason has made no concessions despite 37 days of relentless strikes. On the contrary, Iran’s terms are those of a victor: war reparations, no limits on uranium enrichment, recognised control of the strait, and an end to sanctions.

The 30-day negotiating window is not a path to a deal. It is a path to a new normal. Iran has been using the ceasefire to normalise its control over the strait — compelling oil-importing nations to negotiate transit agreements with Tehran, charging fees on vessels, granting priority to strategic partners like Russia and China, and denying access entirely to nations it regards as adversaries. South Korea, Turkey, and Iraq are already negotiating. Once Trump makes clear he has no intention of fighting to reopen the strait — and the letter of intent makes exactly that clear — the stampede will begin. Every nation dependent on Persian Gulf energy will rush to cut its deal with Tehran. International sanctions will collapse. Iran’s central role in the global economy will be normalised.

Netanyahu’s “hair was on fire” after the call, according to one U.S. official. As well it should be. Iran will emerge from this war stronger and more influential than it was before it started. The Abraham Accords will die, because the Gulf states will need to make their peace with Tehran to survive economically. Israel will be more isolated than at any point in its history — including from the United States. And when Trump turns his back on Israel, as the logic of surrender requires, MAGA will follow.

Kagan’s closing line deserves to be read alongside the jailbreak that opened this essay: “Most likely, the new normal in the Persian Gulf will be chronic instability and frequent disruptions in shipping. That’s what happens when the hegemon cedes hegemony.”

As if to prove the point, the New York Times reported yesterday that Iran is in active discussions with Oman — an American ally — to create a joint system charging fees for vessels passing through the Strait of Hormuz. Iran’s newly created Persian Gulf Strait Authority announced on social media that it had “defined the boundaries of the Strait of Hormuz management supervision area” and that passage would require a permit.

The legal architecture is precise. A toll — charging simply for transit — would violate international maritime law. So Iran is calling them “fees” for “specialised services.” A professor of international maritime law at the U.S. Naval War College called it “almost like the mafia saying you have to pay protection money.” Oman initially rejected the partnership but is now negotiating its share of the revenue, having realised the economic potential. Iran’s system would grant priority to strategic partners — Russia and China — and deny access entirely to adversaries.

Trump, speaking in the Oval Office on Thursday, rejected it: “We want it free. We don’t want tolls. It’s international. It’s an international waterway.” Rubio called it “unacceptable.”

Secretary of State Marco Rubio speaking to the press at Homestead Air Reserve Base. Mr. Rubio rejects the idea of a tolling system in the Strait of Hormuz.Credit...Pool photo by Julia Demaree Nikhinson

But wanting it free and making it free are different things. The United States launched a naval operation to open the strait in early May. It lasted 48 hours. The strait remains closed. The toll system is being built while the president says he won’t allow it. The new normal is not arriving in 30 days. It is arriving now.

Oil responded on Friday. Brent crude jumped 3 percent to $106 a barrel. The average price of gas nationally ticked to $4.55 — up more than 50 percent since the war began. Diesel hit $5.65, also up 50 percent. Thirty-year mortgage rates reached 6.51 percent, the highest since August. “Uncertainty reigns,” analysts at ING wrote. “This is not the first time a deal seemed close, only for negotiations to break down.”

They went in to install Ahmadinejad. They are coming out having installed Iran as the gatekeeper of the global economy.

Meanwhile, at home, Congress cracked.

On Tuesday, the Senate voted 50–47 to advance a war powers resolution that would force the president to end hostilities or obtain congressional authorisation to continue them New York Times. It was the eighth attempt. The first seven failed.

Senator John Kennedy of Louisiana — whose solitary dissent this series has tracked from Part I — is no longer alone.

Bill Cassidy of Louisiana, who lost his primary over the weekend after Trump targeted him for defeat, switched his vote. Freed by electoral death to vote his conscience, he became the fourth Republican to join Democrats. Lisa Murkowski of Alaska, Susan Collins of Maine, and Rand Paul of Kentucky completed the quartet. John Fetterman of Pennsylvania remains the only Democrat voting with Republicans to block it.

Three Republican absences — Tuberville, Tillis, and Cornyn — prevented the majority from beating back the resolution as they had seven times before. Tim Kaine of Virginia, who has led the effort for months, put it simply: “The momentum is moving our way slowly.”

Then, on Thursday evening, the House proved him right.

Republican leaders abruptly pulled a war powers resolution that had been scheduled for a vote — because they did not have the votes to defeat it New York Times. Last week, an identical measure had failed by a single tie vote. This week, with more Republicans preparing to defect and several others absent, Speaker Mike Johnson shelved the resolution rather than hand Democrats a victory on the floor.

Representative Brian Fitzpatrick, a Pennsylvania Republican who voted with Democrats last week and planned to do so again, was blunt: “They probably did it because they didn’t have the votes. I don’t think they’re going to have the votes when we get back.”

Republican Representative Brian Fitzpatrick

He added: “The next time they bring it, it’s passing.”

Both chambers. Both cracking. The Senate advanced the resolution 50–47. The House couldn’t even risk holding the vote. Congress leaves for Memorial Day recess on Friday. When they return in June, their constituents will have spent a week looking at $4.52 gas and asking what it was for.

“People are going to hear an earful when they get home about gas prices,” Kaine said.

The resolution, even if passed by both chambers, faces an all-but-certain presidential veto. But the political significance is not the veto. It is the erosion. Seven consecutive defeats in the Senate, then a breakthrough on the eighth. A tie vote in the House, then a pulled vote because the tie was about to become a loss. Cassidy freed by electoral death. Fitzpatrick predicting passage. Johnson retreating from his own floor. The accountability mechanism is finding its pulse. No longer faint.

And in Tehran, the IRGC issued its clearest threat yet.

If “aggression against Iran is repeated,” the Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps warned on Wednesday, it would deliver blows “in places you cannot even imagine” New York Times.

Beyond the Middle East. That is the escalation threshold this war has now reached. A criminal complaint unsealed last week accused a senior Kataib Hezbollah commander of planning attacks in the United States, Europe, and Canada since the war began. The threat is no longer theoretical. It has a defendant and a docket number.

Trump said he had postponed a “very major attack” at the request of Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Qatar. Vance told reporters: “There’s an option B, and the option B is that we could restart the military campaign. But that’s not what the president wants, and I don’t think it’s what the Iranians want either.”

The IRGC claimed 26 vessels had passed through the Strait of Hormuz under Iranian naval “coordination and security protection.” South Korea confirmed one of its tankers was transiting with Iranian consent. If true, this is Iran asserting sovereign control over an international waterway — setting a precedent that will outlast this war.

Analysts warn that if bombing resumes, Iran could extend to the Bab al-Mandeb Strait — the narrow channel linking the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden through which a tenth of global maritime trade passes, flanked by Houthi-held Yemen. Two straits. Two choke points. One war with no congressional authorisation.

And then there is the record of the president’s own words.

The New York Times published a detailed analysis this week mapping Trump’s public statements about the war against the reality of each moment. It is the newspaper of record doing, with charts and data, what this series has been doing in prose since Part I.

The pattern is now documented beyond dispute. A claim of progress. A market rally. A subsequent collapse. A threat of annihilation. A climbdown. A new claim of progress. Another rally. Another collapse. The cycle repeats on a loop measured in days, sometimes in hours.

March 9: “The Iran war is very complete.” It was not.
March 13: “Iran, which is totally defeated and wants a deal.” It did not.
March 23: “Very good and productive conversations.” Iran’s parliament speaker said “fakenews is used to manipulate the financial and oil markets.”
March 26: “They are begging us to make a deal.” They were not.
March 30: Threatened to destroy energy infrastructure. The S&P 500 hit its lowest point of the year.
March 31: “We’ll be leaving very soon.” They did not.
April 4: “48 hours before all Hell will reign down on them.” It did not.
April 7: “A whole civilization will die tonight.” A ceasefire was announced instead. May 6: “Very good talks over the last 24 hours.” The proposal contained no new concessions.
May 18: “We were getting ready to do a very major attack tomorrow.” He postponed it.

Oil prices have moved more than 10 percent in a single day on presidential statements that proved hollow. The NYT charts show it. This is Truth Social as a market instrument — the same mechanism documented in Part 41 of the Trump Accountability Project, now operating in a theatre of war rather than a trading floor.

The bond market has taken notice. Thirty-year Treasury yields hit 5.18 percent on Tuesday — their highest since 2007 New York Times. Japan’s 30-year bond yield reached an all-time high. Britain’s hit the highest since 1998. Investors who had expected rate cuts before the war are now pricing in a rate increase. The 30-year mortgage rate has risen to 6.36 percent. The war is in your house payment now.

And the war is burning through munitions faster than America can build them New York Times. More than 1,200 Patriot interceptors fired — at $4 million each, 36 months to produce — against $35,000 Shahed drones that Iran manufactures at 200 a month. Robert Gates, who spent two administrations railing against this exact mismatch, asked the question that contains the answer: “Ukraine is going to produce seven million drones this year. Come on. Why can’t we do that?”

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is attempting to take on the shortcomings in the Pentagon’s weapons procurement systems while pushing for a $1.5 trillion budget.Credit...Haiyun Jiang/The New York Times

Hegseth’s proposed solution is a $1.5 trillion defence budget — the largest in modern American history. The DOGE commission, which was supposed to impose fiscal discipline, produced $1 billion in durable savings. The grain silo empties faster when each interceptor costs 114 times what it is shooting down.

And then, on Thursday evening, as the House was pulling the war powers vote it couldn’t win — the president posted on Truth Social that he was deploying 5,000 troops to Poland. The Pentagon, which had cancelled the same deployment a week earlier, was not informed in advance. Hegseth’s spokesman described the three weeks of deploy-cancel-redeploy whiplash as “the result of a comprehensive, multilayered process.”

Meanwhile, in the Senate, Republicans quietly jettisoned the president’s $1 billion ballroom from the budget bill. They also revolted against his $1.8 billion fund to compensate people who claimed political persecution — a fund that the former chief of the Capitol Police said would “reward people who attacked the U.S. Capitol.”

Virginia Foxx, the House Rules Committee chairwoman, spoke for all of it: “All I want is just one normal day. Just give me one normal day.”

She will not get one.
