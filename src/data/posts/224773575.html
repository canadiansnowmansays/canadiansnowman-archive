In every gold rush the surest fortune goes not to the prospectors but to whoever sells them shovels. In the artificial intelligence boom the shovel seller was Nvidia, which controls an estimated 90 percent of the market for the chips that train and run the largest A.I. systems, and the man behind the counter was its co-founder, Jensen Huang, in the black leather jacket that became as recognisable as the company’s logo.

On November 12, 2026, Huang resigned as Nvidia’s chief executive. The company’s chief financial officer, Colette Kress, became interim chief executive, and its board began looking for someone who could replace a man nobody had ever planned to replace. The following day The New York Times published a timeline of his undoing that runs from 2023 to the Senate Banking Committee’s summons for December 3.

That timeline does not begin with Huang. It begins with a woman in Shanghai, a Singaporean company that had been Chinese until shortly before, and a set of American export rules that were stricter about paperwork than about where the chips actually ended up. Before anyone can ask what the president knew, or what the Commerce Department did, they need to understand the customer.

The rule, and the way around it

In late 2022 the United States began restricting sales of advanced A.I. chips to China, out of fear that they could give Beijing an economic and military edge. The rules hinged on corporate identity. To buy the chips, a company had to be domiciled outside China and show no obvious ownership ties leading back to it.

That is a sensible rule as far as it goes. It assumes, however, that ownership is something you can see. Anyone who has spent time around offshore finance knows it is something you can arrange.

Megaspeed is a case study in arranging it. In 2023, according to Paul Mozur, Ana Swanson, Tripp Mickle and Michael Forsythe in The New York Times on November 10, 2026, a British Virgin Islands company bought the Singaporean subsidiary of 7Road, a Chinese online gaming company linked to a Shanghai investment network. 7Road had ties to state-backed investors, and its owners included the Chinese central government and several local governments. The subsidiary was renamed Megaspeed.

Later that year Megaspeed was transferred to a Singaporean shell company. On paper it now belonged to a Singaporean director named James Tan. When The New York Times went looking for him in its first Megaspeed investigation, published October 9, 2025, the shell company’s address led to an accounting firm whose staff did not know how to reach him. A human-resources manager at Megaspeed’s office said Tan was never present, that most of the directors were in China, and that most of the clients were Chinese.

The person who ran the company day to day, according to people familiar with it, never changed. She was a Chinese executive named Huang Le, who goes by Alice Huang. She is not related to Jensen Huang.

Huang Le, of the golf club

For a woman now at the centre of an international espionage case, Alice Huang left a remarkably faint trail. The earliest corporate records bearing her name date to 2010: a small “sports and leisure club” company registered inside Beihu No. 9, a private golf club in Beijing popular with officials, where the buffet ran to foie gras, hairy crabs and Häagen-Dazs.

Her online résumé says she spent four years as a television reporter before moving into finance at HSBC’s private bank and CITIC Securities, the state-backed Chinese investment bank. The Times could not verify the television years. She later became executive director of a Shanghai fund that had invested in 7Road, and she invested in a Chinese medical equipment maker through an offshore company.

Her constant business partner was Kang Wei, a 51-year-old fund manager whom Chinese financial media once treated as a stock-market boy wonder. He has been introduced to investors and Megaspeed employees as her husband; the Times could not confirm they are married. China’s securities regulator has fined him at least twice for failing to disclose his stakes in listed companies. According to one person familiar with the matter, he bragged about Alice Huang’s connections to Nvidia’s chief and used them to raise money.

By 2020 the pair ran an interconnected web of companies in Shanghai’s investment circles. That spring their network won a contract to help build and operate an A.I. computing centre in Shanghai for Tencent, the Chinese internet giant. The network also had a financial link to China’s military establishment, through an investment in a cybersecurity subsidiary of China Electronics Technology Group, one of the country’s largest makers of military technology.

In other words, by the time the American rules arrived, Alice Huang already knew three things that would matter: how to run a data centre for a Chinese tech giant, how to move ownership offshore, and how to raise money on the strength of who she knew.

Dinner in Singapore

In December 2023 Alice Huang had dinner at an Italian restaurant in Singapore with Jensen Huang and a small group of colleagues. A photograph confirmed by the Times shows her smiling beside him; he is in the jacket, holding a glass of red wine.

She had reason to celebrate. Megaspeed’s restructuring had just been completed. Four days after the ownership change, Nvidia named Megaspeed a cloud partner, which gave it access to the most sought-after components in the world.

Nvidia’s compliance team examined Megaspeed and concluded, in the words of its spokesman John Rizzo to the Times in 2025, that it was “wholly owned and operated by a company based and headquartered outside China, with no China shareholders.” A person close to the compliance division said Nvidia regarded the company as a newcomer to cloud computing that would compete with American firms, and that it raised no red flags.

On the paperwork, that was true. The difficulty is that the rule tested only the paperwork.

“I bet you guys I can get Jensen here”

In June 2024, after a technology trade show in Taipei, Alice Huang was mingling with a crowd of executives in a bar with sweeping views of the city when, just after midnight, she made an offer. “She said, ‘I bet you guys I can get Jensen here,’” recalled Parker Schmitt, chief executive of the data software company NetThunder, “and then Jensen immediately showed up.” Huang arrived and drank a whiskey shot with the group. NetThunder posted the photographs on LinkedIn.

A person close to Nvidia later said Huang had planned to attend the party anyway, with two other Nvidia executives. That may well be so. But it rather misses the point, which is that Alice Huang wanted the room to believe she could summon him, and that the room believed it. Schmitt said her social media was full of photographs of the two of them, at a golf course and over wine. “Photos with Jensen,” he said, “are a point of pride.”

Within weeks, Megaspeed’s shipments began.

Two hundred shipments

Megaspeed bought through a Malaysian subsidiary called Speedmatrix, set up in early 2024. Its registered address on shipping records led to a dilapidated storefront outside Kuala Lumpur with a construction company’s sign out front. Its working office was on an upper floor of a shopping mall in Johor, between snack shops and beauty parlours, behind reflective glass and fingerprint scanners. When a Times reporter asked an administrator there what the company did, she replied: “It’s not clear.”

What it did, according to trade records obtained through ImportGenius, was receive more than 200 shipments of restricted Nvidia technology between June 2024 and June 2025. The first, dated June 27, 2024, was $25.4 million worth of servers containing Nvidia H800 chips, flown from Los Angeles to Kuala Lumpur.

The supplier is the detail that should have stopped someone. Most of the chips came from Aivres Systems of San Jose, California, which WireScreen identifies as the American subsidiary of Inspur, the Chinese server maker. Washington put Inspur on the entity list in 2023 for supporting the Chinese military. But Aivres is incorporated in California and records its sales as an American company, so it could buy Nvidia chips freely. A blacklisted company’s American arm was selling banned-for-China chips to a formerly Chinese company’s Malaysian arm, and every step was arguably legal.

The money involved has been reported in different figures, and they are worth putting side by side. Speedmatrix bought nearly $2 billion of Nvidia equipment in its first year, June 2024 to June 2025, which is the figure the Times used in October 2025. On October 29, 2026, The New York Times reported that the total had reached at least $4.8 billion by February 2026. Both are correct; they simply cover different periods. Either is an extraordinary sum for a company that had no track record in cloud computing.

Its main customer was Alibaba. Months after the move to the Singaporean shell, Megaspeed’s Malaysian subsidiary pledged its servers and intellectual property to an Alibaba subsidiary called Apex, and Alibaba became its dominant client. Renting computing power in Southeast Asia to Chinese companies is not illegal in itself; the Trump administration rolled back the Biden-era rules that targeted the practice. But it is the loophole through which the restrictions drain away, and U.S. officials suspected Megaspeed was doing more than renting. They believed some of the chips were going on into China.

Boxes in Johor

In September 2024 an official from the Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security visited a Megaspeed data centre at Sedenak Tech Park in Johor to check that the chips Megaspeed had bought were actually there. Some Nvidia equipment was still in its boxes. That arouses suspicion, because companies seeking to evade controls sometimes show inspectors the hardware and ship it elsewhere afterwards.

After the visit, U.S. officials passed their concerns to Singapore, Malaysia and Nvidia. Taiwan began monitoring Alice Huang’s visits at Washington’s suggestion; she entered on a Hong Kong passport and made trips beyond her public appearances with Huang. In 2025 Singapore barred her from leaving the country and questioned her in an investigation into false import declarations. A person familiar with the case said there was insufficient evidence that she was complicit, and the case against her was closed. Singaporean police say they are still investigating Megaspeed. Singapore’s government has since said it held her passport from June 2025 to February 2026 over “potential false declarations” about Megaspeed, then released her and barred her from coming back. On November 15, 2026, Megaspeed broke its silence about her for the first time, saying she had left the company on August 30, 2025, and had no current role, as Ali Watkins reported in The New York Times. “Megaspeed does not comment on Ms Huang’s personal and private matters,” it said. The date can’t be independently verified, and U.S. officials say her relationship with Huang went on long after it.

Her social calendar, meanwhile, carried on. In March 2025 she visited Nvidia’s California headquarters with other executives. In May 2025 she dined with Huang and others in Taipei, where Taiwanese media photographed her kissing him on the cheek.

Nvidia stood by its customer. In October 2025 Rizzo said Nvidia employees had just visited Megaspeed’s facilities, “found no evidence of diversion,” and confirmed it was running “a small commercial cloud, like many other companies throughout the world.” Megaspeed said it was “operating fully in compliance with all applicable laws, including U.S. export control regulations.”

What the spy agency wanted

On November 6, 2026, The New York Times reported, through Julian E. Barnes, Tripp Mickle, Paul Mozur and Maggie Haberman, what a memo circulated among top Trump administration officials the previous spring had said. According to people briefed on the intelligence, Alice Huang had been recruited by China’s Ministry of State Security to collect intelligence on Jensen Huang, as part of a drive to learn more about American A.I. chips and potentially influence Nvidia to push for its most advanced chips to be sold to China.

That reverses how the story looks. On this account Megaspeed was not simply a buyer that happened to have a well-connected executive. The executive had a second employer, and the man she was close to was the assignment.

“Jensen is someone you’d want to influence,” said Peter Mattis, a former C.I.A. counterintelligence analyst who now runs the Jamestown Foundation, “because he wields a lot of power and is at the crux of a world-shaping technology.”

The Ministry of State Security was a backwater until Xi Jinping took power in 2012 and began investing in it. Today it is many times larger than the C.I.A. Its current head, Chen Yixin, is a Zhejiang native who spent much of his career in the province’s Communist Party apparatus and served under Xi when Xi ran Zhejiang in the early 2000s. Alice Huang’s network of companies and investors was clustered around Shanghai and Zhejiang. In a September 2026 essay in the state-run magazine China Cyberspace, Chen wrote that China needed to assert control over A.I. to protect political stability and compete militarily with the United States.

China denies all of it. Liu Chang, a spokesman for the Chinese Embassy in Washington, called the reporting “unsubstantiated” and “nothing but smears against China.” Alice Huang has not responded to repeated requests for comment. When U.S. officials first briefed the president, in October 2025, they did not believe Jensen Huang knew she was gathering information for Beijing. Some senior officials have since said they doubt he could have remained unaware.

Still a partner

Here is the part of the story that sits least comfortably with the idea that the system worked.

Through all of this, Megaspeed kept going. After the Singapore investigation, Alice Huang flew to Shanghai and a new boss began giving orders to Megaspeed’s staff. Another executive tied to her network visited the Malaysian office to keep permits moving. Shanghai Shuoyao, another company in her network, sent large groups of Chinese engineers to work on a new data centre in Johor. A Malaysian property developer reported receiving the final payment for the 53-acre site, and a person with knowledge of the plans said the chips for the first phase had already been secured. Back in Shanghai, the Songjiang district government held up Shuoyao as a model company, showcasing an A.I. server factory where, it said, more than 100 technicians could repair servers down to the chip level and reconfigure them.

As of late October 2026, Nvidia still listed Megaspeed on its website as a cloud partner. That same month Megaspeed’s Indonesian branch posted a job advertisement describing itself as “a premier business partner of Nvidia.” One more detail reads differently now. By the middle of 2025, as Matteo Wong noted in The Atlantic on November 12, 2026, Singapore-based customers, by then under scrutiny as possible middlemen for China, accounted for one-fifth of Nvidia’s revenue. After news of the U.S. investigation into Megaspeed broke, Nvidia appeared to switch to an accounting method that no longer showed any revenue from Singaporean customers. A year-long investigation by The New York Times, published on November 14, 2026, filled in the numbers. By the summer of 2025 Singapore accounted for 22 percent of Nvidia’s quarterly sales, about $10 billion, second only to the United States. After the Times’s October 2025 article on Megaspeed, Nvidia began reporting sales by “customers’ headquarters” rather than “billing location,” and Singapore vanished from its disclosures. The company had said large customers billed through Singapore, where taxes can be lower, but that the products “are almost always shipped elsewhere.”

The same reporting described how Nvidia handled the first questions. When the Times called in September 2025 about Megaspeed and Huang’s closeness to Alice Huang, he was agitated. He didn’t deny knowing her but bristled at the suggestion they were close. The company pushed back hard, with help from Heller, a crisis communications firm that had worked for the Huang family since early 2025, and insisted the relationship was purely business. Huang stuck to that story for 13 months.

What kind of spy she was is still being worked out. People briefed on the intelligence told Julian E. Barnes, Maggie Haberman and Jonathan Swan of The New York Times on November 13, 2026 that she appears not to have been a trained intelligence officer but an “asset,” someone secretly passing information to the Ministry of State Security. U.S. agencies had been watching her for well over a year, and she was already close to Huang when the surveillance began. What they still don’t know is when she started working with Chinese intelligence, whether she did so willingly or under pressure, how much direction Beijing gave her and how much she passed on. Chinese executives, current and former officials say, often believe they can’t advance without being aligned with the state, and many feel they can’t refuse when asked. Singapore’s authorities questioned her last year. Where she is now is unknown.

How much equipment went through is still disputed. The technology site The Information has suggested that the data centres served as a way station for smuggling some $7 billion of equipment into China, as Stephen Witt, Huang’s biographer, noted in The New York Times; that figure has not been confirmed. The Wall Street Journal has since put it higher, reporting on November 15 that Megaspeed arranged to send nearly $12 billion worth of top-of-the-line Nvidia chips to companies in China between March 2024 and June 2025, as Michelle Goldberg noted in The New York Times. Neither figure has been confirmed by the U.S. government.

Since the scandal broke, Megaspeed has said it has hired outside counsel, is conducting an internal investigation, and has offered to cooperate with the U.S. government.

Nvidia’s position until very recently was that Megaspeed was like any other regional customer, and that Jensen Huang’s meetings with Alice Huang were “not unusual or unexpected,” because a chief executive meets thousands of customer executives a year. On November 8, 2026, Huang told Nvidia’s board that the relationship had been an affair.

The paperwork was perfect

It would be satisfying to call this a story about one reckless man and one skilled operative. It is partly that. But the more uncomfortable lesson of Part I is that almost none of the steps described here required anyone to break a rule that anyone was checking.

A gaming company changed its name. A shell company changed its owner. A blacklisted manufacturer’s American subsidiary sold to a Malaysian subsidiary. A Chinese tech giant rented what it could not buy. An American compliance team read the ownership documents and found them in order. The one government inspector who looked at the actual hardware found it in boxes, and the investigation he helped begin was, as Part V will show, later taken away from the department that started it.

The export rules asked whether a customer was Chinese. They did not ask whose customer it was, or what else she might be collecting. The shovel seller kept selling, and the shovels kept moving.
