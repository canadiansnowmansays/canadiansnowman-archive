By Stephen Gordon | CanadianSnowmanSays

Part of the series: Israel/Palestine: The Original Sin and Its Compound Interest

In August, Part XIV of this series asked whether “pariah state” had become the accurate term for Israel’s position in the world. I offered it then as an impression, built from news coverage, polling and the general weather of the summer. Impressions are cheap. What they lack is a signature.

On Friday, October 9, 2026, the impression acquired two.

The first came from Oslo at 11 a.m. local time, when the Norwegian Nobel Committee awarded the Peace Prize to Navi Pillay, the South African jurist whose UN commission concluded in September 2025 that Israel had committed genocide in Gaza. The second appeared the same morning in the opinion pages of the New York Times, where Shira Efron, the Israel policy chair at RAND, published a guest essay titled “This Is How to End Israel’s Isolation.” She wrote it from Tel Aviv.

The two documents have nothing in common in tone, method or intent. One is a moral and legal judgement delivered with Scandinavian restraint. The other is a hard-headed strategic memo written by someone who wants Israel to succeed and is alarmed by the direction it is travelling. Read side by side, they describe the same thing from opposite ends: a country whose isolation is no longer a matter of protest marches and campus encampments, but of prizes, bond markets, import bans and trade law.

This part is about that morning, the afternoon that followed it, and the election eighteen days later that will show whether anyone in Jerusalem was listening.

Track One: Oslo

The committee’s citation was careful. As UN News reported, it honoured Pillay “for her efforts to promote peace and international law,” and the emphasis fell on a long career: the apartheid courtrooms, the Rwanda tribunal, the ICC, the UN High Commissioner’s office. The committee chair, Jørgen Watne Frydnes, set the award against a global “shift towards power politics” in which, as he told NBC News, international law and its institutions are under attack.

In the announcement, Frydnes noted that Pillay had chaired the UN commission but did not mention Gaza or Israel. Pressed afterwards by Agence France-Presse, he said the genocide finding was the commission’s work, that the committee had great faith in Pillay’s professionalism and integrity, and that ruling on it was for international courts, because the Nobel committee “is not the court.”

That is a deliberate two-step. The committee declines to adjudicate the finding while making plain that it trusts the person who reached it. Israel’s government heard only the second half. Its foreign ministry said, as CBS News reported, that if there were an award for “lies, hatred, and blood libels,” Pillay would be more than a contender. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said the committee had “lost its moral compass,” according to CNN.

A word of caution belongs here, because Part XVI of this series spent considerable time on it. The word “genocide” remains legally and historically contested in this conflict. The Israeli government rejects the commission’s finding, and South Africa’s genocide case at the International Court of Justice, filed in December 2023, has not yet produced a final ruling on the merits. A Nobel Peace Prize is not a verdict, and the committee went out of its way to say so. What the prize does is narrower, and in its way more consequential. It tells the world that a finding reached through a formal UN process, by one of the most respected jurists alive, is to be honoured rather than sanctioned. For a government whose strategy has been to treat such findings as illegitimate on arrival, that is not a small message.

Track Two: The Opinion Page

Efron’s essay does not mention the Nobel, the genocide finding or the word “pariah.” That is precisely what makes it worth reading alongside them. She is a senior analyst at one of the most establishment research institutions in the United States, and her argument is built entirely on Israeli self-interest.

Her inventory of recent months is sobering. Luxembourg, a European centre for issuing Israel Bonds, stopped approving their sale on September 1, 2026, a decision Al Jazeera noted leaves Israel’s future borrowing from European investors uncertain. On September 8, 2026, Britain, France and Canada pledged to ban imports of goods produced in West Bank settlements, and nine more European countries signalled support, as The Canadian Press reported. Efron’s conclusion is that G7 members have now made the sanctioning of Israelis a normal policy option, which would have been unthinkable a few years ago. She allows that Israeli complaints about European election interference and influence campaigns may hold some truth, but calls them only part of the story.

The larger part is the premise on which the whole international posture rested for half a century. Israel officially treated its occupation as temporary, a situation to be resolved through negotiation. The negotiations kept failing, at Oslo, Camp David and Annapolis, and Efron assigns responsibility to both Israelis and Palestinians. The international community accepted the arrangement. The settlements were illegal, but the occupation was tolerable so long as everyone agreed to call it temporary.

Efron’s argument is that the bargain broke when Israel stopped pretending. Once the government ceased to treat the West Bank’s status as open to negotiation, Europe ceased to treat its own response as open to negotiation either.

The Breaking Point Has a Name: E1

E1 is a strip of land east of Jerusalem, and for decades building a settlement there was avoided because it would split the West Bank in two and make a contiguous Palestinian state physically impossible. Successive governments planned it, threatened it and postponed it. None built it.

The Netanyahu government revived the plan in August 2025, when Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich said openly that it would bury the idea of a Palestinian state. On August 18, 2026, as the Foundation for Middle East Peace documented, the Israel Land Authority published a construction tender for 1,234 housing units, with bidding set to close eight days before the election. It was that tender, and the settler violence around it, that the twelve governments cited when they announced their measures in September.

Readers of Part IX will recognize the pattern. For years the map spread like a rash while officials insisted it was a temporary skin condition. E1 is the moment the government stopped insisting. Efron places it on top of a surge in outpost construction, settler attacks carried out with what she describes as impunity, and ministers speaking openly about what she calls, without euphemism, ethnic cleansing.

This is the compound interest of the series’ title in its purest form. The principal, the original decision to hold territory without resolving the status of the people living on it, was never paid down. It was rolled over, decade after decade, on the understanding that the creditor would not call the loan as long as the borrower kept promising to repay. E1 was the borrower announcing that he did not intend to repay at all. The creditors are now calling.

And the sanctions, Efron warns, will not stay where they are aimed. Israeli banks, builders and shipping companies do not divide their business by the Green Line, so the effects will reach into Israel proper, and sanctions tend to widen rather than narrow.

One more turn belongs to the election. On September 26, 2026, as Israel Hayom reported, the Attorney General’s Office pushed the bid deadline back a month, past October 27. Smotrich accused her of meddling in the vote. The corridor that broke the international bargain will land on the desk of whoever wins.

October 27: New Faces, Same Map?

Many Israelis, and many of Israel’s friends abroad, are pinning their hopes on the election. Efron reads the challengers’ platforms and finds little to justify the hope. Avigdor Lieberman proposes dissolving the Palestinian Authority, annulling the Oslo Accords and handing day-to-day administration of West Bank Palestinians to Jordan. Naftali Bennett, running jointly with Yair Lapid, would annex parts of the West Bank and offer Palestinians in the rest autonomy without sovereignty. Gadi Eisenkot offers a modest plan to manage the conflict more humanely, while ruling out a Palestinian state.

None of them offers anything beyond a return to managing the conflict, the very posture that collapsed with E1. And they seem to assume, Efron warns, that the diplomatic damage can be easily reversed.

Her prescription is concrete: curb settler violence by pulling settler-heavy reserve battalions off West Bank duty and cutting off the money and land that keep illegal outposts alive; freeze annexation; reopen a credible horizon for Palestinian sovereignty. She also offers precedent. Menachem Begin, a lifelong hard-liner, gave back Sinai. Yitzhak Rabin, a hawkish general, was pursuing peace when he was assassinated. Ariel Sharon, the architect of the settlement enterprise, withdrew from Gaza. Each broke with his own base when he judged that the cost of not doing so had become higher.

That is the real question for October 27. It is not which face will occupy the prime minister’s office. It is whether whoever wins will decide that the price of the current course has finally exceeded the price of changing it. On the morning of October 9, the price went up twice.

The Afternoon Track

Part XII of this series traced the American veto record at the Security Council, the shield that made every other country’s condemnations cost-free. Efron reports that shield wearing thin. The Trump administration neither ordered Israel to halt E1 nor, by reports, pressed Britain to hold back its ban. In Congress, Democrats have refused to back arms sales, and a populist Republican wing is pushing to cut aid or attach conditions. Toward Israel’s settlement policy, Washington has shrugged.

Toward the court, it did not. On the afternoon of October 9, 2026, as Edward Wong and Jeanna Smialek reported in the New York Times, Secretary of State Marco Rubio announced sanctions on the International Criminal Court itself. Its judges and prosecutors were already sanctioned. This blocks the court’s property under American jurisdiction and, after a six-month grace period, prohibits the banking, insurance, software and other services that let an institution function day to day. As Al Jazeera reported, Rubio put the policy in one line: “Either the ICC will end its threats, or we will end the ICC.”

The timing may be coincidence. Pillay sat as a judge on that court from 2003 until 2008. And the American campaign against it has never been coy about whom it protects: the February 2025 executive order that began it named one ally specifically, Israel. The president added his own commentary. That morning, as NBC News reported, Trump reshared a Truth Social post calling the new laureate “an unabashed hater of America and Israel”; that evening, as The Hill reported, he called the choice “an indelible stain on Norway.”

So the shape of American policy is stranger than either loyalty or abandonment. Toward Israel’s settlements, Washington is indifferent. Toward the institutions that sit in judgement on Israel, and potentially on the United States, it is openly hostile. The shield no longer guards Israel from criticism so much as it guards Washington from courts. A patron that will sanction a tribunal but will not spend capital on your diplomatic standing is defending its own exposure, not yours.

The morning brought two creditors to Israel’s door. The afternoon brought a third party offering to shoot the debt collector. That is not the same as settling the account. A court can be starved of funds, but the charges it has already filed, and the record it has already built, do not disappear when the money does.

A Note from the Northern Creditor

Canada has a stake in both halves of that day. Ottawa has pledged to join Britain’s ban on settlement goods. For much of the past two decades, Canada was among Israel’s most reliable defenders in international forums. Its position was never the leading edge of anything in this conflict. It was the centre of gravity. When the centre moves, the edges have already gone.

In honesty about my own country, the step is modest, safely within the long-standing Canadian view that the settlements are illegal, and still a pledge rather than a law. But Canada was also among the ICC’s founding states, and the court’s first president was a Canadian jurist, Philippe Kirsch. Six months from now, Canadian banks and firms that deal with the court will learn what their closest ally’s penalties mean in practice.

Two Tracks, One Destination

The Nobel committee and the RAND analyst would probably not enjoy being filed together. One deals in justice, the other in interests. But they arrived at the same place on the same morning, which is the point.

For half a century, Israel’s governments could treat international criticism as noise, because the criticism was moral and the protection was material. October 9 suggested that the two tracks are converging. The moral judgement now carries the world’s most famous peace prize. The material protection is being withdrawn, one import ban and one bond sale at a time, by governments acting not on morality but on the simple conclusion that the old arrangement is finished. Only Washington is moving the other way, and it is defending its own exposure rather than Israel’s standing.

Efron ends her essay with an image from David Grossman’s novel To the End of the Land, of a mother who walks the Galilee to avoid hearing news of her son, as though not hearing it could keep it from being true. Israel, she suggests, has behaved like that mother for decades. Her final judgement is that the current government, in its attempt to bury the Palestinian question for good, “may have exhumed it.”

That is as good a summary of this series as anyone has written. The original sin was never buried. It was only deferred, and deferral is how compound interest works. On October 9, 2026, two very different creditors presented their statements on the same morning. On October 27, Israel’s voters will decide whether to keep walking.
