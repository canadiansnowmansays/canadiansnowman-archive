Here is the assumption buried under this series’ opening deals, MVNO arrangements, and femtocell schematics so far: that the phone in your pocket is a passive endpoint, ready to receive whatever signal a satellite operator decides to beam down to it. Sign the carrier deal, launch the satellites, and the hardware sorts itself out.

It doesn’t. And the way it doesn’t sort itself out turns out to split the entire direct-to-cell industry into two genuinely different technologies wearing the same marketing language — one of which needs nothing new in your phone at all, and one of which needs an entirely different radio.

Two technologies, one word

Start with the model the last two parts of this series have been describing without naming as a hardware choice: spectrum reuse. Starlink’s Direct to Cell service, marketed to T-Mobile customers as T-Satellite, and AST SpaceMobile’s competing architecture both work by having the satellite masquerade as an ordinary cell tower, transmitting on the same mid-band frequencies your phone already uses to talk to terrestrial infrastructure. Your handset’s existing LTE modem and antenna don’t know the tower five hundred and fifty kilometers overhead is a satellite instead of a rooftop mast. US Mobile‘s own technical explainer puts it plainly: no special hardware, software, or modifications are needed, because the satellite simply appears as a normal cell tower to the phone’s radio.

That is the model this entire series has been built around, because it’s the model behind the Rogers-Starlink and Bell/Telus-AST split that opened the Introduction. It’s also the model that makes the buy-versus-partner framing of Part I possible in the first place — SpaceX and AST can strike deals with T-Mobile, Rogers, and dozens of international carriers precisely because they don’t need those carriers’ customers to buy new phones. The installed base is instant. Hundreds of millions of existing handsets are theoretically compatible the moment a carrier flips the switch.

Theoretically. Because the second technology — dedicated satellite spectrum — tells a completely different story, and it’s the one that’s easy to confuse with the first if you only skim the press releases.

The chip that has to exist before the deal matters

Apple’s satellite features, running since the iPhone 14 launched in September 2022, don’t use terrestrial cellular spectrum at all. Emergency SOS via satellite, and the messaging and location-sharing features added since, run on Globalstar’s dedicated satellite frequencies — which means the iPhone needs purpose-built radio hardware capable of finding and holding a satellite link on a completely different band than the one its regular modem uses. That capability didn’t arrive by carrier agreement. It arrived by Apple engineering it directly into the phone, then paying to keep Globalstar’s constellation solvent — over a billion dollars in commitments since 2022, including a further $1.1 billion pledge and a 20 percent equity stake reported in late 2024.

Qualcomm tried to build the equivalent for the rest of the smartphone industry, and it’s worth sitting with how completely that attempt collapsed. Snapdragon Satellite launched at CES in January 2023 with partnership announcements from Xiaomi, Vivo, Honor, and Nothing, riding on the Snapdragon 8 Gen 2 platform and backed by Iridium’s constellation — a direct answer to Apple’s proprietary approach, open to the whole Android ecosystem. None of those announced phones ever shipped the feature to consumers at scale. Qualcomm terminated the direct-to-device deal with Iridium on November 9, 2023, less than a year after the CES unveiling. The chip existed. The satellite network existed. What didn’t exist was enough OEM commitment to make either side’s investment pay off — a hardware chicken-and-egg problem that spectrum-reuse operators like Starlink and AST simply don’t have, because they aren’t asking phone makers to install anything new.

That failure didn’t kill dedicated-spectrum satellite hardware, but it rerouted it. Qualcomm and Iridium reunited in October 2025 with a narrower, less consumer-facing target: integrating Iridium’s Short Burst Data service into a chipset called Snapdragon Mission Tactical Radio, aimed at government and allied-military users rather than a Samsung flagship. Separately, Qualcomm’s newer X80 modem picked up a genuine consumer deployment — but through Skylo, a third satellite operator most people have never heard of, and initially exclusive to Google’s Pixel 9 and 10 on France’s Orange network. At Mobile World Congress in March 2026, Qualcomm unveiled its next modem, the X105, promising satellite connectivity for flagship phones arriving toward the end of the year — the Xiaomi 18, the OnePlus 16 — a full three years after the original Snapdragon Satellite announcement and its own separate, incompatible generation of hardware.

MediaTek, the other major supplier of Android chipsets, has run a parallel but separate track since 2023, when it began partnering with Skylo on its own dedicated IoT-NTN chip, the MT6825. Its newer M90 modem, unveiled at MWC 2025, folded that satellite capability directly into the same silicon as its mainstream 5G functions rather than requiring a second, standalone chip — and by MWC 2026 the company was demonstrating Ku-band satellite connectivity field-tested over Eutelsat’s OneWeb constellation, a fourth network entirely, distinct from Iridium, Globalstar, and Skylo alike.

What ties Qualcomm’s and MediaTek’s efforts together, and what eventually could resolve some of this fragmentation, is a shared technical standard: 3GPP’s Non-Terrestrial Network specification, formalized in Release 17 and extended in Release 18. Unlike Apple’s Globalstar arrangement or the original Snapdragon Satellite deal with Iridium, an NTN-compliant chipset isn’t married to one satellite company. It’s built to a common protocol that any compliant satellite operator can, in principle, connect to — which is closer to how ordinary LTE and 5G roaming works across carriers than to the proprietary, single-vendor model Apple and early Qualcomm both chose. That standardization is still years from mattering to an ordinary buyer; today it just means Qualcomm’s and MediaTek’s satellite features are arriving through different partners, on different timelines, in different phones.

Stack those milestones up and the Android side of the dedicated-spectrum story is a hardware rollout arriving chipset generation by chipset generation, carrier deal by carrier deal, with no single moment where “Android phones support satellite” becomes true for the whole ecosystem at once. It’s the same fragmentation problem this series will trace at the carrier level later on, as regional operators lose coverage as a differentiator once satellite access becomes a checkbox anyone can offer — except here it’s happening one layer down, to the silicon itself.

The phone that was never built

No accounting of satellite handset hardware is complete without addressing the loudest rumor in the category, precisely because it’s the one with the least behind it. For roughly five years running, a “Tesla Pi Phone” — Starlink-native, solar-charged, Neuralink-integrated, priced anywhere from $299 to $1,500 depending on which fabricated listing you land on — has cycled through social media on a schedule that has nothing to do with any actual Tesla or SpaceX product calendar. There has never been an FCC filing, a prototype demonstration, a patent application, or an earnings-call mention. Musk has denied it directly and repeatedly — at Tesla’s May 2023 shareholder meeting, again on the Joe Rogan podcast, and again when he said publicly that a phone would only be worth building “if Apple or Google” started restricting his other companies’ apps. A November 2025 wave of the rumor traced to an AI-generated video with fabricated visuals; fact-checkers at AFP and elsewhere found nothing behind it beyond recycled Starlink and Neuralink branding stitched onto a nonexistent device.

The rumor is worth naming in this essay precisely because of the contrast it sets up with everything else in it. Apple’s satellite hardware push is real, expensive, and documented in SEC filings and named modem generations. Qualcomm’s and MediaTek’s efforts are real enough to have visibly failed once and be visibly retrying. The “Musk phone,” running in parallel for half a decade, has produced more headlines than any of them and not one verified spec. If you’re looking for where SpaceX’s own hardware ambitions actually sit, they sit exactly where Part I described them from the start — a partnership with T-Mobile’s existing spectrum and existing customer base, not a handset. The company that keeps getting credited with building a satellite-native phone has never, at any point, filed the paperwork to try.

Apple’s separate air

Apple, meanwhile, has spent the years since 2022 quietly building something that sits entirely outside the spectrum-reuse story this series has been telling. And its own hardware roadmap just took a turn that makes the split sharper, not softer. Amazon announced in April 2026 that it would acquire Globalstar outright, a deal expected to close in 2027, with Apple’s satellite features transitioning onto Amazon’s own Leo constellation once it does. Apple’s response wasn’t to hedge toward Starlink or AST, the two operators this series has tracked so far. It doubled down on the proprietary path: reporting in April 2026 indicated the iPhone 18 Pro, Pro Max, and a new Ultra model, expected this fall, will ship with Apple’s own custom C2 modem — successor to the C1 chip the company first built in-house rather than licensing from Qualcomm — adding 5G-over-satellite capability designed specifically around the upgraded Globalstar-then-Amazon infrastructure.

That is a genuinely different answer to the buy-versus-partner question Part I posed about carriers, applied one layer down to silicon. SpaceX and AST are partnering with carriers who already have hundreds of millions of compatible phones sitting in customers’ pockets. Apple is building its own modem, financing its own satellite backbone, and running the entire stack itself — vertical integration, at the device level, by the one company with enough unit volume and margin to make that math work without anyone else’s cooperation. The largest single handset maker in the world has, in effect, opted out of the “who owns the air” fight this series has been tracking, because it’s building its own sky.

The gatekeeping that didn’t disappear

Here’s the twist that matters most, and it’s easy to miss if the “no special hardware needed” marketing line is taken at face value: spectrum reuse eliminates the chip problem, but it does not eliminate gatekeeping. It just relocates it.

As of January 2026, T-Satellite’s confirmed compatibility list ran to roughly sixty phone models — a real number, publicly documented, and considerably shorter than “any LTE phone will work.” SatelliteInternet.com‘s tracking noted that T-Mobile and Starlink have said older models are technically capable but require further optimization work and, more consequentially, that expanding the list further depends on a Federal Communications Commission certification the two companies have had to formally request a waiver for. The bottleneck isn’t a missing chip. It’s a compatibility list controlled by the carrier and the regulator — closer to a firmware whitelist than a hardware requirement, but a gate all the same, and one an individual phone owner has no way to open early no matter how new or capable their device actually is.

That is the detail worth sitting with if you’re a Koodo customer, as this series’ recurring personal hook has been throughout. AST SpaceMobile’s own pitch to Telus and Bell has always emphasized exactly this point — no new hardware, no new app, service that works with the phone already in your pocket, because the whole architecture depends on a large antenna array in orbit doing the heavy lifting that a satellite-specific chip would otherwise have to do on the ground. That’s a genuine engineering trade-off, not just marketing: AST’s satellites are physically enormous, with phased-array antennas built to compensate for exactly the weak, unmodified signal an ordinary phone transmits, which is what lets the company promise compatibility with the installed base rather than a future one. Which means this household’s own eventual access to satellite coverage won’t be gated by whether the phone in it is new enough or expensive enough. It will be gated entirely by whichever certification list, carrier rollout schedule, and regulatory approval Telus and AST decide to run first — a bureaucratic bottleneck rather than a shopping decision, but a bottleneck nonetheless, and one this series’ earlier parts on spectrum and femtocells never had reason to mention because it lives one layer below where those parts were looking.

Where this leaves the series’ central question

This series has so far asked who owns the satellites, who owns the spectrum, and who owns the terrestrial buildout underneath them. The handset turns out to be the one place where the answer splits cleanly in two. For the carriers this series has tracked — T-Mobile and Starlink, Rogers and Starlink, Bell and Telus with AST — the phone was never the constraint; the certification list and the regulatory calendar are. For Apple, the phone was always the point, and the company has spent four years and well over a billion dollars making sure it would never have to ask anyone else’s permission to put a satellite radio in its own hardware.

Both answers are, in their own way, about who owns the air. One group of companies decided the air was worth partnering for. The other decided it was worth owning the modem that listens to it.
