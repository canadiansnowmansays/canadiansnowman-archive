On Thursday, standing in the Oval Office flanked by Republican governors and cabinet officials, the President of the United States announced federal funding to build the first new coal-burning power plants in the country since 2013.

“Today we’re taking historic action,” he said, “to bring down the price of energy and the cost of living for all Americans with the power of clean, beautiful coal.”

Clean, beautiful coal.

You will want to hold that phrase in your hand for a moment and turn it over, because there is something almost Dadaist about it — a sentence that manages to be factually false in two of its three substantive words. Coal is not clean. No credible authority anywhere in the world describes it as clean. It produces more harmful air pollutants, more mercury, more planet-warming carbon dioxide than any other fuel used to generate electricity. And while beauty, of course, is in the eye of the beholder, the phrase “clean, beautiful coal” is, and has been since its coinage, a marketing slogan aimed at a political base rather than a description of the physical world.
But we are not here to parse the aesthetics of a fossil fuel. We are here to follow the money — and where that money came from, where it is going, and who is not getting any of it.

Carolyn Kaster/Associated Press

I. The Numbers

The announcement bundled roughly $700 million in federal funding across several coal industry priorities:

  • $425 million to upgrade and extend the life of 12 coal plants that were otherwise headed toward retirement, invoked under the Defense Production Act — a Korean War-era law designed to protect domestic industries essential to national security.

  • Up to $350 million from the Energy Department, including funding for two companies to build new coal plants: one in Alaska (the Terra Energy Center in the Matanuska-Susitna Valley), and one in West Virginia (a company called TerraPurus), plus money to restart a shuttered plant in Maryland.

  • $75 million for a new coal export terminal in Oakland, California, a project the city has fought for nearly a decade.

The Energy Department, in an additional announcement Thursday afternoon, also invoked emergency authority to prevent the closure of a coal plant in Florida scheduled to retire in June.

That is, in a single day: funds to keep old coal plants open, funds to build new ones, funds to ship coal abroad, and emergency authority to block a retirement. Not a policy. A campaign.

II. Where the Money Came From

Here is the part that deserves to be read twice.

The funding for the new coal plants is coming from money that Congress originally allocated for carbon capture technology — the process by which carbon dioxide is sucked from smokestacks and stored underground. Carbon capture was a bipartisan priority, funded through the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act of 2021, a law that passed with Republican votes.

Last year, the Energy Department quietly issued a notice informing companies they could use the carbon capture money for “reliability upgrades” in the near term, with the understanding that carbon capture would be installed “later.” As a former Energy Department official during the Biden administration told the New York Times, there are “basically no guardrails or restrictions on when carbon capture has to be implemented.”

So: Congress voted to remove carbon from smokestacks. The Trump administration is using the money to build more smokestacks. The laundering of the legislative intent is, if nothing else, impressively brazen.

Some congressional Democrats have called this what it plainly is: a loophole that undermines the purpose of the program. They are not wrong. The money was never meant to extend the commercial life of the most polluting fuel on the grid. It was meant to retire that pollution. The distinction is not technical. It is the difference between two opposite policies.

III. The Economics, Which Nobody Asked About

The president said the coal investment would help lower consumers’ energy bills. The experts — the ones whose job it is to actually know the answer to this question — say otherwise.

According to data from the financial advisory firm Lazard, building a new coal plant in 2025 costs between $70 and $170 per megawatt-hour to generate electricity over the plant’s lifetime. Utility-scale solar, by comparison, ranges from $28 to $117 per megawatt-hour; onshore wind from $23 to $139 per megawatt-hour — with the bulk of projects in both categories coming in well below coal’s floor.

Coal generated more than half of America’s electricity in 1990. By last year, that share had fallen to roughly 16 percent, while renewables surpassed 25 percent of total U.S. generation. Since 2010, 330 coal plants have retired, and 60 more have announced plans to close by 2031. The industry is not being strangled by regulation. It is being retired by the market.

Building new coal plants into this landscape is not a strategy. It is a subsidy — a direct transfer of public money to an industry that cannot survive on its own terms. The president said this would lower energy bills. The math says the opposite.

IV. The Forty Thousand

There are roughly 40,000 people employed in the American coal industry today, down from approximately 173,000 in 1985. That is a real human story — of communities built around a single industry, of generational work, of genuine dislocation. Nobody serious disputes any of that, and it deserves to be acknowledged.

But it is worth knowing what 40,000 looks like in context.

Renewable energy supported 596,100 jobs in the United States in 2024. Solar alone employed 370,600 people; wind, 133,000. Wind turbine service technicians and solar photovoltaic installers are, for the third consecutive year, the two fastest-growing occupations in the country.

A University of Michigan study found that local wind and solar jobs can fully replace the coal-plant jobs lost as the nation’s power-generation system moves away from fossil fuels — that across most U.S. regions, annual renewable energy employment fully replaces coal employment.

This is not theory. It was already happening — before the current administration began systematically dismantling the conditions that made it possible.

V. The Workers Who Did Not Get the Memo

While Thursday’s announcement was being made in the Oval Office, it might be worth checking in with some other workers.

In August 2025, the Trump administration issued a stop-work order against Revolution Wind, a 65-turbine offshore wind project located 15 miles south of the Rhode Island coast. The project was 80 percent complete — all offshore foundations installed, 45 of 65 turbines up — and was expected to power 350,000 New England homes. More than 1,200 workers were involved in the effort when the order came down, many of whom were immediately pushed out of work.

Revolution Wind first power

The North America’s Building Trades Unions president put it plainly: “President Trump just fired 1,000 of our members who had already labored to complete 80% of this major energy project. Trump keeps talking a big game about energy dominance, but actions like this prove it’s another empty promise.”

After a federal judge declared Trump’s offshore wind executive order unconstitutional in December 2025, the administration shifted strategies — beginning to pay offshore wind developers to walk away from permitted projects entirely, with deals struck involving TotalEnergies, Golden State Wind in California, and Bluepoint Wind off New Jersey and New York. The buyout money flowed through the Department of Interior’s Judgment Fund, intended for legal settlements, despite there being no active litigation with TotalEnergies.

The total paid to wind developers to not build wind energy has now approached $2 billion.

To summarize the ledger: $2 billion to cancel wind. $700 million to build coal. Carbon capture money redirected to smokestacks. And 1,000 union workers sent home from an 80-percent-complete project, with no plan and no alternative.

The workers of Revolution Wind — the electrical workers, the pipe fitters, the iron workers, the crane operators, the tug crews — are not in any of Thursday’s press releases. They are not standing behind a president in the Oval Office. They did not get a Defense Production Act invocation. They were, to borrow a phrase from their union president, fired.

VI. The Political Geometry

West Virginia has five electoral votes. It voted for Donald Trump in 2024 by 39 points. It is one of the most reliably Republican states in the country. And it is getting a new federally funded coal plant.

Rhode Island has four electoral votes. Connecticut has seven. Both states went to Kamala Harris by double digits. Both states had 1,200 union workers displaced from a nearly-complete wind project that was already fully permitted by federal agencies, reviewed by the Coast Guard, and under contract to sell power to state utilities.

The Oakland coal export terminal has been fought by the city for nearly a decade. Oakland is in California, which has 54 electoral votes and which the Republican party has not won since 1988. The terminal is now getting $75 million in federal support.

This is not energy policy shaped by economics, or by need, or by the engineering realities of a grid that is already struggling to meet AI-era demand. It is energy policy shaped by the electoral map — with blue-state workers and blue-state ratepayers paying the bill.

There is a phrase for this in political science. It is called distributive politics — the allocation of public resources to reward supporters and punish opponents. The Trump administration is practicing it with unusual transparency.

VII. The Matanuska-Susitna Valley

A brief word about the new coal plant proposed for Alaska.

The Terra Energy Center, an affiliate of Canada-based Flatlands Energy, would be built in the Matanuska-Susitna Valley north of Anchorage — a region known for its glaciers and salmon fishing. The developers say the plant could power gold mines or data centers in an area where natural gas from Cook Inlet is running out.

Terra Energy Center does not appear to have a functioning website. It has shared few public details. Similarly, TerraPurus in West Virginia — the other new-build recipient — is largely unknown to industry analysts. Representatives for neither developer could be reached for comment.

As the New York Times reported, analysts say both projects could face serious risk if a future administration changes course on coal. As one coal market analyst put it: “President Trump has been very pro-coal. Now, what happens when the next administration comes in? Will they put a stop to it? I think that’s an enormous risk.”

We are, in other words, talking about public money flowing to unverified developers for projects of uncertain viability, in a sector that the market has been exiting for twenty years, in exchange for political optics that will not survive the next election cycle. The coal industry’s own trade group spokesperson expressed cautious optimism. That is not a ringing endorsement. That is the sound of an industry that has learned to survive on hope and government subsidy.

VIII. The Record, Again

This series has now documented, across 25 essays, a pattern of energy policy that is not primarily about energy.

We have watched carbon capture funding become coal funding. We have watched $2 billion in public money go to wind developers specifically to ensure that wind is not built. We have watched a nearly-complete offshore project frozen, 1,200 workers displaced, and two states’ worth of ratepayers left scrambling — while the administration invoked national security as the pretext for a decision that had nothing to do with national security and everything to do with demonstrating contempt for blue states and their governors.

And now we have the president of the United States standing in the Oval Office and announcing the construction of new coal-burning power plants — funded by money that Congress voted to use for the opposite purpose — and calling it historic.

It is historic. Just not in the way he means.

The United States has not built a new coal plant since 2013. It had not done so for a reason. The reason is that coal costs more, pollutes more, employs fewer people than renewables, and is being replaced by market forces that no executive order can reverse. The administration is not fighting the market. It is spending public money on a theatrical intervention that will accomplish nothing permanent, harm the workers it claims to champion, and leave the grid more expensive than it found it.

Clean, beautiful coal.

I have now written twenty-five essays trying to give the administration’s energy policy the benefit of analytical doubt. I am running out of doubt.
