There is a peculiar Ottawa-adjacent satisfaction, watching from north of the border, in seeing a story you thought was over turn out to have been the trailer for a longer picture. When I closed out this series’ last addendum, “The Handcuffs That Never Came,” on August 28, 2026, the shape of things seemed reasonably settled: Rand Paul had his contempt vote, the Trump Justice Department had the referral sitting on a desk somewhere, and word out of Washington — by way of Adam Kinzinger’s newsletter, sourced to the Wall Street Journal — was that DOJ officials were quietly declining to act on it. The theatre had played out. The prosecutors, it appeared, had read the file and found it thin.

On September 5, 2026, Benjamin Mueller and Glenn Thrush reported for The New York Times that this was, at best, half the story — and possibly the less important half.

The Times exclusive does not describe an investigation that stalled and then politely died. It describes one that has been running for years, expanded rather than contracted under the second Trump administration, and now reaches at least eight scientists, not one. Dr. Fauci remains the headline name. He is no longer, on this telling, the whole story.

The Meeting That Started It

The piece opens with a scene worth sitting with: in early 2025, Dan Bongino — then newly installed as the FBI’s deputy director, having arrived by way of a career in conservative talk radio rather than law enforcement — summoned dozens of agents to Washington and demanded to know why a years-long inquiry into Covid’s origins had stalled, and why investigators hadn’t gone harder after the government scientists he believed were responsible. The meeting reportedly grew heated enough that Mr. Bongino asked a dissenting official to leave the room.

Mr. Bongino has since stepped down and returned to podcasting. The investigation he set in motion has not stepped down with him. That is the throughline of the whole piece, and it is worth pausing on, because it is a small case study in how institutions outlive the individuals who redirect them. A talk-radio host spends most of a year inside the FBI, leans on the machinery once, and the machinery keeps turning long after he has gone back to his microphone. Whatever one thinks of the underlying merits, that is not nothing.

Eight Names, Not One

The investigative reach described here is genuinely broader than anything this series has previously catalogued. Federal agents and prosecutors have sought records — phone, email, grant, payment — and grand jury testimony touching at least eight scientists. Among them: Dr. Fauci, and Richard J. Roberts, a Nobel laureate in medicine who had organized a letter opposing the cancellation of EcoHealth Alliance’s NIH grant back in 2020.

This matters for how we read everything that came before it in this series. Parts VIII and IX treated the Senate contempt vote and the Fifth Amendment hearing as though Dr. Fauci were the target — singular, central, personally imperilled. The Times reporting suggests something closer to a dragnet: a list of researchers connected, however loosely, to EcoHealth’s collaboration with the Wuhan Institute of Virology, being worked through methodically rather than theatrically. Mitchell Benzine, who ran the House subcommittee’s Republican staff through 2025, is quoted describing the strategy in blunt architectural terms — start at the bottom of the pyramid, work up. Dr. Morens, Dr. Fauci’s former adviser, sat nearer the base. Dr. Fauci sat, or sits, near the top.

The Pardon Nobody in This Series Had Mentioned

Here is the detail that reorders the whole story, and one this series has not previously addressed: Dr. Fauci received a pre-emptive pardon from President Biden before he left office, covering official actions taken between 2014 and 2025.

I want to be honest about what that means for the argument I have been making across eight parts and two addenda. If Dr. Fauci is shielded from federal prosecution for his official conduct across the exact period under investigation, then “The Handcuffs That Never Came” may have had the right observation — DOJ reluctance — for partly the wrong reason. I framed that reluctance as institutional caution, perhaps even institutional conscience, prosecutors quietly declining to build a weak case. Mr. Bongino himself, on his podcast, offers a blunter explanation: he has conceded there are, in his words, serious complications with any case against Dr. Fauci because of the pardon. That is not prosecutorial restraint. That is a wall.

It does not follow that the pardon ends the story for anyone else on the list. Dr. Roberts is not pardoned. The unnamed EcoHealth staff member ordered before a federal grand jury is not pardoned. Whatever protection the outgoing president extended to his most prominent scientist did not extend to the seven other names, and the investigation’s architecture — the pyramid, worked from the bottom — suggests the pardon may simply have redirected the pressure rather than relieved it.

Wine, Witch Trials, and the Morens Precedent

Readers of Part IX and the first addendum already know Dr. Morens pleaded guilty in August 2026 to a records-concealment charge — steering government business into a personal email account to evade Freedom of Information Act requests. The Times account adds texture I had not previously included: a subpoena from September 2025 sought evidence of any payments Dr. Fauci received from EcoHealth “in currency, gift baskets, wine, meals, travel” — and Dr. Morens himself pleaded guilty to accepting an illegal gratuity of two bottles of a particular Napa Valley red blend from Peter Daszak, EcoHealth’s former president. Dr. Daszak has called the wine a black joke referencing 2020-era calls for scientists’ imprisonment — a joke that did not survive contact with a federal indictment.

Dr. Morens also described, in emails now public, an effort to protect scientists under political attack that he likened to the Salem witch trials. It is a comparison history will judge on its own terms, but I note that a man who wrote sincerely about protecting “scientific independence” also wrote about learning to make emails disappear, and it is possible to hold both facts about him at once without letting either one cancel the other. That has been this series’ operating principle from the Introduction onward, and this new reporting does not change it — it just gives it more to hold.

A Second, Quieter Front

Beyond the records and testimony campaign, the Times describes a separate line of inquiry out of the FBI’s Cleveland field office, examining whether Dr. Fauci and other government scientists pressured virologists in early 2020 to publicly downplay the possibility of a lab origin — the long-standing Republican theory that a landmark March 2020 paper on the virus’s natural origins was shaped, in part, by a grant decision. Dr. Fauci and the scientists involved have denied this, and have said the grant in question was approved before the relevant conversations took place; the researchers have said their own doubts were resolved independently, by further comparative work on coronaviruses in bats and pangolins.

I raise this mainly to note how much of this dispute still turns on a question this series flagged in the Introduction and has never pretended to resolve: what did the virologists actually believe, and when, and why did their public statements shift. Two years of subpoenas have not settled that question. Nothing in this report suggests they are about to.

What This Changes, and What It Doesn’t

It changes the moral framing I owe this addendum, if not the whole series. “The Handcuffs That Never Came” implicitly treated Dr. Fauci as the investigation’s centre of gravity — a man DOJ officials had quietly decided not to pursue. The Times reporting suggests the centre of gravity was never really him. It was a theory: that Covid was not merely a lab leak but an American-scientist-engineered cover-up of one, a theory Mr. Bongino has stated on-air in those terms, and has separately connected — without evidentiary support any investigation has produced — to 2020 pandemic-era voting measures he has called, on his podcast, an effort to steal the election. Senator Blumenthal’s comparison to McCarthyism is doing real work in the piece, and I think it is the right comparison: not because every named scientist is innocent of everything, but because the through-line from “the virus escaped a lab” to “therefore mail-in ballots were fraudulent” is not an evidentiary chain. It is a narrative one, built to hold together a set of grievances that predate the pandemic and will outlast this investigation regardless of what it finds.

What doesn’t change is the standard this series has tried to hold from the start: sympathy for Dr. Fauci’s decades of public health work is not a licence to wave away the Morens emails, and scepticism of Mr. Bongino’s theory of the case is not proof that eight scientists have nothing to answer for. The Times piece, credit to it, resists tidiness on both counts. It quotes Dr. Fauci’s lawyer calling the entire effort baseless. It quotes Dr. Daszak calling it retaliation. It also quotes, without editorial comment, a subpoena asking for wine receipts. All three things can be true of the same investigation at once.

Part VIII of this series ended with Dr. Fauci reciting the same twenty-one words a hundred times before a Senate committee. The Epilogue asked what the republic owed a man who had spent forty years being right about diseases and wrong, repeatedly, about how to survive being right in public. Neither of those endings anticipated a pre-emptive pardon, a Nobel laureate on a subpoena list, or an FBI deputy director who left the building but not the case. This series was never going to have a tidy last word on Anthony Fauci. It is starting to look like it will not get one at all.
