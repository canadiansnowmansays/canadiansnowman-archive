In mid-October 2026, three weeks before the midterms, Donald Trump was asked whether he would accept the results. “Of course I will,” he said. “But we don’t want cheating.”

This series’ coda, The Excuse Written in Advance, took the second sentence more seriously than the first. Four essays had tested whether Trump applied any consistent standard to the running of elections, and found only one: verification counts when it favours him. The coda turned that finding into a prediction. On the night of 3 November, it said, “cheating” would mean losing.

The Grievance Machine was built as a falsifiable audit, not a character judgement, so its prediction has to be graded like any other finding. Here is the grade. Trump lost, by the widest margin his party has suffered in half a century. And about that result, he did not say the word.

The word he didn’t use

Start with the scale. Republicans lost the national House vote by about 13 points, by the Times’ estimate. That is the worst defeat for either party since the aftermath of Watergate, as Shane Goldmacher, Jonathan Swan and Maggie Haberman reported for The New York Times. Democrats flipped seven Republican Senate seats, from Maine to Texas, and took the House by a margin that grew with every batch of ballots.

Then the reaction. At about 10:30 p.m. on election night, with the networks still calling races, Trump told The New York Times that he was “absolutely willing to accept those results.” Asked the same question by Fox News, he answered with a question of his own, as Quinta Jurecic recorded in The Atlantic: “Sure, I’ll accept [the results]. Why wouldn’t I?” By the next morning he was telling Fox that Democrats “did a good job” and “fought hard,” and that “I don’t view it as a hit on me,” as Forbes reported.

What he offered instead of fraud was absence. “I wasn’t on the Ballot and if I was, we would have won IN A LANDSLIDE!” he posted on election night. A White House official gave the Times the same line in prose: “President Trump was not on the ballot, and it’s proven that Trump voters show up when he is on the ballot. Candidate quality matters.” On 4 November, when Vice President JD Vance wrote that “the reason we lost is the war,” Trump called the assessment “largely true,” according to the live coverage in The New York Times the following day. Then he added: “If I had to do it again, I’d have to do it again.”

That is blame-shifting, and plenty of it. It is not a denial of the count. The coda’s prediction, as worded, was wrong. A series that scores only its hits is not an audit, so the miss goes on the record first.

Why the excuse stayed in the drawer

The explanation is arithmetic. Tom Nichols put it most plainly in The Atlantic: the result was too big to challenge, because “any attempt to undermine the result would look either violently dictatorial or patently ridiculous.” Tammy Patrick, a veteran election administrator quoted by Jurecic, needed only four words: “High turnout, wide margins.”

A fraud story needs somewhere for the fraud to hide. In 2020 the presidency turned on margins of tens of thousands of votes in a handful of states, few enough to imagine being manufactured in a few counties. A 13-point national landslide has no hiding place. Kansas, South Carolina and Texas do not share a counting room.

The people who would have had to sell the story knew it before anyone voted. Six days before the election, according to the Times, more than two dozen of Trump’s top strategists joined a private video call. “I’m getting my affairs in order,” said James Blair, who had left the White House to run the president’s midterm operation. “I suggest everybody else do the same.” Then he mentioned a Trump ally in Minnesota who had demanded a recount of a primary he lost in a landslide. “Look, it’s fine,” Blair said. “I think we’ll just pull a Mike Lindell and demand a recount regardless of how many points we’re down.”

The call went on in the same register. Tony Fabrizio, the president’s pollster, wondered whether they might get asylum in Israel. “At least Bibi won. He’ll protect us,” Blair said, sarcastically. Israel had voted the day before. Benjamin Netanyahu’s bloc fell short of a majority, and his party soon claimed that election had been stolen.

That exchange is the most revealing evidence this series has found, and it comes from Trump’s own people. Inside his own operation, the stolen-election reflex has a name. It is a move, a “Lindell,” something you can propose as a joke on a strategy call because everyone on the line knows what it is and what it is for. Nobody on that call seems to have believed it would work this time. Nobody seems to have believed it described anything real.

Where the machine did run

If the excuse stayed in the drawer where the margins were wide, it came out in two places where a pretext had been prepared before anyone voted.

The first was Alaska. On 22 October 2026, the Justice Department sued to block the state from certifying a win by Mary Peltola, the Democratic challenger, so long as a second candidate named Daniel J. Sullivan, a retired teacher who shares a name with the Republican incumbent, remained on the ballot. The suit asked for a hold “pending an investigation to determine whether votes cast for Daniel J. Sullivan were meant to be cast for Senator Sullivan.” Trump did not leave the authorship in doubt. “I’m the one that said do it,” he told reporters the next day, as The Associated Press reported. Derek Muller, an election-law scholar at Notre Dame, observed that the statute the department cited “simply doesn’t let the Department of Justice enjoin certification.”

Judge Sharon Gleason dismissed the suit on 1 November without holding a hearing, ruling that the department lacked standing. The criminal side kept moving. On election morning came the news that the department had sent letters to about 20 people aligned with the Democratic Party, among them the election lawyer Marc Elias, telling them they were targets of its inquiry, as PBS NewsHour reported. Investigators had issued more than 100 subpoenas and preservation letters, and FBI agents had visited the homes of the second Sullivan’s donors. Michael Heyman, the U.S. attorney for Alaska, would not say what crime he was investigating: “I’m not making any assumptions on whether a crime has been committed or not.” The president was less cautious. On Fox & Friends that morning he said Elias “should go to jail.”

The second was California. The day before the vote, the administration asked the Supreme Court to set aside about 20,000 mail ballots it said might have been cast by noncitizens. The Court refused, as The Washington Post reported on 2 November.

Set beside the 2020 record in Part I, the continuity is exact. In 2020 the complaint came after the count and asked courts to stop counting or discard what had been counted. In 2026 the Alaska suit was filed before a single ballot had been cast, asking a court to withhold certification from a result that did not yet exist, and only if it went the wrong way. The coda called the story an excuse written in advance. In Alaska the excuse became a pleading.

The machinery, scored

Parts II and III catalogued what was built between the two elections. Here is how it performed.

Federal agents at the polls. Part III recorded the administration’s refusal to rule them out. They did not appear. As of 5 p.m. on Election Day there were no confirmed reports of federal law enforcement at polling places, and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said he had sent no immigration agents, according to ProPublica. The nearest thing was in Mesa, Arizona, where Homeland Security Investigations agents arrested five people outside a grocery store about half a mile from a vote centre, as FOX 10 Phoenix reported. ICE called it a targeted operation; advocates called it intimidation. The Justice Department’s own monitors drew complaints in Dearborn and Flint, Michigan, and in Bernalillo County, New Mexico, and Michigan said the department later called their conduct inconsistent with its training. That is friction. It is not the scene Part III feared.

Redistricting. This was the first mechanism in Part II, and it failed spectacularly. Republicans lost nearly all of the seats they redrew, Madeleine Carlisle and Russell Berman reported in The Atlantic. Democrats won four of the five Texas seats drawn to defeat them. In North Carolina, Representative Don Davis survived a map drawn to remove him, and Democrats came within several thousand votes of sweeping all 14 districts. Southern Republicans used the Supreme Court’s Voting Rights Act decision to break up Black districts, and Black turnout surged in response. LaTosha Brown of Black Voters Matter called it “the great Blacklash.” Shomari Figures held his redrawn Alabama seat by more than seven points.

Florida supplied the punchline. Its constitution bans partisan redistricting, so Republicans redrew the map in the spring while refusing to say why. “Anything you say will get you subpoenaed,” one Republican consultant told Berman for The Atlantic in April. Hakeem Jeffries called the result “the DeSantis dummymander.” It won none of the four seats it targeted. “We had 20 Republicans going into the election,” Governor Ron DeSantis said afterwards. “We have 20 Republicans in our congressional delegation now. So it is what it is.” Anne Applebaum noted in The Atlantic that this was the first gerrymandering campaign ever directed from the White House.

The election bill. Part II included the SAVE America Act as a mechanism that had not succeeded, for fairness. It still has not, and its afterlife says more than its failure. Michael Scherer described on The Atlantic’s Radio Atlantic how the biggest affordability measure of the year, a bipartisan housing bill sponsored by Senator Elizabeth Warren, passed with huge majorities. “And President Trump would not sign it,” Scherer said. “And the reason he wouldn’t sign it was he was upset with Senate Republicans that they wouldn’t pass his election-reform bill.” In a campaign about the cost of living, the president held a housing bill hostage to a voting bill.

How the close counts ended

Two Senate races took another two weeks to settle. They are the cleanest test in this whole audit, because both were close, and only one of them was called rigged.

On 5 November, Scott Colom, a Democratic prosecutor, led Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith of Mississippi by about 1,130 votes. Mississippi has no mechanism for a recount in a statewide general election; a losing candidate can only go to court to include or exclude specific groups of ballots before certification, Muller told the Times. “We will protect the process of this election,” Colom said, “we will protect the integrity of this election.” The language of integrity had changed sides. Hyde-Smith’s lawyers demanded “the immediate separation, secure custody, and preservation of all affidavit ballots,” as Yahoo News reported, and the state Republican chairman, Mike Hurst, said the party would be ready “to avail ourselves of every legal option at our disposal.”

Then the counties counted the affidavit ballots, by hand, and the lead grew. The Associated Press called the race on 11 November: Colom 49.2 per cent, Hyde-Smith 48.7, a margin of 4,645 votes, as Mississippi Today reported. Hyde-Smith conceded the same day. “While this is not the outcome we worked for or hoped for,” she said, “I respect the decision of Mississippi voters.” Colom became the first Democrat Mississippi has sent to the Senate since John Stennis in 1982, and its first Black senator since Reconstruction. Trump, asked about him on Stephen A. Smith’s podcast, gave Colom “a lot of credit” for working the line outside his Corinth rally, as WLOX reported. Nobody went to court. Nobody needed the recount law Mississippi does not have.

Alaska went differently. On 10 November, with 76 per cent of the vote counted and Peltola ahead by 2,517 votes, Trump took to Truth Social. “Dan Sullivan PLUS Dan Sullivan wins the Alaska Election by thousands of votes,” he wrote. The votes for “the Fake Dan Sullivan” had been cast “solely for the purpose of fraud and confusion,” and should be handed to the real one. “There should be no question concerning this Rigged Election. Time is of the essence!!!” Forbes reported the post.

The count answered him. When the Associated Press called the race on 17 November, Peltola had 144,302 votes, Senator Sullivan 131,844 and the second Sullivan 4,760, according to Newsweek. That put Peltola at 50.1 per cent of first choices. Had every vote for the second Sullivan gone to the first, as Trump demanded, Peltola would still have won by more than 7,000. Senator Sullivan has not conceded, and the state plans to certify on 25 November. The machinery is still running. The Justice Department has appealed Judge Gleason’s dismissal to the Ninth Circuit, and its criminal inquiry has issued more than 200 subpoenas by Fox News’s count without charging anyone, as Newsweek reported on 18 November. An Alaska lawyer, David R. Weber, has asked Judge Gleason to sanction three of the department’s lawyers, Heyman among them, for “calculated abuse of this Court for improper political purpose.”

Anne Applebaum had advised readers to “watch for lawsuits, or unexplained delays” after the vote. Mississippi, close as it was, produced neither. Alaska produced the lawsuit, the subpoenas and the accusation the coda predicted.

One scenario can be retired. In the weeks before the vote, Robert Rubin warned in The New York Times that Trump’s allies might use a procedural loophole involving the clerk of the House to refuse to seat elected Democrats when the new Congress convenes on 3 January 2027. As Jim Saksa explained for Democracy Docket, the scheme could only work in a close election with a united Republican caucus. Speaker Mike Johnson had already said that “for anyone to suggest otherwise is absurd.” With a Democratic House majority as large as this one, the scenario has no arithmetic left.

The verdict

This series asked one question: did Trump apply a consistent standard to election administration in 2020 and 2026? The concluding essay found that he did not, except for one principle that survived every test: verification counts when it favours him.

The midterms add a corollary, and it is less flattering than it looks. In 2020 he refused a count he had lost narrowly. In 2026 he accepted a count he had lost overwhelmingly. That is not a change of principle. It is a calculation of cost. Where the margin was too wide, the excuse stayed in the drawer, and the blame went to the candidates, the ballot and the war.

Mississippi and Alaska sharpen the rule. Both were close, and only one was called rigged. Mississippi, decided by half a point in a state with no recount law, had no story prepared for it, and Trump praised the winner. Alaska had a second Sullivan, a lawsuit filed before the vote, a grand jury and target letters timed to election morning, and there Trump reached for the word. The grievance did not simply follow the margin. It followed the margin to wherever the excuse had already been written.

So the coda was wrong in its letter and right in its logic. “Cheating” did not mean losing. It meant losing narrowly, somewhere the excuse had been prepared in advance.

The machine did not break on 3 November. It idled. The Justice Department that sent target letters on election morning is still there. So is the gutted election-security programme, and so are the emptied commissions that Part III described. A strategist can still propose a Lindell on a conference call, because everyone on the line knows what one is.

This series closes as it began, with a claim that the record can prove wrong. Trump’s own name may never appear on another ballot. Someone who learned from him will, and will one day face a count that is close. That is when the machine will start again.
