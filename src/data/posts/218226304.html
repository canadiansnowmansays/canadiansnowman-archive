In 2023, researchers at the Beijing Institute of Technology sat down to test how well a new American chatbot handled questions in Chinese, and came away with a tidy little catalogue of embarrassments. The bot described Yao Ming, the former N.B.A. centre, as the first Chinese woman to play professional basketball in the United States. It confused “Journey to the West” with “Dream of the Red Chamber,” two classics of Chinese literature written roughly two centuries apart. It also, the researchers noted with evident irritation, produced what they characterised as biased commentary about China and would not reliably decline to answer politically sensitive questions about the country.

As David Pierson and Berry Wang reported for The New York Times on August 17, 2026, that 2023 study has aged into something more consequential than a list of trivia errors. It crystallized, for Beijing, the actual shape of a strategic vulnerability: the systems increasingly mediating how the world understands current events, history, and politics are trained overwhelmingly on English-language data, encoding what Chinese analysts describe as a Western default. On contested questions — Taiwan’s status, the nature of human rights, the character of the Chinese Communist Party’s rule — that default does not stay neutral. It just means one civilization’s framing wins by volume before the argument even starts.

A Blueprint for Becoming the Supply, Not Just the Demand

China’s answer, laid out earlier this year by the National Data Administration, is not to out-argue that default. It is to out-supply it. The agency’s blueprint sets a goal of transforming China into what the Times piece calls a data powerhouse by the end of 2028, building “high quality” data sets across more than two dozen strategic fields — scientific research, industrial manufacturing, autonomous vehicles among them — and, critically, sharing those data sets with the world. That commitment was reaffirmed in July at the World Artificial Intelligence Conference in Shanghai, where Beijing pledged to help dozens of developing countries build their own AI systems using Chinese-supplied data, and it has already released substantial troves curated by government labs and state media for download, worldwide, at no cost.

The largest of these is WanJuan — “ten thousand scrolls” — built by the state-backed Shanghai AI Laboratory and freely available to developers on repositories like GitHub and Hugging Face. It spans history, sports, law, current events, medicine, and literature, is explicitly designed to align with what the Times piece describes as mainstream Chinese values, and is offered not only in Chinese but in Arabic, Korean, Russian, Thai, and Vietnamese — a distribution list that reads less like an academic resource and more like a map of where Beijing would like its framing of the world to take root next.

The multilingual collection has its own technical paper. Researchers at the Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory described it on arXiv on January 24, 2025 under the name WanJuanSiLu. It holds more than 350 million documents, over 1.2 terabytes and more than 300 billion tokens, with more than 150 gigabytes in each of Thai, Russian, Arabic, Korean and Vietnamese. Among the cleaning steps the authors list is “multilingual sensitive word filtering.” The paper does not say which words count as sensitive.

The domestic motive behind all this is more mundane than the geopolitical one, and worth understanding on its own terms, because it explains why China needs partners rather than just self-sufficiency. Despite sitting on immense volumes of data from state surveillance systems and hundreds of millions of platform users, Chinese labs reportedly struggle to actually use most of it, because it sits fragmented across government departments and companies that do not share. Xiaomeng Lu of the Eurasia Group told the Times that resolving those domestic data silos is China’s top priority, ahead of almost anything else in its AI strategy. That fragmentation is also, not coincidentally, part of why Chinese firms lean so heavily on distillation — training their own models by extracting patterns from the outputs of more powerful foreign systems — a practice American companies including Anthropic have already complained amounts to unauthorized copying of their technology. The export push and the distillation complaint are, in an important sense, two sides of the same resource gap: China does not yet have enough clean, high-value data of its own, and is working both angles — pulling from the leading foreign models while pushing its own curated sets outward — to close it.

The Study That Found the Seam

The most striking evidence in the piece isn’t about deliberate Chinese data exports at all. It’s about what has already leaked in, unbidden, through the ordinary mechanics of how large language models are trained. A study published in Nature — cited in the Times report — tested American models including ChatGPT and Claude by asking politically loaded questions in both English and Chinese: is China an autocracy, is Xi Jinping a good leader, and similar. The responses in Chinese skewed measurably more favourable to Beijing than the English responses to the identical questions. The likely explanation, per the researchers, is not ideology baked into the model itself but simple data availability: Chinese-language material on the internet is disproportionately produced by China’s own vast state media apparatus, while independent or critical Chinese-language voices are comparatively scarce, censored, or drowned out by volume. The model isn’t taking a side. It’s reflecting whichever side left more text lying around to learn from.

The paper behind that finding, “State media control influences large language models,” was published in Nature on May 13, 2026 by Hannah Waight, Eddie Yang, Yin Yuan, Solomon Messing, Margaret E. Roberts, Brandon M. Stewart and Joshua A. Tucker. Its figures are worth having. In CulturaX, an open training data set, the authors found more than 3.1 million Chinese-language documents that matched scripted state news or articles from the party’s Xuexi Qiangguo app, which they put at “approximately 41 times the number of documents that come from the Chinese-language Wikipedia domain.” When annotators were shown a commercial model’s answers to the same question asked in Chinese and in English, and asked which was more favourable to the Chinese government, “they chose the Chinese-prompted response 75.3% of the time.” The pattern was not unique to China. Across 37 countries whose languages are spoken mainly within their own borders, the authors found that those with more state media control got more favourable portrayals of their governments from the models.

Brandon Stewart, the Princeton sociologist who worked on the study, offered the Times a single sentence that I think is the best distillation of what this entire essay is actually about: “What A.I. does is it disconnects the messenger from the message.” A reader who encountered the same paragraph with a People’s Daily byline would evaluate it completely differently than the identical paragraph delivered in the neutral, helpful voice of a chatbot answering a question. The chatbot doesn’t launder propaganda by adding false claims to it. It launders propaganda by removing the one piece of context — who is actually speaking — that used to be free.

“Open” as a Word Doing Two Different Jobs

There is a rhetorical move worth flagging here, because this series has flagged a version of it before. At the Shanghai conference, Xi Jinping positioned China as the champion of an open approach to artificial intelligence — a framing that, on the surface, sounds like the opposite of the propaganda concern, since “open” usually means transparent, inspectable, uncontrolled. But Chinese chatbots, per the Times reporting, are bound by strict rules requiring adherence to official party narratives; DeepSeek and similar popular Chinese models reliably evade questions about Xi personally or about the government’s handling of “zero Covid,” even when users route around the country’s standard internet controls to ask. “Open,” in this context, means open architecture — code and weights anyone can download and modify — not open discourse. That is precisely the same open-model category that sails through the Trump administration’s new AI review process on a technicality that exempts freely modifiable models from the scrutiny closed frontier systems now face. The Washington Post reported the exemption on August 4, 2026, and Owen Dahlkamp, Brendan Bordelon and Cheyenne Haslett reported for E&E News by Politico the next day that the vetting framework, which follows a June 2 executive order, would not apply to “so-called open-source or open-weight models,” a category that has seen a surge of activity “including from AI developers in China.” As of early October 2026, no reversal of the exemption had been reported. China gets to be “open” in the deregulatory sense that earns it a pass from Washington’s own review regime, while remaining tightly closed in the discursive sense that actually matters for what the model will and won’t tell you about its own government.

Lock-In Dressed as Generosity

None of this requires any single dramatic act of censorship to work, which is what makes it a genuinely difficult problem to legislate against. A developer in a country with limited AI infrastructure of its own, offered a free, capable, well-documented dataset in their own language, has very little reason to say no on the grounds that the dataset’s framing of history and politics happens to align with Beijing’s preferences rather than nobody’s. The alternative isn’t a neutral dataset; there mostly isn’t one. The alternative is usually an English-language set that encodes its own, different, unstated assumptions with equal invisibility. Kenton Thibaut of the Atlantic Council frames the strategic logic plainly in the Times piece: the data exports function as a form of technological lock-in, useful to Chinese companies commercially and useful to Beijing politically, with the broader aim of making the global AI ecosystem more comfortable terrain for the party to operate in. Alex Colville of the Australian Strategic Policy Institute puts the downside just as plainly: the practical effect is to hand authoritarian governments more leverage over what a chatbot, anywhere, will treat as true.

The Strategy, Already Running in the Field

Everything above describes a blueprint: a stated intention, a dataset released, a conference pledge. Part 73 followed the same contest to Nairobi and Kampala, through the reporting of Paul Mozur, Adam Satariano and Aaron Krolik, where developers are choosing Chinese open models on price. One detail from that reporting belongs here too, because it turns this essay’s argument into a product. When an outside researcher tested Sunflower, the Ugandan farming chatbot Ernest Mwebaze built on an Alibaba model, and asked whether China is a democracy, it said yes. Asked about China’s economic and environmental impact on Uganda, it sidestepped. Chinese state media praised the tool, and Huawei called Mwebaze about moving it onto Chinese cloud servers. He has since moved the project to one of Google’s open models.

That is the Nature study’s finding, arriving in the field. Nobody set out to make Sunflower speak for Beijing. It learned from the text that was available, and the text that was available leaned one way. Brandon Stewart’s messenger, disconnected from the message, was answering farmers’ questions before anyone involved had noticed whose voice it was using.

The Same Seam, Worked on Purpose

Everything above describes influence that arrives through volume and free downloads. Moscow has found a cruder route to the same place, and it does not need anyone to choose its dataset. In a guest essay for the Times on 6 October, Anthony Vinci, a former senior intelligence officer and the first chief technology officer of the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency, described a Russia-backed network called Portal Kombat that runs hundreds of websites built less for human readers than for machines. According to NewsGuard, which tracks online disinformation, the network pushed more than 3.6 million stories in dozens of languages in 2024, and when its researchers tested the leading chatbots, Portal Kombat’s propaganda was regularly passed on to users. Vinci’s analogy is the K.G.B. cultivating fellow travellers in London and New York, except that the powerful figures being cultivated now are ChatGPT, Grok and Gemini, and the people they pass the material on to number in the tens of millions.

This is Brandon Stewart’s seam, worked deliberately. The Nature study found that a chatbot reflects whichever side left more text lying around; Portal Kombat’s business is leaving more text lying around. Vinci catalogues the rest of the toolkit. Reporters Without Borders found Alibaba’s Qwen model calling the Uyghur internment camps “education and vocational training centers” and dismissing questions about them as “baseless speculation.” Vanderbilt researchers, working from leaked documents, describe the Chinese firm GoLaxy profiling social-media users and then engaging them in conversation to shift their politics, in Hong Kong in 2020 and ahead of Taiwan’s 2024 election; GoLaxy denies doing election work. In June, OpenAI reported that its own models had probably been used in a Chinese campaign it called “Data Center Bandwagon,” which generated posts about electricity costs to stoke American opposition to data centres. And Vinci cites a study in Nature finding that A.I. dialogue is roughly four times as persuasive as a traditional political advertisement. A pamphlet cannot answer back. A chatbot can.

His remedy is the spy’s: triangulate across sources, test a source on questions you already know the answer to, and never treat anything as permanently true. “If you are a Claude user, then check ChatGPT, or vice versa,” he writes. Readers of a series written in collaboration with Claude should take that advice literally. But notice to whom it is addressed. Every defence Vinci proposes is a burden placed on the individual user, supported by a school curriculum and some crowdsourcing. None asks anything of the companies whose models are being fed the material, or of the governments that license them. Counter-intelligence used to be a state function. In Vinci’s version, everyone becomes their own counterspy.

What This Series Keeps Circling Back To

I have spent a good deal of this series on the question of who controls a model’s behaviour after it is built — the export controls, the safety reviews, the Pentagon’s fights with Anthropic over end-use restrictions. This piece is a reminder that there is an earlier and in some ways harder question sitting upstream of all of that: who controls what the model learned before anyone thought to regulate it. A watermark can tell you a paragraph came from a machine. A safety review can tell you a model won’t help build a weapon. Neither one can tell you whose account of the Taiwan Strait, or of 1989, or of the current government’s legitimacy, quietly won the argument inside the training data before you ever typed your question. That contest is already underway, it is being conducted largely through the unglamorous mechanism of dataset licensing and free downloads on GitHub, and — unlike a chip export ban or a Pentagon procurement fight — it will not announce itself with a press conference when it’s won.

The Asymmetry Nobody Is Pricing In

There is also a harder-edged asymmetry buried in this story that deserves its own line, because it inverts the usual framing of the U.S.-China AI competition as a contest of raw model capability. On capability, the consensus estimate this series has cited before puts China perhaps six months behind the American frontier. On data influence, there is no equivalent clock running, because the mechanism doesn’t require China to catch up on model quality at all. A developing-world engineer building a modest regional chatbot on top of a free, well-documented, Chinese-curated dataset does not need China to have the best model in the world. They need China to have offered the easiest, cheapest, most complete answer to the specific problem in front of them — a shortage of usable training data in their own language — at the moment they were building. Model capability is a race with a scoreboard everyone can see. Data adoption is a race that gets decided quietly, developer by developer, dataset download by dataset download, and the winner is often just whoever showed up first with something usable and free.

That is also why the American side of this story is comparatively thin, and not through any particular failure of policy. The United States has never needed to build an equivalent public data-export apparatus, because its dominance in this domain was never really a state project to begin with — it was the accumulated byproduct of English being the internet’s default language for thirty years before anyone was training a language model on any of it. That advantage was inherited, not manufactured, which may be exactly why it has taken this long for anyone in Washington to notice that Beijing is now trying to manufacture the equivalent from scratch, deliberately, as a matter of declared national strategy. An advantage nobody had to build is also an advantage nobody in government has ever had to defend. China’s blueprint is the first serious attempt by any state actor to treat training data itself as a strategic resource worth subsidizing and exporting the way an earlier generation of great powers subsidized and exported everything from wheat to weapons. The summit between President Trump and Xi Jinping in Washington in late September did not change that. As Huizhong Wu reported for the Associated Press on September 26, 2026, the two governments agreed to set up “a communication mechanism for artificial intelligence-related incidents,” with an AI-specific dialogue scheduled for November. Trump made clear what it would not cover: “The United States of America is not going to be putting on brakes.” The published outcomes said nothing about training data. Whether Washington notices in time to answer it with anything more coordinated than a magazine article’s worth of concern is, as of early October 2026, very much an open question.
