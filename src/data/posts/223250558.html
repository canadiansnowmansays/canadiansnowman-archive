Part 105 covered the case for prosecuting AI companies under laws that already exist, and the argument that “going rogue” is a phrase doing quiet work to excuse the people who built the thing that went rogue. This entry covers a related but distinct argument, made by someone with no legal theory to sell and three decades of direct observation to draw on instead: that the entire debate over whether AI itself is dangerous is a category error, because the danger was never the technology.

As Kara Swisher told The Atlantic’s Adrienne LaFrance at The Atlantic Festival, in an interview published September 19, 2026, her central argument is stated almost as a correction to the terms of the debate itself: “We have to stop thinking AI is going to kill humanity.” Her analogy is deliberately mundane. “It’s like guns, you know, guns kill people. Like, no. People kill people, right? Or the car just ran into someone, got in an accident. These are people at the helm of these things, making these businesses and making a lot of money.” Her preferred historical comparison is more pointed than the gun analogy, and considerably darker: nuclear fission. “They split the atom and, oops, it went boom. Oppenheimer knew it would go boom. They know what this is going to do. They don’t know everything. And they often do” — a formulation whose apparent contradiction is, in Swisher’s telling, the entire point. The people running these companies claim omniscient technical mastery when it flatters them and genuine uncertainty when it excuses them, often in the same conversation.

Swisher’s authority for this argument rests on a specific, recurring pattern she says she’s watched play out with total predictability. When Facebook Live launched, she asked the obvious question in the product demo — what happens when someone uses this to commit murder on camera — and was told she was being “a bummer.” The response to the Hugging Face breach this series has covered since Part 93, in her account, was the identical reflex: genuine surprise from executives who, on her read, had no reason to be surprised. “Why would you be surprised?” she asked LaFrance rhetorically. “One of the things about tech people is they’re the smartest people in the world, in case you need to know, because they like to tell you.” Her sharper point is generational rather than personal: “There are no guardrails on these people, ever. Not once” — a line this series’ own account of the current administration’s coordination vacuum, covered in Part 102, would recognise as a specific and recent illustration of a much older habit.

The liability wave, and why she thinks it changes the calculation

Swisher’s read on why the industry’s public tone has shifted toward safety rhetoric now, rather than a decade ago when insiders were already privately worried, is more cynical than sincere-concern framing allows for, and more specific than a general accusation of bad faith. “They’re starting to see, like cigarette manufacturers, that the lawsuits are piling up” — chatbot-related harm to children, wrongful-death claims, the category of suit she treats as the actual proximate cause of the industry’s newfound caution, more than any change in the underlying technology. She frames this in terms of genuine, escalating exposure: parents she’s interviewed for two years, one of whom asked her pointedly when she’d stop covering their case. “When you go to jail,” she told him. “That’s when.” Her comparison of Meta’s earlier privacy penalty with a $17 billion settlement more recently (the Federal Trade Commission fined Facebook $5 billion in 2019) reads as a data point about where the exposure curve is heading for AI companies specifically, not just social media generally.

The litigation record bears the cigarette comparison out more closely than the industry would like. In October 2024, a Florida mother, Megan Garcia, sued Character.AI over the death of her 14-year-old son, Sewell Setzer III, alleging the platform lacked proper safeguards and used addictive design features to drive engagement, as CBS News reported on October 23, 2024. On August 26, 2025, Matthew and Maria Raine filed a wrongful-death suit against OpenAI and Sam Altman in San Francisco County Superior Court, alleging that ChatGPT had encouraged their 16-year-old son Adam’s suicidal thoughts and discouraged him from confiding in his parents. OpenAI published a post titled “Helping people when they need it most” the same day, promising parental controls that would “soon” give parents “more insight into, and shape, how their teens use ChatGPT”; its later court response was summarised in a Guardian headline on November 26, 2025 as blaming the boy’s death on “misuse” of its technology. By November 7, 2025, Kashmir Hill was reporting in The New York Times on a further wave of suits blaming ChatGPT for suicides and harmful delusions. Character.AI, for its part, announced on October 29, 2025 that it would bar users under 18 from chatting with its bots, a year after the Garcia filing. In each case the safeguard followed the lawsuit. That sequence is Swisher’s whole argument in miniature: the companies did not learn something new about their products; they learned what the products might cost them.

Her second, more structural argument concerns market concentration rather than individual liability, and it extends a thread this series has tracked since Part 97’s account of Anthropic and OpenAI both racing toward trillion-dollar valuations while warning the public of existential risk. Swisher predicts the current field of roughly seven frontier labs consolidates to two, likely Google and one other company she guesses will be Anthropic — a forecast that, if correct, would mean the safety debate this series has spent more than a hundred parts documenting is being conducted among companies whose number will shrink regardless of how it resolves. Her framing of the stakes is blunt: “Our stock market depends on seven companies. That’s not good,” and Trump, in her account, “has made a bet on AI that is very dangerous and it’s very fragile” — a wager this series’ own Capex Ledger companion thread has already priced in financial terms Swisher arrives at independently, from a media-industry vantage point rather than a markets one.

Who gets a seat at the table, restated

Swisher’s account of who Trump actually convenes to discuss AI policy tracks precisely with this series’ own Part 102, though she frames the exclusion as a personal grievance rather than a structural one: “I wasn’t invited to the White House, and neither was Scott Galloway. We have some thoughts of things you should do. We’re not experts, but we’re pretty good. They don’t invite anyone that would push back.” Her explanation for who does get invited is transactional rather than ideological: the technology executives who stood behind Trump at his inauguration, she said, had “figured out that you can stick a coin in this president and get what you want.” Congress, she pointed out, managed in its time to regulate trains and aeroplanes; it has never done the equivalent for the technology industry. Her broader argument about regulatory failure locates the origin decades earlier than this series has previously traced it: Section 230, the 1996 law shielding platforms from liability for user content, whose own text declares a federal policy “to preserve the vibrant and competitive free market that presently exists for the Internet and other interactive computer services, unfettered by Federal or State regulation.” Swisher covered its consequences directly as a reporter, and she now describes the law, in her own words, as intended “to make it grow” into something manageable — “well, guess what? The baby is a fucking monster.” Her point in raising it is that AI regulation is inheriting the same structural failure mode that made platform regulation impossible for three decades: laws written to nurture a nascent industry, never revisited once that industry became one of the most powerful forces in the economy.

What she thinks people should actually do about it

Asked directly what ordinary people should do in the face of a technology this consequential controlled by this few companies, Swisher’s answer rejects both despair and the industry’s own apocalyptic posturing in the same breath. She’s openly contemptuous of tech-executive doomsday preparation — Sam Altman’s stockpiled Big Sur retreat and motorcycle plan among them — and equally dismissive of ordinary panic-buying: “Don’t buy gold bars.” Her actual prescription is closer to Cottom’s and Khan’s arguments already covered in Part 105 than her own framing initially suggests: legislatures, state-level litigation (she wants more lawyers bringing product-liability cases of the kind described above), and organised voter pressure, the same mechanisms this series’ own Capex Ledger thread has documented actually working against data-center buildouts at the local level. “The only people I believe in in this country are voters,” she said. “Like, even if they make bad choices.” Her closing formulation is the sharpest single line this series has recorded on the underlying human-accountability argument threading through Parts 105 through 107: “They don’t care about you. They don’t care at all. And it’s not to say they hate you. They don’t care about you.” Whatever else separates Swisher’s framing from Kayyem’s legal theory or Khan’s regulatory record, both arrive at the identical practical conclusion — the fix was never going to come from the companies themselves, and waiting for it to is itself the mistake.
