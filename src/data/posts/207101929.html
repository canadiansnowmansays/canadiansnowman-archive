Stephen Gordon July 24, 2026

Somewhere in an FBI evidence facility, there is a Honda Pilot SUV that has not moved since January. It belonged to Renee Good. For six months, it sat exactly where federal agents left it the day she died — untouched, unreleased, unavailable to the state agency that would need to examine it to conduct anything resembling an independent investigation into her death. As of this writing, its status is still not entirely clear even after the developments described below, and that ambiguity is itself worth sitting with before I get to what changed.

That car was never a minor detail in this story. It was the story, in miniature: a piece of physical evidence in the killing of an American citizen, sitting in federal custody, withheld from the state whose citizen she was, for months, with no public explanation beyond silence. In mid-July, some — not all — of that silence broke. I want to walk through exactly what happened, because the manner of the breakthrough tells you as much as the breakthrough itself.

It is still worth pausing on one detail that has not changed: as of this writing, the federal government has not publicly confirmed the identities of the officers who killed Alex Pretti. Alex Pretti was a nurse. He cared for veterans. He approached federal agents holding a phone, apparently to record them, and he was shot ten times in five seconds. His family does not know, officially, who pulled the trigger. Not who to hold accountable in a courtroom — who, as a basic matter of public record, was involved in the death of their son. That is not a technicality withheld for procedural reasons. It is the single most basic fact a governed public is owed after a government agent kills someone, and months into this story, it is still being withheld.

What Minnesota Sued For, and What It Got

Minnesota and the Hennepin County Attorney’s Office sued the federal government in federal court in Washington, D.C. — not a state court, not a local dispute, but a direct legal action against the Department of Justice and the Department of Homeland Security — to obtain evidence the state said it was owed and had been denied. The list was not exotic. The car. The physical evidence and full investigative record in Alex Pretti’s shooting. And evidence in a third case, the shooting of Julio Cesar Sosa-Celis, an immigrant an ICE agent shot and wounded in the leg after firing into his house following a chase.

In mid-July, some of that finally moved. Hennepin County Attorney Mary Moriarty announced that federal prosecutors — the U.S. Attorney’s office for Minnesota, under Daniel Rosen — had turned over previously withheld hard drives containing statements, police body camera video, and other materials in the Good and Pretti cases. “The wonderful thing now is we have all the evidence,” Moriarty said. Lawyers for Good’s family called it an important and meaningful step toward justice and accountability.

I want to be precise about what that statement does and does not establish, because precision is the whole discipline this series is built on. Moriarty’s office describes this as comprehensive. It does not itemize the car specifically, and no public accounting I have seen confirms the Honda Pilot has actually left federal custody. It is entirely possible it has. It is also entirely possible “all the evidence” means everything except the one physical object this piece opened with six months ago. I am not going to claim certainty I don’t have in either direction. What I can tell you is that even the most favorable reading of that news — full cooperation, car included — took six months, a federal lawsuit, and, as the next section explains, a trade.

The Trade

Court filings tell a less flattering story about why this happened now than DOJ’s silence would suggest a change of heart. The breakthrough did not arrive because the Justice Department reconsidered its position. It arrived because Minnesota had something DOJ wanted: the state’s own evidence in its prosecution of Christian Castro, the ICE agent charged by Hennepin County with assault and falsely reporting a crime in the Sosa-Celis shooting. “We are willing to share evidence with you if the exchange is reciprocal,” Minnesota Bureau of Criminal Apprehension Superintendent Drew Evans wrote to federal officials in a legal filing. Federal officials, in other words, did not open their files because six months of stonewalling had become untenable. They opened their files because the state finally had something to offer in return.

That is worth sitting with. This was not capitulation. It was a transaction, and transactions imply the withholding was never really about protecting an ongoing investigation in the first place — it was leverage, held until the moment it could be traded for something the department actually wanted. A government confident in the integrity of its own investigation does not need to barter its way into cooperating with a state examining a citizen’s death.

Nor is the story fully resolved even now. A lawyer for Pretti’s family, Steve Schleicher, said Rosen’s office would not confirm any actual cooperation agreement between state and federal authorities even as the documents changed hands. “No family should be required to beg federal authorities to do their job,” he said, adding that without a public commitment to cooperate, pursuing accountability for Alex’s death remains difficult if not impossible. Moriarty herself has not dropped Minnesota’s lawsuit against the federal government — she has said she is open to a resolution, but she was not, as of mid-July, prepared to treat the handover as the end of the matter. Neither, evidently, is she certain it is.

Houston Proves the Pattern Repeats

If there were any doubt that this breakthrough reflects a change in Justice Department behavior rather than a one-off trade specific to Minnesota’s leverage, Houston answers it. Harris County District Attorney Sean Teare said in mid-July that his office still did not know the identities of the ICE officers involved in Lorenzo Salgado Araujo’s death, or their whereabouts — nearly two weeks after the shooting at that point, which he called highly unusual for a case involving a death. As of this writing, that information is still not public. “The federal government has not invited us in,” Teare said. “The federal government is not collaborating with us with this investigation.”

That is the same withholding, the same posture, the same silence Minnesota spent six months fighting — playing out in a different state, on a different case, in the same stretch of days as the Minneapolis handover. Whatever changed did so for Minnesota specifically, because Minnesota specifically had something to trade. It did not change as a matter of departmental policy. Houston had nothing to trade, and Houston got nothing.

The Case That Already Shows How This Plays Out

If you want to see what happens when a state actually gets to test the federal government’s account against real evidence, look at Sosa-Celis — the very case whose evidence became Minnesota’s bargaining chip, and which has already run further than Good’s or Pretti’s.

An ICE agent named Christian Castro shot and wounded Sosa-Celis after a vehicle chase in Minnesota. Federal prosecutors moved fast in the aftermath — not against the agent, but against the men in Sosa-Celis’s own vehicle, charging Sosa-Celis and a man named Aljorna with aiding and abetting an assault on a federal officer and impeding him in his duties. A federal judge initially ordered them held for trial on that basis.

Then the case fell apart, and it fell apart in the government’s own hands. A month after the shooting, the same U.S. Attorney’s Office that had brought the charges asked the court to dismiss them with prejudice — the strongest form of dismissal, one that bars the government from ever refiling — writing that newly discovered evidence was materially inconsistent with what its own affidavit had alleged, and with testimony federal agents had given under oath at the preliminary hearing. A federal judge granted the dismissal. Separately, ICE’s own director confirmed that the agency had opened an investigation into whether two of the agents involved had made untruthful statements about the encounter, and whether they had lied under oath — conduct that would itself constitute a federal offense. Both agents were placed on leave.

Meanwhile, Hennepin County did what it has been trying and failing to do in the Good and Pretti cases: it filed state criminal charges against Castro directly, charging assault and falsely reporting a crime. It also filed charges, separately, against an ICE agent involved in an unrelated road-rage incident. These are the first cracks in the wall of federal impunity this series has been describing — proof that when a state prosecutor gets far enough into a case to act, action is possible. The obstacle has never been that these cases are unwinnable. The obstacle is getting far enough into them to find out — and, as Minnesota’s experience showed, sometimes having something the federal government wants badly enough to trade for.

What Cooperation Normally Looks Like

It is worth spelling out what the alternative to this looks like, because withholding is only recognizable as withholding once you know what the baseline is. In the 2020 killing of George Floyd — the case that, more than any other, shaped the modern template for investigating a law-enforcement death in Minneapolis — federal and state investigators examined the same evidence in parallel. There was no fight over the body-worn camera footage, no lawsuit required to obtain the autopsy materials, no standoff over who got to see the scene. The federal government did not treat access to evidence as leverage to be rationed out. Derek Chauvin was eventually prosecuted in both state and federal court, working from a shared evidentiary record that both governments had helped build together.

That is what normal multi-jurisdictional cooperation looks like when the federal government actually wants a case to succeed, regardless of who is bringing it. It looks like shared custody of evidence, joint access to scenes, and prosecutors on both sides working from the same set of facts — not a barter arrangement requiring a federal lawsuit and a reciprocal trade to produce even a partial handover, six months after the fact, in one state, while an identical standoff continues unresolved in another.

This evidence dispute is not just a discovery squabble. It sits directly on top of the deeper legal question this series will turn to next: whether a state can prosecute a federal agent at all, and under what theory a federal officer can claim immunity from state criminal law. Access to evidence and the underlying legal doctrine are not separate fights. They are the same fight, fought on two fronts simultaneously. Minnesota’s partial breakthrough means the Good and Pretti cases may finally be able to test that doctrine against real evidence, the way Sosa-Celis already has. Houston is nowhere close.

The next part of this series goes to that legal argument directly: supremacy-clause immunity, its closest historical precedent in the Ruby Ridge prosecutions of the 1990s, and the open question of whether federal judges who have grown newly skeptical of this Justice Department’s conduct might be more willing than their predecessors to let a state make its case.

A car does not need a legal theory to be returned. It needs a Justice Department willing to return it without being paid for the privilege. Six months, a lawsuit, and a trade got Minnesota most of the way there, maybe. Houston has none of those yet, and the department that moved an inch for one state, in that same stretch of days, stood perfectly still for another.
