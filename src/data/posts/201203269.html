The Hermit Kingdom Ascendant traces North Korea from its ancient origins to its emergence as a nuclear power. Part I established the peninsula’s brutal geopolitical logic. Part II examines the dynasty that chose walls over engagement — and what that choice ultimately cost.

Gyeongbokgung Palace in Seoul, the primary royal residence of the Joseon Dynasty. For five centuries, this was the heart of a kingdom that navigated great-power politics through careful diplomacy and cultural self-containment.

There is a word in the Korean diplomatic lexicon that has no clean English equivalent. Sadae — literally “serving the great” — was the organizing principle of Joseon foreign policy for five centuries. It meant acknowledging China’s centrality in the East Asian order, sending tribute missions to the imperial court, accepting the formal hierarchy in which the Son of Heaven in Beijing stood above all other rulers. It was, on its surface, an act of submission.

It was also, in practice, a survival strategy of remarkable sophistication.

The Joseon Dynasty, founded in 1392 and lasting until Japan extinguished it in 1910, understood something that smaller states have always had to understand: when you cannot match the power of your neighbours, you manage the relationship. You find the arrangement that preserves your autonomy within the hierarchy. You pay the tribute, perform the ceremonies, send the envoys — and in exchange, you are left largely alone to govern your own affairs.

For most of its five centuries, it worked. For most of its five centuries, Joseon was not a vassal colony but a sovereign kingdom that had simply made a calculated peace with the geopolitical reality of living next to China. The Ming court, which received the tribute missions, gave more in gifts than it received — and never seriously attempted to govern the Korean peninsula directly. The relationship was, as one contemporary described it, like lips and teeth: adjacent, interdependent, but distinct.

The trouble came, as it always does on this peninsula, from the other direction.

The Warlord’s Ambition

In 1590, the Japanese warlord Toyotomi Hideyoshi unified Japan after decades of civil war and set his sights on a target the archipelago could no longer contain: China itself. Korea stood in the way, and Hideyoshi sent the Joseon court an ultimatum — grant his armies free passage toward the Chinese border, or face invasion. Joseon, bound by treaty to China, refused. The armies would come, occupy the roads and ports, and never leave; that was not a risk the court could accept.

On May 23, 1592, Japanese forces numbering some 158,000 crossed from Tsushima, brushed aside Korea’s coastal defences, and landed at Busan. Within weeks Seoul and Pyongyang had both fallen and the royal court had fled north. What saved the kingdom, barely, was a combination of Chinese military intervention, the extraordinary naval campaigns of Admiral Yi Sun-sin — whose armoured “turtle ships” disrupted Japanese supply lines with devastating effectiveness — and the sheer grinding resistance of the Korean population. The war ground to a stalemate and ended in 1598 with Japan’s withdrawal; Hideyoshi died the same year, his continental ambitions unrealized.

The toll was civilizational rather than merely military. Over 500,000 combatants from three countries had fought across the peninsula. Up to 100,000 Korean civilians were taken to Japan, among them potters whose kidnapped expertise would go on to transform Japanese ceramics for generations — a measure of how completely the war drained Korea’s accumulated culture along with its cities and farmland.

The Joseon court drew the only lesson the experience seemed to support. The outside world was a source of devastation. It arrived with 158,000 soldiers and the intention of using your peninsula as a corridor to somewhere else. The appropriate response was a closed door.

The Manchu Blow

They barely had time to begin recovering before the second catastrophe struck.

By the 1620s, the Manchu peoples of northeastern China had consolidated power and were pressing against the borders of the Joseon kingdom, which remained culturally and diplomatically aligned with the now-weakening Ming. Joseon declined a Manchu alliance offer — in the court’s eyes, the Manchu were northern barbarians whose military success conferred no cultural legitimacy, while Ming remained the Confucian world as it was meant to be.

The Manchus invaded twice, in 1627 and 1636. The second invasion was decisive. The king fled to Namhansan Fortress and was encircled there, his navy unable to break through Manchu-controlled waters. After months of siege, Joseon capitulated. The terms required a ritual designed for maximum humiliation: the samjeon-gugo-du, in which the Korean king prostrated himself nine times before the Manchu Khan, pressing his forehead to frozen ground in acknowledgment that the old order had passed.

It was one of the most searing moments of national shame in Korean recorded history, and it burned all the more once the Manchus went on to conquer China outright, founding the Qing Dynasty in 1644. Joseon now found itself a tributary of a dynasty it privately regarded as illegitimate — barbarians ruling over what had been the centre of civilization.

Joseon’s response to this paradox is the most consequential idea in this essay. If the Manchu conquest had corrupted China’s claim to civilizational centrality, the court reasoned, then Korea had inherited it. The highest expressions of Confucian values, the most rigorous scholarship, the purest ritual — these now resided on the peninsula, not in Beijing. Korea was not a provincial outpost of someone else’s civilization. Korea was civilization, properly understood.

This idea — that Korea is uniquely itself, the authentic bearer of values the powers around it have compromised — is not an invention of Kim Il-sung. It is four centuries old. Juche, the North Korean ideology of self-reliance, has roots that run far deeper than a Cold War slogan. It is, in distorted and weaponized form, the same claim: we are more purely ourselves than anyone else. We do not need you. We are the standard, not the student.

Sejong and the Golden Age

King Sejong the Great (r. 1418–1450). The scholar-king who commissioned Hangul and oversaw Joseon’s cultural and scientific golden age — a high point before the wars that convinced later rulers to close the gates.

To understand what Joseon lost when the walls finally failed, you need to understand what Joseon was at its height.

The reign of King Sejong, who ruled from 1418 to 1450, is remembered as the Joseon golden age — and not without justification. Sejong was a scholar-king of rare seriousness, a man who understood that power was inseparable from knowledge and that a state whose ordinary people could not read was a state building on sand.

In September 1446, King Sejong proclaimed and published the Korean alphabet, Hangul — one of humanity’s greatest linguistic achievements. Before Hangul, Koreans wrote using Chinese characters, or hanja — a system that required years of intensive study to master and that effectively confined literacy to the aristocratic yangban class, who alone had the leisure and resources to memorize thousands of characters. The majority of the Korean population — farmers, merchants, women, anyone without the means to spend a decade in scholarly training — was functionally illiterate in any formal written system, despite speaking a language perfectly capable of being written. Hangul changed everything. Designed with such deliberate clarity that, in the words of the document explaining its creation, “a wise man can acquaint himself with them before the morning is over; a stupid man can learn them in the space of ten days,” Sejong’s new alphabet meant that people of all ages and classes could learn to read and write Korean in days rather than years.

The opposition Sejong faced was led by Choe Manri, a senior scholar-official who submitted a formal protest arguing that abandoning Chinese script would sever Korea from the prestigious literary tradition of the Confucian world and amounted, in his framing, to imitating the unlettered scripts of “barbarian” peoples like the Mongols and Jurchen. The more candid reading of Choe’s objection, and the one most historians now favour, is that the yangban class — having invested years acquiring a literacy that doubled as their claim to social status — recognized with some accuracy that an alphabet learnable in ten days would dissolve the educational barrier separating them from everyone beneath them. Sejong proceeded anyway, reportedly developing much of the alphabet’s structure personally and in considerable secrecy before unveiling it to his own scholars.

That argument — that literacy is never merely technical, that who can read and who cannot is a question of power before it is a question of pedagogy — was radical for its time and remains, when traced forward, the argument that Kim Jong-un inverted when he began executing people for watching South Korean dramas. The logic is the same: language, culture, and information are not incidental to political power. They are the substrate of it. Sejong built a tool to open access to Korean identity beyond a narrow aristocracy. Kim builds walls to restrict access to information beyond a narrow, state-approved channel. The insight — that controlling the written and spoken word is controlling the country — is identical. The direction in which that insight is applied could not be more opposed.

Early Joseon-era Hangul calligraphy. Sejong’s alphabet was a radical act of cultural independence, designed so that ordinary Koreans — not just the yangban elite — could read and write their own language.

Sejong’s reign extended this same instinct into nearly every domain of governance. He established the Hall of Worthies, a royal research institute that produced advances in astronomy — including a water clock and a rain gauge, among the first standardized rainfall-measurement instruments in the world — as well as medicine, cartography, and agricultural science. He commissioned farming manuals written in accessible language and distributed to local administrators specifically so that agricultural knowledge would not remain trapped in texts only the elite could read — the same logic, again, that produced Hangul. He reformed the tax system to account for harvest quality rather than imposing flat rates that fell hardest on the poor in lean years. He expanded the legal protections available to commoners and reportedly insisted that even slaves be permitted maternity leave, a policy startling for its era. He governed, in the assessment of one prominent Korean historian, as if the welfare of the common people was the actual point of kingship — a proposition that was contested even in his own court and is certainly not practiced by his ideological descendants in Pyongyang now.

The golden age did not last. Sejong’s successors lacked his particular combination of intellectual seriousness and political security, and within a generation the court had returned to the factional intrigue that would eventually consume the dynasty from within. But for the three decades of his reign, Joseon demonstrated something that the isolation of later centuries would make easy to forget: that a state turned toward its own people, rather than merely turned away from the outside world, was capable of a kind of strength that closed borders alone could never produce.

The Long Closing

After the double trauma of the Japanese and Manchu invasions, Joseon entered what its own historians describe as a two-hundred-year period of relative peace and gradual isolation. The isolation was never absolute, and it is worth being precise about what it actually restricted. Trade was not eliminated but tightly channelled — Chinese merchants conducted business at designated border towns, Japanese merchants were confined to a walled compound at Busan, and the tribute missions that sadae required continued without interruption throughout this period. Foreign missionaries, when they arrived outside these sanctioned channels, were expelled or executed. Koreans were forbidden to travel abroad except on the diplomatic missions to China or Japan that the state itself organized. This was a regime of controlled contact rather than total severance — Joseon never withdrew from the world so completely as the “hermit” label implies — but the control was severe enough, and the channels narrow enough, that the kingdom’s understanding of developments beyond its borders grew correspondingly thin.

This was not, it should be said, a policy of total intellectual stagnation. Joseon produced extraordinary scholarship during these years — philosophical texts, historical records, scientific treatises, literary works of lasting power. The isolation was political and commercial, not intellectual in the complete sense. But it created a brittleness, an unfamiliarity with what was happening in the wider world, that would prove catastrophic when the wider world came looking.

It also created something else, something the standard accounts of Joseon’s decline tend to underweight: a political class that had turned its considerable energies almost entirely inward. The yangban aristocracy — the Confucian scholar-officials who staffed the bureaucracy and dominated court life — had by the eighteenth century fractured into bitterly competing factional blocs whose infighting consumed the energies of government. These were not disagreements about policy in any substantive sense. They were dynastic and ideological vendettas, disputes about ritual propriety and philosophical interpretation, elaborate status competitions conducted through the machinery of the state.

The costs of this paralysis accumulated quietly while the court argued. While officials debated the correct mourning period for deceased queens, the military rotted. Tax collection systems collapsed under the weight of elite exemptions and systemic corruption. Local officials extorted peasants with impunity. The gap between the Confucian ideal of virtuous governance and the squalid reality of late Joseon administration became impossible to ignore.

This matters because the external walls failing is only half the Joseon story. The walls failed partly because the state behind them had hollowed out. A kingdom genuinely unified around its own survival might have modernized — selectively, carefully, on its own terms — the way Japan eventually did. Joseon could not, because the political class that would have had to manage that transformation was too consumed with its own factional wars to pursue anything as ambitious as national reform. The isolation preserved the facade of the Confucian order while the substance decayed.

The parallel with North Korea is uncomfortable and direct. The Kim regime has maintained its own version of the yangban class — the Pyongyang elite, the party faithful, the military officers — whose loyalty is purchased with privilege and whose corruption is tolerated as the price of political stability. The ordinary population pays the cost of that arrangement, as ordinary Koreans paid the cost of yangban corruption for centuries before them. The walls are real. The regime behind them is also, in ways Kim would never acknowledge, following a script that has been performed on this peninsula before.

By the nineteenth century, that world was very much looking. The Industrial Revolution had transformed European and American military capacity. The same Western powers that had forced China and Japan to open their ports were now turning their attention to the peninsula. Joseon did not yield to pressure applied in the form of naval attacks in 1866 by the French and 1871 by the Americans.

One of the anti-foreign steles erected during the regency of Heungseon Daewongun. They declared defiance against Western “barbarians” and embodied the final, desperate attempt to keep the hermit kingdom sealed.

The man behind this final, doomed resistance was the Regent Heungseon Daewongun, who governed in place of his child-king son. In the face of Western pressures to open its doors, Daewongun opted for isolationism to preserve Korea’s Confucian order against what he called “the base spirituality and the lack of morality of the Westerners.” From 1866 to 1871 he launched brutal persecutions against Korean Catholics in the bloodiest purge of the entire Joseon Kingdom, torturing and executing thousands of believers, including several foreign priests.

Daewongun was not stupid. He understood, with considerable clarity, what Western engagement had meant for China — forced concessions, unequal treaties, the humiliation of a civilization that had considered itself the world’s centre discovering that the world’s centre had moved. He wanted no part of it. He fought it with the only tools available to him: executions, closed ports, and defiant stelae erected across the country reading, in effect, those who do not fight against the Western barbarians are traitors.

It could not hold. In 1875, Japan dispatched a battleship to attack Korean island positions, demanding that Joseon open its doors to foreign trade missions. Ultimately, Joseon was forced to sign the highly unequal Ganghwa Treaty with Japan in 1876 under military threat. The hermit kingdom had been broken open. Korea soon became an arena for competition among the powers. Japanese influence became predominant, especially after the Japanese victories in wars with China in 1894–95 and Russia in 1904–05. Korean opposition to Japanese dominance grew, and in 1895 Japanese agents assassinated Queen Min, who was suspected of encouraging resistance.

The end, when it came, was swift. The last emperor, Sunjong, saw the 500-year kingdom disappear in disgrace in 1910. The whales had finally swallowed the shrimp.

What the Walls Could Not Do

Stone guardians at a Joseon royal tomb. While the yangban elite engaged in bitter factional infighting, the kingdom’s institutions gradually hollowed out, leaving it unprepared for the modern powers that would eventually force its doors open.

The Joseon experiment in isolation failed for a reason worth dwelling on, because the failure speaks directly to the North Korean present.

Joseon’s walls were built to preserve something real: a distinctive culture, a coherent social order, a Confucian vision of what human civilization should look like. The walls were not merely fearful or irrational. They reflected a genuine assessment of what contact with more powerful and predatory states had repeatedly delivered. The invasions were real. The humiliations were real. The desire to be left alone was entirely understandable.

But isolation is not stasis. While Joseon turned inward, Japan was undergoing the Meiji Restoration — deliberately absorbing Western technology, reorganizing its military on modern lines, building the industrial and naval capacity that would allow it to defeat both China and Russia in the space of a decade. The hermit kingdom, by the time Japan came for it seriously, was not in the same world. It was in a different century.

The lesson is not that isolation is always wrong. It is that isolation without development is not preservation but slow surrender. The walls kept the outside out. They also kept the inside in — sealed off from the transformations that would have allowed Joseon to defend itself when the moment came.

Kim Jong-un has studied this history. He knows how the Joseon story ends, and the conclusion he appears to have drawn is not that isolation itself was the mistake, but that Joseon’s isolation was incomplete — that the hermit kingdom didn’t wall off enough, didn’t build sufficient internal strength, didn’t acquire weapons that made it too costly to attack. He is building those weapons now.

Whether he has drawn the right conclusion from the right history is a question this series will spend many more essays examining. But the lineage is direct. The Joseon choice to close the gates, the Daewongun’s furious resistance to Western gunboats, the determination to preserve Korean identity against absorption by more powerful neighbours — these are not ancient history in Pyongyang. They are the curriculum.

What makes this more than an antiquarian observation is that the curriculum is still being applied, in real time, to choices Kim Jong-un is making now. Every border closure, every execution for consuming foreign media, every rejection of a diplomatic opening that might require concession — these are not improvised responses to twenty-first-century pressures. They are the same response Joseon gave to the nineteenth century, reissued with twenty-first-century instruments. The Daewongun closed the ports and executed believers because he had concluded, from direct experience, that the outside world arrived to take something. Kim closes the border and executes K-pop distributors because he has reached, by inheritance rather than independent discovery, the identical conclusion. The method is heredity, not coincidence.

The hermit was not born in 1948. The hermit has been there for five hundred years.
