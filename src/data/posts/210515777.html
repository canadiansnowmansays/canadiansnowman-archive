Stephen Gordon August 10, 2026

On Saturday, August 8, the secretary of Iran’s Supreme National Security Council published his government’s price for reopening the Strait of Hormuz.

Mohammad Bagher Zolghadr wanted the American naval blockade lifted. He wanted sanctions lifted on Iranian oil and petrochemical exports. He wanted Iran’s frozen assets released. He wanted the United States military withdrawn from the ring of bases around his country, and he wanted attacks on Iran’s allies in the region — Lebanon among them — to stop, along with the threats.

And he wanted reparations.

Within thirty-six hours Zolghadr no longer held the job. On Sunday, August 9, Tehran announced that he had resigned and would be replaced as secretary of the Supreme National Security Council by Mohsen Rezaei, who commanded the Revolutionary Guards through the Iran-Iraq War. We will come back to that.

As Steven Erlanger reported for the New York Times on August 9, in a piece that ought to be read slowly, every item on that list except the last one is already written into the fourteen-point memorandum of understanding that Donald Trump signed on June 17.

Not proposed. Not demanded. Signed.

One qualification matters, and I want to put it in early rather than bury it. The memorandum provided that sanctions would be fully lifted only upon a final agreement on Iran’s nuclear programme. So Tehran is asking for delivery slightly ahead of the schedule it agreed to. That is a real difference, and it is a difference of sequencing. It is not a difference of substance. Every item on the list is a commitment the United States has already made in writing.

I. What Five Months Bought

Set the two documents side by side and the war acquires a shape it has been resisting since February.

The memorandum was signed at the Palace of Versailles, in the room where the First World War was formally concluded, with what the president’s own account described as a flourish. Most of its terms were violated within weeks. By July 8 Trump had declared the thing over and called its authors scum. Then came thirteen consecutive nights of bombing, the strikes on Egypt and Iraq, Saudi Arabia’s entry as a belligerent, and two pauses taken to conserve interceptors.

The negotiated destination now on the table is that same document. The one signed in June. The one that failed. Plus a claim for damages caused by the five months of war fought in the interval between the signing and the return.

Iranian officials have told local media that the reparations demand is a bargaining chip meant to extract as much sanctions relief as possible, which is almost certainly true and does not make it less remarkable. Tehran has invoiced Washington for the cost of persuading Washington to honour Washington’s own signature.

Esfandyar Batmanghelidj of the Bourse & Bazaar Foundation makes the point that should be carved above the door of the State Department: Iran could have demanded a great deal more. It chose instead to make the June memorandum the basis for a permanent end to the war. What matters now is less any particular figure or concession than whether the United States can demonstrate that it is capable of making good on a commitment at all.

That is what this war has produced. Not a denuclearized Iran, not an open waterway, not a new government in Tehran. A credibility problem where an agreement used to be.

II. The Sword Over the President

Why hold the strait if the terms are already agreed?

Because, Batmanghelidj explains, Iranian officials expect that the moment the waterway opens, the American president will lose interest in everything else. His political problem is petrol at four dollars and ten cents a gallon. Solve that and the memorandum’s other provisions — the assets, the sanctions, the blockade, the withdrawal — become somebody else’s file.

So Tehran is holding the strait as what Batmanghelidj calls a commitment mechanism. Ali Vaez of the International Crisis Group puts it more vividly: the strait hangs over the president like the sword of Damocles, and Iran means to front-load its benefits without surrendering the one thing that guarantees delivery.

Here is the part that should trouble anyone reading in Washington. Sanam Vakil, who directs the Middle East and North Africa programme at Chatham House, notes that there are American officials who believe exactly the same thing — that once the strait is open, Trump would be tempted to walk away from the whole business and leave Iran’s highly enriched uranium buried where it is.

Both governments have independently concluded that the President of the United States will abandon his own war aims the moment his petrol prices come down. One of them has built a negotiating strategy on it.

III. The Map That Changes Nothing

Last week produced what looked like a breakthrough. Yeganeh Torbati and Sanam Mahoozi reported for the Times on August 5 that Iran’s foreign ministry spokesman had announced agreement on the geographic coordinates of a shipping route, with a joint Iranian-Omani statement in final drafting.

The accompanying map is the most eloquent document this war has generated. It shows the pre-war shipping lanes running down the middle of the strait, open to all comers under an international designation. Superimposed directly on top of them is the mine danger area.

Iran mined the traditional route. The traditional route is therefore unusable. New routes are therefore required. Iran is therefore drawing them, and taking the inbound channel — the one that hugs its own coastline, inside its own waters, precisely where the tankers it fired on in June were trying not to go.

There has been a great deal of discussion, this series included, about whether the arrangement will involve tolls. Iranian officials say there will be no tolls, only a service fee covering environmental impact, security and staffing, with revenue split evenly with Oman. An American official says that account is not accurate. It is a distinction worth roughly nothing, and Vakil says so directly: the strait is not about fees. It is about control, and about leverage over everything else in the package.

And then, on Sunday, Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi removed the last of the ambiguity. The Oman talks, he said, are progressing well and nearing completion, with experts working on the maps. But an agreement with Oman does not mean the strait reopens. That remains dependent on conditions conveyed through mediators.

On Monday, August 10, the foreign ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei removed even the diplomatic softening. The Oman talks, he said at a news conference, concern reaching an understanding on a route for the safe passage of shipping — and that issue is separate from the question of reopening the strait, which is contingent on the removal of the conditions imposed on Iran. It is up to the American side, he added, to stop and to compensate for its actions before anyone discusses next steps.

Vaez confirms the Iran-Oman negotiation is essentially finished. So the coordinates are settled, the cartographers have done their work, the joint statement is drafted — and the strait stays shut, because the map was never the thing being negotiated. Iran has completed the technical arrangement and is holding it hostage to the political one.

IV. Third in the Queue

The war began, according to the man who began it, to prevent Iran from acquiring a nuclear weapon.

Here is where that objective now sits in the queue. Iran will not open the strait until it receives economic relief. It will not discuss the broader settlement until the memorandum is restored. And any talks on the nuclear programme, Vaez says, are dependent on that restoration.

Relief, then the memorandum, then the bomb.

As Michael Crowley reported for the Times on August 4, the United States has meanwhile assessed that Mojtaba Khamenei — installed in March after his father was killed on the war’s opening day — is more interested in acquiring a weapon than his father ever was. Suzanne Maloney of Brookings notes that the war has strengthened Iran’s religious and military leadership and left no viable alternative centre of power. And the Revolutionary Guards, whose commanders now sit across the table from American negotiators, have claimed a more prominent role in government since the fighting started.

The war has produced a leadership more committed to a bomb, more powerful domestically, and third on the agenda of the negotiations meant to stop it.

V. Two Divided Governments

Adina Renner’s timeline for the Times, published August 6, plots every American and Iranian attack since April against every announced agreement. The result is a graphic that argues faster than prose: five grey markers for agreements, and strike columns resuming after every one of them.

Laura Wise of the University of Edinburgh, who studies these processes for a living, concedes that seesaws between fighting and calm are ordinary in peace negotiations. What is happening here, she says, is not ordinary — the volatility, and how public the whiplash has been, is outside the normal range. Her diagnosis of the cause is six words long and worth the whole essay: an American government focused on getting a deal rather than making peace.

Vakil supplies the machinery underneath. Iran is split between officials who see diplomacy as the only route to reconstruction and hardliners so sceptical and paranoid that they would rather keep their leverage than think about the economy, gambling that Trump will not restart the war. And the Iranians see Washington as equally divided — some pushing diplomacy, some pushing more bombing, and the president blowing one way and then the other.

That is the chart explained. Not one indecisive man, but two factional systems, each timing its moves to the other’s swings, each betting on the other’s incoherence. The columns and the grey dots are what that looks like drawn to scale.

And on Sunday one of those systems moved. As Yeganeh Torbati reported for the Times, Zolghadr — the man who had published Iran’s demands the day before — was replaced at the security council by Mohsen Rezaei, who led the Revolutionary Guards through the eight years of the Iran-Iraq War and has been a fixture at the top of Iranian politics for four decades. He had been serving as an adviser to the supreme leader. The post he now takes was last held by Ali Larijani, killed in an Israeli strike earlier this year.

Hamidreza Azizi, a Berlin-based analyst, reads the appointment two ways: as the old security establishment entrenching itself rather than yielding to a younger generation, and as a counterweight to Ghalibaf, who leads the negotiations with Washington and who has defended the June agreement against hardline attack. On that reading, the harder-line side of the system has just gained ground. Saeid Golkar of the University of Tennessee at Chattanooga is less certain the shift is ideological and more certain it is personal: both men have run for president, both intend to matter, and everybody wants to be a king.

Note what this means for the negotiations now underway. The council that agreed to the June memorandum has a new secretary. The man who set out the terms for reopening the strait was gone within two days of setting them out. And the officially unconfirmed account circulating in Tehran — denied by the supreme leader’s office and the president’s — has Khamenei restraining Pezeshkian, tiring of his resignation threats, and telling Araghchi he is not to involve himself in the negotiations at all.

Washington is trying to determine whether the American signature is worth anything. Tehran is rearranging the people who would be signing.

VI. Low-Keying It

The president’s own descriptions of this war, in order:

The ceasefire is on massive life support, he said on May 11. Ships of the world, start your engines, he wrote on June 15. They’re scum, he said at the NATO summit on July 8. Very friendly negotiations, he told a rally in Michigan on July 27. Locked and loaded, he told reporters on August 1, before calling the attack off for the future benefit of the world.

And to Axios on Sunday, August 9, expecting Iran to come around under economic pressure and issuing no new threat: “We are low-keying it.”

Six registers in ninety days, describing one relationship. Against which stands Ghalibaf, on July 9, saying that the strait would open only under Iranian arrangements and not American threats — a single position, stated once, and now, per the maps, essentially conceded.

VII. The Clock Nobody Is Mentioning

One question that no one in Washington or Tehran appears willing to raise in public.

The memorandum signed on June 17 provided for sixty days. Sixty days from June 17 is August 16 — six days from now. The no-charge provision in Article 5 ran on the same clock.

If Iran’s central demand is the restoration of that document, restoration of what, exactly? A sixty-day instrument that has spent fifty-four of its days being violated? Does the clock restart on signature, or has the thing quietly expired while both parties negotiated over its corpse?

Nobody has asked. It may be the most consequential unasked question of the war, and it will be answered by default, this week, whether or not anyone acknowledges it.

VIII. By the Numbers

  • 14 points in the June 17 memorandum. 13 of Iran’s current demands are already in it.

  • 60 days the memorandum was written to last; it expires on or about August 16.

  • 18 American service members killed; 653 wounded, across two Pentagon webpages.

  • 1,700+ Iranian civilians killed, per the Human Rights Activists News Agency, including at least 175 at a school on the war’s first day, most of them children — as Erica L. Green, Zolan Kanno-Youngs, Sanam Mahoozi and Yeganeh Torbati reported for the Times on August 7, American military officials have privately acknowledged that U.S. forces carried out that strike and called it an intelligence failure.

  • 26 Iranians executed in connection with the January protests since the president promised help was on its way, per Amnesty International.

  • 759–827 high-end Patriot interceptors remaining, from 2,330 before the war, per the fact-check by Linda Qiu for the Times on August 1.

  • $4.01 a gallon for petrol on Monday, August 10, down about ten cents in a week and still 35 percent above February — as Erica L. Green reported separately for the Times on August 4, prices last month hit their highest since Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022. Diesel: $5.30, up 41 percent.

  • $84–85 Brent crude on Monday, up 1.5 percent, and 16 percent above its pre-war level.

  • ~12 ships a day through the Strait of Hormuz, against a pre-war average of 130, per Kpler.

  • 74% of Americans who say the war has not been worth its cost, including about 43% of Republicans.

  • 0 of the war’s three original objectives achieved.

  • Narges Mohammadi remains in Evin Prison, held by the government the United States promised to help remove and then, in the same document now being restored, pledged not to interfere with.

IX.

Vakil’s verdict on where the diplomacy stands is that the memorandum is the floor from which everyone will now try to build a structure — and that we are nowhere near it.

The floor. The document signed in June, before the thirteen consecutive nights, before Dover, before Damietta, before the family on Qeshm Island, before two-thirds of the interceptors, is no longer the ceiling anyone is reaching for. It is the ground on which construction has not yet begun.

Batmanghelidj is right that the sticking point is no longer any term in the agreement. Both sides know the terms; the terms have been known since June. The sticking point is whether an American signature means anything, and that is not a question military force can answer. It is the one thing five months of bombing was structurally incapable of producing, and the one thing now standing between this war and its end.
