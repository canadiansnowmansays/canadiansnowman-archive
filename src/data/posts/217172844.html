The ban lasted six days. Trump announced it on Friday, September 18. Credentials stopped working Saturday morning. In the early hours of Thursday, September 24, Judge Timothy J. Kelly issued a temporary restraining order telling the White House to immediately return, reinstate, and restore the press credentials of CNN, MS NOW, and Politico. The order runs fourteen days while the underlying case continues — this is a pause, not a verdict — but for now, the thing this series has tracked since Part 15 has been undone by the same mechanism that undid its predecessor in 2018: a court finding that the White House cannot take press access away without giving the affected journalists a chance to contest it first. How the court got there, over one hearing and one overnight ruling, is worth walking through in full.

The Hearing

Wednesday’s hearing opened with the judge doing something unusual: he told both sides, before either had argued a word, what he had already learned the hard way. In 2018, presiding over Jim Acosta’s case, Kelly said he had been “surprised” to discover that legal precedent already established a First Amendment interest in a White House press pass, and Fifth Amendment due-process rights attached to revoking one. He was not describing an open question. He was describing settled law he had personally applied once before, to a case involving the same network and, functionally, the same fact pattern the administration was now defending a second time.

Theodore Boutrous, representing CNN, MS NOW, and Politico — the same lawyer who won both the Acosta and the Brian Karem cases during Trump’s first term — built his twenty minutes around a simple observation: Trump had spent nine days telling the public, on the record, that he banned the outlets because of their coverage. “He keeps proving our point,” Boutrous told the court. “He’s really our lead witness here.” Boutrous also raised a detail this series had not previously tracked: the letters the White House sent Tuesday, offering the outlets a chance to contest the ban by Friday, arrived with no letterhead, prompting him to question in open court whether they were authentic government documents at all.

Kelly did not sit passively through the government’s side either. He tested the administration’s national-security defence directly, asking Boutrous whether disclosing information the White House genuinely considered a security risk could justify pulling a credential. Boutrous’s answer went to the heart of Part 17’s argument: even a real national-security rationale, applied selectively against three outlets rather than as a content-neutral standard, would still amount to unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. Michael Velchik, the Justice Department’s senior counsel for the civil division and a first-term veteran of the White House counsel’s office, dismissed that reasoning as an untenable stretch of the First Amendment, arguing the outlets could not knowingly publish classified material and still expect privileged access to the grounds — the same privilege-not-right framing this series traced to Tuesday’s filing in Part 17. But when Velchik pointed to Tuesday’s letters as adequate notice, Kelly cut him off: “I don’t know how a letter that they got after the pass was revoked could provide them with the kind of — the standard that they’d have to conform their conduct to.” A judge interrupting government counsel mid-argument to restate the plaintiffs’ own due-process theory back to them, in his own words, is not neutral courtroom management.

Boutrous’s rebuttal then supplied the fact that may matter more than any constitutional argument made in the room. One of the specific stories the White House’s letters cited as grounds for banning Politico — a June report estimating an 80 percent chance of a preliminary Iran deal, attributed to a senior administration official — turned out to have originated on a press call the White House itself organised and authorised, one that the Times also attended and reported from under the identical sourcing. The administration was, in other words, citing as misconduct a story built from information its own press office distributed on the record to multiple outlets at once. If that detail holds up to scrutiny, it undercuts the national-security defence more thoroughly than any First Amendment doctrine could: it suggests the letters were not compiled to identify genuine security breaches at all, but assembled after the fact to dress a decision already made in the appearance of a factual basis. Boutrous made the same point in blunter terms, dismissing the Friday deadline as a bad-faith formality and arguing the administration would keep shifting its justification rather than defend the ban on its actual merits — a charge the White House’s own conduct, from “fake news” to national security to a call it organised itself, did little to rebut.

The Coalition

Outside the courtroom, the solidarity this series flagged in Part 16 widened further still. Fifty news organizations, including the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press, the White House Correspondents’ Association, and The New York Times, filed a joint amicus brief arguing that the Constitution leaves the choice of how to cover public officials to the press itself, not to the discretion of whichever administration is in office. The Times went further than signing a brief: it announced it would keep withholding White House pool photography from publication until the litigation was resolved, and by Wednesday The Washington Post had joined it, with NPR confirming separately that it had stopped using White House audio altogether. That is three of the country’s most prominent news organizations voluntarily declining to use material their own access still entitled them to, purely in protest of restrictions imposed on three of their rivals — a mirror image, in miniature, of the self-censorship this series has tracked at ABC and Media Matters, except deployed here in solidarity rather than under duress.

The most telling detail belongs to Fox News, whose relationship to this fight sits at an angle no other network shares. The current chair of the five-network pool consortium is Fox’s Washington bureau chief, Bryan Boughton — the man whose memo, quoted in Part 16, ended pool coverage in the first place. The current president of the White House Correspondents’ Association is Fox’s Jacqui Heinrich. Two of the loudest institutional voices demanding the ban’s reversal draw their paycheques from the network friendliest to the president in America, and Fox signed the five-network statement anyway, on the same historical logic Ari Fleischer had already supplied in Part 15: the pool defended Fox from a similar exclusion attempt by the Obama administration in 2009, and Fox’s own leadership appears to understand that the precedent it sets now is the precedent a future administration could use against it. Newsmax’s chief executive, Christopher Ruddy, made the same calculation from further out on the right, telling reporters the ban would set a bad precedent for every outlet, conservative ones included.

CNN’s own leadership treated the fight as bigger than one network. At a staff town hall Wednesday morning, before the hearing, CNN’s chief executive Mark Thompson told employees the network was not fighting alone, citing an unprompted call from Warner Bros. Discovery’s David Zaslav pledging the parent company’s full backing — a media conglomerate’s chief executive volunteering support for a First Amendment fight before being asked, in an environment where several of this series’ earlier parts have documented corporate owners doing the opposite.

The President, Meanwhile

Trump did not wait for a ruling to comment on the judge assigned to decide his case. In a Tuesday post, he described Kelly as a “great, for them, and totally disloyal Judge” — a public characterisation of a sitting federal judge’s expected disloyalty, delivered while that judge’s court was actively hearing the matter. He also kept running into the press he could not fully banish: at the United Nations on Tuesday, he encountered CNN’s Kaitlan Collins, credentialed by the U.N. itself rather than the White House pool, and told her plainly that she should not be covering him — a smaller, stranger echo of the helipad-to-Gracie-Mansion irony this series noted in Part 16, this time playing out at the one venue on earth where the White House press pool’s rules simply do not apply.

The Ruling

Kelly wrote that CNN, MS NOW, and Politico were likely to succeed on their due-process claim, because the credentials were revoked with no advance notice and no opportunity to respond before the fact. That is the argument this series flagged as the sharper of the two legal theories back in Part 16, and it is the one Kelly reached for again rather than the broader First Amendment viewpoint-discrimination claim Boutrous had also argued. The pattern matches 2018 almost exactly: in the Acosta case, Kelly went out of his way to say he had not determined that the First Amendment was violated, resolving the dispute on the narrower procedural ground instead. Eight years and a different administration’s target later, he did the same thing again, for the same reason — the government cannot survive a due-process challenge when it revokes something first and explains itself four days later, whatever the merits of the underlying explanation turn out to be.

Those merits did not fare well either. Kelly found the record lacked factual support for the claim that restoring the credentials would endanger national security — not a procedural dodge but a direct rejection of the substantive defence this series traced through Part 17. He dismantled the retrofitted “professionalism and decorum” standard in the Tuesday letters just as directly, writing that it “hardly does the trick” as a legal standard, and noting the cited stories were routine and stretched back months or years — a judicial way of saying the letters described ordinary journalism, not security breaches. Boutrous had told the court on Wednesday that the Supreme Court requires government justifications for restricting speech to be genuine rather than invented after the fact in response to litigation, and had gone further still in his rebuttal, dismissing the administration’s Tuesday letters as one in a series of manufactured due-process efforts rather than a real one — precisely the standard the Politico press-call revelation above suggests this administration failed to meet. Kelly reached for older authority too, citing a 1936 case describing the free press as one of the great interpreters standing between government and the people, language that sits comfortably beside everything this series has argued about what press restriction is actually designed to remove.

The order carried one more wrinkle this series had not anticipated: such orders, Kelly noted, are “generally unappealable.” That undercuts something Trump had already promised. In a Monday post naming Kelly directly and recalling the 2018 ruling, Trump wrote the outcome was coming “almost without question” and that, “as usual, we’ll go for appeal.” He predicted the loss correctly. He may not get the appeal — a temporary restraining order is treated differently from a preliminary injunction, which is the kind of order that normally opens a path to the D.C. Circuit. The administration tried a smaller version of the same move inside the ruling itself, asking Kelly to delay compliance by two days; he declined, and “immediately return, reinstate, and restore” took effect at once rather than on a schedule the government could have used to buy time.

The ruling landed at an almost comically inconvenient moment for the administration’s messaging: in the middle of a high-profile Trump-hosted summit with Chinese President Xi Jinping, pulling a story about a federal judge finding the president’s own press-access claims factually unsupported into a news cycle the White House had presumably hoped to control for entirely different reasons.

What Is Not Resolved

A fourteen-day order is a pause, not a verdict. The core First Amendment question — whether banning outlets specifically because of the content of their coverage amounts to unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination — remains open, exactly as it did after Acosta’s case concluded without ever reaching it. The likelier routes from here are the ones this series has already flagged: wait out the fourteen days and contest the eventual preliminary-injunction ruling, which is genuinely appealable, or take Kelly’s own reasoning as an unintended checklist — real advance notice, an articulated standard, a genuine chance to respond — and attempt the ban again with the missing paperwork supplied this time. Kelly’s opinion, by identifying precisely what was absent, has told the administration what a more careful version of the same ban would need to include. Whether the television pool resumes its coverage immediately, or waits to see if the restored credentials hold, was still unclear as the order came down; by Thursday morning the consortium had not yet issued updated guidance on when, or whether, filming would resume. The solidarity this series has tracked across Parts 16 through 18 does not automatically unwind the moment a court acts, and how quickly it does may say as much about the durability of that coalition as the ruling itself.

Boutrous, for the outlets, credited the court’s speed and called the result a vindication of press freedom, due process, and the rule of law. That framing is accurate as far as it goes. What it leaves out is the same thing this series has argued from the start: a due-process win restores the specific journalists this time, on this fact pattern, without settling whether a White House that follows the correct paperwork next time can achieve the same result lawfully. The mechanism this series named in its opening instalment — that press restriction is not the end in itself but the tool for removing whoever would otherwise hold the government accountable — has just lost one specific battle. It has not, on the evidence of Thursday’s order, lost the argument that it is entitled to keep fighting the same battle again, more carefully, later.

One more detail is worth flagging, even though its connection to the press ban is this series’ inference rather than anything reported outright: the same week as the ruling, the administration launched Trump TV, an in-house streaming operation providing live and recorded coverage of the president’s public events. No reporting connects the two developments directly, and it may simply be coincidental timing. But it fits a pattern this series has returned to since Part 16’s pool suspension: when the normal channels for reaching the public become contested or litigated, the response is not only to fight for control of those channels but to build a parallel one that answers to nobody’s press-credential rules at all. A due-process win restores three newsrooms’ access to the building. It does nothing to slow construction of a channel that was never going to need that access in the first place.
