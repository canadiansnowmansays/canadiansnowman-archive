⚖️ Serious, with one note of grace that I do not intend to apologise for. It is about the year the war began, about two lists of refugees, one kept by a young man in London and one kept by my own government in Ottawa, and about a scrapbook that sat in an attic for fifty years until somebody opened it. There is no wink. The dog stays out of it.

I count my essays, and every so often the count lands on a year that already belongs to someone. This series has met a great many lists on its way through the centuries: William’s Domesday Book, the census returns of every empire that wanted taxes, the casualty tables that were too long to finish. At 1,942 it will meet the worst list ever drawn up. But first, at essay 1,939, it meets the best one I know.

It was kept by a stockbroker. It contained six hundred and sixty-nine names. And the man who kept it never mentioned it to anybody for half a century.

A holiday, cancelled

In December 1938, Nicholas Winton was twenty-nine years old, a stockbroker in the City of London, and about to go skiing in Switzerland. Three months earlier, at Munich, Britain and France had agreed to let Hitler take the Sudetenland, the German-speaking borderlands of Czechoslovakia, in exchange for what Neville Chamberlain called peace for our time. Tens of thousands of refugees, many of them Jewish, many of them political opponents of the Nazis, had fled inward toward Prague, and were living in camps in the cold.

A friend working with the refugees asked Winton to come to Prague instead of the Alps. He went. What he saw there was a gap in the arithmetic. After the November pogrom in Germany, Britain had agreed to admit unaccompanied Jewish children from Germany and Austria, and the rescue that became known as the Kindertransport was already running. Nobody was organising it for the children of Czechoslovakia. So he did.

He set up an office in his hotel room in Prague, took the names and details of the families who came to him, and then went home to London and kept going. By day he went to work at the Stock Exchange. In the evenings and the hours around them, he ran a rescue. The British government’s conditions were exact, and this series has learned to respect an exact condition: every child needed a British family willing to take them in, and a guarantee of fifty pounds, a great deal of money in 1939, to cover the cost of sending the child home again when the trouble was over. Winton printed the children’s photographs to find families for them. He pestered the Home Office for the permits. He raised the money. In Prague, others did the dangerous half of the work, and he insisted for the rest of his life that they be named: Trevor Chadwick, who escorted children out under the eyes of the occupation, and Doreen Warriner, who was already working to get refugees out when Winton arrived, among others.

Eight trains

On 15 March 1939, the German army marched into what was left of Czechoslovakia, and Prague was an occupied city. The trains kept going anyway.

Between March and August of 1939, eight transports carried children out of Prague, across Germany, through the Netherlands, over the sea and into Liverpool Street Station in London, where foster families were waiting on the platform with the paperwork. In all, the trains carried six hundred and sixty-nine children. Most of them never saw their parents again.

I want to stay with that number for a moment, because it is not like the other numbers in this series. Almost every figure I have written down in two thousand essays has been an argument: how many died at Hastings, how many in the Black Death, how many in 1914, how many at Hiroshima, ranges and estimates and figures that somebody rounded. This one is not an estimate. It is a list of names, written down one at a time, each with a photograph, each with a family waiting at the other end. Six hundred and sixty-nine is not a range. It is a roll call.

The ninth train

There was meant to be a ninth.

It was scheduled to leave Prague on the first of September, 1939, with about two hundred and fifty children on board. That morning, Germany invaded Poland. The borders closed. The train never left the station.

Almost none of those children survived the war.

I will not dress that up, and I will not hurry past it. If the eight trains are the best count in this series, the ninth is one of the cruellest: a list that was complete, checked, signed and ready, and that ran out of time by a single day. Winton carried that train with him for the rest of his life. It is the reason, I think, that he never talked about the other eight.

The other list

Now the entry I owe my own country, because this series keeps an honest ledger, and the honest ledger of 1939 has a Canadian page.

On 13 May 1939, the German liner St. Louis sailed from Hamburg for Havana with 937 passengers, nearly all of them Jewish refugees holding landing permits for Cuba. When the ship arrived, the Cuban government refused to honour most of the permits. Twenty-nine passengers were allowed ashore. The rest were not. The ship’s captain, a German named Gustav Schröder, did everything a captain could to find them a harbour. The United States would not take them. Then it was Canada’s turn.

On 7 June, forty-one prominent citizens of Toronto, clergy and academics among them, petitioned Prime Minister Mackenzie King to offer the passengers sanctuary. Halifax was two days’ sailing away. The question went to Frederick Blair, the director of the Immigration Branch, who opposed it, and to the Minister of Justice, Ernest Lapointe, who opposed it strongly. Blair’s position was that the refugees did not qualify under the law, and that “the line must be drawn somewhere.” On 9 June, the government decided not to intervene.

The St. Louis sailed back to Europe and docked at Antwerp on 17 June. Britain, France, Belgium and the Netherlands divided the passengers among them. Those who went to Britain were safe. Within a year, Belgium, France and the Netherlands were under German occupation, and 254 of the passengers who had been sent there died in the Holocaust.

It got worse before it got better. Late in the war, an unnamed senior Canadian official was asked how many Jewish refugees Canada should admit once the fighting was over. His answer was three words long: “None is too many.” The historians Irving Abella and Harold Troper used it as the title of their history of Canada’s record, and the record bears it out. Between 1933 and 1945, Canada admitted about five thousand Jewish refugees, one of the worst records of any country in a position to help. In November 2018, the Prime Minister of Canada stood in the House of Commons and apologised for the St. Louis.

So here is the ledger of 1939, side by side. A stockbroker in London, working evenings, with no authority and no budget, counted to six hundred and sixty-nine. A government in Ottawa, with a whole country and two days’ sailing to spare, counted to none. I would like to tell you my country kept the better list. It did not.

The scrapbook

After the war Winton went on with his life. He married a Danish woman named Grete Gjelstrup in 1948, raised a family in Maidenhead, did charity work, and said nothing. He kept the records, though. He kept everything, in a scrapbook: the lists of the children’s names, their photographs, the letters from their parents. And he put the scrapbook in the attic.

In 1988, fifty years after the cancelled skiing holiday, Grete found it.

What followed was one of the most famous few minutes in the history of British television. In February 1988 the BBC programme That’s Life!, presented by Esther Rantzen, told the story on air, with Winton sitting in the studio audience, not knowing what was coming. Rantzen held up the scrapbook. She mentioned a girl named Vera, one of the children on his list, and then told him that Vera Gissing was sitting right beside him. He turned, and they embraced. In a later broadcast she asked whether anyone in the audience owed their life to Nicholas Winton, and if so, to stand. The people around him rose, row after row. He had been sitting among his list for the whole programme.

I have watched that clip more times than I would care to admit, and it undoes me every time. It is the moment in this series when the count stands up and looks back at the man who kept it. If the series has a man in a white hat, it is this one.

The count that grows

He was knighted in 2003. He died on the first of July 2015, at the age of one hundred and six.

And here is the last thing I want to enter, because it is the rarest kind of number this series ever gets to record. Every other count in these essays shrinks with time: the veterans die, the survivors die, the witnesses die, and the figure on the monument stays fixed while the living who remember it thin out. Winton’s count went the other way. The six hundred and sixty-nine children grew up and had children of their own, and grandchildren, and great-grandchildren. The usual estimate now is that some six thousand people are alive because of the trains that left Prague in 1939, and the number is still rising. It goes up every time one of them has a baby.

The children gave him a ring, engraved with a line from the Talmud: Whoever saves one life saves the entire world. I have spent two thousand essays proving that the count is never merely the count. I have never had better evidence than this.

The closer

So 1939 is the year two lists were kept. One was in a scrapbook in an attic in Maidenhead, six hundred and sixty-nine names long and growing. The other was a decision taken in Ottawa that the line must be drawn somewhere, and it was drawn at none. Between them ran a ninth train that never left the station. On the third of September Britain declared war on Germany. Canada waited a week and declared war on the tenth, on its own authority, for the first time in its history. The counting machines of the century were about to be put to work.

The count moves on, and I must tell you plainly where it is going, the way this series does before its gravest numbers. While some of those machines were being used to break codes and save lives, the other side of the war had counting machines too, punch-card tabulators of the kind that had been sorting census returns since the 1890s, and they were put to a different use: not to read messages, but to sort human beings, by ancestry, by religion, by address. The next landmark is essay 1,942, and it is the year a meeting of fifteen officials in a lakeside villa outside Berlin drew up the most terrible table of figures ever recorded, a country-by-country count of the people they intended to murder. I will write it the way this series writes the gravest entries, slowly, and I will not write it tired.

Nicholas Winton kept a list to save the people on it. At 1,942 we meet the list that was kept to do the opposite.

See you at 1,942.
