Stephen Leacock once observed that advertising is the science of arresting the human intelligence long enough to get money from it. He was writing about soap and patent medicine, but he would have recognised the method instantly in the foreign policy of the second Trump administration, where the product on offer is peace, the price is always negotiable, and the fine print is where the allies live.

Two long reports by Simon Shuster in The Atlantic — “Will Taiwan Just Give Up?” (September 8, 2026, in the October print issue) and “Trump’s Latest Appeal to Russia Could Outrage Even His Allies” (September 29, 2026) — were written weeks apart about opposite ends of the Eurasian landmass. Read together, they describe the same machine. In Eastern Europe, the administration has discovered that political prisoners can be exchanged for sanctions relief. In the Western Pacific, it has discovered that arms sales to a threatened democracy can be exchanged for a warmer reception in Beijing. In both places, the thing being traded is something America used to treat as a commitment rather than an inventory item.

Call it the Bargaining Chip Doctrine. It is not quite a strategy, and it is certainly not a principle, but it is the closest thing to a coherent pattern this series has found since it began asking whether Trump has a foreign policy at all.

The Potash Road to Moscow

Start with the Russian file, because it is newer and stranger.

According to Shuster’s September 29 report, John Coale — Trump’s former lawyer, his envoy to Belarus, and, as of September 28, 2026, his nominee for special presidential envoy for hostage affairs with ambassadorial rank — brought the president a proposal some months ago. The Kremlin would release political prisoners; Washington would reward each release by easing sanctions on the Russian economy. Trump approved it. The initiative, Shuster reports, is in its early stages and had not previously been made public.

The template is Belarus. Over more than a year of talks with Alexander Lukashenko, Coale has helped win the release of roughly 500 political prisoners, including a few Americans. In return, Washington has loosened its sanctions on the Lukashenko regime and pursued a deal to buy Belarusian potash, the country’s most valuable export. On September 22, 2026, Trump announced on Truth Social that a “massive” potash deal with Belarus was in the works, good news, he said, for American farmers and ranchers.

Here is the part the Truth Social post left out. Because the European Union has refused to ease its own sanctions on Belarus or to let Belarusian potash cross its territory, the fertiliser would most likely have to travel east through Russia before reaching world markets. That makes the Kremlin a party to the deal. Coale confirmed to Shuster that Russia is part of the negotiations. Since February 2022, the United States has avoided trade arrangements involving Russia; this would be a rare exception, and it would arrive by way of a side door marked “Belarus.”

Coale’s theory of the case is that commerce breeds normality, and that normality, step by step, breeds peace. It is not a stupid theory. It is also not a new one — it was the operating premise of Germany’s Russia policy for two decades, a policy that ended with the Nord Stream pipelines at the bottom of the Baltic and German leaders apologising for having believed it.

The dissident who inspired the plan, Nobel laureate Dmitry Muratov, made a subtler argument. At a May lunch in Washington, he presented Coale with a medallion Ronald Reagan had given Mikhail Gorbachev and reminded him that Reagan’s envoys routinely handed the Soviets lists of jailed dissidents and asked for their release. That was humanitarian diplomacy grafted onto arms control. Reagan did not, however, pay for each prisoner by lifting the grain embargo a notch. The Coale version keeps the lists and changes the currency.

The most devastating critique came from someone the policy had already helped. Svetlana Tikhanovskaya, leader of the Belarusian democratic opposition, whose own husband was freed in a Coale-negotiated release, told Shuster during the UN General Assembly that the arrangement merely teaches the regimes to take more prisoners. “This trade in human beings costs them nothing,” she said. The logic is brutal and correct: a government that can manufacture its bargaining chips by arresting a blogger has an infinite supply, while the sanctions it is trading them for took years of Western coordination to build and cannot be rebuilt on demand.

A Law Signed With One Hand

The timing is where the Bargaining Chip Doctrine starts to look less like improvisation and more like a man arguing with himself in public.

On September 18, 2026, Trump signed the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act, named for the South Carolina senator who died in July. As Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty reported, the bill passed the Senate 86–11 in August and the House 262–159 on September 16, giving the president sweeping authority to sanction Russia’s energy sector and to impose tariffs on countries that help Moscow evade existing restrictions. Four days later, at the UN General Assembly, Trump told an audience that included Ukrainian and Russian officials that he would use those powers if necessary.

In the same weeks, by Shuster’s account, his envoys were quietly searching for ways to ease the very sanctions the new law codifies. This is the familiar TACO pattern — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — running both halves simultaneously on the same file: the threat signed in the Oval Office, the accommodation drafted over sushi at a Washington members’ club.

The accommodation extends further. Jared Kushner, speaking by video to the Yalta European Strategy conference after his September Kremlin visit, described “prosperity agreements” as an essential part of the administration’s plan, the promise of investment as a kind of security guarantee. Ukrainian negotiators told Shuster they see it the other way round: every dollar of Western trade with Russia is a dollar for Russian missiles. Volodymyr Zelensky, at the UN, put it in the language of a man whose capital is being struck in daylight — more money for Moscow means more time for the war.

And the war is not slowing. On September 5, 2026, Putin told Witkoff and Kushner in the Kremlin that he had ordered a halt to strikes on Kyiv to allow their visit, and thanked them for their trust. That same morning, according to Shuster’s Ukrainian sources, a Russian drone struck the grounds of Boryspil airport, where the two Americans had been scheduled to land the next day. They took the train from Poland instead. (A U.S. official denied that the strike changed their plans.) Two days earlier, a drone had exploded inside the office of the director of Ukraine’s military intelligence agency. Officials in Kyiv told Shuster they do not expect Putin to negotiate seriously before spring; he has been stockpiling missiles for the winter.

Into this, the administration has now invited Putin to the G20 summit in December — at Trump’s golf club in Miami. Marco Rubio announced the invitation on September 23. Putin has not accepted. He does not need to. The invitation itself is the concession.

Taiwan, Priced to Move

Now turn the globe.

Shuster’s Taiwan report is built around a single uncomfortable idea: that Taiwan may be talked out of its own defence without a shot being fired, and that Washington is doing a good deal of the talking. The central character is Cheng Li-wun, chair of Taiwan’s Nationalist Party (the KMT), who returned from an April lunch with Xi Jinping — chicken and clam soup, the same dish served to Richard Nixon in 1972, blue ties all round in deference to the KMT’s party colour — convinced that Taiwan and China are one big family and that anything can be negotiated.

Her party’s control of Taiwan’s legislature has let it pare down President Lai Ching-te’s special defence budget, strip out the money for a domestic drone industry, and leave Taiwan’s nascent drone programme stranded in procedural limbo. Shuster visited Thunder Tiger, the most successful member of Taiwan’s national drone team, a company that makes its money on dental drills and remote-control toys. At its current capacity it would need two months to match a single day’s output at one Kyiv drone factory.

But the report’s sharpest material is about Washington. After his May summit in Beijing, Trump described U.S. arms sales to Taiwan as a “very good negotiating chip” for talks on trade, nuclear weapons, and artificial intelligence, and told reporters aboard Air Force One that the last thing America needed was a war 9,500 miles away. (Taiwan is closer to 7,800 miles from Washington, as more than one reader noted; Trump rounds up when it helps the argument.) The White House has since withheld the bulk of pending arms packages. A spring survey cited by Shuster found fewer than a quarter of Taiwanese voters regard the United States as a trustworthy ally.

What happened next confirmed the reporting almost to the week. Xi arrived at Joint Base Andrews on September 23, 2026, for his first Washington state visit in eleven years, and Trump — in a gesture without precedent — met him on the tarmac. In a pre-summit briefing for the Council on Foreign Relations, Rush Doshi noted that the honours extended to Xi exceeded anything Trump had received in Beijing, that Beijing had reportedly threatened to cancel the visit if new arms sales went forward, and that a Taiwan arms package had been delayed possibly until year’s end. Doshi’s larger point was that Trump’s unscripted remarks on Taiwan may matter as much as any formal change in policy.

On September 24, as Bloomberg reported, Xi used the White House summit to press Trump publicly, for the first time, to shift U.S. policy from “does not support” Taiwan independence to actively opposing it. Three days later, Ambassador David Perdue went on Fox News Sunday to insist that nothing had changed: Washington supports neither independence nor coercion. That is the correct formula. It would be more reassuring if the arms Taiwan has already paid for were on ships rather than on the table.

The Counter-Case, Fairly Stated

Shuster’s Taiwan piece drew an unusually fierce response, much of it from Taiwanese readers, and the criticism deserves a hearing here, because it complicates the “Taiwan will fold” narrative without rescuing Washington from blame.

The core objection is that the KMT chair is not Taiwan. Cheng won her post with the votes of roughly 65,000 party members in a nation of 23 million, and her party has not held the presidency since 2016. Lai, whose Democratic Progressive Party has won three consecutive presidential elections, spent September 2, 2026 — Armed Forces Day — telling officers that next year’s proposed defence budget, reported by Reuters as exceeding T$1 trillion for the first time, was proof of Taiwan’s determination to defend itself. His stated goal remains 5 per cent of GDP by 2030. On this reading, Taiwan’s problem is not a lack of will but a divided legislature and a patron that keeps changing the terms.

Both things can be true. Taiwan may have more backbone than the headline suggests, and it may still be rational for Taiwanese voters to hedge when the American president treats their weapons as poker chips. Deterrence is not a quantity of missiles; it is a belief held in Beijing about what Washington will do. That belief is shaped far more by what Trump says on Air Force One than by what the Taiwan Relations Act says in 1979.

One Doctrine, Two Theatres

What connects Minsk and Taipei is not geography but accounting.

In the Bargaining Chip Doctrine, every commitment is reclassified as an asset, and every asset is available for sale if the price is right. Sanctions are not a coalition’s response to aggression; they are a stock of concessions to be spent on prisoner releases and potash contracts. Arms sales are not a statutory obligation to a democracy under threat; they are a sweetener for the next summit. Alliances are not insurance; they are subscriptions, and subscribers who complain about the service can be dropped.

There is a certain honesty to this. Trump has never pretended that American values count for much in his foreign policy, and Shuster notes that even Taiwan’s defenders in Washington now seem faintly embarrassed to invoke them. The trouble is that the doctrine ignores the one thing every competent negotiator knows: a chip is only valuable if the other side believes you won’t give it away. Once Beijing learns that arms sales can be paused for a state dinner, it will ask for a pause before every state dinner. Once Minsk and Moscow learn that prisoners convert into sanctions relief, they will keep the cells full. Tikhanovskaya understood this. So does Xi, who has been perfecting the art of the patient ask since before Trump’s first term.

The doctrine also misreads its counterparts. Coale’s belief that trade produces normality assumes Putin wants normality. The drone at Boryspil on the morning of his ceasefire pledge suggests otherwise. Kushner’s belief that prosperity is a security guarantee assumes that Russia’s war is a problem of insufficient investment rather than imperial appetite. And the bet that Xi can be managed through personal warmth ignores that Xi’s most intimate gestures — the Nixon soup, the lowered armchair in Beijing — are themselves instruments of pressure. Both men are playing a longer game than the Miami G20.

There is one last irony, which Shuster’s Russia report saves for its final lines. Muratov, the dissident whose idea started all this, still lives in Moscow. The Kremlin has opened an investigation into his taxes. Under the doctrine he helped inspire, a Nobel laureate in a Russian cell would be the most valuable chip of all. Muratov told Shuster he accepts the risk if it frees those already imprisoned.

It is a braver position than anyone in Washington has taken. It is also the clearest illustration of what the Bargaining Chip Doctrine does to people: it turns them into currency, and it rewards whoever is willing to print more.

From up here in Toronto, the lesson is not abstract. Canada spent 2025 discovering that a trade relationship with Washington could be reclassified overnight as leverage. Taiwan and Ukraine are learning the same lesson with higher stakes. An American commitment used to be the thing you built a strategy around. Under this president, it is the thing that goes into the pot.
