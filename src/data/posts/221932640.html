At 3 a.m. Kyiv time on Tuesday, October 27, the guns around Oleshky fell silent. By Thursday, October 29, the ceasefire had held for 54 hours, and Ukraine’s human-rights ombudsman, Dmytro Lubinets, said “no violations of the ceasefire have been recorded,” as VOA reported. Sappers had cleared the routes into the town. Delegations from Ukraine, Russia and the United States gathered, and a convoy set out with food, water and medicine. Evacuations are due to begin on Friday, October 30, and the truce runs until late on Sunday, November 1.

Ukraine wants every one of the roughly 2,000 people left in Oleshky to get out alive, and it will take that outcome from whoever can deliver it. It is worth remembering, though, how long the town waited. Lubinets proposed local humanitarian ceasefires for Oleshky in May, July and September. The Kremlin rejected all three. It said yes only when the request came from the White House, eight days before the American midterms.

Zarina Zabrisky, the only foreign journalist based full-time in Kherson, across the river, wrote in The Atlantic this week about what comes with the corridor. Newsmax has “exclusive television access” to the delegation entering the town, and Melania Trump has called its crew “neutral observers.” Fox News, by Zabrisky’s sources’ account, will be allowed in too. Most Ukrainian and independent international reporters have been refused. The cameras will film Russian soldiers handing out food, under Russian escort, with Kremlin-supplied interpreters. “Whatever these journalists learn about life here almost certainly won’t be the truth,” Zabrisky wrote.

She quoted a woman named Liudmila, who got out this spring after four years under occupation. Russian troops poisoned her dog in the first days. Her husband, Andriy, died in 2025 after she pushed him to a hospital in a wheelbarrow and found it had no medicine and no fuel for its generator. Liudmila wrote to Zabrisky about the town she left: “Mines lie below; drones hover above. Death is everywhere: beneath your feet and over your head.”

And the way out runs through Russia. Residents who choose Ukraine will be taken first to the Russian resort city of Anapa, then flown on a military aircraft to Gomel, in Belarus, with a Ukrainian delegation as escort. To reach free Ukraine from occupied Ukraine, they must pass through the country that occupied them and the country that helped it invade.

Kyiv has agreed to stay quiet so that the operation works. It will let Moscow look like a rescuer for a week if that is the price of getting its people out.

Old Ho can’t turn that down

On April 7, 1965, Lyndon Johnson offered North Vietnam a vast development programme, a Mekong version of the Tennessee Valley Authority, if Hanoi would stop backing the insurgency in the South. On the helicopter home he put his hand on an aide’s knee and said of Ho Chi Minh, “Old Ho can’t turn that down.”

Ho turned it down. Thomas Wright of the Brookings Institution, who served on Joe Biden’s National Security Council, opened a piece in The Atlantic on October 23 with that story, because he thinks Donald Trump has made the same mistake with Vladimir Putin. Trump has spent nearly two years trying to persuade Putin that peace would pay. Putin, Wright argues, will only stop when he believes war will cost more than peace, and every concession tells him the opposite. Ukraine, in Wright’s phrase, is Putin’s “white whale.”

It is a useful lens for the week just past, which can be laid out as a ledger: what Washington offered Russia, and what it delivered to Ukraine.

What Washington offered Russia

Start with the money. On Tuesday, October 27, Anton Troianovski of The New York Times reported that the Treasury Department is assessing an application to lift sanctions on the Russian Direct Investment Fund, the Kremlin’s flagship state fund. The Biden administration sanctioned it days after the 2022 invasion and called it “a slush fund” for Putin, “emblematic of Russia’s broader kleptocracy.” A U.S. official confirmed the review to the Times, said it was meant to “improve U.S.-Russia relations and help end the war,” and described the fund’s chief, Kirill Dmitriev, as “a helpful envoy.”

Dmitriev is also the man running Trump’s diesel deal. Three tankers of Russian diesel sailed for the United States this month with what Russia’s deputy prime minister called “the significant support” of his fund. According to people familiar with the negotiations, the diesel was shipped before any American buyer had paid for it. One tanker, the Agioi Fanendes, left Primorsk on October 23 and is due on November 7. It has changed its listed destination from New York to “for orders,” meaning nobody has yet told it where to unload.

At a conference in Moscow on October 16, Dmitriev said the diesel deal was teaching Americans that “sanctions are, in principle, philosophically wrong.” He added: “The ice has finally broken.” In St Petersburg a week later, Steve Witkoff told Putin across the table, “It’s been really, really good dealing with Kirill,” and that “our Treasury Department is working with you now independently.”

Then there is the gas. On October 23, Troianovski and Eric Lipton reported in the Times that Polar LNG, a start-up selling itself with bald eagles as “America’s Arctic Advantage,” had received secret funding from Novatek, the sanctioned Russian gas giant. “The first 5 million were supposed to arrive by today,” Novatek’s deputy chairman texted in September. Novatek now says it wants a minority stake. Putin raised the Alaska plan with Witkoff and Jared Kushner last month. The Times found no sign that either envoy knew of the hidden Russian money, and the project is a long shot. But the door to it is open.

The Graham sanctions law names Novatek specifically. Trump waived its Russia sanctions earlier this month, saying he did not want to “drive, foolishly, Russia away from the bargaining table.”

Add it up for one week: possible relief for Putin’s investment fund, a diesel pipeline it manages, a sanctioned gas giant reaching for American Arctic gas, and a sanctions law set aside. Zolan Kanno-Youngs of the Times, who covers the White House, explained the logic on PBS’s Washington Week on October 23. Trump approaches the world asking, “What am I gaining?” Right now, Kanno-Youngs said, the answer is closeness to Putin, and deals.

What Washington delivered to Ukraine

On Sunday, October 19, Russia fired 16 ballistic missiles and more than 200 drones at Ukraine in several waves. They hit 28 substations and other energy facilities and left at least 550,000 households without power overnight, as the Kyiv Independent reported. Ukraine had no advance warning from its partners, Volodymyr Zelensky said, and it is critically short of the American Patriot interceptors that stop ballistic missiles, and some of those deliveries, he said, are held up by “political agreements that have not been implemented at the working level.” European countries have already paid for many of those missiles through NATO’s PURL scheme. Ukraine is still waiting for Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth to sign off on them. On Thursday, October 29, Euronews reported that NATO allies now doubt the interceptors will arrive before the American midterms on November 3. “He is using as much leverage as he has over Ukraine ahead of the midterms,” one NATO source said of Trump. Russia fired more than 100 ballistic missiles at Kyiv and the region around it in two weeks, Zelensky said. Nancy Youssef of The Atlantic put it plainly on Washington Week: the United States has not delivered the interceptors it promised.

Two nights later, on October 21, Witkoff sat with Putin in St Petersburg and called him “a humanitarian.” That evening, from about 6 p.m. into the early hours of October 22, Russia sent Iskander, Zircon and Oniks missiles and about 210 drones at Kyiv. One person was killed and 24 were injured, four of them children, according to the Kyiv Post. An ambulance station and the area around the South Bridge metro station were hit.

Then there are the satellites. Part VII described the week Ukraine’s drones went dark and nobody in Washington would say who had turned them off. Elon Musk has now said it himself. In a series of posts on X over the weekend of October 24, he admitted causing the outages. “Using Starlink, a civilian commercial service, for offensive drone strikes to kill people has never been allowed, nor should it be,” he wrote. “That loophole was closed last week.” And to those who want more military aid for Ukraine: “I have nothing but UTTER CONTEMPT for the virtue-signaling evil warmongers who push for endless slaughter and offer no realistic solution to the conflict. May you burn in hell.”

Simon Shuster and Isaac Stanley-Becker explained in The Atlantic on October 28 why this matters so much. Ukraine’s army runs on well over 250,000 Starlink terminals, most of them ordinary retail dishes bought in bulk by donors such as Poland and Germany. The military version, Starshield, which the Pentagon pays for and partly controls, accounts for less than one per cent of the terminals in the war zone. Starlink’s terms ban military use; SpaceX simply never enforced the ban. Now Musk has shown he can whenever he likes. “Without Starlink, the war would grind to a halt,” a senior American officer told them.

There is no replacement. Europe’s OneWeb network has a fraction of Starlink’s satellites and launches them on SpaceX rockets. Russia, meanwhile, is building its own constellation, called Rassvet, or “Sunrise.” “We have one very serious weak spot, and that’s our dependence on Starlink,” the drone developer Denys Shtilerman told the reporters. “Before that, America will have enormous leverage, the ability to blackmail us.”

On Wednesday, October 28, at a televised summit in Moscow, Putin offered his own view. “Elon Musk is a genius, really a genius,” he said. Russia, he added, is “in close touch with our American colleagues, including Mr. Musk.”

The ceasefire only one side kept

On October 11, Trump announced an “energy ceasefire.” Its practical purpose, as Washington saw it, was to stop Ukrainian drones hitting Russian oil refineries, which Trump blames for high diesel prices.

Zelensky took it seriously. He told reporters on October 27 that he had ordered a 72-hour pause on refinery strikes, on the condition that Russia ease its attacks on Ukraine’s grid. “I said, ‘Let’s respond calmly by striking other targets for at least 72 hours. We won’t touch the oil refineries,’” he recalled, as the Kyiv Independent reported. “That didn’t help. There was all kinds of rhetoric, and no one thanked us for those three days.”

Russia did not pause at all. The October 19 barrage came eight days into the “ceasefire.” Ukraine’s prime minister, Serhii Koretskyi, told the BBC’s Ukrainian service that Russia has made 2,147 attacks on Ukrainian energy infrastructure since July 16. “Not a single day has passed without attacks on energy infrastructure,” he said.

On Monday, October 26, the United Nations’ Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine said what Ukrainians already knew, as Nick Cumming-Bruce reported for the Times. Russia’s systematic bombardment of Ukraine’s power system is meant to make civilians suffer, and it amounts to a crime against humanity. The commanders knew the plants they hit kept people alive, the commission found, and the strikes grew heavier as the temperature fell. People froze to death in their homes and in hospitals last winter. Doctors treated frostbite and hypothermia. A Kyiv resident told the investigators he had fought to stay awake at night. “The scariest thing was to fall asleep and not to wake up,” he said. “It was like torture to be scared of dying from cold.”

In fairness, the commission noted that Ukraine has also struck energy sites in Belgorod and Crimea, which it could not examine because Russia gave it neither access nor answers. Its chairman, Erik Møse, warned that “as the cold season approaches, the attacks have intensified once again.”

So this is the energy ceasefire as it stands. Washington wanted Russia’s refineries protected and its diesel flowing, and Ukraine gave three days. Russia kept bombing Ukraine’s substations, and a U.N. panel has now named that a crime against humanity. And the only complaint Washington made was about the refineries.

The agency that did not get the memo

One part of the American government is still fighting hard for Ukraine. Shane Harris reported in The Atlantic on October 23 that the CIA’s operations there are, in a Baltic intelligence officer’s words, “much more aggressive” than under Biden, helping Ukraine find targets deep inside Russia. That success is now the problem. Ukraine hit a Russian refinery on average once every three days this year, by the International Energy Agency’s estimate, and Trump wants it to stop. If he orders intelligence support cut, people who have worked with the CIA director, John Ratcliffe, told Harris, Ratcliffe will carry it out without argument.

The table they are setting

On Tuesday, October 27, Finland’s president, Alexander Stubb, and Norway’s prime minister, Jonas Gahr Støre, telephoned Putin directly, following a meeting in Oslo the week before with Zelensky and Trump’s envoys. The call was scheduled for 20 minutes and ran for 90. Støre said much of what Putin told them was “familiar.”

The call split Europe, as The New York Times reported. Denmark’s prime minister, Mette Frederiksen, said she was “very skeptical about this Norwegian and Finnish initiative,” that it was “not coordinated with the rest of us,” and that she could not see “any signs that Russia wants peace.” Sweden’s Ulf Kristersson warned against talks “that risk dividing a united European voice.”

Youssef summed up Ukraine’s position on Washington Week: American support is unreliable, Europe cannot fully make up the difference, and Russia is as close to Washington as it has ever been without having conceded anything on Ukraine. The next round of talks is set for Abu Dhabi in early November. A table is being laid. Ukraine is trying to make sure it is sitting at it, and not on the menu.

Whose side?

Wright ends with advice that sounds almost quaint this week. Trump, he writes, must convince Putin “that war will be more costly than peace”: air defence for Ukraine’s winter, early warning of incoming missiles, a Europe more deeply involved. Most of his readers, judging by the comments, did not think Trump would do any of it.

The week’s ledger explains why. On Russia’s side: a sanctions review for Putin’s fund, Russian diesel shipped to America before anyone had paid for it, a sanctioned gas company reaching into Alaska, a waived sanctions law, and a presidential envoy who finds Putin humanitarian and his financier “really, really good” to deal with. On Ukraine’s side: interceptors stuck in paperwork, a missile attack on Kyiv the night of the salute, a Starlink owner who wishes Ukraine’s supporters in hell, and an energy ceasefire that bound only the side that kept it.

Ukraine is not asking America to fight its war. It is asking whether America still knows which side started it.

On Friday, the first people who want to leave Oleshky are due to set out, by way of Anapa and Gomel. Ukrainians will watch them on Newsmax, under Russian escort, and they will be grateful for every person who gets out. Then winter will come, and the strikes on the grid will come with it. And somewhere in Kyiv, a man who spent last winter afraid to fall asleep will be counting the days.
