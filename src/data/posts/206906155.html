By Stephen Gordon | July 14, 2026

I have spent fifteen parts of this series trying to find the exact moment where the absurdity of this trade relationship curdles into something worth taking seriously. This week, a member of the United States Congress did the work for me, in the bill’s own title.

Congresswoman Claudia Tenney of New York has introduced legislation directing the U.S. Trade Representative to open a Section 301 investigation into Canadian provincial liquor boards. She is calling it the Combating Attacks on our National Alcoholic Drinks by Allies Act. The acronym is CANADA. A bill named Canada, aimed at Canada, over Canadian shelf space. Somewhere, a congressional staffer is very proud of themselves, and they have earned it.

I. What Actually Started This

It’s worth restating the sequence, since Tenney’s own press release declines to. Beginning in early 2025, in direct response to Trump’s tariffs and his repeated musings about a 51st state, most Canadian provincial liquor boards pulled American beer, wine and spirits from their shelves. Not all of them: Alberta and Saskatchewan largely sell liquor through private retailers rather than provincial monopolies, so American products simply never came off shelves nobody controls the same way. Everywhere else — Ontario and Quebec very much included — the boards run the shelves, and the boards said no.

That’s the part missing from Tenney’s release. Her statement describes provinces engaging in “discriminatory treatment” and accuses them of trying to hold American producers hostage and ransom them, without once mentioning the tariffs or the annexation talk that prompted the boycott in the first place. This is obfuscation of a very specific kind: not denying the sequence of events, just declining to include it, and hoping the reader doesn’t ask which came first.

II. The Numbers, Once Again

This series has learned to distrust any trade argument that doesn’t come with a number attached, so here are the numbers. The Distilled Spirits Council of the United States puts the decline in American spirits exports to Canada at 63 per cent in 2025. Separately, American wine exports to Canada fell from $460 million to $103 million over the same period — a 78 per cent collapse, in a single year, for an industry that considers Canada its largest foreign market. Brown-Forman, which owns Jack Daniel’s, has reported a comparable hit to its own Canadian sales. Whatever else this dispute is, it is not imaginary money.

III. The Bridge Nobody’s Built Yet

Here is the detail that matters more than the acronym. Ontario Premier Doug Ford, asked directly last month whether he’d restock American alcohol, didn’t say never. He said once. “Once that deal’s done, I’m going to be sitting down and bringing all the booze back on shelves in Ontario.” That single sentence quietly converts a boycott born of anger into a bargaining chip aimed at a specific outcome: renewal of the very trade agreement this series has spent sixteen parts tracking. Ford isn’t holding a grudge. He’s holding a card, and he’s told Washington exactly what it costs to buy back.

Tenney’s bill, in other words, is trying to solve with a Section 301 investigation a problem Ford has already told the Americans how to solve with a signature. It’s a strange thing to watch Washington reach for the enforcement mechanism when the actual off-ramp has been offered, publicly, by name, for weeks.

IV. About the Messenger

Before getting to the argument itself, it’s worth asking who’s making it, and why now.

Tenney represents New York’s 24th district — Finger Lakes wine country among fourteen counties her own office calls one of the largest agricultural districts in the Northeast. That part isn’t manufactured. Her district has a real, sizable wine and spirits industry that has genuinely lost a major export market, and this isn’t the first narrowly targeted bill she’s built around it: she’s previously introduced the Fairness in Vineyard Data Act for the district’s grape growers, backed the MAPLE Act for its maple producers, and led a letter pushing USTR to prioritize Finger Lakes wine access abroad. The CANADA Act fits a pattern that predates this particular fight.

Here’s the part that complicates any easy “she’s just posturing for the midterms” read: her seat isn’t actually in danger. NY-24 is rated Solid Republican, she won her last general election by more than 31 points, and her primary was already decided in June. This isn’t a vulnerable incumbent flailing to survive a swing district in an election year — by the ordinary math of American politics, she didn’t need this bill to win in November.

So I’ll leave the two readings on the table rather than pick one for you. Is this a genuine, longstanding advocate for a hard-hit local industry doing exactly what a member of Congress from a wine district is supposed to do? Or is it a low-cost, high-visibility gesture — a bill with essentially no chance of becoming law on its own, that exists mainly to produce a press release, a WineAmerica quote, and a friendly headline back home, safe seat or not? And does it matter, if a district that genuinely needed the help is getting a bill that genuinely can’t help it very much? I don’t think the two readings are mutually exclusive, and I’d rather you sit with that tension than have me resolve it for you.

V. The Argument, Applied to Its Author

There’s a premise buried in Tenney’s bill worth dragging into the light, because once you see it, you can’t unsee it: the theory that a government declining to buy a product is itself an unfair trade practice, actionable under federal law. Not a tariff. Not a quota. Not a subsidy distorting the playing field. Just a customer — in this case a provincial liquor monopoly — choosing not to purchase. Section 301 exists to police discriminatory barriers to commerce. A government simply not buying something is usually just called a market, functioning normally, in a direction someone doesn’t like.

It’s worth asking what happens if you take that premise seriously and point it back at Washington, because Ottawa has spent this year running the exact same experiment at a much larger scale. Canada didn’t put a single American shipbuilder on the shortlist for its new submarines — not out of spite, but because the U.S. doesn’t build the type of boat Canada needs, and went with Germany instead. It picked Sweden’s GlobalEye over Boeing’s Wedgetail for airborne early warning. And the big one: Carney ordered a formal review of the $19-billion F-35 purchase from Lockheed Martin back in March 2025, citing, in almost exactly Tenney’s own vocabulary, concern about Canada’s over-reliance on the U.S. defence industry. That review is still open more than a year later, Canada is actively pursuing observer status in the UK-Japan-Italy fighter program as a hedge, and Washington’s own ambassador has warned it would force changes to NORAD if Canada doesn’t fully commit to the American jet.

By Tenney’s stated logic, every one of those decisions is discriminatory treatment of American producers by a foreign government, and arguably a much larger one in dollar terms than anything happening on a liquor shelf in Kingston. Nobody in Congress has introduced a bill about it, because everybody involved understands, correctly, that a sovereign buyer gets to choose its suppliers — including for reasons that have nothing to do with the product’s quality and everything to do with not wanting to be dependent on a country currently threatening your borders. The United States extends itself the identical courtesy every time a federal contract carries a Buy American preference, which is often, and always has been. What Tenney’s bill actually proposes is that this logic runs in one direction only: an entitlement to Canadian shelf space that Canada is not, by the same standard, entitled to expect at the Pentagon.

And this isn’t a policy Ottawa is being cagey about. Carney has said the quiet part out loud, repeatedly, in his own words: “our decades-long process of an ever-closer economic relationship with the United States is now over.” He’s called that closeness “a weakness that must be corrected,” put a number on the correction — doubling Canada’s non-U.S. trade to $600 billion within a decade — and told reporters outright that he’s pursuing a dozen new trade deals across four continents specifically to get there. This is not a government quietly hoping nobody notices it’s buying German submarines. It’s a government that has published the exit strategy, priced it, and put a date on it. Tenney’s bill treats Canadian diversification as an underhanded tactic to be investigated. Carney has been describing it, on the record, as the entire point.

VI. The Statute That Keeps Reappearing

Section 301 is becoming this series’ most reliable recurring character. It’s the same authority behind the forced-labour investigations into 60 countries this series covered back in Part XIV, the same authority now aimed at Turkey’s Gümüşay-adjacent trade file, and now the same authority a backbench New York congresswoman wants pointed at a liquor shelf in Kingston. It is, at this point, less a specific legal tool than a general-purpose lever Washington reaches for whenever a relationship needs pressure and nobody wants to do the paperwork on a new law. There’s also a detail almost too on-the-nose to include, except that it’s true: USMCA, the agreement Tenney’s bill claims to be protecting, already guarantees zero tariffs on alcohol between the three countries. The dispute her bill is trying to litigate was never actually a tariff problem. It’s a retail-shelf problem, dressed in trade-law language because that’s the only vocabulary Washington currently has for being annoyed at Canada.

VII. The Ledger

There’s a detail worth sitting with before the ledger closes, because it complicates the picture of Ontario as the disciplined party in all this. Ford’s other retaliatory measure — a 25 per cent surcharge on electricity exports to Michigan, New York and Minnesota, the one lever with genuine bite, since it touches American households directly rather than American shelf space — lasted exactly one day. It raised roughly $260,000, prompted Trump to threaten doubling steel and aluminum tariffs, and was suspended within twenty-four hours in exchange for what Ford called an “olive branch”: a meeting. It has stayed suspended, “on the table” by his own account, ever since.

That’s the tool that should have stayed on. Electricity is inelastic in a way liquor never was — Americans in Rochester and Detroit can’t simply choose a different supplier the way a shopper can choose a different whiskey. The liquor ban survives, sixteen months on, mostly because giving it up costs Ontario very little and costs American producers a great deal. The one measure that would have actually hurt — and that Washington visibly flinched at, within hours — was the one Ford folded on first. If this series has a policy opinion buried in it anywhere, it’s that one: Ontario had the lever that mattered, and put it down before anyone made it pick the leverage back up.

Sixteen parts in, the pattern holds with almost comic reliability. Ottawa is building submarines in Germany and airborne radar planes in Sweden, a genuine hedge against a partner it no longer fully trusts. Washington, meanwhile, is drafting legislation named after the country it’s targeting, over a dispute the two governments’ own trade agreement already resolves on paper. One side is quietly building an off-ramp. The other is naming its exit signs after the destination it says it wants to avoid.

Give Congresswoman Tenney this much: in a series about the gap between rhetoric and substance, she has produced the single most efficient piece of evidence yet. She didn’t need me to write the joke. She filed it with the House Clerk.
