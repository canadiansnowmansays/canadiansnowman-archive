A word to my regular Sunday readers before we begin. Sundays here usually belong to the Court of King Donald, and I try to keep the day as light as the news allows. The satire is still here today. But I owe you something closer to an apology for slipping this in beside the funny stuff. This week’s news left little room for jokes, and it couldn’t wait until Monday.

This essay is harsher than most I write. I thought about softening it and decided not to. Some weeks the record is harsh enough on its own, and the kindest thing a friend can do is read it back aloud. Read this one first, if you can bear it. The Court of King Donald will be waiting when you are done.

I am a Canadian, writing from Toronto. My country has stood beside the United States in nearly every fight that mattered for more than a century. More than a million Canadians claim Ukrainian ancestry. So when I ask the President of the United States whose side he is on, I am not asking as an enemy. I am asking as a neighbour who remembers what the answer used to be.

One week in October

Here is the week, in order.

On Friday, October 9, 2026, President Trump spent 90 minutes on the phone with Vladimir Putin, lifted American sanctions on Russian diesel, and then thanked him in public. “I want to thank President Putin, to be honest with you,” he told reporters, as Jonathan Chait noted in The Atlantic. “We have massive amounts of oil coming into our country, and it’s diesel, which is what we want. So, thank you.”

Over the weekend he declared an “energy ceasefire” that, in Washington’s own reading, protects Russia’s refineries from Ukrainian drones and nothing in Ukraine from Russian missiles. He called for Ukraine to get a new president.

On Monday and Tuesday nights, Russian missiles hit Kyiv with no early warning from Ukraine’s partners. The cause is still disputed; it may have been the speed of the missiles rather than anyone’s decision. On Wednesday, Russia fired 39 ballistic missiles at Ukraine, the largest such salvo of the war, and hit all three of Kyiv’s heat-and-power plants. The same day, the satellite network Ukraine’s army depends on was suddenly cut off for anything moving faster than 100 miles an hour, which includes the drones Ukraine uses to shoot down Russia’s. Elon Musk indicated that the decision lay with the President, and a Reuters report, cited by Brynn Tannehill in The Atlantic, said the order came from inside the White House. The President denies ordering it. As late as Saturday evening, Kyiv still could not confirm that full service had been restored, and said four days of questions to the Pentagon and SpaceX had gone unanswered, as Constant Méheut reported for the New York Times.

Late on Friday night, October 16, the White House released an executive order exempting Russia from the sanctions law Congress had passed in the name of the late Senator Lindsey Graham. The President had signed that law himself. In Moscow, Kirill Dmitriev, Putin’s economic envoy, called it “an important and truly historic moment,” as Alan Rappeport reported for the New York Times.

On Saturday, October 17, Anton Troianovski and Jim Tankersley of the New York Times reported that his envoys, Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, had urged Ukraine and Europe to consider restarting the pipelines that once carried Russian gas west. Putin’s economic envoy, Kirill Dmitriev, was delighted all over again. “The ice has finally broken,” he said.

And what did Russia give in return? Nothing. “Mr. Putin, in return, offered more war,” wrote Paul Sonne of the New York Times. Asked about a pause in the fighting, Russia’s foreign minister, Sergey Lavrov, said of Ukraine’s president: “I don’t think he deserves a respite.”

What the President did not say

Through all of this, one thing was missing.

Kim Barker, Maria Varenikova and Oleksandra Mykolyshyn of the New York Times spent an ordinary Wednesday in Kyiv this month. The air-raid alarm went on and off seven times. Six people died. One was Anatoliy Kavunenko, 66, a security guard who went to his early shift with a lunch his wife had packed. By morning she was standing in her pyjama trousers over a burned body. “My brain can’t accept that this black piece of coal is my Tolik,” she said.

The reporters noted, almost in passing, that the President has said nothing about what Russia is doing to Kyiv. Moscow is killing more Ukrainian civilians than at any time since the full-scale invasion began. He has found words of thanks for Putin, and words of complaint about Zelensky: “Zelensky is not a good guy for us to deal with,” he told Newsmax this week. For Antonina Kavunenko, he has found none.

The case for the defence

Fairness requires me to set out the other side, and there is one.

The White House says it is building “a comprehensive peace proposal that includes economic incentives for both Russia and Ukraine,” and that its aim is “lasting peace, economic recovery and greater stability across the region.” Administration officials argue that showing Putin a profitable future with the United States could coax him to the table, as Troianovski and Luke Broadwater reported for the New York Times. Americans are paying about $6.32 a gallon for diesel, and the President’s party faces the midterms in a fortnight.

It is also true that not everything has been cut. Julian E. Barnes, John Ismay and Edward Wong of the New York Times reported that intelligence sharing has, in some ways, deepened under Trump, and that from June 2025 American intelligence helped Ukraine aim its drones at Russian refineries, “with Mr. Trump’s permission.”

But the argument fails on its own terms. The incentives have been on offer since last year, and Putin has not moved an inch from his demands. Diesel was higher on Thursday than it was a week before. Most of the shortage comes from the war on Iran, not from Ukraine. Even the President’s own Treasury Secretary once understood the trick. At his confirmation hearing last year, Scott Bessent said the Biden administration’s Russia sanctions had not been tough enough, and explained why: “I believe that the previous administration was worried about raising U.S. energy prices during an election season. A peace plan in which one side receives sanctions relief, pipeline deals and a thank-you, while the other receives blackouts and a demand to change its president, is not a peace plan. It is a list of gifts.

It is not America

I want to be careful here, because the question in my title is addressed to one man, not to a country.

Americans have not changed sides. The Pew Research Center found in March that 83 per cent of Americans had an unfavourable view of Russia, and so did 83 per cent of Republicans. Twelve senators, six from each party, Mitch McConnell among them, wrote to the President that his diesel deal was “a gift to our adversary and a betrayal of our allies,” as Robert Jimison reported for the New York Times. “Putin is a tyrant,” they added. Ashley Hinson, the Republican Senate candidate in Iowa, said in a debate: “We need to lower diesel prices, but I don’t think we need to be making a deal with Russia to do it.” Meaghan Mobbs, a conservative whose father, Keith Kellogg, was Trump’s own envoy to Ukraine, called the policy “Obama 2.0,” which in today’s Washington is about the worst thing one Republican can say of another.

Even the woman who now holds Lindsey Graham’s seat spoke up. Senator Darline Graham, appointed in July to replace her late brother and campaigning for a full term with the President’s endorsement, told reporters on Saturday: “Personally, I would choose to put the pressure on Russia to end the war and get gas prices down. Because I don’t trust Putin, and I don’t trust Russia,” as Michael Gold reported for the New York Times. It was a gentle break, but not a small one; the President is due in South Carolina next week to rally for her. John Cornyn was blunter: “Lindsey would be appalled.” Thom Tillis called the exemption “beyond irresponsible,” and added: “If the President wants to actually bring down energy prices, he can do it by solving the Iran problem, not by doing a solid for Vladimir Putin.”

Illia Ponomarenko, the Ukrainian historian, told David Frum of The Atlantic that he tries to separate what this administration does from the American people, most of whom still support Ukraine. “It is something that is to be deeply appreciated,” he said. A man whose country is being bombed is still making the distinction. So will I.

When America was the good guys

Someone will reply to my title that America was never the good guys. I have read my history, and I will not pretend the record is spotless. But there were moments when the description fitted, and they are worth naming.

In 1948, when Stalin cut West Berlin off by land, the United States and Britain flew coal, flour and milk into the city for nearly a year rather than let two million people be starved into Moscow’s arms. In the same years, the Marshall Plan rebuilt the economies of countries that had just been at war, including enemies. America did not ask the Berliners to change their mayor. It did not thank Stalin for the blockade.

And Ukraine does not need a history book to remember. Days after Russian tanks crossed the border in February 2022, Kyiv began receiving American Javelin missiles to stop them, and Stingers to shoot down Russian aircraft. Then came howitzers and more than three million shells, HIMARS, Patriots, Abrams tanks. Between 2022 and early 2025, the United States sent Ukraine about $67 billion in weapons and the money to buy more.

That is the point. The question “what happened to America being the good guys?” is not a Canadian’s sentimental complaint. Ukraine knows exactly what American help looked like four years ago. It can see, precisely, what is missing now.

The answer, from Kyiv

Ukrainians have stopped waiting for the President to answer my question. They have answered it themselves.

Asked by Piers Morgan this week whether Ukraine was “losing” the United States, Volodymyr Zelensky said simply: “Yes.” On Friday, in Belgium, he was asked whether he was being made a scapegoat for Trump’s problems. The former comedian smiled sideways, let the silence run until the room laughed, and said: “This is my answer.” This morning, Ukrainian drones set a large Russian oil terminal in St. Petersburg ablaze.

“We will die because we won’t receive warnings about ballistic missile attacks,” the Ukrainian military analyst Oleh Katkov told Barker. “That means we won’t be able to get to shelters in time.”

And from Germany, which once bought more Russian gas than anyone and knows what that dependence cost, came the sentence I have not been able to shake. Robert Habeck, who was Germany’s economy minister when the war began and weaned his country off Russian gas, said of the idea of an American company owning a share of a Russian pipeline: “In the old days, I would be glad if an American company would be part of the ownership,” he said. “Now I’m afraid, because we see that Trump seems to have more sympathies with Vladimir Putin than he has with the Ukrainians.”

The question stands

Frum ended his conversation with Ponomarenko with a line I will borrow to end mine. “For those of us who, like you, believe in the role of the United States as the guarantor of the free world, and who believe that the phrase free world means something, this has been a shameful hour.”

So I will ask it once more, plainly, as a neighbour. Donald, whose side are you on?

Ukraine knows how it would like you to answer. So do 83 per cent of your own party’s voters, and twelve senators, and a woman in Kyiv standing over her husband’s body. The only person who seems unsure is you.
