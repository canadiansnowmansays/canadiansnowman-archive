by Stephen Gordon

Essay 1,769 is the happiest entry on the map, so I will declare the register at the top as this series does: after the fifteen minutes of the last essay, we have earned a morning of pure wonder, and history, obliging for once, has supplied one. Because on June 3, 1769, for the first time in the life of this species, humanity performed a single coordinated measurement. Not a nation. Not an academy. The species — dozens of expeditions, dispatched by rival empires in the middle of their rivalries, scattered from the Arctic to the South Seas, all pointed at the same small black dot crossing the face of the sun, all counting the same seconds, for the same reason: to compute the one number nobody on Earth possessed. The distance to the sun. The scale of everything.

Let me explain the problem first, because the elegance of it still lifts my morning two and a half centuries later.

By 1769, thanks to the gentlemen of essays 1,543 through 1,687, humanity knew the shape of the solar system perfectly and its size not at all. Kepler’s laws — audited and derived by the Principia — gave the proportions: if the Earth’s distance from the sun is called one, then Venus orbits at 0.72 of it, Mars at 1.52, Jupiter at 5.2, and so on, the whole machine drawn to scale. But a scale drawing without a scale bar is a map of nowhere. Every distance in the universe was expressed in a unit — the Earth-sun distance, the astronomical unit — whose actual length was unknown, and the estimates in circulation disagreed enormously. The cosmos, as of the mid-eighteenth century, was a perfectly ruled ledger denominated in a currency of unknown value. I have spent a career among balance sheets and I know of no condition more maddening: every entry correct, every proportion exact, and the unit itself undefined.

The fix had been proposed by Edmond Halley — yes, him again; the man financed the Principia in fish books, scheduled a comet from beyond the grave, and now returns for a third act, and I begin to suspect the eighteenth century was largely a Halley production. In a paper written decades before, Halley had shown that a transit of Venus — the rare occasion when Venus passes directly between Earth and sun, appearing as a small black disc crawling across the solar face — could be turned into a surveyor’s baseline for the cosmos. Observers stationed far apart on Earth would see Venus trace slightly different paths across the sun, and the tiny difference in the timing of the crossing, compared between stations, would yield by parallax the distance to Venus, and therefore, through Kepler’s proportions, the distance to everything. The method needed only clocks, telescopes, clear skies, and — here is the catch that gives this essay its heart — distance between the observers. The farther apart the stations, the better the number. Which meant the measurement could not be taken by any one nation, however grand. The baseline was the planet. The instrument, for the first time in history, was the species.

And here is the detail that must be savoured, given the date. The Seven Years’ War — the war of essay 1,759, the actual world war — had ended barely six years earlier; its sequel was already gathering; France and Britain were enemies by profession. And for the transit, they cooperated. Passports were issued for enemy scientists; navies were instructed — imperfectly, but instructed — that vessels on transit business were not lawful prizes; the French Academy and the Royal Society corresponded and coordinated their stations like colleagues, because a number was at stake that no amount of victory could supply. The transit before this one, in 1761, had even furnished the pattern’s tragicomic saint: the Frenchman Guillaume Le Gentil, who sailed for India, found his observing station in British hands when he arrived, missed the 1761 transit from a pitching deck, and then — this series bows — waited in the region eight years for the next one, built his observatory in Pondicherry, and on the morning of June 3, 1769, after a month of flawless skies, was clouded out entirely. He returned to France after eleven and a half years to find himself declared legally dead and his estate in probate. He is the patron martyr of everyone who has ever held a position too long waiting for the data, and I light this small candle to him from a desk that has done the same, and we move on, as he eventually, remarkably, cheerfully did.

Britain’s principal station was to be in the South Pacific, on an island lately reported by European ships: Tahiti. And the Royal Navy, asked to convey the astronomers, made a selection that this series must dwell on, because it is the finest personnel decision in these eighteen centuries of entries. They did not send a courtier or an admiral’s nephew. They sent a forty-year-old lieutenant named James Cook — a Yorkshire farm labourer’s son who had learned his trade hauling coal on the North Sea and had then, in the late war, charted the St. Lawrence approaches whose soundings put Wolfe’s boats safely under the cliffs of essay 1,759; the man who measured the river that carried the fifteen minutes, promoted now to measure the sky. Cook was that rarest of instruments, a person of complete accuracy — his charts of Newfoundland were used into the twentieth century — and the Admiralty, in effect, had learned the lesson this series keeps teaching from the other direction: when the number is everything, send the man who counts honestly, whatever his birth certificate says. The Endeavour sailed in August 1768 with Cook, the astronomer Charles Green, the young botanist Joseph Banks, and instructions in two envelopes — the transit first, and afterward a sealed packet directing him south to search for the rumoured continent, which is how the measurement of Venus became, incidentally, the European charting of New Zealand and the east coast of Australia, the survey smuggled inside the stopwatch.

At Tahiti they built a small fort on the shore of Matavai Bay — they named the spit of land Point Venus, and it bears the name today — and on June 3, 1769, under skies Le Gentil would have wept for, they observed the whole transit. The observation itself supplied this essay’s necessary imperfection, and I record it with affection: the timings of Cook, Green, and the others disagreed — by many seconds — because of an optical mischief called the black-drop effect, the little disc of Venus seeming to smear and cling to the sun’s edge at the critical moments, defeating the very precision the voyage existed for. Cook, characteristically, did not harmonize the numbers. He reported the disagreement — posted the discrepancy itself, which readers of this series will recognize as the Pigafetta move, the honest auditor’s signature. And when the world’s stations were combined — Tahiti, Hudson Bay, Lapland, Baja California, dozens more — the astronomers computed the astronomical unit to within roughly one percent of the modern value. The currency of the cosmos had been given its exchange rate, by committee, by the species, on a baseline the width of the Earth. Every distance you have ever heard — to the sun, to the stars, to the edge of the observable everything — descends from that afternoon’s stopwatches.

And now the second ledger, the one I promised at essay 1,522, two hundred and forty-seven years of table-waiting ago. That essay counted Magellan’s Pacific: the wormy biscuit, the rat at half a ducat, nineteen dead of scurvy on one crossing — and scurvy went on collecting its toll for two and a half centuries, the reliable tax on every long voyage, tens of thousands of seamen paid out to it, more fatal to navies than any enemy. Cook took the Endeavour and her successors across the same ocean, on voyages of three years — and lost to scurvy, essentially, no one. Not through genius; the science of the day was muddled, and Cook backed several remedies at once — the sauerkraut by the ton, the wort, the fresh greens gathered at every landfall — but through the discipline of a man who treated his crew’s diet as a set of accounts to be kept daily and enforced without exception, and who famously got sailors to eat their sauerkraut by serving it at the officers’ table until the lower deck demanded its share. The Royal Society gave him its Copley Medal not for Venus and not for the charts, but for the health of his men. Set the two ledgers side by side, because together they are the whole essay: in 1522, humanity could circle the planet but paid a fifth of a crew per ocean for the privilege; in 1769, a collier’s son crossed the same waters counting cabbage rations, and the tax was retired. The sea did not become kinder. The counting became continuous. That is the entire difference, and it generalizes further than I can politely insist on: most of the mortal hazards this series has covered were never defeated by heroism. They were defeated by someone keeping a boring ledger every single day and refusing to stop.

Dior participated in this essay by way of demonstration, as the veterans will expect. The transit method, I explained on the lakeshore, requires two observers, widely separated, timing one event. We ran the experiment: a single squirrel, crossing the path ahead — I timed its transit from bench to oak at my end; Dior, sixty leash-lengths of baseline away in spirit if not in fact, timed it by departing at maximum acceleration, which is her form of a stopwatch. Our observations disagreed, as Cook’s and Green’s did. I recorded four seconds. She recorded, by her account, an outrage of infinite duration that justified everything that followed. We have combined our results in the manner of the astronomers and computed the distance to the squirrel, which was, at the moment of measurement, increasing. Science advances observer by observer, and some observers are simply built closer to the phenomenon.

The count moves on, as it does, and I will be honest with you about what is coming, because this series does not ambush its readers in either direction. The next landmark is essay 1,776, and you have all been able to see it approaching for eighteen hundred numbers, sitting on the horizon of this project like weather. The species that cooperated across its wars to measure the sky in 1769 will spend the next two entries on the other thing it does: declaring, dividing, and deciding what governments are for. I have been composing my approach to that number for as long as this series has existed, and I live beside its loudest current consequence, and I will write it when the count arrives and not before.

One species, one baseline, one number, brought home by a man who counted cabbage. Some years redeem the whole enterprise. This is one, and I am glad the count stopped here.

See you at 1,776. You knew it was coming. So did I.
