Five days is not a long time, even by the standards of this series. In five days, President Trump threatened to shelve his own attorney general nominee, reversed himself completely, revived the very fund that had caused the standoff in the first place, watched his nominee post a midnight settlement on social media, and cleared the Senate committee that had been blocking him — all without the thing at the center of the dispute, the audit immunity itself, changing in any way that matters. If there is a single sentence that captures how this fight has gone so far, it is one the president said himself, almost by accident: "This was me."

I. The Threat

As Michael Gold reported for the Times, the week opened on July 30 with Mr. Trump floating the idea of temporarily withdrawing Todd Blanche's nomination rather than narrow the I.R.S. audit protection two Republican holdouts, Senators John Cornyn of Texas and Thom Tillis of North Carolina, were demanding. His stated plan was to leave Mr. Blanche running the Justice Department in an acting capacity indefinitely, then renominate him once the two senators — both lame ducks, having declined to seek re-election after Mr. Trump backed primary challengers against them — were gone from the Senate. It was a threat that treated the confirmation process itself as an inconvenience to be waited out rather than a check to be satisfied. Mr. Cornyn was quick to note the math cut against the White House as much as for it: Republicans are likely to hold a smaller Senate majority next year, not a larger one, which meant waiting out the holdouts carried its own risk

The underlying order at issue dates to May 19, when Mr. Blanche directed the I.R.S. to drop any pending or potential inquiries into Mr. Trump, his family, and an unspecified list of “affiliated individuals,” and to open no new investigations into tax matters already covered. No one at the I.R.S. signed that order. A federal judge in Miami, Kathleen M. Williams, later found in a 56-page ruling that it “directly contravenes” the statute barring political direction of I.R.S. audits, though she confined that finding to the underlying lawsuit and explicitly declined to rule on the order’s legality more broadly.

II. The Reversal, and the Admission

That posture didn’t survive the weekend. By Friday, Mr. Trump had reversed course entirely, demanding the Senate “immediately” confirm the man he’d been prepared to leave in limbo a day earlier — with no indication that the underlying dispute had been resolved. Then, at a cabinet meeting at Camp David, he undid even that reversal, telling reporters the anti-weaponization fund — the $1.8 billion pool meant to compensate people who claim they were unfairly prosecuted, potentially including participants in the January 6, 2021 Capitol riot — was dead, “but, you know, I wish it weren’t, to be honest with you.” He went further on social media, defending the fund on the merits and crediting his 2024 election win as evidence “millions of people agree with me.”

Buried in the same stretch of statements was the one unguarded sentence worth keeping. Discussing the fund, Mr. Trump said: “This has nothing to do with Todd. This was me.” This series has spent nine parts documenting an administration that reliably declines to locate responsibility in the person actually exercising it. An unprompted admission that the obstruction to his own nominee’s confirmation traced back to the president himself — not Mr. Blanche, not the two holdouts — is about as close to an unscripted truth as this fight produced.

By Saturday, Mr. Trump had gone further still, saying that if the Senate failed to confirm Mr. Blanche, the fund would go “back on the table” and he would “get it done” regardless. Senator Tillis read that correctly as confirmation the fund was never actually dead, writing that the president “clearly intends to resurrect the payout pot for punks either by inappropriately establishing another bogus fund or pushing Congress” to pass one by statute.

III. What Sunday Night Actually Bought

As Michael Gold, Andrew Duehren and Hari Raj reported for the Times, Mr. Blanche broke the standoff just before midnight on Sunday, posting two documents on social media: a signed order formally rescinding the May 18 fund, and an unsigned statement narrowing the scope of the I.R.S. protection to Mr. Trump, two of his sons, and the Trump Organization, and clarifying that the immunity applies only to past tax returns, not future ones. Mr. Cornyn and Mr. Tillis announced Monday morning that they were satisfied, calling it a "binding written order" and confirming they'd allow the nomination to advance.

It’s worth reading exactly what those documents say rather than what either side is calling them. The order rescinding the fund states plainly that it “shall have no force or effect,” and that no members were ever appointed and no funds transferred — meaning what was killed was, by the department’s own account, a fund that had never actually operated. The second document does not rescind the May 19 immunity order at all. It restates that the order “applies by its terms only retroactively” and only to the named parties in the underlying lawsuit — which is to say, it clarifies the scope of the shield without removing it. The audit immunity that a New York Times legal analysis previously estimated could be worth over $100 million to the Trump family remains in place. What changed is that it now covers a defined set of people instead of the broader, undefined “affiliated individuals” language in the original order — a real concession, but a narrower one than “we killed the fund” made it sound.

Whether the rescission itself is even properly executed is a live legal question, not just a hedge. As Devlin Barrett reported for the Times, the original settlement's own terms state that it 'may be modified only with the written agreement of the parties' — meaning Mr. Trump personally, his sons Eric and Donald Jr., the Trump Organization, and the named government agencies. Sunday's order carries only Mr. Blanche's signature. Matthew Platkin, the former New Jersey attorney general now representing plaintiffs suing over the fund, put the implication bluntly: 'So absent any, a new executed agreement that they enter into, all of this is meaningless as a matter of law.' Senator Adam Schiff made the same point from the other direction, warning in a statement that nothing in Mr. Blanche's documents stops the administration 'from bringing the slush fund back from the dead next week,' and that the tax-immunity language 'still leaves it vague who and what is covered.' One federal judge overseeing a related suit, Leonie M. Brinkema, has separately demanded a sworn declaration from the acting attorney general before she'll treat any of this as settled — a signal that a memo posted to social media is not, in at least one courtroom, being read as the last word.

There is also a timing detail worth flagging on its own terms. Mr. Blanche’s Sunday statement says the department “always advised Congress” that the fund was not moving forward, including under oath at his confirmation hearing — a claim consistent with what he told Mr. Cornyn in person weeks earlier. If that was already his position, then the practical difference Sunday night’s order made was not informing Congress of something new, but converting a verbal assurance into a piece of paper, at the exact moment the president’s own Saturday comments had made that paper politically necessary.

IV. The Loyalty Ledger

None of this happened in a vacuum of trust. As Glenn Thrush reported for the Times, Mr. Blanche's entire rise has been built on an audience-of-one approach to Mr. Trump — telling him, when asked how he'd respond to being fired, "I love you, sir," and taking on a damage-control role during the Epstein files release. Since replacing Pam Bondi, he has approved the indictment of James Comey over a seashell photograph read as a jab at the president, and pursued actions against former C.I.A. director John Brennan and former White House aide Cassidy Hutchinson — each tied to a personal grievance of the president's rather than an independent prosecutorial priority. He has also maintained that he didn't personally negotiate the I.R.S. settlement's terms, which were drafted by a subordinate working alongside the president's private lawyer, while acknowledging internal concerns about the deal before signing off on it as legally sound.

That history is the backdrop against which Mr. Cornyn’s and Mr. Tillis’s celebration should be read. As Mr. Cornyn put it earlier in the fight, grinning, about why the president wanted the shield in the first place: it “provides immunity from audits that no other taxpayer could possibly get.” The two senators held the line for weeks and extracted a real, if narrower, written concession. What they are about to hand permanent power to is the same official who built the arrangement they were fighting, on the theory that his written word, this time, will hold — against a president who told the country, days earlier, that reviving the very thing they thought they’d killed was entirely his call to make.

V. The President Who Didn’t Sign Off

If the concession was supposed to settle the matter, nobody told Mr. Trump. Asked about it Monday in the Oval Office, hours before the committee vote, the president said plainly that he hadn’t reviewed the documents his own nominee had just used to secure his confirmation: “I didn’t look at it. All I know is that Todd Blanche is great.” Pressed further, he added, “I understand there’s been a sign-off. But I don’t know what they agreed. I think Todd Blanche agreed to reconfirm things that he already said, and what he said was OK.” In the same breath, he reiterated support for the fund itself — the exact position that had triggered Mr. Cornyn’s and Mr. Tillis’s objections in the first place.

That is not a president ratifying a deal. It is a president declining to either endorse or disavow one, while leaving the door open to the position that caused the standoff to begin with. Combined with the paragraph-8 question above, it means the two senators voted to confirm Mr. Blanche on the strength of a written commitment that the one man with the actual authority to honor or ignore it has pointedly declined to say he agrees with.

VI. Committee, Not Confirmation — and Cornyn's Own Doubts

As Glenn Thrush and Michael Gold reported for the Times, the Judiciary Committee voted Tuesday, 12 to 10 along party lines, to advance Mr. Blanche’s nomination — a committee vote, not the floor vote that actually confirms him. That floor vote is expected as early as this week, and Mr. Blanche is expected to be confirmed, though several Republicans have yet to commit publicly. That gives this installment of the series a milestone the earlier drafts didn’t have, but not yet the ending: the audit shield survived every version of this fight intact, the fund’s rescission may not be legally enforceable against the one party who never signed it, and the president who caused the standoff spent the hours before the vote declining to say the deal that ended it was one he actually agreed to.

What makes the committee vote more revealing than a simple party-line tally is what Mr. Cornyn himself said about it. He acknowledged on the record that the written assurances he’d extracted “could be reversed” by Mr. Trump — the same vulnerability this piece has been tracing through the paragraph-8 signature question — and then asked Mr. Blanche, in essence, to be better than the deal guaranteed: to “capitalize on that earned trust” to tell the president “hard truths,” even if ignored. A senator voting yes while publicly conceding that what he won could be taken back at will is not an endorsement of the deal’s durability. It’s an acknowledgment that durability was never actually on offer.

Former Justice Department officials and tax experts quoted in the same reporting made the structural version of the same point: the written assurances “can be easily reversed, or evaded,” and the narrowed tax immunity “do[es] nothing to keep Mr. Trump and his family from an enormous windfall offered to no other taxpayers.” Senator Richard Durbin, the committee’s ranking Democrat, went further still, accusing Mr. Blanche of perjury — saying Mr. Blanche had privately called the fund a “mistake” in front of witnesses, then denied saying so under oath at his confirmation hearing. That is a live allegation, not yet adjudicated, but it is now part of the record this series is tracking.

What doesn’t resolve is the question this series keeps returning to: what actually stops a president from treating a written commitment as provisional the moment it’s inconvenient. Mr. Trump said the fund would come back if Mr. Blanche wasn’t confirmed. Mr. Blanche got confirmed, and the immunity that outlasted every draft of this dispute is the one piece nobody asked him to give up. The scorecard:

Positions taken on the fund’s status, in five days: dead, then wished-for, then “back on the table,” then dead again. Times the president named himself as the source of the delay: 1. Scope of the audit immunity narrowed to: Trump, two sons, and the Trump Organization — exactly who it was designed to protect. Signatures on the rescission order from parties actually required to sign it, per the settlement’s own terms: 1 of at least 4. Times the president confirmed, on the record, that he agreed to the deal ending the standoff: 0. Committee vote: 12–10, along party lines. Confirmed: not yet — floor vote pending. Republican senators, on record, acknowledging their own concession could be reversed at will: 1.

Update, Aug. 8:

The floor vote this piece left pending has since happened, and it was far closer than the committee tally above suggested. The Senate confirmed Mr. Blanche 50 to 49. Senators Susan Collins of Maine and Lisa Murkowski of Alaska crossed over to vote no, both citing his role in the I.R.S. settlement directly. The nomination was saved by a third skeptical Republican, Senator Bill Cassidy of Louisiana, who flipped to yes on the eve of the vote — not because he trusted the deal, but because he’d concluded a confirmed attorney general would have more leverage to resist the president than one left “acting” indefinitely, a view he said was shaped in part by private lobbying from Bill Barr.

Ms. Murkowski’s stated reason for voting no is worth setting down in full, because it is the sharpest on-the-record confirmation yet of the argument this piece has been making since the paragraph-8 question above: “I am also keenly aware that the department’s nearly $2 billion dollar slush fund — which likely would have rewarded Jan. 6 protesters — is only off the table because this nomination is pending and the Senate has leverage. Once we vote, that will end, and there is no telling what the future holds.” A sitting senator, voting against confirmation for exactly that reason, is not a hypothetical this series raised. It is the mechanism working exactly as warned, narrated in real time by someone with a vote to cast on it.
