In the quiet corridors of European capitals, in secure conference rooms in Tokyo and Canberra, in diplomatic channels from Seoul to Ottawa, allied leaders spent Trump’s first year back conducting the same conversation.

Not in public statements — those remained carefully diplomatic. But privately, urgently, throughout those twelve months, the question dominated every strategic discussion:

Can we count on America?

Not as abstract principle. As immediate strategic reality. After a full year of Trump’s return, they had their answer: No.

But as the year deepened, that question was joined by a second, more unsettling one — a question that would have been unthinkable to any allied leader twelve months earlier:

Is America itself now a threat to us?

That second question — asked in Ottawa more than anywhere else, but whispered in Copenhagen, in Panama City, in every capital where leaders watched Trump threaten to annex democratic allies — marks the profound and terrible territory into which Trump’s first year back took American foreign policy.

These weren’t hypothetical concerns during Trump’s first year back. They were operational questions determining military planning, alliance commitments, strategic partnerships. And after twelve months, the answers were devastating: American reliability is gone. American intentions toward allies are uncertain. The framework that prevented great power conflict for 75 years spent a year fracturing.

The counterfactual isn’t whether President Harris would have solved all global challenges during her first year. It’s whether American commitments would have been credible again — and whether allied leaders would have spent the year planning for partnership rather than planning for abandonment or worse.

The 51st State: When Partnership Became Threat

Two longtime allies, now standing in uneasy tension. During Trump’s first year back, the familiar partnership between the United States and Canada was replaced by public threats of economic coercion and annexation.

I need to begin here, because nothing else in this essay makes full sense without it.

I live in Toronto. My husband is a Canadian permanent resident, soon to be a Canadian citizen. We own a condo here. We walk these streets. Canada is not an abstraction for me — it is home.

And during Trump’s first year back, the President of the United States repeatedly, explicitly, and without apparent irony declared his intention to absorb my home country.

Not through negotiation. Not through diplomacy. Through economic warfare — tariffs designed to harm Canadian workers and destabilize Canadian industries — combined with sustained public messaging that Canada should cease to exist as an independent nation and become the 51st state of the United States. When asked whether he would rule out military or economic coercion to achieve this goal, Trump declined to rule it out.

Simultaneously, he threatened to seize Greenland from Denmark — a NATO founding member — by what he called “economic force or military force.” He threatened to retake the Panama Canal. He made clear that he views allied sovereignty as negotiable when it conflicts with American desires.

This is the context in which every other question about Trump’s first year in international affairs must be understood.

The man who questioned whether America would defend NATO allies from Russian aggression also threatened to coerce those allies himself. The man who praised Ukrainian resistance to Russian annexation threatened to annex his democratic neighbor. The man who spoke of American strength spent a year demonstrating that American strength now means threatening friends rather than deterring enemies.

The practical consequences for Canadian international relations during that year were immediate and fundamental. Canada began pivoting aggressively toward European trade partnerships — not out of sentiment, but out of strategic necessity. The CUSMA framework was treated by Washington as a pressure instrument rather than a binding agreement. Canadian businesses began pricing “Trump risk” into every American-linked decision. Canadian military planners, who had spent careers assuming continental defense partnership with the United States, began developing scenarios their predecessors would have found absurd.

Most fundamentally: Canada’s political consensus — left, right, center, provincial, federal — hardened around a single principle that had not required stating in living memory. Canadian sovereignty is not negotiable. Canada is not for sale. Canada will not become anyone’s 51st state.

That this needed to be said. That it needed to be said repeatedly. That it needed to be said in response to the sitting President of the United States.

That is what Trump’s first year back cost North American diplomacy.

Under Harris’s first year: None of this happens. Harris does not threaten Greenland. She does not call Canada the 51st state. She does not treat allied sovereignty as a bargaining chip. The entire framework of allied relationships operates from the baseline assumption that partners are partners — not territories awaiting absorption.

And we spent a year watching the impossible become policy.

The NATO Question: What Twelve Months of Threats Produced

Allied leaders gathered under the NATO banner. Behind the diplomatic smiles lay a year of urgent private discussions about American reliability — and whether the United States itself had become a source of strategic risk.

NATO’s Article 5 is simple: An attack on one member is an attack on all. That commitment prevented war in Europe for 75 years.

Trump questioned that commitment repeatedly throughout his first year back. He suggested America might not defend allies who “don’t pay enough.” He praised Putin while insulting allied leaders. He described NATO as obsolete. And by threatening to seize Greenland from Denmark — a NATO member — he demonstrated that the threat to allied security might come from within the alliance itself.

What allied strategists experienced throughout year one:

European defense ministers spent the entire year planning for American unreliability — a planning exercise that had a deeply surreal dimension, since they now had to consider not just American absence but potential American aggression against NATO territory.

Increased national defense spending accelerated throughout the year — not because Trump bullied them into burden-sharing, but because they recognized they could not count on American protection. European defense integration moved from aspiration to urgency. Nuclear weapons reconsideration entered serious policy discussion across Scandinavia and the Baltic states.

The counterfactual: Under Harris’s first year, Article 5 would have been unequivocal from day one. No conditions. No threats. No uncertainty. Allied territory would have been defended, not coveted. Burden-sharing concerns would have been addressed through traditional diplomatic channels.

The result after one year: Stronger NATO, confident European allies, clear deterrence, maintained American leadership.

We spent a year watching fracture. And the fractures run deeper than they appear.

Ukraine: What Twelve Months of Betrayal Produced

Symbolic of shifting alliances. While Trump posed with European and Ukrainian leaders, American support for Ukraine was conditioned and reduced, sending a clear message across the democratic world about the fragility of U.S. commitments.

Ukraine faced existential war against Russian aggression. What happened over those twelve months was precisely what Trump promised: systematic betrayal in service of a “deal” that rewards Russian aggression.

Military assistance was cut or conditioned throughout the year. Ukrainian defense needs mattered less than Trump’s relationship with Putin. The administration pushed Kyiv toward negotiations that required conceding significant territory — demanding, in effect, that Ukraine ratify Russian conquest as the price of peace.

The message to Ukraine and allies became inescapable: Democratic allies cannot count on American military commitments when those commitments become politically inconvenient. Authoritarian aggression, if sustained long enough, will eventually be accommodated by the democracy it threatens.

The counterfactual: Under Harris’s first year, American support for Ukraine would have continued based on strategic logic: Allowing Russian territorial conquest undermines international order. Military assistance would have flowed predictably. Peace negotiations would have occurred when Ukraine chose, on terms that didn’t reward aggression.

There is a categorical moral difference between the difficulty of sustaining a defensive alliance and the catastrophe of abandoning one to please the aggressor.

And we spent a year watching the catastrophe.

The Middle East: What the Khamenei Strike Changed

On February 28, 2026, American and Israeli forces conducted strikes that killed Iranian Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei.

The assassination of a foreign head of state by American forces represents a threshold that no postwar American president had previously crossed.

This requires direct statement: Under Harris’s first year, this strike would not have happened.

Not because Harris would have been soft on Iran. But because the decision to kill a foreign head of government represents a level of deliberate escalation that a functionally normal American foreign policy apparatus — with its interagency review processes, its legal counsel, its congressional consultation norms — would not have reached through normal deliberation.

This was not a decision produced by normal deliberation.

The counterargument — that Khamenei directed terrorist operations and was a legitimate military target — deserves engagement. There is a legal case to be made. But even granting those arguments their full weight, the questions remain: What precedent has been established? Which foreign leader is next? By what standard is a head of state elevated to legitimate military target? With what accountability to Congress, to allies, to international law?

These questions would have been grappled with in a Harris administration — before the decision, not after it.

The Middle East is now more dangerous, more unpredictable, and more likely to produce regional escalation than it was when Trump’s second term began.

And we spent a year watching impulse replace governance.

The China Challenge: What a Year of Chaos Produced

Trump’s China policy during his first year back was incoherent chaos: trade wars announced via tweet, tariffs imposed then suspended then reimposed, personal flattery of Xi alternating with accusations.

Meanwhile, China advanced systematically: Belt and Road expanding, military capabilities growing, technology leadership advancing, relationships strengthening with countries exhausted by American volatility.

Asian allies faced impossible strategic choices all year. They hedged constantly — maintaining relationships with both powers, reducing trust in American leadership, cautiously cooperating militarily while preparing for American withdrawal.

The counterfactual: Harris’s first year would have maintained the strategic competition framework — compete where necessary, cooperate where possible, coordinate with allies, invest in American competitiveness. Consistent policy. Systematic technology competition. Predictable military presence.

Strategy beats improvisation over twelve months. Allied coordination beats solo flailing.

And we spent a year watching solo flailing.

Trade: What Twelve Months of Economic Warfare Produced

Trump weaponized trade against allies throughout the year. Tariffs threatened constantly based on presidential mood. Agreements violated when politically convenient. Partners treated as marks to be exploited.

The damage to American interests was profound and self-inflicted. Businesses couldn’t invest when policy was chaos. Allies explored alternatives to American markets. De-dollarization accelerated as a practical hedge against American economic coercion.

When America treats its closest democratic allies as adversaries in trade — imposing tariffs on Canada, threatening Panama, declaring that trade relationships exist for American extraction rather than mutual benefit — the message to every nation is clear: American economic partnership is a liability. Diversify now, before you become the next target.

The counterfactual: Under Harris’s first year, trade relationships return to partnership. Agreements are honored. Disputes are resolved through agreed mechanisms.

That’s not utopia. That’s functional commerce between nations that share values and interests.

And we spent a year watching those relationships corrode.

Climate: What Twelve Months of Sabotage Produced

Trump withdrew from the Paris Agreement again, gutted environmental regulations, promoted fossil fuel production, and mocked climate science throughout his first year back.

The damage to international climate cooperation was immediate and compounding. For Canada specifically: our climate action was undermined throughout Trump’s first year back. Continental climate challenges — forest fire response, Arctic policy, Great Lakes management, energy grid integration — require American partnership. Trump made that partnership impossible for twelve months.

The counterfactual: Harris leads rather than sabotages. America participates rather than undermines. The technological and financial resources of the world’s largest economy are directed toward the problem rather than away from it.

And we spent a year watching those resources directed away.

The Democratic Coalition: What a Year of American Collapse Taught

What we watched die during year one was something more specific and more painful than general democratic decline: we watched America demonstrate that a democracy, under sufficient stress, will abandon its democratic allies to authoritarian aggression while threatening its democratic neighbors with annexation.

That lesson — that democracy produces neither reliable allies nor reliable principles — spread through the global democratic community throughout that year.

The counterfactual: Harris’s first year would have demonstrated that January 6th was a failed coup, not a successful revolution. That democratic resistance worked. That institutions held. That the fever broke.

Instead, Trump’s first year back proved: Authoritarians win. Coups need only wait. Democratic resistance fails eventually. And when democracies survive, they may still elect leaders who behave like authoritarians toward their neighbors.

We spent a year proving all of that to the world.

What My Family History Makes Me Mourn After This Year

My relative Hugh Llewellyn Keenleyside served alongside Lester Pearson in Canada’s Department of External Affairs and played a senior role in Canadian-American wartime diplomacy during World War II — serving as Canadian Secretary of the Permanent Joint Board on Defence and as a key advisor to Prime Minister Mackenzie King during the Churchill-Roosevelt negotiations.

He helped build the institutions that were designed to make territorial aggression by great powers against their neighbors unthinkable.

He succeeded for 75 years.

I am watching those institutions corrode under the weight of an American president who threatens to annex allies, who abandons democratic partners to authoritarian aggression, who treats the postwar order as an obstacle rather than an achievement.

The counterfactual asks: What would allied leaders have felt during Harris’s first year if American leadership returned? If sovereignty was respected? If commitments were reliable?

The answer from every democratic capital, and from my own: Relief. Profound, exhausted relief.

Instead, we spent a year watching the opposite — and in Canada, something worse than mere unreliability. We spent a year watching a supposed ally declare that we should cease to exist.

The Quiet Conversations in Ottawa Throughout the Year

Canadian security policy for 75 years assumed American partnership: NORAD, NATO, integrated continental defense.

Trump made that assumption not just uncertain but dangerous. The question no Canadian had ever seriously had to ask was now being asked in strategic planning sessions: What if we need capabilities to defend against American pressure?

The Canadian response — dignified, firm, bipartisan — was to begin diversifying everything that could be diversified: trade toward Europe, military procurement toward European suppliers, diplomatic alignment toward multilateral institutions that don’t depend on American leadership.

These shifts will take years to complete. They represent a fundamental reorientation of Canadian strategy away from the continental partnership that has defined Canadian security since the Second World War.

In the counterfactual: None of these conversations happen during Harris’s first year. Her election would have signaled that American respect for Canadian sovereignty is restored.

Instead, we spent a year defending our right to exist as an independent nation from the country that was supposed to be our closest friend.

The Exhaustion of a Year of Contingency Planning

Here’s what killed allied strategists during Trump’s first year back: endless contingency planning for American unreliability — and now, for American hostility.

Military planners considered all year: What if America withdraws from NATO? What if America pressures allied nations to accept its territorial demands? Diplomats planned constantly: What if American policy reverses tomorrow? Economic ministers factored monthly: What if Trump imposes tariffs without warning?

This exhaustion was universal across democratic allies throughout the year.

The counterfactual: Under Harris’s first year, there would have been no endless contingency planning. American commitments would have been reliable. Policy would have been predictable. No allied government would have spent a single hour planning for American territorial aggression against them.

That’s not utopia. That’s basic alliance function. It’s how relationships worked for 75 years.

And we spent a year mourning its loss — while building the alternative arrangements that its loss made necessary.
