On Tuesday, September 22, 2026, the President of the United States stood at the rostrum of the United Nations General Assembly and described the choice in front of him. He could make a deal with Iran that let it rebuild. Or he could annihilate the Islamic Republic, and do it quickly. He presented the second option the way a man presents a dessert menu.

On Wednesday, September 23, Masoud Pezeshkian stood at the same rostrum. No leader of a country at war with the United States had ever addressed the Assembly in person. He held up photographs of schoolchildren. The Shajareh Tayyebeh Elementary School in Minab was struck on the first day of the war, and the Washington Times put the toll at 156 dead, more than 120 of them children. He held up a photograph of the late Ali Khamenei as well, which rather muddied the message for anyone inclined to hear it. As Nick Schifrin reported for PBS NewsHour, the lone member of the American delegation in the hall listened for a few minutes and then walked out.

Between those two speeches, in rooms without cameras, Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner sat with Abbas Araghchi for the first mediated meeting of its kind since June. By Friday, Tehran had put a plan on the table. By Saturday morning, the President had rejected it on the White House lawn. By Saturday night, the most important sentence of the week was one he never said in public: that he expects to start bombing Iran again after the votes are counted on November 3.

This is the week the war acquired an election calendar.

The Seven Days

Araghchi laid out the proposal to reporters at the United Nations on Thursday, September 24, as Farnaz Fassihi and Pranav Baskar reported for the New York Times. It had travelled to Washington through Qatar. If certain conditions were met, he said, the Strait of Hormuz would be open by the end of the seventh day and talks would restart.

The conditions were not new, and nobody pretended otherwise. Washington would lift its naval blockade of Iranian ports, waive sanctions on Iranian oil sales, and observe a ceasefire that extended to Lebanon. Iranian officials said the plan was effectively the June memorandum of understanding signed in Islamabad. The difference was speed: the June agreement allowed 60 days to reach nuclear negotiations, while this one compressed the sequence into a week. The first four or five days would go to initial steps, the strait would reopen on the sixth, and nuclear talks would begin on the seventh.

Pezeshkian spent the week selling it. He told reporters he wanted a deal before the midterms, contradicting the President’s claim that Tehran was waiting to see how he fared at the polls. In an interview with Margaret Brennan for CBS News, he twice confirmed that Iran would admit U.N. nuclear inspectors as part of a longer-term ceasefire. He repeated the old line that nuclear weapons are religiously forbidden.

It is fair to be sceptical of all of this, and the essay owes the sceptics their due. Cyrus Schayegh of the Geneva Graduate Institute told Al Jazeera that essentially nothing had changed. In his reading, Iran wants to keep the leverage the war has handed it, and Washington refuses to go back to the June framework. A market analyst quoted by CBS called it a dubious offer for the same reason. The offer’s substance was the June deal. Its novelty was the clock. Anyone who expected the United States to accept terms it had walked away from in July, simply because they now arrived in a smaller box, was going to be disappointed.

“I’m Rejecting Their Deal”

The Wall Street Journal broke the story on Friday night, September 25, reporting that the President had turned the proposal down. On Saturday morning, walking to Marine One for a trip to Tennessee, he confirmed it himself. He said he was rejecting their deal. He said Iran wanted to open the strait immediately because it was losing so badly, and that the fake news wasn’t reporting this. He said the United States had total control of Hormuz and that 29 ships had come out the night before. He said he liked making deals too, but this one was not acceptable.

Earlier that morning he had posted on Truth Social that Iran could not have a nuclear weapon, with three exclamation marks. Nobody had asked.

The public rejection is the least interesting part of the story. The Journal’s reporting, which Reuters and CNBC could not immediately confirm, contains the part that matters. According to U.S. officials, the President has told aides he expects to resume bombing Iran after the November midterms. Privately he doubts Tehran will meet his demands on its nuclear programme. The scale of any renewed campaign is unclear. As Iran International summarised the Journal’s account, one reason for his reluctance to restart major combat operations now is that Washington wants to preserve dwindling munitions for other contingencies. The officials added that his view could change in the coming weeks. Washington has also told Tehran and the mediators that the maritime blockade is staying.

Put those sentences side by side and the shape of the policy is plain. Bombing is expected, but not yet. Munitions are short, but the blockade continues. A deal is possible, but after the election. What an election has to do with the enrichment capacity at Fordow is a question the Journal’s sources did not answer. What it has to do with the price of gasoline in Pennsylvania is a question that answers itself.

The Calendar Is the Strategy

For several instalments now, this diary has argued that the war’s decisions are being made on a clock that has nothing to do with the battlefield. This week the clock was stated outright by both sides. Pezeshkian said he wanted a deal before November 3. Trump said at the United Nations that a deal would likely come after it. Araghchi, in a phrase the Times recorded, said it would be better if an agreement came before the midterms.

Here the TACO framework does most of the work. The Threaten came on Tuesday, from the rostrum. The Accommodate ran all week in the Witkoff-Kushner channel, which a U.S. official described to CBS as positive and constructive. The same official added that there was no rush, because the United States held the stronger hand. The Obfuscate is the 29 ships. U.S. officials told CBS that nearly 40 million barrels of crude had left the Gulf under military escort in 48 hours, which they put at prewar levels. Central Command says the blockade has redirected 122 vessels since it was reinstated on July 14. Both figures may well be accurate. Neither tells you what the strait looks like to a tanker captain without a destroyer beside him. That remains the question the June deal was supposed to settle, and it was the reason the deal collapsed.

The missing letter is the C, and that is the week’s genuine novelty. For once, the Capitulate has a date on it. The deal that nearly everyone expects to be signed eventually will look a great deal like the one signed in June. It is being held until after the election, and the bombing is being held until after the election too. Whichever comes first will be announced as the one that was always planned.

Operation Economic Outcast Takes Flight

While the diplomats talked, the Treasury closed the skies. Scott Bessent had declared that Iranian airlines would be shut down from Wednesday, September 23. He threatened countries that let them land with expulsion from the dollar system, the machinery this diary first examined in Part XCIV. The countries complied. The United Arab Emirates stopped all Iranian carriers. Turkey and Oman halted Mahan Air. Iraq’s Najaf airport suspended Iranian flights. Najaf is the airport that carries Shia pilgrims to the shrine cities, Iraqi students to Iranian universities, and patients to Iranian hospitals. The Iraqi government is now negotiating with Washington for a humanitarian exemption. The UAE central bank blocked transactions by branches of Bank Melli, and Turkey revoked the licence of Bank Mellat.

Tehran’s response came in two voices, and the pair is instructive. On Friday, Mohammad Mokhber, an adviser to the Supreme Leader, posted a threat on X: flights in the region would be free for everyone or for no one. On Saturday, Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of the Supreme National Security Council, told ISNA that Iran would not retaliate militarily. Negotiations over the restrictions were being pursued seriously, he said, and any response would come through unspecified non-military options. One man threatened the region’s airspace, and the next day another man walked it back. Readers of Part XCVII will recognise the pattern. When one wing of the state speaks and another wing contradicts it, the useful question is which one controls the missiles.

Seven Hours with the Supreme Leader

That question leads, as it always does, to Mojtaba Khamenei, who has not been seen in public since the strike that killed his father on February 28. Pezeshkian was asked about him directly on Face the Nation. He said the Supreme Leader has no ongoing health issues and that his injuries to the face and legs have healed. Speaking as a physician, he told Brennan to accept his word. He said they had last met for seven hours, and that both men shifted positions over that time because neither was used to sitting so long.

Then he described how decisions are made. A team of six drawn from the security forces, parliament and government agencies consults and reaches decisions through coordination.

A healthy Supreme Leader does not ordinarily need a committee of six to decide things for him. Seven months without a public appearance, a seven-hour meeting described by the one man with the strongest reason to describe it well, and a governing structure that sounds more like a board than a throne: none of it proves incapacity. It does explain why a seven-day plan announced in New York can sit beside a threat to close the region’s airspace issued from Tehran. It also explains why the man who has now rejected that plan says he doubts Iran can deliver on it.

The Second Strait

At the other end of the Arabian Peninsula, the war this diary followed in Part XCVIII kept widening. On Friday, September 25, the Security Council unanimously condemned the Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia, a rare show of unity from a body that seldom manages one. Pakistan’s Prime Minister told the Assembly that any further attack on Mecca would be a red line. The Houthis deny the Mecca drone ever existed. The military chiefs of Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey met in Riyadh under last month’s mutual-defence pact. They reaffirmed joint efforts. As the Washington Times reported, however, neither partner has yet shown much appetite for fighting. The same piece quoted a Gulf analyst, identified only as Mr. Abdulla, with a plea to the Americans: “please get the Strait of Hormuz open.”

The Houthis sent more drones toward Riyadh and ballistic missiles toward the air base at Khamis Mushait overnight into Saturday, and a member of their political bureau called the operation a small-scale rehearsal. The Saudis struck the al Dalil bridge in Ibb governorate, a supply link between Sanaa and the Taiz front, according to the Critical Threats Project. The head of Yemen’s Saudi-backed government called for a general mobilisation. The International Organization for Migration counts more than 130,000 people displaced by the fighting on the west coast. The Associated Press found a seven-year-old from Mokha named Tarek al-Salami at a feeding centre in Aden. His father had carried him out on foot, and it took two days to find a hospital with a bed.

Saudi Foreign Minister Faisal bin Farhan told the Assembly on Saturday that conditions in Hormuz must return to what they were before February 28, with no fees or taxes. He is asking for the world before the war. The President is offering it after the election.

The Cost Ledger

Brent crude closed the week at $104 a barrel, where it had started, after touching $108 on Thursday on the absence of any breakthrough. Europe’s energy commissioner, Dan Jorgensen, wrote to EU ministers warning of a price crisis tied to a supply crisis and urging them to consider curbing gas and electricity demand. The August personal consumption expenditures report lands on Wednesday, September 30, with inflation still stuck above three percent. Damien Cave’s September 15 New York Times survey of fuel-price protests from Indonesia to Portugal, parked here since Part XCVII, reads a little more urgently each week.

The White House’s fact sheet on last week’s state visit contained a sentence that belongs in this ledger. According to it, the President urged Xi Jinping to increase production of refined petroleum products to stabilise global supply. The same week, the U.S. ambassador to Beijing said China had been warned that any help to Iran was totally unacceptable. China’s vice-president then told the Assembly that the sovereignty of Gulf states should be respected, without naming the country he meant. The President asked Beijing for oil to offset a war that Beijing is suspected of quietly helping to prolong. That sentence also belongs in the ledger.

The Numbers

  • Seven: the days in Tehran’s proposal between American acceptance and the restart of nuclear talks.

  • Five weeks: the distance to November 3, the date both capitals now treat as the hinge of the war.

  • 122: vessels redirected by the U.S. blockade of Iranian ports since July 14.

  • 29: ships the President said were escorted out of Hormuz on the night of September 25.

  • $104: Brent crude at Friday’s close.

  • 156: the dead at the Minab elementary school on the war’s first day, whose photographs were held up in the General Assembly on September 23.

  • 130,000+: Yemenis displaced by the fighting on the Red Sea coast.

  • Six: members of the committee Pezeshkian says makes Iran’s decisions.

  • Zero: public appearances by the Supreme Leader since February 28.

And Narges Mohammadi, for whom this section is always kept. She told the Associated Press that economic misery and repression are leaving Iranians no room to breathe. Her memoir, smuggled out of prison a few pages at a time by fellow inmates, is being published this month in eleven languages. Its English edition will not appear until next year. Her sentences total 44 years, and she has served ten of them. She is recovering. Both of the men who spoke at the rostrum last week said they wanted peace, and neither of them mentioned her.
