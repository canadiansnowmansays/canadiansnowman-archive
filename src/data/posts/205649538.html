Deng Xiaoping’s strategic guidance for China’s foreign policy in the 1990s was summarised in a phrase that has been variously translated but consistently means something like: “Hide your brightness, bide your time.” The Chinese original — tāoguāng yǎnghuì — carries connotations of concealing one’s capacities, accumulating strength quietly, and avoiding actions that would provoke premature confrontation with more powerful adversaries.

The phrase has been debated extensively among China scholars, partly because it is unclear whether Deng meant it as a temporary tactical posture or as a permanent strategic orientation, and partly because it became the central question of early 21st-century Chinese foreign policy: was China still hiding its brightness, or had it decided that enough time had been bided? The decade from 2001 to 2012 — the period this part covers — is the decade in which that question moved from academic to urgent.

What happened in those years is not dramatic in the way that the Tiananmen crackdown or the Great Leap famine was dramatic. It is dramatic in the way that compound interest is dramatic: quietly, at a rate that seems modest in any given year, producing consequences that are enormous when measured across the full period. China joined the WTO, absorbed the global financial crisis better than any major economy, became the world’s largest exporter, built the world’s largest high-speed rail network, launched its first astronaut into space, hosted the Olympics, and displaced Japan as the world’s second-largest economy. All of this happened while most Western attention was focused on Iraq, Afghanistan, the global financial crisis, and the domestic politics of recovery.

The Quiet War series called this “the peace that’s being won.” The Patient Decades are what the patience looked like from the inside.

The 2008 global financial crisis was the defining event of this period, and not primarily for the reasons Western accounts emphasise.

For the United States and Europe, 2008 was a catastrophe: banking systems that required government bailouts, unemployment that took years to recover, political legitimacy that was damaged in ways still being felt. For China, 2008 was a demonstration. The Chinese banking system, which had been criticised throughout the 1990s and 2000s for its opacity, its state direction, and its insulation from market discipline, turned out to be insulated from the specific form of market dysfunction that destroyed Western banks. Chinese banks had not packaged subprime mortgages into collateralised debt obligations. They had not created the shadow banking structures that amplified the American housing bubble. They had, in short, not done the things that Western financial theory said efficient markets do — and the consequence of not doing those things was that they did not experience the collapse that efficient markets experienced.

China’s response to the crisis was a four trillion yuan stimulus package — roughly $586 billion — announced in November 2008. The stimulus was directed primarily at infrastructure: railways, roads, airports, power grids, affordable housing. It was implemented with a speed that no democratic government could have matched, because it did not require legislative approval, environmental review, or public consultation at the scale that Western democracies demand. Shovels were in the ground within weeks. The Chinese economy grew at 9.2 percent in 2009 while the United States contracted by 2.5 percent.

The lesson that Chinese leadership drew from this — and that Xi Jinping, who was watching from the position of Shanghai party secretary and then Vice President during these years — was that the Western model had failed a decisive test. The combination of financial deregulation, independent central banks, and democratic accountability had produced a crisis that required state intervention to resolve. China’s combination of state-directed banking, party-led stimulus, and absence of the political constraints that slow democratic governments had produced faster, more effective crisis management. The conclusion was not that China should become more like the West. The conclusion was that the West should learn something from China.

The infrastructure built during the patient decades — both during the pre-crisis growth years and the post-crisis stimulus — is the most tangible legacy of the period.

China’s high-speed rail network is the most dramatic example. In 2007, China had essentially no high-speed rail. By 2012, it had more than 9,000 kilometres of track operating at speeds of 200 kilometres per hour or above — more than the rest of the world combined. By 2020, the network had grown to more than 37,000 kilometres. The engineering challenges involved — building at this scale, across terrain that included mountains, rivers, and earthquake zones, on a timeline that Western infrastructure projects would measure in decades — were solved through a combination of state land acquisition that bypassed the negotiation processes that delay Western projects, workforce mobilisation on a scale that only China could achieve, and a willingness to accept engineering risk that democratic accountability would not permit.

The high-speed rail network is not simply a transportation achievement. It is an economic integration achievement. Before high-speed rail, the economic gradient between China’s coastal cities and its interior was steep — the coast was wealthy, the interior was poor, and the distance between them was measured partly in hours of travel time. High-speed rail compressed that distance. A journey from Wuhan to Shanghai that took eleven hours by conventional train could be completed in five. Wuhan — a city of eleven million people that most Westerners had never heard of before COVID — became an industrial and logistics hub partly because of its position in the high-speed rail network. The patient construction of infrastructure was simultaneously patient construction of economic geography.

The space programme is the patient decades’ least discussed achievement and in some ways its most significant. China launched its first crewed space mission — Shenzhou 5, carrying Yang Liwei — in October 2003, becoming only the third country after the United States and the Soviet Union to independently send a human to space. The programme had been developed over decades with minimal international assistance, partly because American law prohibited technology transfer to China’s space programme and partly because China preferred the strategic autonomy of indigenous development. By 2012, China had conducted six crewed missions, developed its own space station module, and demonstrated the capacity to conduct spacewalks and orbital docking. The technological base being built — in rocketry, materials science, guidance systems, and miniaturisation — was directly applicable to both the civilian space programme and the military’s counterspace capabilities. The patient decades were building military and strategic capability under the cover of scientific achievement.

The South China Sea is where the patience began to show its limits.

China’s claims in the South China Sea — the nine-dash line that encompasses roughly 90 percent of the sea’s waters, including areas claimed by Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaysia, Brunei, and Taiwan — had existed on Chinese maps since the Republic of China government drew them in 1947. For most of the post-1949 period, China had lacked the naval capacity to enforce them and had prioritised economic development over territorial assertion. During the patient decades, both of those conditions began to change.

The construction of artificial islands on submerged reefs in the Spratly Islands chain, which China began in earnest around 2013 but which had antecedents in the patient decades, was the most visible expression of the change. China was not simply claiming territory on paper. It was building it — dredging sand onto coral reefs and constructing military facilities on the resulting islands, in a construction programme that eventually added more than 3,200 acres of land to the South China Sea. The programme was conducted during a period of ostensible diplomatic engagement with the United States and the region, which is to say: during a period when hiding brightness and biding time was still the official posture. The construction was the brightness beginning to show.

The accumulation of foreign exchange reserves during this period was itself a strategic instrument, though it was rarely described as such.

By 2014, China held roughly $4 trillion in foreign exchange reserves — the largest foreign exchange holding in the world by a substantial margin. The majority was held in US Treasury bonds, making China the largest foreign holder of American government debt. This position gave China a theoretical form of leverage over the United States that was unprecedented: a creditor relationship in which the largest developing economy held significant claims on the world’s reserve currency issuer.

The leverage was largely theoretical, because unwinding it would damage China as much as the United States — selling US Treasuries rapidly would crash their price and destroy the value of China’s own holdings. But the position was strategic in a different sense: it reflected the degree to which China had become structurally integrated into the global financial system during the patient decades, to the point where the costs of confrontation for both sides had become genuinely prohibitive. The mutual financial entanglement that American policymakers had hoped would produce Chinese political liberalisation had instead produced something more like mutual assured economic destruction — a relationship in which neither side could easily decouple without severe self-inflicted damage.

This is the architecture within which the trade disputes of the late 2010s and the technology competition of the 2020s have been conducted: two economies so deeply intertwined that confrontation is enormously costly, and yet so strategically competitive that accommodation is politically unsustainable. The patient decades built the entanglement. Xi Jinping inherited it and has been trying, with the 141 pages, to reduce China’s vulnerability to it.

The one-child policy, which had been enforced since 1980, was beginning to show its demographic consequences during this period in ways that planners had not fully anticipated.

The policy had achieved its primary goal: China’s total fertility rate had fallen from more than five children per woman in the late 1960s to below two by the 1990s. The population growth that had seemed, in Mao’s era, like China’s greatest resource and, in the 1970s, like its most pressing problem, had been brought under control. But the mechanisms of control — mandatory contraception, coercive abortion, financial penalties for excess births, and the social pressure of the party’s reproductive surveillance apparatus — had produced side effects that the planners had not adequately modelled.

The gender imbalance, produced by a combination of the cultural preference for male children and the one-child limit that made that preference consequential in a way it had not been when families could have multiple children, was becoming visible in the demographic data. By the early 2000s, the sex ratio at birth in China had reached 120 boys per 100 girls in some provinces — far above the natural ratio of approximately 105. The missing girls — estimated at somewhere between 30 and 60 million over the policy’s enforcement period — represented a demographic distortion that would take generations to correct and that was already beginning to reshape the marriage market in the ways that the Price of the Bargain essay describes.

The ageing of the population was the other consequence. A country that had rapidly reduced its fertility rate would, within a generation, have a workforce that was shrinking relative to the population of retirees depending on it. China’s demographic window — the period in which a large working-age population and a small dependent population enabled high savings rates and rapid growth — was open during the patient decades. It was beginning to close.

This is the demographic context in which Xi Jinping took power. He inherited an economy that was still growing rapidly but that faced structural headwinds — a shrinking workforce, a rising dependency ratio, a property market that had been inflated by the stimulus of 2008 to levels that would eventually require correction — alongside the external strategic challenges that the patient decades had positioned China to address but not resolved.

The scientific and educational investment of this period is the final element of the patient decades that demands attention, because it is the foundation on which the 141 pages rest.

China’s R&D spending grew from roughly 0.6 percent of GDP in 1996 to 1.98 percent by 2012 — still below the OECD average but accelerating rapidly, and applied to a GDP that was itself growing at ten percent annually. In absolute terms, the increase was enormous. The number of Chinese students enrolled in universities grew from approximately 1 million in 1978 to more than 20 million by 2010. The number of Chinese PhD graduates in science and engineering, which had been negligible in 1985, exceeded American PhD production in those fields by the mid-2000s. Chinese researchers began appearing in international scientific journals in significant numbers — first in applied science, then increasingly in basic research. The quality of Chinese scientific output, measured by citations and by the judgment of international peer reviewers, rose steadily through the period.

The investment was not uniform. Basic research remained underfunded relative to applied research. Academic freedom was constrained in ways that affected the social sciences and humanities far more than engineering and the natural sciences. The pressure to produce publishable results quickly, which afflicts research systems globally, was intensified by the competitive metrics that Chinese universities used to evaluate faculty. But the trajectory was unmistakable: China was building the scientific and engineering workforce that the 141 pages would put to work. The patience of the patient decades extended to human capital as well as physical infrastructure.

The patience ended on November 15, 2012, when Xi Jinping walked to the front of the Great Hall of the People and introduced himself to the world.
