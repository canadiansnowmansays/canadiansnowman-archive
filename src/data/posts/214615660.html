by Stephen Gordon

I count my essays. Readers of this series know that by now, and know that every so often the count lands on a number that was already a year before I got to it, and I stop whatever I was doing and write about it.

This is essay 1,633, and it is not funny. I want to say that plainly at the top, as this series does, so you know what kind of essay you are holding. Two essays ago I showed you a man on a bell tower selling the future to a senate, and I told you then that we would meet him again kneeling, and that I would not be able to make it funny, and I cannot. What I can do is what this series exists to do: keep the account straight. Because the trial of Galileo Galilei is, before it is anything else, a proceeding about a number — about who may say a true one aloud, in what tone, with what disclaimers, and what the institution may extract from a man when he says it without permission. Those questions did not retire in 1633. I file stories about them in the present tense, under other datelines, most weeks of the year.

The facts, kept honest.

After the Starry Messenger, Galileo’s standing rose and his caution did not. In 1616, the year the censors went through Copernicus line by line — readers of essay 1,543 will remember the ten corrections — Galileo was formally warned: the Sun-centred system was not to be held or defended as true. As a calculation, a convenience, an Osiander-style hypothetical — that could be tolerated. As a fact about the world — no. The number could circulate provided it was wearing the preface. For a decade and a half Galileo, no fool, worked within the terms, and when an old acquaintance and admirer of his work was elected pope as Urban VIII, he judged the climate had softened, and he wrote the book he had been holding back.

The Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems was published in 1632 — with a license; I want that on the record, because it matters to what followed. The censors read it, required changes, and passed it. The book presented the old system and the new as a conversation among three men, formally balanced, and the balance fooled no one, least of all the pope, who found arguments he himself had urged upon Galileo placed in the mouth of the character named Simplicio — the simple one — and who concluded, with the whole apparatus of the Counter-Reformation at his disposal in a Europe sliding through the Thirty Years’ War, that he had been made a fool of in print by a man he had favoured. Historians still argue over how much of what followed was doctrine and how much was a patron’s wounded vanity. This series has covered enough powerful men to decline to choose. It has never yet needed to.

Galileo was summoned to Rome in the winter of 1632–33 — sixty-eight years old, in poor health, pleading age and infirmity, and summoned nonetheless. The proceedings before the Inquisition ran through the spring of 1633. He was interrogated four times. On the record, he was threatened with torture; the threat was formal, a stage of the procedure, and most scholars hold it was not meant to be carried out against a man of his age and fame — and I pass that judgment along exactly as the scholars give it, while noting that the man in the room did not have the benefit of the scholarship. On June 22, 1633, in the Dominican convent of Santa Maria sopra Minerva, Galileo Galilei knelt in the penitent’s habit before the assembled cardinals and read aloud the abjuration composed for him: that he abjured, cursed, and detested the error and the heresy of the Earth’s motion, and would never again say or write anything to suggest it. The Dialogue went onto the Index of Forbidden Books, where it remained for nearly two centuries. He was sentenced to imprisonment at the Inquisition’s pleasure, commuted almost at once to house arrest, under which he lived the remaining nine years of his life, going blind — the man who had seen more of the universe than anyone in history, ending in the dark at his villa in Arcetri.

Legend says that as he rose from his knees he muttered Eppur si muove — and yet it moves. There is no contemporary evidence for it; the phrase surfaces generations later; and by now you know the standing rule of this series and can say it with me. The words are disputed. The motion is not. That is the entire trial in six words, and I would trade every essay in this series to have written them, and history assigned them to an anonymous embellisher instead, which is the sort of thing history does to keep writers humble.

Now the accounting, which is why we are here.

Attend to what the institution demanded, because the demand is the signature of the thing, and I have taught you to read it across five centuries of entries. It did not demand that Galileo be wrong. Wrongness was never on offer; the mathematics was not refuted at the trial because the mathematics was not refutable, and the men in that room included some who knew it. What the institution demanded was the reversal of the posting. Say the entry aloud, in public, in the penitent’s habit: the count was in error, I detest the count, I will not count again. The truth of the number was, in the end, a matter of indifference to the procedure; the submission of the counter was the deliverable. Essay 1,517 showed you an institution reducing ninety-five debatable items to a single yes-or-no box. Essay 1,633 is the box, sixteen years’ wide, closing on one old man, and the box has a script, and the script must be read kneeling. Institutions in trouble do not want your correction. They want your recantation, because a correction fixes the books and a recantation fixes the precedent — it announces to every other counter in the jurisdiction what the saying-aloud will cost.

And here is the entry the institution could not make, the one this series holds up against the whole proceeding: the recantation changed nothing except the man. The Earth did not pause on June 22, 1633, to honour the paperwork. Jupiter’s moons — his moons, the four entries of essay 1,609 — kept their circuits without interruption. Within five years, blind and confined, Galileo had written another book, on motion and the strength of materials, the true foundation of physics — and since no press in Italy could touch him, the manuscript went north and was published in 1638 in the Protestant Netherlands, where papal jurisdiction over arithmetic did not run. Readers of this series will feel the click: the publication thread, Gutenberg to Luther to Leonardo’s shut drawer to the Starry Messenger, arrives here at its destination. A number denied a door will find a border. It is as close to a law as fifteen hundred essays have produced. The abjuration silenced one man in one jurisdiction for nine years. The count crossed the Alps in a satchel and has never stopped moving since.

There is one more clause in the sentence of June 22, and I have saved it because it is the entry in this whole proceeding that I cannot read as an accountant, though it is made of pure arithmetic. Among the penalties, the tribunal ordered Galileo to recite the seven penitential psalms once a week for three years — a penance denominated, like everything else that day, in numbers: seven psalms, fifty-two weeks, three years, some eleven hundred recitations, to be performed by an old man as the ongoing service charge on a true statement. He did not perform them. His eldest daughter did. Virginia — Sister Maria Celeste, placed in a convent near Arcetri years before, his closest correspondent and by the surviving letters the person who understood him best — petitioned to take the recitations upon herself, and the substitution was allowed, and so the count was kept, week by week, in a woman’s voice in a poor convent while her father sat under arrest down the hill. She died in the spring of 1634, months after his return, and the letters say what that did to him better than I will attempt. I enter it here because this series has spent fifteen hundred essays on who keeps the count and who pays for it, and it has never before had to record them as different people in the same family. The institution sentenced the counter. The count was assumed by someone who had done nothing at all. That, too, did not retire in 1633; it is the oldest arrangement in the world, and the ledger has a column for it that no one ever audits.

One more figure, and I give it to you because essay 1,582 made a claim that must now be corrected, and this series corrects itself in public or it is nothing. I wrote there that Roger Bacon’s calendar memo — three hundred and fifteen years from filing to action — likely stood as the record for institutional lag. It does not. In 1633 the Church declared the Earth’s motion a heresy on a man’s knees; the formal acknowledgment that the tribunal had erred, delivered by John Paul II after a commission’s long study, came in 1992. Three hundred and fifty-nine years. The record is broken, and I note, in fairness across the centuries, that the acknowledgment did come — institutions being, on the evidence of this series, capable of correction at roughly the speed of glaciers and for roughly the same reason: pressure, applied without pause, from something larger than themselves. The larger thing, in this case, was the accumulated weight of every checkable sky over three and a half centuries. The tube won. It just did not win in time for the man who raised it.

There is no Dior section in this essay. She came to the desk while I was writing it, as she does, and put her chin on my knee, and I will report only that I stopped writing for a while, and that the essay is better for the interruption, and that some accounts are kept off the books.

The count moves on, as it does, and it turns from a man forced to unsay a true number to a continent trying to stop killing over whose truths would count. The next landmark is essay 1,648 — the year the exhausted powers of Europe ended thirty years of religious slaughter and, in the wreckage, invented something we now mistake for eternal: the sovereign state, the country as a bounded box on a map. The Church that broke Galileo was, in those same decades, losing its power to decide whose count ruled which ground. From one man’s forced silence to a whole continent’s redrawn boundaries.

The words are disputed. The motion is not. Keep both on file.

See you at 1,648, when the map becomes the unit.
