There’s a specific kind of decision-making failure that doesn’t announce itself as a mistake in the moment — it looks, at every individual step, like the reasonable, cautious, credible choice. That’s the story of how the United States arrived in Vietnam at the scale it eventually did: not through a single, deliberate decision to fight a major land war in Southeast Asia, but through a decade of incremental commitments, each one justified less by a clear-eyed assessment of Vietnam itself than by a fear of what withdrawal or restraint would signal to Moscow, to Beijing, and to America’s own allies. This is the essay where this series’ subject shifts, for the first time, from a story about Vietnamese resistance to foreign powers into a story about an American superpower’s own internal logic — and it’s worth being honest from the outset that American decision-making in this period was driven overwhelmingly by credibility politics rather than by any serious strategic case that Vietnam itself was worth the eventual cost.

Diem, and the Manufacturing of a Client State

As I noted at the close of Part VIII, the 1956 reunification election never happened, and South Vietnam instead consolidated under Ngo Dinh Diem, the anti-communist Catholic nationalist the United States had backed as the alternative to Ho Chi Minh’s government in the north. Diem, installed as prime minister under the nominal authority of former Emperor Bao Dai in 1954, moved quickly to consolidate personal power, engineering a rigged 1955 referendum that removed Bao Dai entirely and declared Diem president of a new Republic of Vietnam. American support for Diem in these early years reflected a genuine, if flawed, hope that a non-communist Vietnamese nationalist could build a viable, popular alternative to Ho Chi Minh — the kind of client-state success story Washington badly wanted somewhere in Asia after the “loss” of China to communism in 1949 had already produced a bruising domestic political reckoning in the United States.

Diem’s government, though, proved to be a poor vehicle for this hope. His rule was authoritarian, favoured the Catholic minority at the expense of the Buddhist majority in ways that produced genuine and escalating unrest, and relied heavily on family members — particularly his brother Ngo Dinh Nhu, who ran a shadowy internal security apparatus — for actual governance. Meanwhile, communist-aligned insurgents in the south, organised by 1960 into the National Liberation Front and its armed wing (universally known, in American usage, as the Viet Cong), began a guerrilla campaign against the Diem government with backing and direction from Hanoi, exploiting rural grievances against Diem’s land policies and the broader unpopularity of his rule.

The Domino Theory and the Logic of Commitment

American engagement deepened steadily through the Eisenhower and then Kennedy administrations, framed throughout by the domino theory — the belief, first articulated clearly by Eisenhower in 1954, that a communist victory in Vietnam would trigger a cascading collapse of non-communist governments across Southeast Asia, each falling in turn like a row of dominoes, threatening American strategic interests across the entire region and beyond. Whatever the theory’s ultimate empirical validity — and most historians today regard it as considerably overstated, given how differently the actual political trajectories of Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia, and the Philippines played out in the decades following the war’s end — it functioned, through the late 1950s and 1960s, as the dominant lens through which senior American officials interpreted events in Vietnam, making even modest setbacks there appear to carry outsized global stakes.

It’s worth situating this theory within the domestic American political context that gave it such enduring force, because the domino theory wasn’t purely a dispassionate strategic assessment — it was also a response to a very specific and painful recent memory in American politics. The “loss” of China to Mao’s communist forces in 1949 had produced a bitter domestic political reckoning in the United States, with a wave of accusations, especially from the Republican right and figures like Senator Joseph McCarthy, that the Truman administration and the State Department’s China experts had been insufficiently vigilant, or even secretly sympathetic to communism, in allowing China to fall. That episode left a durable scar on how both parties approached the question of communist advances in Asia for the following two decades: no president, Democrat or Republican, wanted to be the one accused of “losing” another Asian country to communism, and this anxiety about the domestic political consequences of appearing weak on communism in Asia shaped Vietnam policy across four successive administrations, arguably more powerfully than any specific strategic assessment of Vietnam’s actual importance to American interests.

Kennedy’s administration, inheriting a few hundred American military advisors already in Vietnam from the Eisenhower years, expanded that advisory presence substantially, reaching roughly 16,000 American personnel in South Vietnam by the time of Kennedy’s assassination in November 1963 — a dramatic escalation from the advisory-only mission of a few years before, though still short of committed American combat troops in the sense the war would later be remembered for. Kennedy’s own private doubts about the wisdom of deeper American involvement are reasonably well documented in the historical record, and some historians have argued he might have moved toward disengagement had he lived to see a second term — a genuinely unresolvable historical counterfactual that nonetheless underscores how contingent and uncertain American strategy in Vietnam remained even at this relatively early stage.

The Diem Coup

By 1963, American confidence in Diem had collapsed, driven especially by his brutal suppression of Buddhist protests — most searingly captured in the international press by the self-immolation of the monk Thich Quang Duc in a Saigon intersection in June 1963, an image that shocked global opinion and made clear how badly Diem’s government had alienated much of South Vietnam’s own population. With at least tacit American knowledge and, by most historical accounts, active American encouragement communicated through CIA channels, South Vietnamese generals launched a coup in November 1963 that overthrew and killed Diem and his brother Nhu, an outcome that left Washington having removed its own client leader without any clear plan for what political structure should replace him. The instability that followed — a rapid succession of short-lived military governments in Saigon over the following two years — did nothing to strengthen South Vietnam’s position against the growing insurgency, and arguably deepened the very credibility trap American planners were trying to escape: having helped remove Diem, Washington now had an even greater stake in preventing South Vietnam’s outright collapse, since a collapse now would reflect not just on containment policy in the abstract but on the immediate consequences of an American-encouraged coup.

Lyndon Johnson and the Gulf of Tonkin

Kennedy’s assassination just weeks after the Diem coup left Lyndon Johnson to inherit a South Vietnamese political situation considerably more precarious than the one Kennedy himself had inherited from Eisenhower. Johnson, whose domestic political energies and ambitions were overwhelmingly focused on the Great Society legislative agenda, approached Vietnam with what many historians characterise as reluctant, credibility-driven escalation rather than any personal enthusiasm for a major war — Johnson later told the historian Doris Kearns Goodwin that he had dreaded being drawn into “that bitch of a war” at the expense of his domestic priorities, and his taped White House telephone calls from 1964 and 1965 record the same private doubt, even as he simultaneously felt unable to accept the political consequences, at home and internationally, of what a South Vietnamese collapse under his watch would represent.

The decisive turning point came in August 1964, in the Gulf of Tonkin, where American destroyers reported being attacked by North Vietnamese torpedo boats in two separate incidents days apart. The first incident, on August 2, appears well-documented and largely undisputed among historians. The second, reported on August 4, has been the subject of considerably more scholarly doubt in the decades since, with subsequent analysis of sonar and radar data, along with the later declassification of internal National Security Agency assessments, suggesting the August 4 attack likely never occurred at all, and that Johnson administration officials moved to seek retaliatory military action and congressional authorization with a confidence in the incident’s reality that the underlying evidence, even at the time, didn’t fully support.

Whatever the precise truth of the August 4 incident, Johnson used the reported attacks to secure passage of the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution through Congress on August 7, 1964 — a resolution passing the Senate 88-2 and the House unanimously, granting the president sweeping authority to take “all necessary measures” to repel armed attacks against American forces and to prevent further communist aggression in Southeast Asia, without any formal declaration of war. This resolution would become the primary legal basis Johnson and later Nixon administrations cited to justify years of escalating American military action in Vietnam, passed by a Congress acting on a factual predicate that, at least in its second and more consequential half, has not held up well under later historical scrutiny.

From Advisors to Combat Troops

Following the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution and Johnson’s decisive electoral victory over Barry Goldwater in November 1964 — an election in which Johnson had campaigned as the candidate of relative restraint against a Republican opponent seen as recklessly hawkish on Vietnam — the administration moved through 1965 toward the escalation Johnson had spent the preceding year trying to avoid committing to directly. Operation Rolling Thunder, a sustained bombing campaign against North Vietnam, began in March 1965, justified as a way to pressure Hanoi into abandoning support for the southern insurgency without requiring American ground combat troops. Within a week of the bombing’s start, the first Marine combat units landed at Da Nang on March 8, 1965, officially described as a defensive deployment to protect an American airbase. When the bombing failed to produce the intended pressure and South Vietnam’s military position kept deteriorating, the mission expanded rapidly from there, and in July 1965 Johnson approved a major build-up of American ground combat forces.

By the end of 1965, American troop levels in Vietnam had reached roughly 184,000; by the end of 1968, more than 536,000 American personnel were deployed in Vietnam, on the way to a peak of roughly 543,000 in April 1969 — a scale of commitment that had grown almost entirely through this pattern of incremental escalation — each individual step justified as a necessary, limited response to immediate circumstances, each one in practice deepening an American commitment that senior officials, including Johnson himself in his more private moments, harboured genuine doubts about even as they continued approving it.

General William Westmoreland, who commanded American forces in Vietnam through most of this buildup, pursued a strategy centred on attrition — the calculation that superior American firepower and mobility could inflict casualties on North Vietnamese and Viet Cong forces at a rate they could not sustain indefinitely, eventually breaking Hanoi’s will to continue the war. This strategy depended on a body-count metric that became, in retrospect, one of the most criticized features of the entire American military approach to the war: official reports emphasizing enemy casualties as the primary measure of progress, a metric that proved notoriously unreliable, subject to inflation at every level of command, and fundamentally unsuited to measuring progress in a conflict where the actual strategic objective — breaking the political will of an adversary whose historical identity, as this series has traced, was built around patient endurance of exactly this kind of attritional cost — had little to do with any specific casualty ratio.

Reading This Against the Rest of the Series

I want to close this essay by returning, one more time, to the pattern this series has traced since Part I: Vietnamese resistance built on patience, local knowledge, and a willingness to absorb catastrophic cost, defeating larger and more materially advantaged invading forces that had underestimated exactly those qualities. What’s notable about the American escalation this essay has described is how little serious strategic analysis of Vietnam specifically — its history, its terrain, the depth and durability of Vietnamese nationalist and communist commitment, the lessons available from France’s own catastrophic experience there barely a decade earlier — actually drove the escalation decisions at each step. Credibility politics, domino theory anxieties, and the accumulating weight of prior American commitments did far more work in Washington’s decision-making than any genuine assessment of what victory in Vietnam would actually require or whether it was achievable at any acceptable cost.

This matters enormously for how the rest of this series will unfold, because it means the United States entered a war against an adversary whose entire historical identity, as this series has traced across nearly two thousand years of Chinese, Mongol, and French incursions, was organized around exactly the doctrine most likely to defeat precisely this kind of gradually escalating, credibility-driven external intervention: patience, absorption of catastrophic cost, and confidence that time itself was on the defender’s side. The Tet Offensive of 1968 — the moment American domestic public opinion turned decisively against the war, even though the offensive itself was, in narrow military terms, a significant defeat for North Vietnamese and Viet Cong forces — is where this series turns next, and it’s the moment where the gap between American credibility politics and Vietnamese strategic patience became undeniable, on television, to the American public itself.
