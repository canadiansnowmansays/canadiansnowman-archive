On Tuesday, September 29, 2026, the president of the United States told the country’s A.I. chiefs that concern about data centres was a Democratic hoax and that the buildings would soon be “very popular,” as The New York Times reported. The next morning, The Atlantic published a long report from a town where nobody seems to have got the message. George Packer’s “The Great Data-Center Rebellion,” in the magazine’s November 2026 issue, is the most complete portrait this ledger has read of the backlash it has been tracking since August. It also takes the backlash more seriously than any politician in Washington does.

Saturday River

Clinton, Iowa, sits on the Mississippi. It is flat, shrinking and economically bruised. The sawmills that made it rich in the late nineteenth century are now a museum; the population has fallen to about 24,000; the local liberal-arts college was bought by a for-profit, moved online and closed. On the west side of town, miles of corn and soya fields are zoned for heavy industry and crossed by transmission lines. On paper, it is the ideal site for a very large data centre.

The residents found out about theirs by accident. In January, Stephen Figg, who builds technical infrastructure for data centres, heard from a customer that a broker was asking about farmland north of Highway 30. In February, Ryan Wisor saw surveyors with boring rigs in the field behind his house and asked his stepsister, who sits on the city council, what was going on. The answer, weeks later, was a proposal for a data centre that would border his home on two sides and come within 65 feet of his well.

City officials, utility companies and a pro-business nonprofit called Grow Clinton had been discussing the project for nearly two years, under nondisclosure agreements and a code name, “Saturday River.” The developer turned out to be Quality Technology Services, owned by Blackstone, building on behalf of a client that, as of early October 2026, was still anonymous: ten buildings of 500,000 square feet each. Theresa Andrews, a quality analyst at a chemical company, stayed up several nights mapping the parcels from the city’s own records. The site covered 1,100 acres, about the size of Central Park. Freedom-of-information requests showed that plans to upgrade utilities for it had been in motion since 2024.

The city council voted down a 120-day moratorium on June 9 and began drafting an ordinance under which construction could go ahead. The residents formed Clintonians Against Data Centers.

A coalition that should not exist

The group’s seven-member steering committee is the most striking thing in Packer’s report. Four women and three men, most of them middle-aged, Republicans and Democrats, probably more of the former, who did not know one another’s party and did not want to. Political posts unrelated to A.I. are deleted from their Facebook page. “Outside of this,” the editor of the Clinton Herald, Chris Baldus, told Packer, “there’s no way in hell these people are talking to each other.”

Their reasons are not the same. Celeste Robbins, who works in law enforcement and calls herself “artsy” and “very religious,” is a disenchanted Trump voter who lives nowhere near the site; her objection is to A.I. itself, the way it replaces human thought and threatens jobs. Figg, who voted for neither candidate in 2024, works in tech and welcomes A.I.’s progress; he had just spent eight months on an Amazon data centre in Indiana. He started attending council meetings to offer his expertise, found officials less receptive the more he offered, and ended up trying to stop the project instead. Wisor wants to protect his home and his nine-year-old daughter. “I go sit outside and all I think is, This could all be gone,” he said. “You can’t turn it off.”

Nationally, the numbers now support them. Packer cites an NBC poll in which nearly 70 per cent of Americans oppose a data centre in their area. More than 500 jurisdictions have passed moratoriums or bans. Kevin Bradley, a member of the Clinton group, observed that this many Americans had not agreed on anything since 9/11. And Packer notes that on this issue most of Trump’s own base is angry at him, with only one recent precedent: Jeffrey Epstein.

The case for the building

Packer gives the other side its due, and so should this ledger. The mayor, Scott Maddasion, says the project could bring $20 million a year in property taxes to a city with a $24 million budget; Grow Clinton’s president, Andy Sokolovich, says it would be closer to $30 million. Sokolovich says QTS has asked for no tax incentives and will pay for all the utility upgrades, and Alliant Energy has promised that the costs of the centre’s demand will not fall on existing customers.

That is worth noting next to Part XXV, in which Meta claimed billions a year in federal research credits for its data centres on top of an 80 per cent local tax break in El Paso. If Grow Clinton’s account is accurate, Clinton is being offered a better deal than most. The opposition is not simply about money.

Nor is it immune to bad information. One member of the group speculated that three giant troll sculptures, made from reclaimed lumber by the Danish artist Thomas Dambo to commemorate the trees the sawmills consumed, had been paid for by QTS as a diversion. A pro-development councilman mocked Robbins for reporting that she had seen only one bird on a tour of another QTS site. Baldus complained that the opposition does not understand how city government works “and they come in hot.” Sokolovich compared data-centre opposition to Covid scepticism and election denial. The mayor received a death threat.

Not NIMBYism

Packer’s central argument is that the facts about water, power and tax revenue, important as they are, are not the heart of the matter. What he heard over and over in Clinton was the language of powerlessness: people felt “misled, ignored, disrespected, betrayed,” with no say in basic decisions about their own lives. The project looked like a deal made among insiders: Silicon Valley, Wall Street, Washington, and their own city leaders, meeting under nondisclosure agreements to decide what would happen to the field behind Ryan Wisor’s house. And the industry wanting the land is in the business of replacing human beings with machines, and, according to some of its own experts, possibly worse. That, Packer writes, “only intensifies a sense of powerlessness.”

He places the revolt in a long American line: the eighteenth-century farmers who rose against taxes and seizures, and Louis Brandeis’s warning about “the curse of bigness,” concentrations of wealth so great that the individual almost ceases to count and democracy itself is threatened. Clinton has lived through what a Grow Clinton executive, Matt Parbs, called “a history of abandonment” since 1980. Packer asks what happens if, in a few years, five million square feet of steel, concrete and chips in the cornfields become obsolete and go the way of the sawmills. Readers of Parts III and XXI, on how fast A.I. hardware depreciates, will recognise the question.

This is the thread that connects every channel this ledger has followed. Part IX’s recalled councilman, Part XII’s teenagers, Part XVII’s governor, Part XXIV’s Republican candidates running from the issue: each looked like a local dispute about land, rates or taxes. Packer shows the common element underneath. People do not want to be the empty space on someone else’s map.

Five hours in a pole-frame lodge

The scene at the centre of Packer’s report is a council meeting in early September, moved to a pole-frame lodge in a riverside park to fit several hundred people. It began with the Pledge of Allegiance and ran for more than five hours as darkness fell over the Mississippi. The mayor, an Air Force veteran, took the council through the draft ordinance section by section, then heard the opposition. The fight came down to setbacks, the distance between the buildings and the nearest homes. The draft said 1,000 feet. Andrews, citing a Pennsylvania study, argued for 1,500, which might have made the project unworkable. Councilman Cody Seeley said, “I think 500 feet is adequate,” which happened to be QTS’s position.

Wisor answered, his voice unsteady: “This ordinance is meant to protect the citizens and our property. This isn’t meant to conform so that we can fit a particular business in a particular piece of land.” The room applauded.

Packer found the meeting “both mind-numbing and inspiring.” He does not expect the rebels to win. In all likelihood, he writes, a hyperscale data centre will come to Clinton, amid widespread unhappiness and broken friendships, “and AI will continue to roll over the fields of the republic. But a democratic pulse was still beating along the Mississippi River.”

The meeting Packer describes appears to be the council’s Committee of the Whole session of September 1 at Eagle Point Lodge, which WVIK had previewed on August 29, 2026, reporting that QTS wanted the setback cut to 500 feet and wanted individual property owners to be able to waive it by recorded agreements binding on future owners. The fight did not end that night. The Clinton Herald reported on September 12, 2026 that at a special workshop on September 9 the council had voted 5 to 2 to set a 1,000-foot setback from residential property lines for large facilities, but also to allow developers and property owners to negotiate their own arrangements, which goes some way toward QTS’s request for waivers. Seeley voted with the majority. The council also agreed to sort data centres into three size classes and to prohibit evaporative and other water-intensive cooling. “You guys have got their plan and you’re trying to fit their plan into this ordinance,” the resident Randy Heppner told the council. “That’s not true,” Councilman Connor Lee replied.

On September 30, 2026, the Herald‘s Chris Baldus summed up the state of play: “Not a square foot of land has been purchased on the west side of Clinton for data center development, but an ordinance is advancing, a protest group is petitioning and a national corporation is trying to assuage fears.” The council had voted 5 to 1 to set a public hearing on the ordinance for October 8. Clintonians Against Data Centers had submitted a petition with more than 1,400 signatures from registered Clinton voters, proposing an ordinance of their own. QTS had published what it calls “The Good Neighbor Standard,” and its co-chief executive, Tag Greason, explained why: “We thought it would be appropriate to put out the standards so people can kind of understand what we’re talking about and what we mean when we say ‘a good neighbor.’” As of early October 2026, the ordinance had not been finally adopted.

It moved a step closer on October 27. After a public hearing at Eagle Point Lodge, the council passed the ordinance’s first reading, with Councilmember Janet Turpin the only vote against, Maddie Franz and Nora Reichardt reported for WQAD. Two more readings are needed before it takes effect. The opponents asked for a 1,500-foot setback, protection for livestock and “stronger language throughout the ordinance.” Their own petition, which had gathered about 1,500 signatures and proposed keeping hyperscale data centres a mile from homes and workplaces, was turned away: the city attorney, Pat O’Connell, said Clinton’s charter does not allow zoning to be changed by citizen initiative. “I would say that our goal at this point is just to slow this process down,” Robbins said. The group is now seeking a citywide vote on the project, KWQC reported. Councilman Seeley said the ordinance includes ways to reduce the risks. “Yes, I’m well aware of them,” he said.

The ledger

Bill Gates told Ezra Klein, on The Ezra Klein Show on September 29, that “stopping data centers isn’t going to slow this thing one iota,” and that opposing them is “a waste of effort.” In the narrow sense he is probably right. Clinton will not change the trajectory of A.I. But Packer’s report suggests that the value of the rebellion may lie somewhere Gates was not looking. In Washington that same day, the future of the technology was settled over lunch by a president and a table of billionaires, with a one-page pledge and a new brand name. In Clinton, it was argued over for five hours in a lodge by people who had to sit in the same room, look at one another, and listen. The city may lose the vote on the building. It has already rebuilt something the country has been short of: strangers from both parties, arguing face to face, about who gets to decide.
