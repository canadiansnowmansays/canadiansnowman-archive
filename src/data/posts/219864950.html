⚖️ Serious, with wonder in it — because this is the most beautiful number in the series, and because it carries an invoice that will not come due for forty years. I will tell you plainly where the invoice goes. The dog makes one appearance, early, before the arithmetic gets heavy.

I count my essays, and for most of this series the numbers have been about how many: how many ships, how many dead, how many days deleted from a calendar. Essay 1,905 is about a different kind of number entirely. Not a quantity — a rate. In 1905 a twenty-six-year-old clerk at the Swiss patent office in Bern, working on physics in the hours his employer was not paying for, published a short calculation showing that two things the human race had always kept in separate columns of its ledger were in fact the same thing in two currencies, convertible at a fixed rate. The things were mass and energy. The rate was the speed of light, squared. And the rate is so monstrous that the century spent the next forty years discovering what it meant.

A clerk, third class

Begin with the job title, because this series has a weakness for the clerks. Albert Einstein, in 1905, was a Technical Expert, Third Class, at the Federal Office for Intellectual Property in Bern. He had failed to get an academic post. His work was assessing patent applications — electrical devices, a good many of them, gadgets for synchronising clocks among them — and deciding whether the claims held up. He was, in other words, a professional auditor of other people’s inventions, sitting at a counter not so different from the one where, at essay 1,876, Alexander Graham Bell’s patent was issued three days before his telephone worked.

In that single year, in his spare time, the clerk published four papers, any one of which would have made a career. One proved that atoms were real by counting the jiggles of pollen grains in water — the random jostling called Brownian motion — and showing that the count matched what invisible molecules would produce. One argued that light itself comes in discrete packets: not a smooth stream but a count of particles. One, on what we now call special relativity, demonstrated that time is not absolute — that two honest clocks, moving relative to one another, will disagree about the time, and both be right. And the fourth, a paper of three pages published that September, asked a question so modest it sounds like a footnote: Does the inertia of a body depend upon its energy content?

The answer was yes. And the answer is the most consequential sentence in the history of arithmetic.

The quantum, folded in

Before the rate, the packets, because they belong to this essay and I have chosen not to give them one of their own.

Five years earlier, in December 1900, the German physicist Max Planck had been trying to explain how hot objects glow — the colour of a heated filament, the light of exactly the kind of bulb essay 1,882 put in Edison’s windows. Every smooth theory failed. In what he later called “an act of desperation,” Planck tried a trick: he assumed that energy could only be emitted in discrete lumps, whole-number multiples of a tiny fixed amount. The trick worked perfectly, and Planck spent years hoping it was only a trick.

It was Einstein, in 1905, who took it seriously — who said the lumps were real, that light is counted, not poured. Sit with what that means for this series. For eighteen hundred essays I have been arguing that the count is never merely the count, that human beings impose numbers on a continuous world and then forget they did it. In 1900 and 1905 physics discovered that the world had been counting all along, underneath us, in units too small to see. Nature, at the bottom, is not a river. It is a tally. The quantum is the one count in these files that nobody imposed.

The rate

Now the three-page paper, and the number.

Einstein did not write it the way it appears on T-shirts. He wrote that if a body gives off an amount of energy as radiation, its mass diminishes by that energy divided by the square of the speed of light. Rearranged and tidied by later hands, it becomes the five characters everyone knows: E = mc². Energy equals mass times the speed of light, squared.

Read it as this series reads everything — as a ledger entry. Until 1905, mass and energy sat in separate columns. Mass was stuff: the weight of a coin, a stone, a husky. Energy was activity: heat, motion, light. You could no more convert one into the other than you could pay a grocer in hours of daylight. Einstein’s equation said the two columns had always been one column, denominated in two currencies — and that there was a fixed exchange rate between them, posted, permanent, and non-negotiable. The rate is c², the speed of light multiplied by itself: roughly ninety thousand trillion, in the units physicists use.

That is what makes the rate terrifying. A single gram of matter — a paperclip, a raisin — is worth, at the posted rate, about ninety trillion joules of energy. Roughly the explosive force of twenty thousand tonnes of TNT. In a gram. The universe had been carrying a fortune in every pebble, every grain of sand, every hair on the dog, and nobody knew, because no one had ever found a way to cash it.

Einstein, to be clear, had no idea how one might. He thought, at the time, that the conversion might be detectable in radium. He was posting a rate, not opening a bank. But this series has watched enough rates posted to know that a rate, once published, waits. Someone always comes, eventually, to make the exchange.

The audit Newton closed

There is a second reason this essay matters to the architecture of the series. At essay 1,687, I wrote about Newton’s Principia — the book that settled the accounts of the heavens, in which time flowed “equably without relation to anything external,” the same for every observer everywhere, an absolute clock ticking behind the universe. That audit stood for two hundred and eighteen years. Every engineer, every navigator, every railway timetabler of essay 1,914 kept their books in Newton’s absolute time.

In 1905 a clerk who spent his days examining patents for clock-synchronising devices reopened the audit and found the clock was not absolute at all. Simultaneity depends on where you stand and how fast you are moving. There is no master clock behind the universe — only clocks, each honest, each local, each disagreeing with the others in ways that can be calculated exactly. It is the most dignified correction this series has ever recorded: Newton was not wrong so much as provincial, correct within a neighbourhood that happened to contain everything humanity had yet measured. The new books balanced the old ones and extended them. That is what an honest audit looks like.

Dior and the relativity of dinner

The household has its own demonstration of relative time, and I offer it in the spirit of popular science. The forty minutes between my announcing that dinner is coming and the bowl reaching the floor are experienced by me as a brief and reasonable interval. They are experienced by the husky as a span of geological duration, during which empires rise and fall and she grows visibly older. We are both honest observers. We cannot both be right. Einstein would say we are — that each of us keeps true time in our own frame — and I have come to accept this, though I note that hers is the frame in which the complaints are filed.

The invoice, posted

Now the grave turn, and I will make it plainly, as this series promised to at the top.

A rate posted in 1905 was cashed in 1945. When the first atomic bomb used in war detonated over Hiroshima on August 6 of that year, the matter actually converted into energy — the mass that vanished, paid over at Einstein’s rate — was less than a gram. Less than the weight of a paperclip. That was the whole of the transaction. Everything else that happened that morning was the exchange rate.

Einstein did not build the bomb. He was a pacifist by temperament and spent much of his later life arguing for its abolition. But in 1939 he signed a letter to President Roosevelt warning that Germany might build one, and the letter helped set in motion the programme that did. He later called signing it the one great mistake of his life, and said he would have done otherwise had he known the Germans would fail. I do not think the series needs to sit in judgement over that sentence. I only note the shape of it, because by now you know the shape: a man posts a number in good faith, and the number outlives his intentions.

That is the dread-tease, the one this series has been holding since its first July. At essay 1313 I wrote that you cannot skip a floor that has already booked you. In 1905 the floor was booked. The bill was written in three pages by a clerk, third class, and left on the counter for forty years, and the century walked toward it, essay by essay, the way 1337 walked toward 1347.

The closer

So 1905 is the year the universe’s ledger turned out to have one column, not two, and the exchange rate between them turned out to be the speed of light, squared. The clerk who posted it also told us that time itself is local, and that the world counts in packets underneath. It is the most beautiful arithmetic in these files, and it is a loaded gun on the mantel of the century.

My relation Hugh, whose year was the last essay, was seven years old.

The count moves on, and it moves from the clock that is relative to the clock that was made absolute — by force, on a factory floor. The next landmark is essay 1,913, and it is the year a man in Michigan took time, which Einstein had just shown to be personal, and cut it into identical, countable, saleable pieces: the moving assembly line, where a worker became a station and a car became a sum of seconds. Einstein freed the clock from Newton. Henry Ford chained every worker to it. And the year after Ford perfected the schedule, the schedules of Europe started a war no one could stop.

See you at 1913, where time is cut into pieces and sold by the minute.
