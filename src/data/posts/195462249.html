I. The Letter

One week after firing Pam Bondi, the administration played its final card.

On April 9, 2026, Assistant Attorney General Patrick D. Davis sent a letter to House Oversight Chairman James Comer explaining that Bondi would not appear for her scheduled deposition on April 14. The argument was elegant in its circularity: Bondi had been subpoenaed in her official capacity as attorney general. Since she was no longer attorney general — having been fired by the man she served — the subpoena, the DOJ argued, no longer applied.

The administration fired its own attorney general. Then the administration’s Justice Department sent a letter explaining that, since she no longer works for the administration, the subpoena is moot.

In the entire history of accountability avoidance — and this series has documented a considerable volume of it — this may be the most structurally perfect example. No documents destroyed. No witnesses intimidated. No break-in required. Just a termination letter, a legal argument, and the quiet hope that the news cycle would move on.

The legal argument, upon examination, was significantly less sturdy than it appeared. Congressional subpoena power flows from Article I and has been interpreted broadly for over two centuries. The critical question is not what title the recipient held when the subpoena was issued, but whether the testimony sought relates to matters within Congress’s legitimate oversight interest. Bondi’s knowledge of what happened inside the Department of Justice does not evaporate when she cleans out her desk.

House Oversight Committee hearing room. The bipartisan subpoena for Pam Bondi was issued by name, not title. On April 9, 2026, the DOJ argued it became moot the moment she was fired

Nancy Mace understood this. “Our motion to subpoena Pam Bondi, which was passed by the Oversight Committee, was for Bondi by name, not by title,” she said.

Robert Garcia was more direct: “Our bipartisan subpoena is to Pam Bondi, whether she is the Attorney General or not. She must come in to testify immediately, and if she defies the subpoena, we will begin contempt charges.”

The subpoena was designed to survive exactly this manoeuvre. Whether it would was now a question for Chairman Comer, who had not voted for the subpoena in the first place, who said he didn’t think it was necessary, and who responded to the DOJ letter by saying: “I haven’t talked to Republicans, I’m sure I’ll give them a call this weekend. I don’t know what is going to happen.”

This is not the language of a chairman pressing forward. It is the language of a man waiting to see which way the wind blows before committing to a direction.

The wind, in this reading, blows from Mar-a-Lago.

II. The Unnamed Job

Pam Bondi testifying before Congress. The smirk bothered me too.

Consider the precise language of Trump’s Truth Social announcement: Bondi will be “transitioning to a much needed and important new job in the private sector, to be announced at a date in the near future.”

The job exists. It is already arranged. It is important and much needed. But it cannot yet be named.

In ordinary English, this is called a new opportunity. In the specific dialect of Trump administration departures, it is called a settlement.

Pam Bondi knows things. She knows the full contents of the Epstein files — unredacted, unfiltered, in their entirety. She knows what was in the white binders and what was deliberately excluded. She knows what was said in the angry confrontation with Bongino in July. She knows every conversation in which Trump named a target and demanded an indictment. She knows what “all that’s left is child pornography” actually means in context, and what the context actually was. She knows the internal deliberations behind every reversal, every delay, every strategic document dump that revealed nothing.

She is, in the technical sense, a catastrophic liability.

A non-disclosure agreement would be the minimum instrument. Given what she knows, the terms would be extensive. The golden parachute whose silk is stitched from omission, the glowing farewell post whose warmth is contractually enforced. We love Pam. Of course they do. They have to.

But here is where the NDA meets its structural limit.

A non-disclosure agreement is a civil instrument. It operates in the domain of contract law, breach-of-contract suits, and liquidated damages. A congressional subpoena is a creature of Article I. It does not recognize NDAs. It does not negotiate with them. It does not care what Pam Bondi has signed or what the unnamed private sector job requires of her in exchange for its unnamed compensation.

If Mace and Garcia hold the line — if the deposition survives the firing, survives Comer’s reluctance, survives the DOJ’s attempt to assert privilege over a former official — then Pam Bondi will sit in a chair, raise her right hand, and be asked, under penalty of perjury, what she knows.

No NDA covers that. No private sector job is worth a perjury charge.

Which may be precisely why the firing happened on April 2 and not April 15. Get the agreement signed. Get her out of government. Assert executive privilege over everything she touched. Then see if the subpoena holds.

The sequencing is too clean to be coincidental.

III. The Sledgehammer and Its Successor

Stacey Young, the founder of Justice Connection and a former Department of Justice official, assessed the damage Bondi left behind with a single metaphor: “She took a sledgehammer to the department.”

The damage is generational. Career officials in the public corruption and national security divisions have departed in numbers not seen since the Saturday Night Massacre. The department’s historic independence — never perfect, always contested, but structurally real — has been surrendered. Politically motivated investigations have been launched against administration critics. Prosecutors have resigned rather than follow orders they considered unlawful.

None of this is reversible by a personnel change at the top. The sledgehammer’s work is done regardless of who holds the handle.

And then Young said the thing that matters most: “Replacing her with a more competent attorney general who — like her — believes their sole client is the president and not the country may just make things worse.”

Todd Blanche is Trump’s former personal defence attorney. He represented the president through the New York criminal trial — the hush money case that produced a felony conviction. He knows the full architecture of Trump’s legal jeopardy, in the precise way that only a defence attorney can.

He is now the acting attorney general of the United States.

Todd Blanche, former personal defense attorney to Donald Trump and now Acting Attorney General. The scalpel has replaced the sledgehammer

Bondi was a loyalist who was also a liability — a communicator who couldn’t communicate, a prosecutor who couldn’t prosecute, a political operative who kept generating the wrong kind of politics. She was incompetent in service of the agenda.

Blanche is not incompetent. Blanche is precise, strategic, and professionally trained in the art of protecting Donald Trump from legal consequences. He has now been handed the instrument through which those consequences can be generated or suppressed.

Young is right. This may be worse.

IV. Meanwhile, Across Town

At approximately 3:59 p.m. on April 9 — the same day the DOJ sent its letter arguing that Bondi need not testify — Melania Trump summoned reporters to the entrance hall of the White House to make a statement about Jeffrey Epstein.

Nobody had asked.

There was no prominent story breaking that morning linking the first lady to Epstein. No congressional hearing had been announced. She simply decided that this particular day, of all available days, was the moment to clear her name.

She acknowledged attending some of the same parties as Epstein — “overlapping in social circles” in New York. She addressed a 2002 email to Ghislaine Maxwell in which a woman named “Melania” praised a magazine profile of Epstein and received in return the salutation “Sweet pea.” She characterized this as “casual correspondence.”

She closed by calling on Congress to continue its investigation. The same investigation that, hours earlier, the DOJ had moved to obstruct.

Whether this was coordination or coincidence is a question only the people in the White House entrance hall can answer. What we can say with confidence is this: on April 9, 2026, the Trump administration did two things related to Jeffrey Epstein. It moved to kill the deposition of the woman who oversaw the Epstein files. And it put the first lady in front of cameras to talk about something else entirely.

The survivors, one suspects, noticed the timing. They tend to notice everything. They have had years of practice.

Melania Trump addresses reporters at the White House on April 9, 2026 — the same day the DOJ moved to block Pam Bondi’s deposition on the Epstein files.

V. The Arc, Complete

Let us now see the full arc clearly.

In 2011, in her first year as Florida’s attorney general, Pam Bondi fired two investigators who were pursuing a company that had donated to her campaign. Nobody was held accountable.

In 2013, she postponed an execution for a fundraiser. She apologized. The apology was accepted.

In 2013, she accepted $25,000 from a man whose company her office was reviewing, and the review disappeared. A bribery investigation was opened. It went nowhere.

In 2016, she stood at a podium and chanted “Lock her up” about another woman’s emails, while her own ethical record was a catalogue of selective enforcement.

In 2019, she lobbied for foreign governments.

In 2020, she defended the president during impeachment.

In 2024, she promoted election fraud claims on television without evidence.

In 2025, she was confirmed as attorney general, shut down foreign influence investigations on her first day, weaponized the department against political enemies, presented empty binders as evidence of transparency, smiled during testimony about sex trafficking, refused to face survivors, and quietly closed every investigation into Epstein’s co-conspirators.

In 2026, she was fired twelve days before a sworn deposition.

In every instance — from the foreclosure investigators to the Epstein files — the pattern held. When the interests of justice collided with the interests of the powerful, Pam Bondi chose the powerful. Every time. Without exception. For fifteen years.

Florida taught her the lesson. Washington gave her the stage. And Trump, having used her for as long as she was useful, disposed of her with a Truth Social post and an unnamed job whose terms will never be disclosed.

VI. The Lesson from Across the Fence

From up here in Toronto, where I have watched the American Department of Justice transform from the institution that prosecuted Watergate into the instrument that enables its modern equivalent, Pam Bondi’s story offers a simple lesson that Americans may find uncomfortable.

She was not an aberration. She was a product.

And lest anyone mistake this for a partisan argument, consider the most nakedly loyal attorney general appointment in American history: Robert F. Kennedy, installed by his brother John in 1961. You cannot get more loyal than family. The nepotism was so brazen that Congress later passed a law — the Federal Anti-Nepotism Statute of 1967 — specifically to prevent it from happening again.

But here is what makes the Kennedy precedent instructive rather than equivalent. Bobby Kennedy could say no. He did say no — or at least “not like that” — on civil rights strategy, on the handling of organized crime investigations, on the limits of executive power. He pushed back on the Bay of Pigs aftermath. He agonized over wiretapping Martin Luther King Jr. and later came to regret it. He was not a rubber stamp. He was a brother, which meant his position did not depend on a performance review, a Truth Social post, or an unnamed private sector job.

A brother can disagree with a president and still be at the dinner table on Sunday. A Pam Bondi cannot. A Todd Blanche cannot. Their tenure is contingent not on blood but on utility, and utility in Trump World has a single metric: did you protect the principal today?

The structural difference is not who the president appoints. It is what the appointee can afford to say when the president is wrong. Bobby Kennedy could afford to say it because his relationship survived disagreement. Bondi could not. Blanche will not. The loyalty that comes from family is elastic. The loyalty that comes from ambition is brittle. And brittle loyalty, in the attorney general’s chair, is not loyalty to the Constitution. It is servitude to a man.

This is not a Republican problem. It is not a Democratic problem. It is a structural problem that both parties have exploited and neither has solved. But the current version — in which the president’s personal defence attorney inherits a department already reshaped to serve one client — represents something the Kennedy appointment, for all its impropriety, never approached. Bobby Kennedy was his brother’s attorney general. Todd Blanche is his client’s.

The system that produced Bondi — the campaign donations from regulated entities, the revolving door between prosecution and lobbying, the media ecosystem that rewards loyalty over competence, the confirmation process that treats ethical lapses as “old news” — that system did not malfunction when Pam Bondi became attorney general. It functioned exactly as designed.

The $25,000 that purchased her silence on Trump University in 2013 was not a bribe in the legal sense. The bribery investigation confirmed this. It was something more corrosive: it was a relationship investment. It was the price of admission to a network in which the rules apply to other people, and the attorney general’s job is to ensure they stay that way.

Bondi is gone now. The unnamed job awaits. The NDA, if it exists, binds her silence to compensation. The subpoena may or may not survive Comer’s weekend phone calls. The survivors may or may not ever hear her testify under oath. And somewhere, in some state attorney general’s office, the next Pam Bondi is accepting a donation from a regulated entity and quietly deciding that certain investigations are not worth pursuing.

But this story does not end with the system. It ends with the man who now sits in Bondi’s chair.

VII. The Scalpel

Todd Blanche is not Pam Bondi.

This is not a reassuring statement.

Bondi was a loyalist who was also a liability. She fumbled the binders. She smiled at the wrong moment. She couldn’t deliver grand jury indictments against political enemies. She generated the wrong kind of headlines. She was, in the final analysis, incompetent in service of the agenda — a sledgehammer when the job called for a scalpel.

Todd Blanche is the scalpel.

He is Trump’s former personal defence attorney. He sat beside the president through the New York criminal trial — the hush money case, the falsified business records, the felony conviction that still stands with its sentence deferred. He knows the full architecture of Trump’s legal jeopardy. Not in the abstract way that a policy advisor knows it, or the political way that a campaign operative knows it. He knows it the way only a defence lawyer can — every document, every witness, every vulnerability, every pressure point.

That man now runs the Department of Justice.

Consider what this means in practice. The attorney who spent years constructing a legal defence for Donald Trump now controls the institution that could prosecute him — or protect him. He controls the FBI. He controls federal prosecutors. He controls the investigative apparatus that could be aimed at Trump’s enemies or, more importantly, aimed away from Trump’s allies. He inherits the weaponized department that Bondi built but couldn’t operate, and he knows how to operate it.

Bondi took a sledgehammer to the DOJ because she didn’t know any other way. Blanche will not need a sledgehammer. He knows exactly which walls are load-bearing, which doors lock from the inside, and which files should never see daylight. He has been studying the building’s blueprints for years — from the defence table.

The rise and fall of Pam Bondi is not a story with a reassuring ending. It is not even, properly speaking, a story with an ending at all. The loyalist who couldn’t deliver has been replaced by one who can. The sledgehammer has been retired. The scalpel has been sterilized. And the Department of Justice — the institution that once prosecuted Watergate, that once stood as the structural guarantee that no one, not even the president, was above the law — is now in the hands of the president’s personal lawyer.

If Pam Bondi was what incompetent loyalty looks like in the attorney general’s chair, Todd Blanche is about to show America what the competent version can do.

The bill came due for Bondi on April 2, 2026, at 1:07 p.m.

“We love Pam.”

Of course they did. They always do, right up until they don’t.

The question now is not whether they loved Pam. The question is what Todd will do with the weapon she built him.
