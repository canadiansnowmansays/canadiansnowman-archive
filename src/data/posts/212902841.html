The previous instalment closed on housing, the single largest fixed cost most households carry. This one turns to the costs that sit beside it: the revolving and instalment debt a household takes on to bridge the gap when the fixed costs — a mortgage or rent payment, a grocery bill, a tariff-inflated appliance replacement — leave less room than they used to. Credit cards and auto loans are, for a great many Americans, not discretionary borrowing for luxuries. They are the mechanism by which a paycheque-to-paycheque household absorbs an unexpected expense without missing a housing payment, and the price of using that mechanism has climbed to levels that make the absorption itself increasingly expensive. As of this month, that price is climbing again.

Where the rates actually sit

The average credit card interest rate currently sits at roughly 21 percent, according to Federal Reserve data tracking commercial bank rates — a figure that has held near multi-decade highs since the Fed’s own rate-hiking cycle began several years ago, and one that, unlike a mortgage, resets essentially in real time rather than locking in for decades. That real-time reset matters more this month than it has in years. Most credit card rates are variable, pegged to the prime rate, which moves in step with the Federal Reserve’s benchmark — and the Fed raised that benchmark at its September meeting. A cardholder carrying a balance does not need to sign anything or be notified of anything for that increase to arrive; it simply shows up on a statement within a billing cycle or two.

Total revolving consumer debt, the category that includes most credit card balances, now stands at roughly $1.34 trillion nationally — a scale that means even a modest shift in the average rate charged on that balance translates into billions of dollars in additional interest paid across the economy, month after month. A household carrying a balance rather than paying it off in full each month is, at that rate, paying more than a fifth of the outstanding amount in interest annually — a cost that compounds specifically on the households least able to pay a balance down quickly, since carrying a balance in the first place is usually a signal of tighter cash flow rather than a lifestyle choice. Charge-off rates — the share of credit card debt lenders have written off as uncollectable — currently run at about 3.84 percent, itself a signal that a meaningful slice of that $1.34 trillion is not simply high-interest but genuinely unrecoverable, a distinction that separates ordinary revolving debt from debt a household has effectively stopped being able to service at all.

Auto financing tells a similarly uneven story, and the unevenness is worth dwelling on because it maps closely onto exactly the income divide traced in earlier instalments of this series. According to Experian’s most recent State of the Automotive Finance Market report, the average new-car loan carried an interest rate of 6.39 percent in the first quarter of 2026, with an average monthly payment of $770. Used-car buyers — typically the more budget-constrained segment of the car-buying population — paid nearly double that rate, 11.43 percent on average, for a lower monthly payment of $531, reflecting the lower price of the vehicle rather than a better deal on the financing itself. Credit score sorts borrowers into radically different outcomes on the same loan: buyers with excellent credit secured new-car financing at an average of 4.55 percent in the same quarter, while buyers with poor credit paid 16.01 percent — a gap of more than eleven percentage points for what is, mechanically, the identical loan.

The households already showing strain

Rates are one part of the picture; whether households are actually able to keep up with them is the other, and the early signs there are not encouraging. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York’s most recent household debt data found that while aggregate delinquency rates across all consumer debt improved slightly in the second quarter of 2026, holding at 4.7 percent of outstanding balances, the improvement masked a divergence by category. Credit card delinquencies actually rose slightly year over year, from 6.93 percent to 6.97 percent. Auto loans entering serious delinquency rose from 2.93 to 3 percent over the same period. Mortgages entering serious delinquency rose more sharply in percentage terms, from 1.29 to 1.52 percent — a smaller absolute number, but a meaningful directional shift for the single largest debt category most households carry, and one worth watching closely given everything traced in the previous instalment about mortgage-rate pressure. Separate industry tracking of auto-loan delinquency specifically shows the share of loans transitioning into 30-day delinquency running around 7.7 to 8.0 percent through the first half of 2026 — well below the crisis-era peak of nearly 11 percent recorded in 2009, but still elevated relative to the more stable 8-percent-or-below range that held through most of the 2010s, and notably higher among younger borrowers, with one industry tracker specifically flagging Gen Z borrowers as the hardest-hit age cohort.

Joelle Scally, an economic policy advisor at the New York Fed, put the trend plainly: delinquency rates across most debt categories have held roughly steady over the past two years, but new delinquencies for auto loans and credit cards specifically remain elevated, a pattern the bank says it intends to keep monitoring closely. That is a careful, technical way of describing something more direct: a meaningful and apparently persistent slice of American households are falling behind on exactly the kinds of debt — credit cards and car payments — that tend to be the last thing a stretched household lets slip, because losing a car or credit access carries immediate practical consequences a missed subscription payment does not.

The car payment specifically

Vehicles deserve particular attention here because the auto-financing squeeze compounds directly with the tariff-driven vehicle price increases traced in Part III of this series. Nearly one in ten auto loan borrowers now carries a monthly payment of $1,000 or more, according to LendingTree’s tracking of the market — a threshold that would have been considered an extreme outlier a decade ago and is now a mainstream, if still minority, experience. Borrowers who stretch loan terms beyond six years to make a payment fit their budget pay nearly $6,000 more in total interest, on average, than those on shorter terms — a trade that lowers the monthly number a household sees but raises the lifetime cost of the vehicle substantially, and one more households appear to be making as a way of coping with both higher prices and higher rates simultaneously.

It is worth connecting this back explicitly to the tariff mechanics traced earlier in this series. A vehicle that costs more to begin with, because of tariffs on steel, aluminum, and imported components, is also a vehicle that costs more to finance, because auto loan interest is calculated as a percentage of a larger principal. The two pressures do not simply add together — they multiply, since a higher purchase price financed at an unchanged rate produces a proportionally larger increase in the total interest paid over the life of the loan. A household facing both a pricier vehicle and an elevated interest rate is absorbing, in effect, a compounded version of the same underlying cost-of-borrowing story this series has traced from the Federal Reserve’s term premium all the way down to a monthly car payment.

Until recently there was a modest counterweight to note: some forecasters expected auto loan rates to ease slightly through the remainder of 2026 for borrowers with strong credit. That expectation has been overtaken by events. With the Fed now raising rates rather than weighing cuts, the relief that was always going to flow disproportionately to the borrowers who needed it least looks unlikely to flow at all — and the used-car buyers and weaker-credit borrowers already paying double-digit rates were never going to see it in the first place.

The rate that just went up

The backdrop to every figure above shifted in the weeks before this instalment went to press. The Federal Reserve raised interest rates in September, its first major move in years to push back against inflation that has now run above its 2 percent target for more than five years. The Fed chairman, Kevin Warsh, described the decision as withdrawing some accommodation — language that made plain the central bank intended to move again, while saying very little about when or how far.

Markets have filled in the blanks themselves. As The New York Times reported, traders in federal funds futures now put the odds of a second quarter-point increase at roughly 70 percent, which would lift the benchmark to a range of 4 to 4.25 percent. The timing is what makes it a household story as much as a financial one: the Fed’s next meeting runs October 27–28, a matter of days before the November 3 midterms. The Fed insists the electoral calendar plays no part in its decisions, and historically it has moved rates close to elections when conditions warranted. But the practical effect for a voter is the same either way — the cost of carrying a balance or financing a car is likely to be higher on election day than it was when the campaign began.

The reasons are the ones this series has been tracing all along. The unresolved war with Iran has held oil above $100 a barrel and pushed diesel higher, raising the cost of making and shipping nearly everything. The August Consumer Price Index showed inflation running at 3.4 percent over the previous year, up from 2.4 percent in January. And the economy, in aggregate, has been running hot enough — a stronger-than-expected jobs report, robust retail sales, business activity at a five-year high — to give the Fed little reason to wait.

Longer-term borrowing costs have moved even more dramatically than the Fed’s own rate. The yield on the 10-year Treasury — the benchmark that sets the tone for mortgages and much consumer lending — stood at 3.97 percent on February 27, just before the war began. It has since climbed above 5.2 percent, its highest level in roughly twenty years, and posted its biggest single-day jump since the tariff shock of April 2025 earlier this week. Mortgage rates crossed 7 percent for the first time since January 2025, which means the payment traced in the previous instalment is not a ceiling. For households shopping for a home or facing a reset, it is still rising.

For the households at the centre of this instalment, the direct arithmetic of a single quarter-point increase is modest: on a $6,000 card balance, it amounts to roughly $15 a year in additional interest. The danger lies elsewhere. The Fed’s tool for bringing inflation down works by slowing the economy, and slowing the economy means, eventually, softer hiring and fewer hours. Austan Goolsbee, president of the Chicago Fed, warned this week that the process of taming inflation could prove “painful,” acknowledging that returning inflation to target quickly would mean pushing employment below where the Fed wants it. Unemployment currently stands at 4.1 percent. A household keeping its credit card and car payment current is, above all else, a household still drawing a paycheque. The delinquency figures above were recorded in an economy with a firm labour market. The Fed is now openly telling Americans that it may have to loosen that labour market to finish its job.

What this looks like from the top of the economy

It is worth being honest that these figures, read from the perspective of the aggregate economy rather than an individual household, look considerably less alarming than they feel from inside a stretched budget. A 4.7 percent overall delinquency rate, and even the elevated rates in the credit card and auto categories specifically, remain well short of anything resembling a systemic credit crisis, and JPMorgan’s own chief U.S. economist has characterized overall consumer spending as resilient even amid these elevated readings. Corporate earnings are expanding rapidly, driven in large part by the artificial-intelligence boom, and the stock market’s bull run that began in 2022 remains intact.

History offers some perspective on the rates themselves, too. As Jeff Sommer noted in his Strategies column for The Times, rates during the so-called Great Moderation — roughly 1984 to mid-2007 — were on average higher than they are today: a federal funds rate averaging 5.3 percent, a 10-year Treasury yield averaging 6.7 percent, mortgages averaging 8.4 percent. The economy grew faster in that period than it has lately. By that measure, today’s rates are a return to something like normal rather than an unprecedented extreme. Sommer’s point, though, was that the level is less worrying than the speed of the climb and the uncertainty about where it ends — and the Great Moderation itself ended in the financial crisis of 2007. That distinction matters, and this series has tried throughout to avoid claiming a crisis where the aggregate data does not support one.

But an aggregate figure describing the whole economy and a lived experience describing a specific household are two different kinds of truth, and both can be accurate simultaneously. The overall credit system is not obviously fraying. A meaningful and apparently growing number of individual households are nonetheless falling behind on exactly the debts — credit cards and car payments — that function as the last line of defence before a more serious financial event, like a missed mortgage payment or an eviction. Delinquency data is, definitionally, a lagging indicator: by the time a household shows up in the New York Fed’s serious-delinquency figures, the underlying strain has typically been building for months. And the delinquency data now in hand predates September’s rate increase entirely. Read that way, the modest upward drift in these two specific categories, even amid otherwise stable aggregate numbers, looks less like noise and more like an early signal worth taking seriously — the leading edge of the affordability pressures traced throughout this series finally showing up in the place where a household’s ability to cope finally runs out, with the cost of borrowing still on the way up.

What comes next

The next instalment turns to the question underlying every previous one: whether wages, for most Americans, are actually keeping pace with any of this — and what the answer looks like broken down by income bracket rather than averaged into a single reassuring national number. The Fed’s October decision, landing in the final week of the campaign, will be taken up in its own right when it arrives.
