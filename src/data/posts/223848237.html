On Tuesday, November 3, 2026, American voters swept incumbent Republicans out of office across the country, and handed Democrats firm control of both chambers of Congress. They did it, in large part, out of frustration with the economy and the cost of living.

On Friday, November 6, at 8:30 in the morning, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that employers had added 165,000 jobs in October, well above expectations, and that the unemployment rate had fallen to 4.1 per cent. Job gains for August and September were revised up by a combined 84,000. Kevin Hassett, director of the White House National Economic Council, went on CNBC within the hour to call it “runaway job growth.”

If you read only Friday’s numbers, Tuesday makes no sense. If you lived through the past year on an ordinary household budget, Friday’s numbers make no sense either. This series has spent twelve parts arguing that both pictures can be accurate at the same time. Part VIII, written before the vote, was called “The Verdict Being Written Now.” The verdict is in. This part is about why a good jobs report could not have changed it.

What the report said

The headline was genuinely strong. As Ben Casselman reported for The New York Times, the October numbers and the upward revisions together made the recent trend look meaningfully better. Employers have now added an average of 137,000 jobs a month over the past three months. Before Friday’s report, that average stood at just 51,000.

The unemployment rate has been at or below 4.5 per cent every month since September 2021. That is a record streak, longer than the run of low unemployment in the late 1960s. (Whether it counts as 60 or 61 months depends on how you treat October 2025, when the government shutdown meant no jobs report was released at all.) Layoffs remain at historically low levels. Data released earlier in the week showed job openings and hiring both up in September, and more workers quitting voluntarily, which usually means they are confident of finding something better.

Hassett also pointed to low claims for unemployment insurance as a sign that the economy is fundamentally strong. On the narrow question of whether Americans have jobs, he is right. Most of them do.

What the report also said

Casselman summarised the paradox of this labour market in one sentence: most people are working, but if you need a job, it is hard to find one. The October report drives that home. Even as the unemployment rate fell, the average length of time a job seeker had been out of work rose to 27 weeks, the highest since the end of 2021. Nearly 2 million Americans have been out of work for more than six months, the standard definition of long-term unemployment.

That is what economists mean by a “low-hire, low-fire” economy, and it is a strange place to be a worker. If you have a job, you are unlikely to lose it. But you are also unlikely to get a better one, because employers are not hiring briskly, and you know it. Your boss knows it too. That is why, as Sydney Ember put it in the Times’s summary, even people with jobs have been “losing leverage.”

Leverage shows up in pay. Average hourly earnings rose 0.4 per cent in October and 3.1 per cent from a year earlier. On Tuesday, November 10, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that consumer prices also rose 0.4 per cent in October, so for the month, pay and prices finished level. Over the year, they did not. The last official annual inflation rate, for September, was 3.7 per cent, and the Bureau’s own measure of real hourly earnings, pay after inflation, was down 0.7 per cent from a year earlier. A household whose pay rises 3 per cent while its bills rise closer to 4 is going backwards, however secure the job. That is the arithmetic Part VII of this series called “Losing Ground,” and nothing in either of this month’s reports reversed it.

The averages also hide who is carrying the weight. The unemployment rate for Black workers fell four-tenths of a point in October, to 6.6 per cent. That partly reversed a jump in September to 7 per cent, which Talmon Joseph Smith reported for The New York Times on October 19 was the fastest one-month rise since 2012, setting aside the brief pandemic shock. Even after October’s fall, it is far above the 3.7 per cent rate for white workers. Smith found Black professionals hit three ways at once: by the disappearance of corporate diversity jobs, which fell hardest on Black women; by the federal firing spree, because Black workers are disproportionately represented on federal payrolls; and by the white-collar hiring slowdown, which is hardest on those without elite networks. In 2025, Black women lost far more jobs than other women, especially college graduates and public-sector workers. Jared Bernstein, who chaired President Biden’s Council of Economic Advisers, put the warning plainly: “When the economy sniffles, Black unemployment catches a cold.” In October, even Hassett called September’s figure “definitely a cause for concern.”

Gena Pemberton is one of the people inside those numbers. She was chief inclusion and impact officer at Omnicom Health until the company eliminated her position in July 2025, and she has had no full-time work since, despite applying for dozens of jobs a week and lowering her sights on seniority and pay. Because she does patchy consulting work, she may not even count as unemployed. She told Smith she has “gutted almost all my savings” and is now drawing on her 401(k). Hardship withdrawals from 401(k) accounts have more than doubled in recent years, partly because the long-term unemployed are spending their retirement savings to get by. That is the household ledger at its starkest: a job market that looks healthy from the top, and a 50-year-old professional emptying her retirement account to stay afloat inside it.

Where the jobs came from

The sector breakdown tells a second story about the economy the administration said it was building.

Manufacturing added 19,000 jobs in October, its fifth straight month of gains. That sounds like the factory revival President Trump has made the centrepiece of his economic agenda. But as Casselman noted, the sector has still lost jobs since the end of 2024. Five good months have not made up the ground.

Professional and business services, a broad category that includes accountants, travel agents and many other office jobs, added 70,000. The financial sector added 7,000. The information sector, which includes much of the technology industry and the media, shrank slightly, as it has been doing steadily since late 2022. Whether artificial intelligence is behind that slow erosion of white-collar work is the most argued-over question in economics this year, and the evidence is still mixed.

The clearest A.I. effect is in jobs nobody associates with chatbots. Casselman reported that the construction of data centres is propping up employment in construction, even as high interest rates have caused a sharp pullback in home building. Demand for chips and A.I. equipment is creating manufacturing jobs that offset losses elsewhere in the sector. Voters on November 3 approved dozens of local measures to halt or restrict data-centre development. Some of the building-trades jobs in Friday’s report depend on projects those voters have just slowed down.

The price tag on the shelf

If the jobs were there, why did voters feel so badly about the economy? Brad Setser, an economist at the Council on Foreign Relations, gave Ana Swanson of the Times the most precise answer of the week. Voters’ cost-of-living anger, he said, was driven more by the high price of gas, housing and medical care than by tariffs. But tariffs were in the mix, and in a way that a household could feel at the checkout.

Swanson reported on November 4 that the U.S. trade deficit was $105 billion in September, the second-largest monthly total since Trump imposed his global tariffs in April 2025. Imports hit a record $426.6 billion. The tariffs were meant to shrink that number. They have not, partly because the administration has largely exempted the goods that make up much of the value of American imports, such as pharmaceuticals and the expensive A.I. chips and computers being shipped in to fill data centres. Everyday consumer products, such as furniture, toys and clothing, were not exempted.

“If you’re going to Walmart and you’re surprised at the cost of some products, that is partly tariffs,” Setser said. “There’s an extent to which the tariffs have been designed to hit people shopping at Walmart and not people building data centers.”

That sentence could stand as the epigraph for this whole series. Part III, “The Receipt at the End of the Trade War,” and Part IV, “The Cart That Costs More Every Week,” followed the tariff bill to the household. The trade figures released the day after the election confirm that the bill landed where those parts said it would, and that the biggest corporate importers were spared it.

The mood, measured

There is a direct measure of how Americans feel, as opposed to how the economy performs, and it was released on Friday too. The University of Michigan’s preliminary survey showed consumer sentiment up 6 per cent from October. That is the good news. The rest is less cheerful. Sentiment is flat compared with a year ago, and still 10 per cent below where it stood in February, when the United States and Israel launched strikes against Iran and oil prices began their climb.

The war is the part of the household ledger that no jobs report can offset. Colby Smith reported in The New York Times that the Federal Reserve counts the energy shock from the war with Iran, along with the spending boom by technology companies, among the primary drivers of inflation, which has now overshot the Fed’s 2 per cent target for nearly six years. Every tank of gas and every heating bill this autumn carried some of that cost.

The one annual figure the bureau could publish for energy was the one households already knew. Gasoline cost 37.4 per cent more in October than a year earlier, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Pump prices eased during the month, from an average of $4.41 a gallon to $4.22, The Guardian reported, citing AAA. But they remain more than a dollar above where they stood a year ago, and diesel is nearly $2.50 higher.

Diesel matters to households that never fill a truck. Heating oil is almost the same fuel, and the same war has squeezed its supply, as refineries in the Middle East and Russia have shut down or cut production. On November 11, Ivan Penn reported in The New York Times that home heating costs are expected to rise more than 7 per cent on average across the country this winter, according to the National Energy Assistance Directors Association. For the almost five million households that heat with oil, most of them in the Northeast and Alaska, the bill could rise by as much as 70 per cent, to about $2,977 for the season. Electric heat is expected to cost about 6 per cent more, and propane about 5 per cent more. Only natural gas is holding roughly steady. Even before this winter, as many as one in six households had fallen behind on gas and electricity bills, according to the Energy Information Administration. "We hope we're wrong," said Mark Wolfe, the association's executive director, "but states need to prepare for the possibility that prices will stay high."

Housing is the other weight. Gregory Schmidt reported in The New York Times that sales of existing homes fell 2.2 per cent in September from a year earlier, as inflation worries and high mortgage rates kept buyers away. Jake Krimmel, a senior economist at Realtor.com, hoped Friday’s report might help. “A more dynamic labor market with real wage growth is sorely needed for buying power and demand,” he said. The operative words are “real wage growth.” At 3.1 per cent, after a year in which pay has mostly trailed prices, households do not yet have it.

The receipt for October

Four days after the jobs report, households got the report that describes their side of the ledger. The Bureau of Labor Statistics said on Tuesday that consumer prices rose 0.4 per cent in October. That was slower than September’s 0.6 per cent, but faster than the 0.3 per cent economists had expected. Core prices, which leave out food and energy, rose 0.3 per cent for the third month in a row. Food rose 0.5 per cent, electricity 0.9 per cent and shelter 0.3 per cent. Used cars were the one notable relief, down 1.2 per cent.

There is one number the report could not give. The government shutdown last autumn meant the bureau never collected prices in October 2025, so it cannot say how much overall prices have risen over the past year. The shutdown that cost the country a jobs report a year ago has now cost it an inflation rate. Economists filled the gap with estimates: about 4 per cent by FactSet’s consensus and 3.8 per cent by Bank of America’s, 24/7 Wall St. reported. Either is higher than the official 3.7 per cent for September, and close to double the Fed’s target. Measured over just the last three months, prices have been rising at an annualised pace of 5.7 per cent.

That is the receipt a household was holding when Hassett called the jobs numbers “runaway.” A low-hire economy with 3 per cent raises and 4 per cent inflation is not running away from anything. It is running in place.

The rate that comes next

A strong jobs report is not purely good news for a household with debt, and October’s will almost certainly mean higher borrowing costs.

The Fed raised rates in September, held them in October, and signalled a further increase in December. Smith reported that the jobs report affirms the Fed's view that the labour market is on solid footing, which frees officials to keep their attention on inflation. Kevin Warsh, the Fed chair, had settled on raising rates gradually. He made a point of saying that the Fed's goal is not "to throw sand in the gears of the economy and throw people out of work." After the jobs report, Matthew Martin of Oxford Economics wrote that the data, with inflation well above target, "adds to the case for a December rate hike." Hassett himself had said in late October that if October's inflation came out "really high," the Fed "would probably appropriately act," American Banker reported.

Tuesday’s report did more than make the case for a December rise. It called the gradual approach itself into question. Hours before the numbers came out, Christopher Waller, a Fed governor, said that if core prices rose around 0.3 per cent or more, “a stronger policy move may be needed,” and that sticking to the usual quarter-point pace would “put our credibility at risk.” Core prices rose 0.3 per cent. Smith reported in The New York Times on November 11 that price increases had spread to food, appliances, recreation and motor vehicle insurance, and that Fed officials now agree rates must rise but are divided on how fast: a half-point move in December, or quarter-point rises at back-to-back meetings. Markets have pencilled in more increases for 2027. Matthew Luzzetti, chief U.S. economist at Deutsche Bank, now expects quarter-point rises in December, January and March.

Neil Dutta, head of economic research at Renaissance Macro, put the dilemma in terms Warsh will not enjoy. “If they’re not willing to hurt the labor market to bring inflation back to 2 percent, then they’re not willing to bring inflation back to 2 percent,” he said. “It’s really that simple.” For the household in this series, that is the choice laid bare: the job Friday’s report said was safe, or the prices Tuesday’s report said are still rising.

For a household, that means credit-card rates, variable-rate loans and the mortgage rates that track long-term yields are unlikely to fall soon. Part VI of this series, “The Cost of Borrowing to Get By,” followed families leaning on credit to cover the gap between their pay and their bills. A strong labour market is good for those families’ jobs. It is not good for their interest payments.

The verdict, delivered

Tony Romm put the political lesson most simply in The New York Times on Friday morning: the jobs data reinforced a point even some Republicans had made before the rout. There were bright spots in the economy, including stable hiring, but none were enough to change the minds of voters hammered by persistent inflation.

That is the gap this series was built to describe. An economy can create jobs and still leave the people in those jobs worse off. A labour market can have record-low unemployment and still be a miserable place to look for work. A trade policy can fail to shrink the deficit and still succeed in raising prices at Walmart. Each of those statements is true, and the White House kept citing the first half of each one while voters lived in the second half.

Hassett’s “runaway job growth” is the same move this series has tracked since Part II, “I’ve Won Affordability”: announce the good number, ignore the household. Voters were not confused about the jobs report. They had their own data, in their own receipts, and on November 3 they reported it.

What changes now is less certain. The new Democratic majorities have promised relief on prices, but the levers that matter most to a household budget, gas prices, interest rates and the war, are not ones Congress controls directly, and the president still holds the veto. The Fed meets again on December 9, and after Tuesday’s inflation report, markets treat a rate rise as all but certain. The ledger stays open.
