Some weeks the news arrives in a single stream. Last week it came in three: a summit in Shenzhen, a lecture series in Toronto, and, closer to home than I expected, a phone bill on national radio.

If you’ve just arrived, welcome. I write from Toronto, mostly about the United States, with history, AI and Canada’s own quarrels along for ballast. The satire is labelled. The rest is sourced.

The Shenzhen Opera followed the summit act by act, in real time. Trump arrived late and weakened, his own cabinet rewriting the score behind him. For the first time the leaders of America, China and Russia shared one stage, and everyone reached for the word Yalta. Moscow and Beijing published what they had agreed. Washington published nothing for a day, then a review of the aeroplane, the welcome and the man. Ukraine, which was not in the room, answered in its own way: it dropped the Crimean Bridge into the sea. To keep those stories side by side, the section that used to be called US at War is now simply War.

Across town from me, The Hinton Lectures: Three Nights in Toronto covered Paul Christiano’s three lectures for the AI Safety Foundation. He put a high risk on an A.I. takeover within three years, said the machines may soon be building themselves, and asked us to slow down now so that we still can later. Geoffrey Hinton said he had not believed the timeline until that week. It was not a comfortable series to write, and The Asimov Gap spent the rest of the week on the same ground.

Then there was the broadcast. CBC Radio’s Cost of Living aired my advance-billing complaint nationally, in an episode about listener grievances, and filed it, more or less, under petty. The next morning Quebecor bid $31 billion to turn four national wireless carriers into three. Your Phone Bill and Mine puts the two side by side, and lets the reader decide which one is the grievance. The CRTC hearing opens in Gatineau a week from tomorrow, and I’ll have a summary in December.

Two new series opened. The Shovel Seller follows the Nvidia chips that reached China anyway, and the courtship that turned a ban into a deal. An Oligarch Review asked why stop at two billionaires, and lined up the field. Elsewhere, The Third Rail reached the cliff: not bankruptcy in 2032, but a 22 per cent cut for every retiree at once if Congress does nothing.

Two series reached their final curtain. The Conditional Women closed with The Ones Who Stayed (Until They Didn’t): women still hold real power in Trump’s Washington, but on terms the men around them have never been asked to accept. The Churchill Inheritance ended where it began, with Anthony Scaramucci’s essay The Long Trade, which first set me wondering what Churchill’s dividend was really worth. My thanks to him for starting a sixteen-part conversation he may not have known he was having. Coming soon, as a companion to The Endless Frontier, is Nature Isn’t Classical, a history of quantum computing for those of us who don’t have the foggiest idea what it is, from the physicists who first noticed that nature refuses to behave to Q-Day, when a quantum machine could break the codes the world runs on.

The satire went to the movies. James Bond got a new Double-O who sparkles in daylight and a villain shortlist outbid by the morning headlines; the Shenzhen framework agreement was negotiated in the tradition of the Marx Brothers; and a retired Baby Boomer, who may sound familiar, filed for a day off from nothing to play Grand Theft Auto VI. Then there is my own favourite of the week, which I’ll leave you to find and ponder for yourself: Harry Honeypotter and the Chamber of Commons, a declassified file on a Minister of War, a swimming pool and a naval attaché who asked a great many questions. Some of you will recognise the year. Everyone will recognise the cross-reference.

That’s the lead. The full week follows.
