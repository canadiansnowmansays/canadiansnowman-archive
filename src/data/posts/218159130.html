Stephen Gordon · October 3, 2026

There is a particular kind of honesty that only appears in a conference room near Capitol Hill, in front of a dozen donors, when the candidate assumes nobody is holding a phone.

On Thursday, September 24, at a small fund-raiser hosted by two Republican lobbyists, someone asked Ken Paxton whether “he” was a bit of a drag. The recording doesn’t make clear who “he” was. Paxton’s answer made it clear enough: “So to be honest with you, the numbers — when we did that convention — it dropped our numbers. Everybody’s numbers dropped. Right now, yeah, not good.”

Theodore Schleifer reported the recording in the New York Times on September 30, 2026, in “G.O.P. Nominee in Texas Privately Said Trump’s Convention Hurt Him.” The attendee who made it opposes Paxton; three others confirmed he was there, and one remembered the answer. Paxton’s senior adviser Nick Maddux, asked to explain, did not explain. He called it “manipulated nonsense,” cited an internal poll showing Paxton up three, and promised his candidate would close strong alongside President Trump.

Regular readers will recognize the O in TACO — Obfuscate — arriving right on schedule. What’s new is that, for once, the thing being obfuscated is something Paxton himself said.

By that afternoon, John Cornyn — the senator Paxton ousted in the May runoff with Trump’s blessing — had delivered the definitive review. “I just have a one-word reaction,” he told reporters. “Whoops!” The president’s numbers, Cornyn added, “are not what they used to be even in ruby red Texas,” and the usual wisdom is to localize a race, not nationalize it — “but the president seems determined to make the election about him at a time when we’ve got 6-plus dollar diesel prices.” There are few pleasures in politics purer than being proved right by the man who beat you.

When we last talked Texas, on August 14th, the question was whether a decade of scandal had finally caught up with Paxton. Now it is whether the man at the top of his ticket has become a drag on him too. The uncomfortable answer for Republicans is that both appear true.

The Convention That Was Supposed to Save Him

It is worth remembering whose idea Dallas was. Paxton himself announced, back in June, that the midterm convention would come to Texas, and Trump said Paxton was the speaker the party most wanted to showcase. “Trumpapalooza,” as it came to be called, was at least partly a rescue mission for a nominee who had spent the summer outspent and outpolled.

What the rescue looked like, as the Texas Tribune reported on September 9, was the president vowing to campaign for Paxton right after an unscripted riff mocking his speech and his looks — allowing that Paxton might not be the best-looking man he’d ever seen, but was the greatest attorney general in America. It takes a rare talent to insult a candidate in the act of endorsing him.

Days later, Trump insisted the convention had “helped Ken Paxton a lot.” Paxton, in private, said the opposite. One of them is wrong, and the polling suggests the answer is more interesting than either man would like.

What the Numbers Actually Say

The Emerson College poll, taken September 12–14 — the first public survey in the field after Dallas — found Talarico at 47 and Paxton at 46, and essentially no movement since August. The Times itself concedes the convention left no visible mark on public surveys of the race.

So Paxton’s numbers may not have dropped because of Dallas. The more damning reading is that they didn’t rise, in a state Trump carried by nearly 14 points.

The rest of September agreed. Texas Public Opinion Research had Talarico up 49 to 44; the NBC 5/Telemundo poll had him up three; Big Data Poll up four. Marist put him ahead 50 to 44 among registered voters — while, remarkably, 52 percent of all voters still expect Paxton to win. That isn’t a contradiction; it’s thirty years of habit. Texas hasn’t sent a Democrat to the Senate since 1993, and voters are pricing in gravity. Paxton’s claimed internal lead is, in effect, a bet on gravity.

The forecasters have stopped betting on it. The Cook Political Report moved the race to Toss-Up on August 20, six days after our last instalment, and it has stayed there.

The Valley That Stopped Waiting

On July 3rd, this series argued that a quieter white-suburban defection might matter as much as the Hispanic one. Three months later, the Hispanic story has grown louder, and it now has faces.

Jennifer Medina and Edgar Sandoval spent days across the Rio Grande Valley for “Hispanic Voters on the Texas Border Have Soured on Trump” (New York Times, September 30, 2026). The region’s 2024 swing to the Republicans, they found, is draining away faster than it arrived. The Trump flags that once covered front yards and pickup trucks, Medina wrote, have simply vanished.

The people in that piece are not activists. Jessica Cantu Vuittonet, who runs a small San Benito coffee shop, cast her first-ever ballot for Trump. Then her brother, who had lived here for decades, was detained days after a routine immigration check-in. She says she has no plans to vote this year, and has concluded that the two parties are just two colours on the same indifference.

Ariel Salinas, a McAllen builder, says builders started fencing job sites over the summer — not against thieves, he told the Times, but against immigration agents — and fencing itself briefly ran short. Benny Melendez in Weslaco used to run ten builds at once; now he manages four, and says the tariffs hurt him before the raids did. Merchants on McAllen’s main street describe foot traffic down to pandemic levels.

And there is Pastor Luis Cabrera of Harlingen, who spent years steering his congregation toward the Republican Party and now chairs the Cameron County GOP’s faith committee. His warning to national Republicans is the sharpest line in the piece: these are “baby Republicans,” brand-new to the party, and a party that breaks its promises to new converts should not expect them to stay converted.

Not everyone has turned. Jerry Hernandez of McAllen told the Times Trump is simply enforcing the law he was elected to enforce. But the arithmetic has moved against him. The Times reports Democrats leading among likely Hispanic voters by roughly 35 points on the generic congressional ballot. Emerson has Talarico up 15 among Hispanic Texans; NBC/Telemundo, polling in both languages, has him up 18.

What turned it, for many in the Valley, was Houston. Lorenzo Salgado Araujo — 52, a construction crew leader, 35 years in the country, no criminal record, not the intended target — was shot and killed by an ICE agent during a traffic stop on July 7th. The agent’s identity was withheld for nearly three months, until the Texas Tribune named him on September 28. A three-time Trump voter who chairs the South Texas Builders Association told the Times it showed an administration that had lost control of ICE.

Texas Republicans redrew several House districts last year assuming the 2024 Hispanic swing was permanent. The 15th, 28th and 34th, across the Valley, were drawn to be won. Those assumptions are load-bearing walls in the party’s House majority, and the walls are moving.

The Rescue Mission, Minus the Candidate

On September 30, the morning both Times stories landed, Vice President JD Vance flew into Brownsville. The Times’ live coverage, “Election Dispatches: Vance Visits South Texas as Support Fades in Key G.O.P. Stronghold,” followed him through two stops that told the whole story.

The first was a shipyard groundbreaking at the Port of Brownsville — the port Vance skipped in August. Beside him, with Governor Greg Abbott, stood Congressman Vicente Gonzalez, the moderate Democrat who held his Trump-carried district in 2024, thanking them both for the “political diversity.” Then Vance drove to Harlingen to rally for Eric Flores, the Republican trying to unseat him.

Republicans redrew that district so Trump would have carried it by ten points; it is now among the most competitive House races in the country.

At the Harlingen Convention Center, Vance opened by shouting out Ken Paxton. Paxton was not there. Vance closed by turning on James Talarico, the seminarian, calling him a “deeply unserious person” whose “real religion” is “crazy left-wing politics.” Talarico, laid up with the flu, was meanwhile telling the U.S. Hispanic Business Council by video link that “the best color for Texas is purple,” because real competition “keeps all politicians on their toes.” One man was making an argument to Hispanic business owners; the other, about another man’s faith.

The next afternoon, Thursday, October 1, the president himself came to Denton, north of Dallas, to speak at a Peterbilt truck plant, pitching manufacturing and his trade policies. Paxton, J. David Goodman reported that morning in the Times’ live coverage (”Election Dispatches: Trump Heads to Texas as Some Republicans Seek Distance on Immigration”), was expected to appear at his side, his spokesman having dismissed the recording as “nonsense.” So the man who told donors the president’s convention had cost him was, two days later, booked to be photographed with the president.

There was no graceful way to play it. Stand beside Trump, and he stands beside the man he privately called a drag. Stay away — as he did in Harlingen — and he confirms the recording. Both doors open onto the same room.

He is not alone in that room. The same dispatch catalogued Republicans edging away from the president on the very issue Trump says won him the White House. Senator Jim Justice of West Virginia, a state Trump carried by more than 40 points, told Politico on September 29 that “you’re dragging people away that have lived on your street corner for 15 years, and I don’t know that we need to be doing that stuff” — then, a day later, declared his support “full stop.” Congresswoman María Elvira Salazar of Miami ran an ad telling Trump to his face that some enforcement “has gone too far,” and the president responded by suggesting Republicans replace her on the ballot. Texas cattle feeders warned that ICE raids are chilling the documented workers who “put beef on the table.” A national Times/Siena poll found 64 percent of independents disapprove of Trump’s handling of immigration, with voters now trusting Democrats more on the issue, 52 to 45. What the Valley lives, Washington now reads as a poll number.

Even the family has noticed the draught: Lara Trump said this week that the Iran war “could cost him these midterms.”

A Candidate Made of Other People’s Money

At the convention, Paxton made a second admission, this one in public: he needed financial backup. It arrived.

AdImpact data reported by KERA projects $440 million in ad spending by November 3rd — double the Texas Senate record. By mid-September, conservative super PACs had spent $98.5 million; Paxton’s own campaign, $465,000. The Dallas Morning News calculated that in the weeks after the convention, Paxton’s own committees accounted for less than one percent of the advertising on his behalf.

The Senate Leadership Fund alone has committed $100 million — the group aligned with Majority Leader John Thune, who spent the primary backing Cornyn precisely because he feared a Paxton nomination would come to this. Trump’s MAGA Inc. has added another $20 million.

On September 30, as J. David Goodman reported in the Times’ live coverage, the Paxton campaign finally announced a $20 million ad buy of its own, starting Friday, October 2, in Houston, Dallas, Austin and San Antonio before spreading statewide. Five weeks out, the candidate has arrived at his own campaign.

The race has become a strange mirror: a Democrat whose own campaign pays for his ads, against a Republican whose ads, until this week, were paid for by everyone but his campaign. For a man whose central liability is the suspicion that he treats public office as a private income stream — recall the Frisco hospital cell tower — it is not an ideal look.

It is also, of course, a very expensive vote of no confidence. Nobody spends $100 million to protect a safe seat.

The Debate That Isn’t

If there is one number that says more than any poll, it is zero — the number of debates Ken Paxton has agreed to.

Talarico accepted three televised debates in July; the first, in the Valley on September 22, came and went without Paxton. Fox News has now agreed to host one if both commit — Talarico offered to meet Paxton in his “safe space” and left a voicemail on Paxton’s personal phone to make the point. Paxton’s campaign has said for three months that he will debate, just not on Talarico’s terms. Texas A&M students have invited both men to College Station on October 14; CNN has scheduled a Talarico town hall for the same night. Early voting starts October 19.

Even Jeff Leach — a 2023 impeachment manager who has since joined Paxton’s campaign — says he should debate. When the man who once tried to remove you from office urges you onto a stage, the advice is probably sound.

A debate would also force Paxton to answer the newest charge in Talarico’s arsenal. As Shaila Dewan reported in “Crime as a G.O.P. Line of Attack Loses Traction” (New York Times, September 30, 2026), Talarico has accused Paxton of an “Epstein-style sweetheart deal” — a plea arrangement from Paxton’s office that would initially have let a child molester spend a single day in jail, and did not require him to register as a sex offender. Paxton’s office says the deal was struck because the victim was unwilling to testify. It is an answer worth hearing under questioning. So far it has come only by statement.

The Crack on Talarico’s Side of the Ledger

Talarico has a trouble of his own.

In August, this series flagged the grievance among Black Texans who felt the primary against Congresswoman Jasmine Crockett had been racially charged, and the $25 million his campaign set aside for Black voter outreach. That money has since gone to Houston’s Wheeler Avenue Baptist Church, the HBCU State Fair Classic at Prairie View A&M and a “Souls to the Polls” partnership with Black faith leaders.

But on September 28, Crockett released a new slate of down-ballot endorsements — and Talarico wasn’t on it. Her plan is to lift Black turnout through local races, which helps Talarico whether she names him or not. But a Prairie View political scientist told Axios he isn’t seeing the campus energy Beto O’Rourke generated in 2018. Black Texans are roughly 13 percent of the electorate. In a race inside the margin of error, 13 percent of anything is decisive.

The symmetry is almost too neat. Paxton’s coalition is leaking because his party broke faith with voters who had only just arrived; Talarico’s is at risk because some voters believe his party broke faith with them long ago. Only one of them has been recorded admitting the problem.

What Comes Next

Meanwhile, Reid J. Epstein reported in the same Times dispatch, the Democratic Governors Association still won’t put $10 million behind Gina Hinojosa, who is within reach of Greg Abbott despite being outspent on ads by more than fourteen to one. Republicans are spending as though Texas is in play. Some national Democrats still aren’t.

The calendar is short enough to read at a glance. The Dallas debate on October 6 that Paxton hasn’t accepted. The October 14 night that will bring either a debate in College Station or a Talarico town hall on CNN with an empty chair implied. Early voting from October 19. Election Day, November 3rd.

Somewhere in that stretch, Paxton will have to decide what he believes about the man at the top of his party. In public, he has spent a decade insisting he and Trump are the same kind of fighter, persecuted by the same enemies. In private, in front of a dozen people who write cheques, he said the president’s party-in-a-box hadn’t helped.

Michael Kinsley famously defined a gaffe as a politician accidentally telling the truth. By that measure, September 24 was the first real gaffe of Ken Paxton’s career. A decade of indictments, impeachment articles, a pocketed pen and an undisclosed cell tower never cost him an election. It would be very Texan, and very fitting, if the thing that finally did was a few muffled seconds of candour.
