Canadian Snowman’s Guide to the Most Important Meeting Nobody Expects to Matter

Senator Steve Daines, former P&G salesman and now Trump’s improbable diplomatic advance man shaking hands with Wang Yi.

Before Donald Trump could go to China, Steve Daines had to go first. Twice.

This is not, in the annals of great-power diplomacy, an unusual arrangement. Summits between heads of state are not spontaneous events. They are the culmination of weeks or months of preparatory work by subordinates whose job is to identify areas of agreement, map areas of disagreement, and ensure that the principals walk into the room knowing exactly what they are going to say and, more importantly, what they are going to pretend they did not discuss. The advance work is where the real diplomacy happens. The summit is where the diplomacy is photographed.

In the case of the Trump-Xi summit scheduled for May 14 and 15 in Beijing, the advance work fell to an improbable figure: a Republican senator from Montana who spent thirteen years selling Folgers coffee and Pampers diapers for Procter & Gamble in China before entering politics. Steve Daines is not a diplomat. He is not a foreign policy strategist. He is a man who knows how to move consumer goods across cultural boundaries, which — when you think about it for more than a few seconds — may be exactly the skill set this particular moment required. The summit, after all, was shaping up to be less about strategy than about transactions: soybeans, Boeings, beef, wheat, pulse crops, potatoes, apples, cherries, grain sorghum, and seafood. If you squint hard enough, the agenda reads less like a diplomatic framework and more like the inventory sheet at a Costco distribution centre. Washington had even developed its own shorthand: the “Five B’s” — Boeing, beef, board of investment, board of trade, and (I am not making this up) soybeans, which begin with the letter S but were apparently admitted to the club on a technicality. Beijing, for its part, was pushing the “Three T’s”: tariffs, technology, and Taiwan. The asymmetry between the two lists tells you everything you need to know about the asymmetry of the two agendas. One side was shopping. The other side was negotiating the future of the western Pacific.

Daines was making his seventh trip to China since being elected to Congress. He sits on the Senate Foreign Relations Committee. He is a close Trump ally. And he was the only senior American figure who appeared to have both the interest and the operational history to serve as the connective tissue between two governments that had spent the better part of three months failing to prepare for a meeting that both sides had publicly described as the most important diplomatic event of the year.

The first trip was in late March, when Daines became the first US senator to visit Hong Kong since 2019.

That date — 2019 — is itself a small history lesson. The last time an American senator had set foot in Hong Kong, the city was in the grip of pro-democracy protests that would culminate in Beijing’s imposition of the National Security Law, the effective end of Hong Kong’s political autonomy, and the imprisonment of, among others, the British citizen and newspaper publisher Jimmy Lai, who was sentenced to twenty years in December 2025. The seven-year absence of American senators from Hong Kong was not an oversight. It was a diplomatic signal, and Daines’s return was a counter-signal — a deliberate indication that the United States was willing to re-engage with Beijing through channels that had been closed since the relationship’s low point.

Daines visited Hong Kong to meet with American business leaders. He went to Shanghai. He went to Beijing. The trip had originally been scheduled to coincide with the summit itself, before the Iran delay pushed both events into May. Daines was, in effect, running reconnaissance — testing the diplomatic temperature, assessing Beijing’s mood, and relaying what he found back to a White House that had been roundly criticized for inadequate preparation.

What he found, by most accounts, was a Beijing that was frustrated but pragmatic. Chinese officials were annoyed by the lack of staff-level preparation, irritated by the postponement, and skeptical of the White House’s ability to deliver anything of substance. But they were also clear-eyed about the stakes. The trade truce from Busan was holding, but barely. The rare earth controls were a card Beijing could play at any time. The Strait of Hormuz crisis had given China leverage it preferred to use quietly rather than publicly. And the alternative to a summit — no summit — was worse for both sides, because the absence of a meeting would signal a breakdown that neither government wanted to trigger six months before the American midterm elections.

The Costco delegation: Boeing, beef, soybeans, and pulse crops. Agenda approved.

The second trip began on May 1, and it was a different operation entirely.

Daines returned to China leading a bipartisan delegation of five senators: Maria Cantwell, a Democrat from Washington state; Jerry Moran, a Republican from Kansas; and Deb Fischer, a Republican from Nebraska. The composition was deliberate. Cantwell represents the state where Boeing builds the 737 MAX in Renton. Moran and Fischer represent agricultural states with direct commercial interests in Chinese markets. This was not a fact-finding mission. It was a sales call, dressed in the language of statecraft.

The delegation held three official meetings in Beijing over the course of a week: with Premier Li Qiang at the Great Hall of the People, with National People’s Congress Chairman Zhao Leji, and with Foreign Minister Wang Yi. The agenda, as released by Daines’s Senate office, covered cooperation on fentanyl precursors, Iran and the Strait of Hormuz, supply chain security, reciprocal trade, and the Boeing aircraft purchase.

Li Qiang told Daines that China hoped to maintain “stable and predictable” trade ties with the United States — a phrase that, in the context of a trade relationship that had been anything but stable or predictable for the past eight years, carried the weight of diplomatic understatement. Li called for “more dialogue rather than confrontation” and “more mutually beneficial cooperation rather than zero-sum games.” These are the phrases that Chinese officials deploy when they want to simultaneously extend an olive branch and remind the audience that the alternative to the olive branch is a stick. They are phrases that Hugh Keenleyside, the Canadian diplomat who spent his career translating between empires and their subjects, would have recognized as the language of a power that believes it holds the stronger hand but prefers not to show it.

Wang Yi, for his part, signalled that Taiwan would be a priority topic at the summit. This was neither a surprise nor, in diplomatic terms, negotiable. Taiwan has been China’s non-negotiable issue since 1949, and every American administration since Nixon has understood that any meeting with Beijing will include the Taiwan conversation whether Washington wants it or not. What had changed was the specific ask: Washington was now hearing from multiple channels that Beijing wanted a shift in American declaratory language — from “does not support” Taiwan independence to “opposes” Taiwan independence. The difference between those two formulations looks trivial on paper. In the grammar of cross-strait relations, it is the difference between a neutral posture and an active commitment, and Taipei was, by all accounts, very worried that Trump — whose interest in linguistic precision has never been his defining characteristic — might concede the shift in a one-on-one with Xi without fully understanding what he had given away.

Daines, for his part, struck the notes his audience expected. “I strongly believe that we want to de-escalate, not decouple,” he told reporters. “We want stability, we want mutual respect.” He expressed hope that the summit would produce Boeing purchases, which was the closest thing to candour the diplomatic setting allowed — the man from Montana was saying, in public, that the point of the meeting was to sell airplanes. He also acknowledged China’s role in easing tensions in West Asia, pointing to Wang Yi’s meeting with Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi as evidence of Beijing’s engagement. This was the diplomatic trade in miniature: we appreciate your help on Iran, now please buy our airplanes.

The Boeing dimension deserves its own examination, because it illustrates with unusual clarity how summits of this kind actually work.

China has not placed a major Boeing order since 2017 — the last time Trump visited Beijing. That order, announced with great fanfare as part of a $37 billion commercial package, involved 300 aircraft. It subsequently stalled. Some of the planes were never delivered. The deal became a cautionary tale about the difference between a summit announcement and a commercial reality, and it hung over the current negotiations like a footnote that nobody wanted to read aloud.

The proposed new deal was substantially larger: up to 500 Boeing 737 MAX aircraft, plus approximately 100 widebody jets including 787 Dreamliners and 777X models. At list prices, the package would be worth tens of billions of dollars, though actual transaction values in aviation are always significantly discounted from published figures. The aircraft would go to multiple Chinese carriers — Air China, China Southern, Hainan Airlines, and others — in a structure consistent with past Chinese aircraft orders announced through state purchasing channels.

For Boeing, the stakes were existential in the way that very large companies experience existential stakes — not as a question of survival but as a question of trajectory. The company had spent years rebuilding after the 737 MAX grounding, the pandemic, and a series of safety and manufacturing crises. China was the first country to ground the MAX in 2019. Chinese regulators were among the last to clear its return to service. A major order would signal that the relationship was healed, that the aircraft was trusted, and that Boeing's commercial future was not entirely dependent on Western markets. Boeing's CEO, Kelly Ortberg, was expected to join Trump's delegation to Beijing, along with Elon Musk of Tesla and SpaceX, Tim Cook of Apple (soon to retire), and the top executives of Nvidia, Exxon, Qualcomm, Blackstone, BlackRock, Goldman Sachs, Citigroup, and Visa — a travelling circus of American capitalism making the pilgrimage to the Chinese market. Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth was announced as an apparently last-minute addition. And then, in a detail that tells you everything about the improvised quality of this trip, Jensen Huang — the CEO of Nvidia, the world's most valuable company, who had been omitted from the official delegation list — was invited by Trump in a Tuesday morning phone call, flew to Alaska, and boarded Air Force One during a refuelling stop. Trump posted that it was an honour to have "the Great Jensen Huang" joining him, and asked Xi to "open up" China so that "these brilliant people can work their magic." The advance work continued, it seemed, even at thirty-five thousand feet.

But here is where the 2017 ghost becomes instructive. That deal, too, had been announced with great ceremony. It, too, had been framed as a breakthrough. And it, too, had been more photograph than contract. The question hanging over the 2026 Boeing deal was whether it would be any different — whether the airplanes would actually be built, delivered, and paid for, or whether they would serve the same function as the 2017 order: a headline number designed to make both leaders look successful, subsequently forgotten when the relationship deteriorated again.

The Foreign Policy analysis of five decades of US-China summits was blunt on this point: the real indicators of success are whether a summit creates processes rather than photo ops. A Boeing order creates a headline. A functioning “Board of Trade” creates a process. The question is which one the summit will actually produce, and the historical record is not encouraging.

There is a darker dimension to the Boeing deal that the headline numbers obscure. Xin Qiang, director of the Center for Taiwan Studies at Fudan University, said it plainly: if the United States sells more weapons to Taiwan, “China certainly won’t be as enthusiastic about purchasing goods from the United States like agricultural products or Boeing aircraft.” The Boeings and the weapons are on the same scale. Buy one, lose the other. The 500-aircraft deal is not merely a commercial transaction. It is a lever — and the lever’s other end is a $14 billion arms package for Taiwan that the White House has been sitting on for months. Ryan Hass, the former National Security Council director for China now at Brookings, said he has “low expectations that Trump will approve another tranche of arms sales to Taiwan between now and this fall.” The Boeings, in other words, may be the price of Taiwan’s security — a trade that neither side will acknowledge publicly but that both sides understand perfectly well.

While Daines was selling airplanes and stability in Beijing, the physical infrastructure of the summit was taking shape around him.

The advance work that actually got done

On May 1, the first US Air Force C-17 Globemaster III transport aircraft landed at Beijing Capital International Airport. A second followed the next day. British-based military aircraft trackers reported two more en route. The C-17 is a heavy-lift cargo plane routinely used to transport a president’s motorcade and support equipment before overseas trips, and its arrival in Beijing confirmed what the diplomatic tea-leaf readers had been debating for weeks: the trip was going ahead.

By May 7, the evidence was rolling down Beijing’s highways. Two black SUVs with tinted windows and US government plates — the armoured Chevrolet Suburbans that serve as the Secret Service’s primary presidential protection vehicles — were spotted on a Beijing highway and promptly photographed by Chinese social media users, who responded with the kind of wry commentary that authoritarianism has not yet managed to extinguish. “Did the foreign cars get an entry permit?” one user quipped, in a remark that contained more diplomatic commentary per word than most State Department briefings.

The Beast has landed. Diplomatic preparation: thin. Security preparation: immaculate.

The centrepiece of the motorcade is the presidential limousine known as “The Beast” — a modified Cadillac CT6 that weighs approximately nine tonnes, features multilayer bulletproof glass nearly eight centimetres thick, armour plating exceeding twenty centimetres, and an independent oxygen supply, satellite communications, and secure telephone lines. A presidential motorcade typically consists of thirty to fifty vehicles, including decoy limousines, counter-assault units, electronic warfare vehicles, medical teams, and motorcycles. Two or more transport helicopters are also pre-positioned for emergency extraction.

The contrast was irresistible: the diplomatic preparation for the summit had been, by near-universal assessment, the worst anyone could remember. But the security preparation was flawless. The advance team that actually showed up was not the team negotiating trade deals or preparing briefing papers — it was the team that moves bulletproof glass and armoured vehicles across oceans. The White House could not get its policy ducks in a row, but it could get The Beast to Beijing. Priorities, as they say, reveal themselves.

There is a pattern here that Canadians — and I say this with the weary affection of a citizen whose country has spent its entire existence navigating between empires — will recognize. The pattern is this: when great powers prepare for summits, the preparation that gets done is the preparation that involves hardware, logistics, and spectacle. The preparation that does not get done is the preparation that involves substance, strategy, and the difficult work of identifying what you actually want and what you are actually willing to give.

Canada has watched this pattern play out from the sidelines for decades. We watched it when Nixon went to China in 1972. We watched it when Reagan met Gorbachev in Reykjavik. We watched it when Trump met Kim Jong Un in Singapore. In each case, the motorcade was immaculate, the photographs were stunning, and the strategic outcome was somewhere between modest and nonexistent. The summit was the thing. The substance was optional.

Robert Hormats, who helped prepare for some of Kissinger’s first meetings with the Chinese, observed this week that “most of a summit’s outcome should be embedded in the draft communiqué, meticulously crafted by senior advisers and pre-agreed by the two leaders.” The purpose, he said, was to leave no room for misunderstandings afterward. The White House has indicated that at the end of this summit, there may be no communiqué at all. Let that sit for a moment. The most important bilateral meeting on the planet, and the two sides have not agreed on enough to write it down.

Daines’s shuttle diplomacy — two trips, three meetings with senior Chinese officials, a bipartisan delegation, a carefully constructed agricultural wish list — was the closest thing to substantive preparation the summit received. And even Daines’s agenda was, at its core, transactional rather than strategic. He was not negotiating a framework for managing great-power competition in the twenty-first century. He was negotiating beef and Boeing and pulse crops. He was, in the language of the Procter & Gamble training he received decades ago, closing the sale.

Whether the sale stays closed — whether the Boeings are delivered, whether the soybeans keep flowing, whether the “stable and predictable” relationship Li Qiang asked for materializes in any recognizable form — depends on forces that no advance trip can control. It depends on whether the Iran war ends or continues. It depends on whether the rare earth controls tighten or ease. It depends on whether Taiwan remains a talking point or becomes a flashpoint. It depends, in short, on whether the performance of the summit has any relationship to the reality that follows it.

The understudy has done his work. The principals are about to take the stage. The script, such as it is, is ready. But in Beijing Opera — the real kind, the kind with centuries of tradition behind it — the greatest performances are the ones where the audience already knows the story, and the art lies not in the plot but in the execution.

The execution, in both senses of the word, begins tomorrow.
