🖋 Wry, with a serious vein running through it — because the man in this essay connected the world and held some ugly opinions about who should be allowed to live in it, and this series does not round either figure.

I count my essays, and I spent eighteen years of my working life inside the industry this one founds, so permit me a professional interest. Essay 1844 is about the year a man in Washington sent a sentence to a man in Baltimore without either of them moving, and in doing so quietly repealed a rule that had governed every human being who had ever lived: that news travels no faster than a messenger can carry it.

Until 1844, information had a speed limit, and the limit was a horse. Or a ship, or a running man, or a fast rider changing mounts at post-houses — but always a body, moving through space, carrying the message inside it. A battle won in the morning was unknown in the capital for days. A price, a death, a declaration of war, all moved at the pace of muscle and sail. For the whole of human history up to that year, distance was a quantity you paid in time, and everyone had always paid it, and no one imagined you could stop.

On 24 May 1844, Samuel Morse sat in the chamber of the Supreme Court in the Capitol and tapped out a short message down an experimental wire strung forty miles to a railway depot in Baltimore, where his partner Alfred Vail received it and sent it back. The message — chosen for him by the young daughter of a friend, from the Book of Numbers, which this series finds almost too neat to be allowed — was What hath God wrought.

What God had wrought was the end of distance as a cost.

The count inside the clicks

Here is the part that makes it mine, and makes it a Numbers Game story and not merely a telecom one.

Morse could not send words. A wire carries one thing — current, on or off, a pulse or no pulse. To send language down it, Morse and Vail had to do what Gutenberg had done four hundred essays ago at 1440: they had to turn language into countable units. Not metal letters this time, but a code of dots and dashes — short pulse, long pulse — a counted alphabet in which every letter of English became a small specific tally of clicks. E is one dot. T is one dash. S is three dots, O is three dashes, and the most famous distress call in history is just the count that is easiest to tap and easiest to hear.

Think about what that is. To make language cross distance instantly, they first had to reduce it to number — to dissolve every word into a string of counted pulses, fire it down a wire as pure quantity, and reassemble it into meaning at the far end. The telegraph did not transmit words. It transmitted counts, and trusted the man at the other end to add them back up into language. Everything that has happened since — every wire, every signal, every essay of mine crossing a continent as a packet of ones and zeros — is that same trick, run faster. We did not start sending words in 1844. We started sending numbers that words could be rebuilt from. We have never stopped.

The man, unrounded

Now the honest ledger, because it is a steep one.

Samuel Morse connected the world and did not think much of a great many of the people in it. He was a committed nativist — a founder, in temper if not always in title, of the anti-immigrant, anti-Catholic politics of his American moment. He ran for mayor of New York on exactly that platform, twice, and lost. He wrote that Catholic immigration was a foreign conspiracy against the republic. He defended slavery as a positive good, sanctioned by God. The man whose invention would, within a lifetime, let a Bengali clerk and a London broker and a Toronto telegraph boy all touch the same instantaneous wire believed, in his own parlour, in a very short list of who truly belonged.

I file both entries because the series demands it, and because the contradiction is the point. The telegraph did not care what Morse thought. The wire is the most indiscriminate thing imaginable — it carries any count from anyone to anyone, and it spent the next century dissolving exactly the distances and divisions its own inventor wanted to keep. He built a machine far more generous than he was. It would not be the last time, as this series has noted at 1623 and will note again: the instrument outgrows the hand that made it.

And the invention was never his alone, which the standing tongs require me to say. The physics underneath it was Joseph Henry’s; much of the actual working code and apparatus was Alfred Vail’s, credited thinly for a century; and across the Atlantic, Cooke and Wheatstone had a rival telegraph running at the same time. “Who invented the telegraph” is itself a disputed count, settled, as these things usually are, in favour of the man whose name was easiest to attach and best at attaching it. Morse was a superb promoter of Morse. The pattern recurs; we met it at 1609, and we will meet it at 1876, where another man’s name swallowed a roomful of collaborators.

What the wire did next

The wire spread faster than anything before it, because it sold something no one had ever been able to buy: the present tense, at a distance. Within a decade telegraph lines webbed the eastern seaboard; within two, a cable crossed the Atlantic; within a generation, the British Empire was governed by a grid of submarine cables that let London know, and command, in hours what had once taken months. Distance had been the thing that protected the far-off from the centre — the colony too remote to micromanage, the garrison that had to be trusted because it could not be reached. The telegraph ended that protection. An empire that can send orders at the speed of light is a different and tighter empire than one that waits for the ship.

And the very next stop on this count shows you the edge of that blade. In 1857, when a rebellion broke across British India, it was the telegraph — the lines the British had just strung — that carried the warning, coordinated the response, and let a distant power concentrate force faster than the rising could spread. A captured rebel is said to have pointed at the telegraph wire and called it the cord that strangled us. The instrument that abolished distance abolished it for the gun as readily as for the gospel. That is the essay after this one, and it is not a wry one.

The first lies at light speed

One more entry the wire made possible, because this series never lets a new instrument off without auditing its shadow.

The telegraph did not only speed up the truth. It sped up everything, and the first people to understand this with real precision were not statesmen or preachers but speculators. If news now moved faster than any human could, then a man who controlled the wire, or who knew a figure a few minutes before the market did, held something that had never existed before: a measurable head start, denominated in minutes, convertible directly into money. The first stock manipulations by telegraph — the planted report, the withheld result, the false figure released to move a price and then corrected once the mover had sold — followed the technology almost immediately. Within a few years of What hath God wrought, people were using the same wires to wire lies, because a lie that arrives first is worth more than a truth that arrives second, and now there was a machine that could sell you the difference.

I have spent a career on the customer side of exactly this principle — the gap between what the fast party knows and what the slow party is told — and I can report that the telegraph did not invent the dishonest advantage. It only gave it a clock. Before 1844 the liar and the honest man travelled at the same speed, because both were on horses. After 1844, the lie could take the express. We have been trying to close that gap ever since, down every faster wire we have built, and we have never once caught up, because each new wire speeds the lie exactly as much as the truth. Morse's wire, it turns out, was only the harbinger. The newest one has done away with the wire altogether. A president can thumb out a figure nobody has checked, press a button labelled, of all things, Truth, and have it in millions of pockets before the first fact-checker has finished reading it; the correction, when it comes, still travels by horse. Morse at least had to spell out every letter, pulse by pulse, and a man in Baltimore had to write each one down. Somewhere between that strip of paper tape and the share button, we lost the man in Baltimore. The number that matters is not how fast your network is. It is who gets to the far end first.

The auditor at my feet

Dior communicates at a distance by exactly one method, and it is pre-1844 in spirit: she barks, and the bark must travel through actual air to an actual ear, and she expects a body to appear in response. She has never accepted the telegraph’s founding premise — that a message and a messenger can be separated, that the signal can arrive without the sender. She is, as I noted when the telephone came for her at 1876, a strict believer that a voice without a body is only a rumour. The entire modern world is built on the opposite faith. She remains unconvinced, and some mornings, watching the whole household stare into glowing rectangles at people who are not in the room, I think she is the last honest witness left in the house.

The closer

So 1844 is the hinge where communication and transportation, bound together since the first human carried the first piece of news, were finally cut apart — where the message stopped needing the messenger, and distance stopped being a quantity you paid in time. A man of narrow sympathies built the widest instrument in history, encoded language as number to do it, and took most of the credit from the men who helped. The wire did not care. It carried everyone’s counts equally, and it is still carrying them, and it carried this one to you.

The next landmark is essay 1857, and the same wire that let the gospel and the stock price fly also let an empire strangle a rebellion — and decide, in the aftermath, whose dead would be counted by name and whose would be counted by estimate, or not at all.

What hath God wrought, the first message asked. The one after it, down the same wires, was an order to open fire.

See you at 1857.
