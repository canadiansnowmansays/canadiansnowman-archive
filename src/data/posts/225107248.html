The election ended on a Tuesday; the counting will go on for years. With a new Congress elected but not yet sworn in, much of last week’s writing was about keeping score: what a lame-duck administration still controlled, what an incoming majority could undo, and the ledgers that would track both.

New here? Welcome. I’m a retiree in Toronto who writes mostly about the United States, because its weather tends to reach us first, with history, AI and Canada’s own arguments along for ballast. The satire is labelled. The rest is sourced.

Fittingly, the week brought Keeping Count, a single index of every public ledger on this Substack, and two new ledgers to put in it. He Loves War: The Hegseth Files watched the House shelve Thomas Massie's impeachment articles, 211 to 204, then answered with The Reckoning Ledger: 23 things the new Congress can still do about Pete Hegseth, and where Republicans may help. RFK Jr Files did the same for Kennedy's HHS: removing him was out of reach, but undoing his work was not.

Four new series opened their doors last week. FIFA Fraud begins with João Havelange, the man who turned world football into a business. The Third Rail introduced Social Security's 2032 shortfall, a promise with a date on it, and will end with a draft bill. The Endless Frontier borrowed its title from Vannevar Bush's 1945 report to a president and asked what American science bought, and what it is now losing; its Series Index and the Seed Corn Ledger went up alongside. And The Shenzhen Opera raised the curtain on the next Trump–Xi summit, with The Opera Cycle printing the programme for all four operas, from Beijing to Miami.

The opposition had become the majority, and Loyal Opposition asked whether Democrats could make oversight part of their cost-of-living agenda rather than a detour from it. The administration answered before they could take their seats. The Enemies List reported a White House suggesting that congressional oversight might itself be a crime, and The Blanche Files heard the attorney general tell every Trump official that he was their lawyer.

Last week’s satire shelf held a lame duck à l’orange, a CV for Mike Johnson’s lawn-care business, letters patent on the royal succession, and, after Friday’s FAA ground stop, a trouble ticket asking whether anyone has tried turning America off and on again. It also held, at long last, the Encyclopædia Stephenica: forty volumes on artificial intelligence, the first of which was correct on Thursday.

A reminder on Your Phone Bill and Mine: the CRTC hearing is November 30 in Gatineau, two weeks from tomorrow, and I’ll provide a summary in December.
