Stephen Gordon for CanadianSnowmanSays

Havana at night during prolonged blackouts, June 2026. Power available for one or two hours a day. Gasoline at $9 a litre on the black market. The infrastructure of daily life has gone dark.

On June 6, 2026, Visa and Mastercard stopped working in Cuba. Not just American-issued cards — all of them. Every Visa and Mastercard on earth, regardless of which country’s bank issued it. The Central Bank of Cuba announced that a foreign processing partner had severed its relationship with FINCIMEX, GAESA’s financial services subsidiary, under the terms of Donald Trump’s May 1 executive order. The deadline for foreign companies to cut ties with GAESA-linked entities was June 5. On June 6, the payment terminals went dark.

The only international cards that now function in Cuba are China’s UnionPay and Russia’s Mir.

Read that sentence again, because it contains the entire Cuba story in miniature. The United States, whose stated objective is to expel Russian and Chinese influence from the island, has now engineered a financial system in which the only functioning payment networks are Russian and Chinese. Every Canadian tourist, every European visitor, every businessman from any country on earth who wants to buy a coffee in Havana must now do so with cash, a Chinese payment card, or a Russian one. The infrastructure of American financial hegemony — Visa, Mastercard, the dollar-denominated correspondent banking system — has been turned into a weapon so effective that it has eliminated itself from the battlefield, leaving only the adversary’s systems standing.

This is not a metaphor. It is the operational reality of Cuba in June 2026. And it is only one item on a list of developments that, in the three weeks since this series last published, have moved the island from crisis to something closer to systems failure.

The Sanctions Reach the President’s Wife

On June 4, two days before the payment networks collapsed, the US Treasury Department added Cuban President Miguel Díaz-Canel to the Specially Designated Nationals list — freezing any assets he holds within US jurisdiction and barring American persons from doing business with him. Also sanctioned: his wife, Lis Cuesta Peraza. His stepson. Alejandro Castro Espín, the son of Raúl Castro. And Castro Espín’s son, Raúl Alejandro Castro Calis.

Cuban President Miguel Díaz-Canel, now on the US SDN list along with his wife, stepson, and key Castro family members. The sanctions reach the very top — and have helped sever Visa and Mastercard from the entire island

The sanctions now reach across both family trees that matter in Cuban politics — the Castros and the Díaz-Canels. The jump from visa restrictions to SDN designations is, as one analyst put it, the difference between telling someone they can’t visit your house and telling every bank in the world to lock them out.

Trump was asked whether the sanctions were intended to accelerate Cuba’s collapse. His answer: “No. We just want them to be a nicely run country that can feed its people.” He then added: “The country is starving and it’s got no energy, it’s got no oil, it’s got no money, it’s got nothing. It’s got a beautiful piece of land. You could have beautiful resorts.”

Beautiful resorts. The country has no oil, no electricity, no functioning payment system, no food distribution network, no functioning public health infrastructure — and the President of the United States looks at it and sees a beautiful piece of land for resorts. This series documented in Part 4 what Cuba looked like the last time Americans built beautiful resorts on the island: Batista’s casinos, American corporations controlling 90 percent of the mines and 80 percent of the utilities, and a population whose misery eventually produced the revolution that has defined the last sixty-seven years. In Part 19, we noted that administration officials told The Atlantic that Trump “is enamored of the opportunities a pliant Cuba could offer domestic businesses, much as it did in the Fulgencio Batista era.” Now Trump has said it himself, on camera, in fewer words. Beautiful resorts. The mask didn’t slip. It was never on.

Frank Mora, the former ambassador to the Organization of American States, cautioned that the escalating sanctions may be self-defeating: “You keep escalating, and you run out of options to continue trying to pressure the regime.”

He is describing the problem that this series identified in Part 17: the administration is negotiating with a gun to Cuba’s head, and every week it pulls the trigger a little more, and every week it has less ammunition left for the threat that was supposed to make Cuba surrender before the shooting started.

The Skies Close

In the same week, Iberia — Spain’s national airline — suspended its direct Madrid-to-Havana route through October 24. CMA CGM, the French shipping giant, and Hapag-Lloyd, the German freight company, suspended cargo bookings to Cuba. Sunwing and WestJet — the Canadian carriers that have historically brought more tourists to Cuba than any other airlines — suspended Cuban flights from June 20.

Canada was Cuba’s largest source of international tourists. That pipeline is now effectively severed — not by Cuban policy, but by the cascade of American sanctions that have made it logistically impossible for airlines to operate, tourists to pay for anything, or shipping companies to deliver goods.

Deserted beaches and empty hotels. Cuba’s tourism sector — once the lifeline built on 22,000 new rooms — now sits at historic lows after Canadian, Spanish, and other airlines suspended flights and payment systems collapsed.

Cuba’s tourism sector was already in free fall. The country recorded its worst tourism numbers since 2002 in 2025 — excluding pandemic years. GAESA had doubled hotel capacity over the preceding decade, building 121 hotels with 22,000 new rooms, betting on the Obama-era opening that Trump reversed. Hotel occupancy in 2024 was 30 percent. The government spent nearly 40 percent of its budget on tourism and hospitality — eleven times the combined spending on education and health care. Now the hotels are empty, the airlines have left, the payment cards don’t work, and there is no jet fuel anyway.

The tourism economy, which was supposed to be Cuba’s lifeline, has become a monument to misallocation — and the sanctions have ensured that no recovery is possible until Washington decides to allow one.

The Numbers

On June 8, United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights Volker Türk called for the United States to lift its sanctions on Cuba. The OCHA fact-finding mission that preceded his statement produced numbers that should be part of every discussion of American Cuba policy:

More than 100,000 surgeries delayed, including 12,000 pediatric procedures. Childhood cancer survival rates dropped from 85 percent to 65 percent. Only 30 percent of essential medicines available. 2.7 million people affected by water shortages. The national water system functioning with only 37 percent of its fuel needs. Eighty-four percent of water pumping runs on electricity — electricity that is available for one or two hours per day. Food prices up 18 percent.

Cuba’s state media reported that locally produced medicines are now unavailable, kidney dialysis has been disrupted, and nutrition programs are operating far under capacity. The health system that was once Cuba’s proudest achievement — the system that sent doctors to Venezuela and across Africa and produced life-expectancy numbers that rivaled the United States — is disintegrating. Not because of ideology. Because of fuel. No fuel means no electricity. No electricity means no refrigeration for medicines, no power for dialysis machines, no lights in operating rooms. The incubator from Part 17 was a single image. The OCHA report is the system-wide version of that image.

Canada’s foreign affairs minister announced C$13.5 million in food and nutrition assistance. It is a gesture. It is not a solution. The solution requires fuel, and no country will ship fuel to Cuba while the United States maintains the threat of secondary sanctions against anyone who does.

What Collapse Looks Like from the Inside

Patrick Oppmann, CNN’s Havana bureau chief and one of the last Western journalists on the island. His reporting from inside the crisis: 22-hour blackouts, siphoned gasoline, and exhausted Cubans saying ‘just let it happen tomorrow so the suffering can end

Patrick Oppmann has reported from Cuba for CNN since 2012. He lives in Havana with his family — four children. He is, by most reckonings, the last Western television bureau chief on the island. In a June 17 interview on David Frum’s Atlantic podcast, he described what daily life in Cuba now looks like, from inside.

Gasoline, if you can find it, costs $9 a liter on the black market — roughly $300 to fill a car. Power outages in Havana run twenty-two hours or more. Internet goes down with the power. Oppmann has installed solar panels and batteries in his home. His office is six flights up; when the power goes out, he walks. He sometimes has to choose which stories to cover based on whether the assignment will use the last of his fuel. “I might be riding a bike to work” when the moment comes, he said.

There is no functioning banking system. Money enters Cuba in satchels. The journalists who remain coordinate with each other: “How are you resolving this?” Often there is no resolution.

Oppmann’s car was broken into. The thieves were after gasoline. He described an epidemic of crime — people drilling holes in gas tanks to siphon fuel. When the police came to take the report, the officer said the cookies Oppmann offered him with coffee were “the first thing I had to eat today.”

Cubans queuing for scarce rations in Havana. The ration book that once guaranteed basic staples now provides almost nothing. Children searching trash for food — scenes Patrick Oppmann says were not seen before.

The ration book — the system that once guaranteed every Cuban family sugar, cooking oil, rice, beans — provides almost nothing now. People might eat one meal a day. Women hold children at car windows, begging. Children root through trash for food. “You didn’t see that before,” Oppmann said. “Regular people looking for something to eat.”

The novelist Leonardo Padura once described Cuba this way: “We don’t get to choose what we eat, but no one goes hungry.” That is no longer true.

Oppmann described the Spanish consulate in his building — “It feels a little bit like Casablanca, where people are just doing anything they can to get a visa to any country that will take them.” The daily answer most Cubans have arrived at is: get out, however you can.

The most chilling detail was about the military preparations. The government is training civilians for what it calls “the war of the entire populace” — a Vietnam-style mass resistance. Oppmann described a training video in which soldiers pulled an anti-aircraft weapon with oxen. Separately, reports have emerged that Cuba has acquired more than 300 military drones from Russia and Iran.

And then the line that might define this moment better than anything a diplomat or analyst has said: ordinary Cubans telling a CNN correspondent, “Let’s just get it over with. If it were gonna happen, let it happen tomorrow — so the suffering can end, and we can begin to rebuild.”

That is not the voice of resistance. It is not the voice of surrender. It is the voice of exhaustion — of a population that has been squeezed to the point where the worst-case scenario seems preferable to the indefinite continuation of the status quo. It is the most dangerous sound a government can hear from its own people.

The Boomerang — Again

This series has documented the boomerang effect in every installment since Part 13: the pattern in which every American escalation designed to sever Cuba from Russian and Chinese influence pushes Cuba deeper into Russian and Chinese dependency. The Visa-Mastercard shutdown is the most vivid example yet, but it is not the only one.

Only Chinese UnionPay and Russian Mir cards now work in Cuba after Visa and Mastercard were cut off on June 6, 2026. American financial hegemony has eliminated itself, leaving the field to its adversaries.

The only oil delivery Cuba received in four months came from a Russian tanker. The only payment cards that now work are Russian Mir and Chinese UnionPay. Cuba has reportedly acquired military drones from Russia and Iran. The nickel and cobalt operations that Part 19 examined — the ones that the Trump administration wants to redirect from Chinese buyers to American ones — are still exporting to China, because China is the customer that doesn’t care about American sanctions.

Every door Washington closes to Western commerce, Western finance, and Western logistics is a door it opens to Eastern alternatives. The administration is not isolating Cuba. It is reorienting Cuba — away from the systems Washington controls and toward the systems its adversaries control. When the dust settles, whether it settles through a deal, a collapse, or a military intervention, the infrastructure that remains functioning on the island will be Russian and Chinese. That is not an unintended consequence. It is the predictable outcome of a strategy that mistakes suffocation for leverage.

The Iran Window

There is one more development that this series must note, because it changes the military calculus that has constrained the administration’s options. The Iran war — the stalemate that multiple analysts cited as the reason a Cuba military operation was “probably not imminent” — appears to be moving toward some form of resolution. Trump’s approval ratings have dropped to the mid-to-upper 30s. The war is widely perceived as a failure.

Cuba is the available win. The Nimitz is already in the Caribbean. If Special Operations Forces redeploy from the Middle East — the constraint that the May 15 New York Times reporting specifically identified — the capacity for a Venezuela-style operation returns. Cuba becomes the midterm narrative: the success that erases the failure, the liberation that replaces the stalemate, the beautiful resorts that replace the rubble.

Whether this happens is unknowable. That it is being discussed — openly, by named officials, in major publications — is itself a development. The administration has made clear that it wants the option. The question is whether anyone will tell the president that seizing a 94-year-old man who can barely speak, from a darkened island where children are eating from trash cans, in order to build resorts, is not the victory he imagines it to be.

Baby Alejandro’s Island

The island that has refused to kneel for five hundred and thirty years is still refusing. It is doing so in conditions that no previous generation of Cubans has experienced — not during the Special Period, not during the Bay of Pigs, not during the missile crisis. Twenty-two-hour blackouts. No oil. No Visa, no Mastercard. No airlines from Canada or Spain. No functioning ration system. Children in the trash. Police who haven’t eaten. Anti-aircraft guns pulled by oxen. And a president in Washington who looks at the wreckage and sees a beautiful piece of land for resorts.

Baby Alejandro is in his incubator, if the power is on. The lights are off in Havana. The last correspondent is choosing which stories are worth the last of his gasoline. The only cards that work are Russian and Chinese.

Five hundred and thirty years of empire, endurance, and the cost of defiance. The cost has never been higher. The defiance has never been more desperate. The empire has never been less interested in what happens to the people it claims to be saving.
