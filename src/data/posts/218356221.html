On September 30, 2026, David Frum asked Eric Holder a question that this series has, for fifty-six parts, mostly answered from one side only. Frum observed, in his podcast for The Atlantic, that Americans have long taken it for granted that the shenanigans stop on voting day. Whatever obstacles stood between a citizen and the ballot box, once the ballot was cast the machinery took over, and nobody much thought about what happened between the count and the swearing-in.

Holder’s answer was the most useful thing he said all episode. He divides the 2026 election into three segments: the structural manipulation before the vote, the conduct of the vote itself from early voting through Election Day, and what happens afterwards, when votes are counted and winners certified. He has, he said, sat through tabletop exercises covering all three.

Read back through this series and the imbalance is plain. We have spent parts on the Postal Service’s ballot-screening rule, the DHS citizenship database, the Missouri maps, FBI “election crisis coordinators” and armed agents near polling places. Segments one and two, in other words. Segment three — the five or six weeks between the polls closing on November 3 and the clerks’ deadlines that follow, and then the strange constitutional limbo of January 3 — has barely been touched.

It deserves its own entry, because it is where the friction thesis this series has been building is at its most efficient. Friction before an election has to survive a court. Friction after an election only has to survive a calendar.

Holder’s quiet admission

Holder’s prescription for segment three was turnout: win by margins big enough that they cannot be monkeyed with. It is sound advice. It is also, if you sit with it, an admission. A strategy of winning by too much to steal concedes that close races remain stealable, or at least delayable, which in the House amounts to much the same thing.

And close races are what this cycle is built to produce. By Holder’s own reckoning, the mid-decade redistricting war netted Republicans roughly five seats — far short of the twenty-one Steve Bannon’s camp once hoped for, but enough to put the majority within reach of a handful of contested districts. The question is not whether someone can overturn an election. It is whether someone can stop enough certificates from arriving on time.

The arithmetic of an empty chair

The best guide to this is a careful piece by Molly Roberts in Lawfare, published August 25, 2026, which set out to deflate the “rogue clerk” panic and, in the process, explained exactly where the real vulnerability lies.

The House is not a continuing body. At noon on January 3, every member’s term ends at once. Mike Johnson will no longer be Speaker; there will be no Republican majority and no Democratic minority, only members-elect. The one official who carries over is the Clerk of the House, who presides until a Speaker is chosen and who prepares the roll of members-elect entitled to vote for that Speaker.

The Clerk’s duty is set out in federal statute, 2 U.S.C. § 26, and it is narrow: put on the roll the people whose credentials show they were regularly elected, and only those people. Roberts argues persuasively that this is a ministerial duty a court could compel. A Clerk who substituted a losing Republican for a certified Democrat would very likely be ordered to fix the roll.

But the same statute cuts the other way. If a state has not issued a certificate — because a county has not certified, because ballots are sitting in an evidence locker, because litigation is still running — the Clerk is obliged to leave that seat empty. Lawful, orderly, and empty.

Roberts notes that a vacancy has only half the effect of a substitution. In a 220–215 House, swapping three Democrats for three Republicans flips control; leaving three Democratic seats vacant does not. But in a House decided by one or two seats, half the effect may be all that is needed. And once a Speaker is elected, the chamber becomes, under Article I, Section 5, the judge of its own members’ elections — the point at which, Roberts concludes, the courts may well step back.

So the target in segment three is not the count. It is the certificate, and specifically its timing.

The machinery already in place

Nothing that follows requires a new plan. Every piece is already built, tested, or announced.

The counties. As Yunior Rivas reported for Democracy Docket on September 21, 2026, Vickie Marquardt — one of the Otero County, New Mexico, commissioners who refused to certify the 2022 primary until the state supreme court ordered them to — says she would do it again if she felt strongly enough. In Nevada, Washoe County Commissioner Jeanne Herman has voted against certifying every election since 2020, including her own re-election. Voting-rights groups counted at least seventeen attempts to interfere with certification in the 2024 cycle alone. Every one of those elections was eventually certified. Some were certified late.

Late is the point. Arizona’s county boards have until November 19 to canvass, with the statewide canvass on November 23. California’s counties have until December 3, and the Secretary of State certifies on December 11. Those deadlines feed recount windows and contest periods, each with its own clock. A county that refuses to certify cannot overturn a result, but it can spend a week of a calendar that has none to spare. As Protect Democracy put it, a refusal does its work even when it fails: it feeds the conspiracy theory and it eats the clock.

The seizures. This series covered the FBI’s January 28, 2026 seizure of Fulton County’s 2020 ballots in Part LI. A second, larger precedent has since run its course in California. In March 2026, Riverside County Sheriff Chad Bianco — then a candidate for governor — had his deputies take roughly 650,000 ballots from the county’s November 2025 Proposition 50 special election, acting on a citizen group’s claim of excess votes that the registrar said rested on a misreading of the data. On September 24, 2026, the California Supreme Court ruled unanimously, as CBS Los Angeles reported, that Bianco had broken state election law and ordered him to return the ballots and end his investigation. They went back to the registrar on September 29, six months after they were taken.

The courts won. It took half a year. Now run that same sequence on ballots from a November 2026 House race, seized in the second week of the canvass. Roberts flags exactly this case: a seizure that breaks the chain of custody could keep litigation going past January 3, and the Clerk’s statutory duty would then be to leave the seat empty. NPR’s reporting, carried by KPBS on September 9, 2026, points to a 2025 Michigan mayoral race, decided by eleven votes, that is still in dispute after the custody of a few dozen ballots was broken. Election officials, the same report notes, now train for warrants served on live ballots.

The monitors. In mid-August, Harmeet Dhillon told Bloomberg that the Justice Department would send roughly 1,000 election monitors into the field in November — by her own description a historic number for a Republican administration, as Torrie Herrington of NOTUS reported via Spotlight PA on August 18, 2026. In 2024 the department covered 86 jurisdictions. The department’s own description of the monitors’ job, as the Washington Examiner reported, includes following up on investigations. A monitor does not need to interfere with anything on Election Day. A monitor’s notes are inputs — the raw material for referrals, affidavits and the kind of thin probable-cause case Part LI dissected in Fulton County.

The emergency that already exists. Much commentary has asked whether Trump will declare an election emergency. In a narrow sense, one has been in force since 2018. On August 31, 2026, the President renewed the national emergency under Executive Order 13848, as every administration has done annually since he first signed it. The renewal itself is routine. What is less noticed is the order’s timetable. It requires the Director of National Intelligence to deliver an assessment of any foreign interference within 45 days of an election — for November 3, that means by December 18, 2026 — followed by an Attorney General and DHS report within a further 45 days, which can land as late as early February 2027.

The order defines foreign interference broadly enough to include efforts to undermine public confidence in an election. The Director of National Intelligence is Tulsi Gabbard, to whom Cochise County’s leaders — the county whose board delayed Arizona’s 2022 canvass — wrote this spring asking her to investigate voting machines that state and federal officials had already cleared, as KJZZ reported on March 9, 2026. Put the dates side by side: an official federal assessment of the election due in the middle of state certification season, with a follow-up report arriving after the new Congress convenes. No new power is needed for that document to become an argument in every close contest. The President, for his part, declined in an August 2026 interview with Real America’s Voice to rule out declaring a fresh election emergency.

The rehearsal

We have, in fact, already seen how a seat is held open without anyone technically refusing to fill it.

Adelita Grijalva won Arizona’s 7th District special election on September 23, 2025. She was sworn in on November 12, 2025 — fifty days later, the longest such delay on record. Speaker Johnson’s reasons shifted as each was overtaken. First he was waiting for certification. Then the House had to return from recess, and the recess kept being extended. Arizona certified her election on October 14, 2025, satisfying his original condition, and the delay continued. Her swearing-in, as it happened, supplied the 218th signature on the Epstein files discharge petition.

The honest caveat matters here. As Rick Hasen has pointed out at Election Law Blog, Johnson will not be Speaker on January 3, 2027; he will hold no gavel to slow-walk anyone with. The Grijalva episode is not a template that transfers directly.

What transfers is the excuse. Johnson’s first justification was that he was waiting for the state’s certification. In segment three, that wait would not have to be invented. It would only have to be produced — by a county board, a seized box of ballots, a federal assessment, a lawsuit timed to run out the clock.

What fighting back looks like here

None of this is a prediction that the midterms will be stolen, and the evidence leans the other way. Roberts concludes that panic about January 3 is likely unwarranted; Derek Muller has argued that this particular Republican conference is poorly suited to the lockstep discipline any scheme would require. Certification is, in every battleground state, a mandatory duty that courts can compel. Michigan transfers an unfinished county canvass to the state board after fourteen days. California tightened its law after Riverside. The California Supreme Court, when asked, said no — unanimously.

And the people hired to prove the 2020 fraud story keep leaving without proving it. Kurt Olsen, the former White House election-integrity director whose referral produced the Fulton County seizure, stepped away from his Justice Department role on September 28, 2026. CBS News reported that even his departure became muddled, with Todd Blanche insisting he had not resigned and Olsen apparently remaining on the payroll. It is a fitting coda for this series: an investigation that cannot find its evidence, conducted by an official who cannot quite be said to have left.

But the defences that worked before an election worked because courts had time. Judge Talwani, Judge Nichols and the Supreme Court’s 7–2 majority all ruled in September, with weeks to spare. After November 3, the margin is measured in days. The defence of segment three is therefore mostly a matter of speed and preparation that is invisible until it is needed: secretaries of state with mandamus petitions drafted in advance, judges ready to rule within hours rather than weeks, registrars who can document every ballot’s custody so that a seizure does not leave a result unknowable, and states that finish certifying well before Christmas rather than days before January 3.

Holder is right that turnout is the first line of defence. A vacancy is only useful at the margin, and large margins leave no margin to exploit. But it would be a strange democracy that could protect its elections only when they were not close.

The shenanigans used to stop on voting day. In 2026, the plan — insofar as there is one — does not need them to continue for long. It needs them to continue until noon on January 3, and then for everyone to agree that an empty chair is simply how the roll came out.
