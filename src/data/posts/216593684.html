Essay 1876 is the breather between the protected numbers — my voice behind us, my family ahead — and the count, which has been suspiciously well-behaved through this whole corridor, has outdone itself: it has landed the retired telecom man on the year the telephone was invented, in the very calendar year the telephone turns one hundred and fifty. The device is born in 1876; this essay is written in 2026; the sesquicentennial arithmetic checks out to the digit, and somewhere the series’ guardian coincidence is looking very pleased with itself. I spent eighteen years in the industry this essay founds. I was never once, in all that time, asked to celebrate its birthday. I am correcting that now, on my own masthead, with the founder’s town an hour’s drive from my desk.

Because the founder’s town — and every Canadian schoolchild of my vintage knows this, and nearly nobody else on Earth does — is Brantford, Ontario.

Alexander Graham Bell was a Scot by birth and a Bostonian by payroll, and the telephone is filed in the world’s memory as an American invention, and the Americans have the paperwork. But the idea — the conception, as Bell himself always insisted — came to him in the summer of 1874 at his parents’ farmhouse above the Grand River at Brantford, at a spot he called his dreaming place, where he had come to recover his health and think about sound. Bell maintained for the rest of his life, in a formulation I pass along with the standing tongs but which Brantford has carved into more than one surface, that the telephone was conceived in Canada and born in the United States. I invite you to appreciate the customs declaration buried in that sentence: the most valuable idea of the nineteenth century originated in Ontario and was exported south for manufacture, which makes it not only the founding event of my industry but arguably the founding event of the entire Canadian economic experience. We have been having that same transaction ever since. We dream it here; they patent it there; the royalties are a matter of ongoing discussion.

And the patent — here the Numbers Game rubs its hands, because the ownership of the century was decided by a timestamp.

On February 14, 1876 — Valentine’s Day, appropriately, for the instrument that would carry more love talk than all the poets combined — Bell’s attorney filed his telephone patent application at the United States Patent Office in Washington. And on the same day, at the same office, an accomplished inventor named Elisha Gray filed a caveat — a formal notice of intent to patent — for a device to transmit speech electrically. The same day. By the traditional account, hours apart; and which hours, and who was truly first to the idea as opposed to first to the wicket, has been disputed by partisans, courts, and historians for a century and a half without final settlement — the litigation over Bell’s patent became one of the longest wars in the history of intellectual property, and pieces of it split the Supreme Court itself. I take no position, as this series takes no position on Wittenberg doors; I merely direct your attention to what the dispute is. The most valuable single patent ever issued — the telephone monopoly, the seed of the Bell System, the ancestor of the industry that employed me for eighteen years and connects every human being reading this sentence — rests, at bottom, on a question of filing order. On which piece of paper was logged first, on one Tuesday, at one counter, by clerks who had no idea what they were timestamping. Eighteen hundred essays ago this series began with the observation that the count is never merely the count. Here is the count as the entire estate: two men, one day, one ledger, and the order of entry worth more than any territory ever fought over in these files. The Armada needed a hundred and thirty ships to lose an empire’s future. Gray, by the traditional telling, needed only to be second in a queue.

Then the sequence that delights me most, as a connoisseur of this series’ pre-booked outcomes: the patent was issued on March 7, 1876 — United States Patent No. 174,465, “Improvement in Telegraphy” — and the telephone did not work yet. The first intelligible transmission of human speech over wire happened three days later, on March 10, in Bell’s Boston workroom, when Bell spoke into the instrument and his assistant, Thomas Watson, in the next room, heard the words and came. The words, per Bell’s own laboratory notebook of that day: Mr. Watson — come here — I want to see you. Consider the accounting. Essay 1588 established that certainty is the counting error committed before the count — the outcome booked in advance, the adjective sailing before the fleet. Here is the one entry in eighteen centuries of files where the pre-booked claim paid in full: the ownership document for talking by wire was granted while talking by wire remained, strictly speaking, a theory, and the theory then had the good manners to come true inside the week. It is the luckiest three days in the history of intellectual property, and I record it as the exception that proves every rule this series owns: sometimes the man who books the revenue before the sale actually makes the sale. It is just that history offers roughly one Bell per fleet of Armadas, and the fleets never believe they are the fleets.

About the famous first sentence, two entries for the honest ledger. First: the legend — supplied by Watson himself, decades later, in his memoirs — holds that Bell had spilled battery acid on his trousers and was calling for help, which would make the first telephone call in history a distress call, and the tongs are hereby applied at full extension: the notebook records the words and not the trousers, the acid appears nowhere in the contemporary record, and an old man improving a story for the lecture circuit is a phenomenon this series has met before, at an orchard in Lincolnshire. The acid is disputed. The summons is not. And second — attend to what the summons was. The first words ever carried by the network were not a proclamation, a price, or a poem. They were come here; I want to see you — a request for presence, spoken into a machine whose entire destiny would be to make presence unnecessary. The telephone’s first sentence was a confession of its own limits, uttered at the moment of its birth: the wire carries the voice, and the voice immediately asks for the body. A hundred and fifty years and some trillions of calls later, I am not sure the network has ever said anything truer about itself. Every video call to Bhutan in this household ends the same way the first call began.

The Canadian ledger closes the founding season. On August 10, 1876, using the telegraph line along the riverside, Bell staged the world’s first true long-distance call — one-way, the voice travelling some thirteen kilometres — from Brantford to the neighbouring town of Paris. Paris, Ontario. I have waited eighteen hundred and seventy-five essays to type the following sentence, and I intend to enjoy it: the first long-distance telephone call in the history of the world was placed to Paris, and Paris picked up, and Paris was a mill town on the Grand River with a population of a few thousand souls, and the other Paris — the one with the opera — would have to wait its turn like everybody else. There is a plaque. Canadians do not gloat, as a matter of national policy, but we do erect extremely specific plaques, and this is the finest specific plaque in the country. The voice went long-distance for the first time on Earth entirely within the county lines of southwestern Ontario, between Confederation’s ninth birthday and its tenth, in a country of barely four million that had just finished wiring itself together constitutionally and now, without pausing for breath, started doing it copper by copper. Essay 1867 said the Fathers built a frame biased toward cohesion. Here is the frame acquiring a nervous system, nine years in, invented by a resident. This country’s founding technologies are a railway, a telephone, and a committee, and I have worked for two of the three, and the committee also had its moments.

I will keep the personal ledger brief, because 1867 carried most of it, but the entries belong here. Eighteen years of my working life were spent in the industry that grew from Patent 174,465 — in the advocacy of customers, which is to say in the auditing of the gap between what the network promised and what it delivered, a gap first opened, I can now report, three days wide in March of 1876 and never entirely closed since. The intervention I have before the CRTC as I write is, in the strictest genealogy, a dispute with Brantford’s grandchildren about billing. And the household runs on the founder’s machine daily: Rikesh’s bear photographs arriving from the census trail, Ugyen’s calls home across the time zones to the country that counts happiness, Asmita waving from a screen for years before she ever reached the front door where six words were waiting for her. Bell built the machine that lets you be heard before you can be embraced. My entire house, I realize, assembled itself in exactly that order — voice first, presence after, come here, I want to see you — and every one of them came.

Dior’s relationship with the telephone deserves its entry, because she has audited the technology and issued a finding Bell would have respected. Dior does not acknowledge telephone voices. Speakerphone is played; familiar humans speak; her ears perform one courtesy rotation and stand down. Her position, as I reconstruct it, is a strict evidentiary standard: a voice unaccompanied by a scent is a rumour — an unverified report of a person, inadmissible in her census, however familiar the timbre. She is, it occurs to me, the last being in this house still holding the line Bell’s first sentence tried to hold: the voice is not the person; come here; I want to see you. A hundred and fifty years of telecommunications, and the husky’s audit stands: the network transmits the claim of presence, never the presence itself, and she will mark the party present when the party smells like themselves at the door, and not one minute before. The industry could have saved itself a great deal of marketing by consulting her first.

The count moves on, as it does — and it stays on the wire, because the wire is not finished with this century. The next landmark is essay 1882, and it is Bell’s dark twin: another man who booked a certainty in advance, as Bell did, but who booked the wrong one. Thomas Edison lit a district of Lower Manhattan and, in the same stroke, invented the strangest transaction in the history of commerce — the sale of a thing no buyer can see, light itself, billed by an unseeable unit from a machine downtown. After him the road finally turns home, toward the third of my three protected numbers — my family, essay 1898, where Hugh Llewellyn Keenleyside is born, who wrote his own life in two volumes, a habit this series has lately had reason to believe runs in the blood. That one I will write slowly, by hand, with the family’s books open on the desk. But the count has a few more wires to string before it gets there.

A dreaming place above the Grand River. Two filings, one Tuesday, hours apart. A patent that beat its own invention by three days, and a first sentence that asked for the one thing the machine could never deliver. The voice went on the wire in 1876, and a hundred and fifty years later it is how my whole household got here — heard first, embraced after.

See you at 1882, where light stops being something you make and becomes something you buy.
