On 15 November 1884, representatives of fourteen states sat down around a horseshoe table in Otto von Bismarck’s official residence on the Wilhelmstrasse in Berlin, under a large map of Africa. There were delegates from Britain, France, Germany, Portugal, Belgium’s King Leopold II through his representatives, Spain, Italy, the Netherlands, Austria-Hungary, Russia, Denmark, Sweden-Norway, the Ottoman Empire and the United States. The conference ran, with breaks, until 26 February 1885.

There was no one at the table from Africa. No African ruler was invited, consulted or informed. The Sultan of Zanzibar, whose claims on the east African coast were directly affected, asked to be represented and was refused.

The Berlin Conference did not, as is sometimes said, draw the map of Africa with a ruler. Most of the actual boundaries came later, in dozens of bilateral agreements over the following twenty years. What Berlin did was more important. It agreed the rules. A European power could claim African territory if it notified the others and could show “effective occupation,” meaning enough officials, troops or treaties on the ground to administer it. The Congo and Niger rivers would be open to all European shipping. The vast Congo basin would be a free-trade zone. And the conference recognised a new state in the centre of the continent, the Congo Free State, which belonged, personally, to the King of the Belgians.

In 1870, European powers controlled perhaps a tenth of Africa, most of it along the coasts. By 1914, they controlled about nine-tenths of it. Only Liberia and Ethiopia remained independent.

Multiple bullies, no referee

Every case so far in this series has had one bully and one target. The Scramble is different, and the difference is why it has its own chapter. Here, half a dozen powers ran the routine simultaneously, across an entire continent, and the only restraint any of them faced came from the others.

The genius of Berlin, from the powers’ point of view, was that it converted a potential war among themselves into a set of procedures. The great danger of the Scramble was not African resistance, which the powers were confident they could crush. It was that France and Britain, or Britain and Germany, might go to war with each other over a river valley neither had seen. Berlin was a referee for the bullies, agreed by the bullies, for the protection of the bullies. The people being divided were not a party to the rules at all.

That structure produced some remarkable diplomacy. In 1890, Britain and Germany exchanged the North Sea island of Heligoland for German recognition of British claims to Zanzibar and parts of east Africa, a trade of territory that belonged to one of the parties for territory that belonged to neither. In 1898, at Fashoda on the upper Nile, a small French expedition under Captain Jean-Baptiste Marchand met a much larger British force under Kitchener, fresh from slaughtering the Mahdist army at Omdurman, and after months of crisis France backed down. The two powers then drew a line between their spheres, and within six years had signed the Entente Cordiale. At no point in any of this was anyone who lived near Fashoda asked.

How a treaty was made

“Effective occupation” was usually demonstrated by treaty, and the treaties were the Scramble’s signature document, the Roman client-king arrangement reduced to a sheet of paper and a mark.

Henry Morton Stanley, working for Leopold in the Congo in the early 1880s, collected hundreds of treaties from local chiefs, many of whom could not read the documents and had no concept of permanently alienating land. Some of those treaties signed away all rights to the land and its produce in exchange for cloth, gin or a few bales of trade goods. The German adventurer Carl Peters did the same in east Africa, returning to Berlin with a sheaf of treaties that became the basis of German East Africa. In southern Africa, Cecil Rhodes’s agents obtained the Rudd Concession from the Ndebele king Lobengula in 1888, granting mineral rights in exchange for a monthly payment, a thousand rifles and a steamboat on the Zambezi. Lobengula later said he had been deceived about what he had signed, and wrote to Queen Victoria to say so. Rhodes used the concession to charter the British South Africa Company, send a Pioneer Column north in 1890, and, when the Ndebele resisted in 1893, conquer them.

That war is where the Scramble’s asymmetry becomes impossible to miss. At the Shangani River in October 1893, a few hundred company troops with four Maxim guns killed around fifteen hundred Ndebele warriors. Hilaire Belloc summed it up five years later in a couplet that was meant as satire and has been quoted as a description ever since: “Whatever happens, we have got / The Maxim gun, and they have not.”

The Congo: the rubric as a business plan

The worst case of the Scramble was also the one wrapped in the most elevated language.

Leopold II had wanted a colony for years, and Belgium’s government did not. So he created one privately. Through a series of philanthropic-sounding bodies, beginning with the International African Association in 1876, he presented his project as a scientific and humanitarian mission to open central Africa to civilisation and suppress the Arab slave trade. It worked. At Berlin, the powers recognised his Congo Free State, in part because each preferred Leopold, a minor monarch with no navy, to a rival great power. In 1889 and 1890 he even hosted an international anti-slavery conference in Brussels.

What he built in the Congo was a system of forced labour on a scale that stood out even in the Scramble. When the invention of the pneumatic tyre in the late 1880s created a world boom in rubber, the Congo’s wild rubber vines became enormously valuable. Villages were assigned rubber quotas. The state’s army, the Force Publique, enforced them by taking women hostage until the men brought in enough rubber, by burning villages that fell short, and by killing. Soldiers were expected to account for their ammunition, and the practice arose of bringing back severed hands as proof that bullets had been used on people rather than wasted on hunting. Photographs of mutilated children, many taken by the missionary Alice Seeley Harris, reached Europe after 1904.

The death toll is still argued over, because no census was taken, and estimates of the population decline run from several million to ten million. It is enough to say that the population of the Congo fell drastically during Leopold’s rule, from killing, starvation, disease and the collapse of village life under the quota system.

The exposure came from outsiders who refused to accept the language. George Washington Williams, an African American Civil War veteran and historian, visited the Congo in 1890 and wrote an open letter to Leopold describing what he saw. The British consul Roger Casement produced a devastating official report in 1904. A shipping clerk named E. D. Morel, who noticed that ships were leaving Antwerp for the Congo carrying guns and ammunition and returning full of rubber and ivory, but carrying almost no trade goods to pay for them, deduced that the rubber was being taken by force, and built the Congo Reform Association into one of the first great international human rights campaigns. In 1908 the international pressure forced Leopold to hand the Congo over to the Belgian state. He had the Free State’s archives burned first.

Punishment, on display

The Scramble also produced some of the most deliberately visible punishments of the whole series.

In January 1897, a British party led by the acting consul-general, James Phillips, set out for Benin City, in what is now southern Nigeria, against the explicit request of the Oba of Benin, who was in the middle of a religious festival during which foreigners could not be received. Most of the party were killed on the road. The British response was a “punitive expedition” of some twelve hundred marines and sailors that took Benin City in February 1897, burned much of it, exiled the Oba, and carried off thousands of bronze plaques, ivory carvings and sculptures from the royal palace. The Benin Bronzes were auctioned off to cover the cost of the expedition and scattered through the museums of Europe and North America. The first formal returns only began in the 2020s.

The Scramble had one great exception, and it is the Suez of the nineteenth century. In 1896, when Italy tried to turn a disputed treaty into a protectorate over Ethiopia, the Emperor Menelik II, who had spent years buying modern rifles and artillery, met an Italian army at Adwa on 1 March and destroyed it. Italy was forced to recognise Ethiopian independence. The first point of the rubric, overwhelming asymmetry, turned out not to hold, and without it the rest of the routine simply failed. Ethiopia stayed free until Mussolini came back with aircraft and poison gas in 1935.

Scoring the Scramble against the rubric

Overwhelming asymmetry. The Maxim gun, the steamship, quinine and the telegraph gave European forces an advantage that was, in most places, decisive. Adwa is the proof that the rubric’s first point matters: where the asymmetry was not real, the pattern broke. Satisfied, with one instructive exception.

Coercion as first resort. Treaties obtained by misrepresentation, backed by troops; punitive expeditions against rulers who refused; hostage-taking as a method of rubber collection. Satisfied.

Extraction is the point. Land, rubber, ivory, gold, diamonds, copper and labour. The stated purposes, the suppression of the slave trade and the opening of Africa to Christianity, commerce and civilisation, diverge from the operative goal in a way documented by the powers’ own consuls. Satisfied.

Self-justifying rhetoric. Livingstone’s “three Cs” of Christianity, commerce and civilisation; Leopold’s humanitarian associations and his anti-slavery conference. The Congo is the purest case in the series of a cover story turned into a business model. Satisfied.

Resistance gets punished. Benin City, the Ndebele, and every village that missed its rubber quota. Satisfied.

Five for five, many times over.

The question the Introduction asked about Canada

The Introduction said that if this chapter’s research turned up a case where Canada scored against the rubric, the series would not look away. Canada was not at the Berlin table and held no African colonies. Its most direct connection to the Scramble is a curious one: in 1884, nearly four hundred Canadian boatmen, many of them First Nations and Métis rivermen, were recruited to take British boats up the Nile for the failed expedition to relieve General Gordon at Khartoum.

But the research does raise a harder question, and it would be dishonest to leave it out. In the same decade the powers were collecting chiefs’ marks on treaties in the Congo basin, Canada was concluding the numbered treaties with First Nations across the prairies, from Treaty 1 in 1871 to Treaty 7 in 1877, often under conditions of famine as the buffalo disappeared, with written terms that differed sharply from the oral promises the Nations understood themselves to be agreeing to. The Indian Act of 1876 followed, and after it the pass system and the residential schools. The parallel with the Scramble’s paper treaties is close enough that it cannot simply be waved away. Whether it satisfies all five points of this series’ rubric, rather than the related but different pattern of settler colonialism, is a question the concluding essay will have to take up honestly.

What the Scramble tells us

Part VI ended with three conditions that stopped the pattern at Suez: a stronger power that objected, an international forum where the target could appeal, and a bully that could no longer afford the bill. The Scramble shows what happens when none of those conditions exist. The only forum was one the bullies designed for themselves. The only stronger powers were fellow participants, who objected only when their own share was threatened. And the bill was paid in Africa.

Part VIII turns to the power that hosted the Berlin Conference, and to the case this series includes as a warning: what happens when a bully forgets to check whether the target is actually weaker than it is.
