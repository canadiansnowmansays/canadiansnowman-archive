“We should regret our mistakes and learn from them, but never carry them forward into the future with us.” — Lucy Maud Montgomery

With deepest affection, on the occasion of his eightieth birthday

OUR earliest and best recollections are associated with home. There the first lessons of infancy are learned. The parents’ examples are first imitated by the child, whose earliest impressions are gained from them. In no way are evil habits more effectually propagated than by example, and therefore parents should be what they wish their children to be.

We offer these precepts — drawn from a handbook of impeccable moral instruction, published in the 1880s and recently celebrated by the Epoch Times as a beacon of Traditional Values — as a birthday tribute to a man who has, across seven decades and five children, demonstrated a truly singular relationship with every one of them.

THE FATHER’S INFLUENCE

To the father belongs the privilege of planting in the hearts of his children those seeds of character which, nurtured and fostered, will bear the fruit of earnest and useful lives.

The seeds one chooses to plant, of course, are everything.

One may plant seeds of humility, of honest dealing, of respect for the truth. One may alternatively plant a golf course in New Jersey and leave the children to figure out the rest. Either approach produces results. The results simply differ.

The handbook is clear on the matter: “A parent’s example outlines the child’s character. It sinks down deep into his heart and influences his whole life for good or for evil.”

We pause here to note that Don Jr. owns seventeen guns and posts photographs of dead elephants. Eric once described himself as working harder than any human being alive. We do not say these things are connected. We merely observe that seeds, once planted, do grow.

PARENTS SHOULD SET GOOD EXAMPLES

The handbook instructs: “If you wish your children’s faces illumined with good humor, contentment and satisfaction, so that they will be cheerful, joyous and happy, day by day, then must your own countenance appear illumined by the sunshine of love.”

There is a photograph — many photographs, actually — of our subject’s countenance. It has been described, across the years, as a scowl, a glower, a smolder, a pout, and, on one memorable occasion by a federal judge reviewing classified documents, as “a look of determined non-compliance.” Sunshine of love does not appear in the photographic record.

The handbook further advises that children will form habits of evil speaking if they hear a parent deal lightly with the reputation of another — if they hear slander, if they hear reviling. “Your example will nullify your teaching,” the handbook warns, “and your admonitions will be lost.”

To date, the subject has called people the following names, in public, on the record, occasionally under oath: Sleepy, Crooked, Crazy, Lyin’, Nasty, Liddle, Wacky, Slippery, Dumb as a Rock, a Dog, a Pig, a Slob, a Dummy, a Total Loser, and, memorably, “not my type.” His children have been watching since birth. The admonitions, one may safely conclude, have been lost.

HOW TO REPROVE

“Parents should never humiliate their children before others. This will not only cause suffering to little sensitive hearts, but will tend to harden them. Reproof, if needed, should be administered to each child singly and alone.”

THE SCORECARD

A brief accounting, in the spirit of fairness and Traditional Values

SELFISHNESS

“Selfishness that binds the miser in his chains, that chills the heart, must never be allowed a place in the family circle. Teach the child to share his gifts and pleasures with others, to be obliging, kind and benevolent.”

The subject’s gifts and pleasures have included: a fleet of aircraft, a social media platform, a line of Bibles, a line of sneakers, a line of steaks, a cryptocurrency, a digital trading card series, and, briefly, a board game called Trump: The Game, the box of which featured the subject’s face and the instruction to “deal for it.” The game was discontinued in 1990 after poor sales. It was relaunched in 2004. It was discontinued again. Some lessons about sharing take longer than others.

BAD TEMPER

The handbook is unambiguous: “Dread an insubordinate temper, and deal with it as one of the greatest evils. Let the child feel by your manner that he is not a safe companion for the rest of the family when he is in anger.”

On the morning of January 6, 2021, the subject delivered a speech in which he used the word “fight” or “fighting” more than twenty times. He then watched television for several hours while the events he had encouraged unfolded at the United States Capitol. He did not, during this period, feel that he was an unsafe companion. He felt, by most accounts, quite pleased.

The handbook recommends that a child in the grip of bad temper be deprived of books and enjoyments until he learns to govern himself.

The subject was, at the relevant moment, seventy-four years old and the President of the United States.

The handbook did not anticipate this scenario.

CHEERFULNESS AT THE TABLE

“Controversy should not be permitted at the table. Every topic introduced should be calculated to instruct, interest or amuse. Reproof should never be administered at the table, either to a child or to a servant; no fault found with anything, and no unkind word spoken.”

Former White House staff have described mealtimes during the relevant administration as follows: fast food served on silver platters; briefings conducted over hamburgers; aides dismissed mid-cheeseburger; and at least one classified document reviewed alongside a well-done steak with ketchup. Whether unkind words were spoken is not in dispute. Whether the ketchup itself constituted a kind of reproof directed at the American cattle industry is a question for another essay.

A NOTE ON OUR SOURCE

It would be remiss not to acknowledge the publication that brought these timeless precepts to our attention: the Epoch Times, which ran this Victorian handbook under the heading “Traditional Values” in March 2023, presenting it as a model for modern family life.

The Epoch Times is, by any serious accounting, among the most enthusiastic media cheerleaders for the very gentleman whose Traditional Values we have been assessing. It has, over the years, described him as a once-in-a-generation leader, a defender of Western civilization, a bulwark against the forces of moral decay.

It has also, apparently without irony, published a guide instructing parents never to slander their neighbors, never to humiliate their children, never to deal lightly with the reputation of another, and always — always — to model for the young the behavior they wish to see in the world.

We do not know what the editors of the Epoch Times make of this apparent tension. We suspect they have not spent a great deal of time with it.

HOME MAXIMS, REVISED FOR THE OCCASION

The handbook closes with a list of maxims. We offer them here, lightly annotated, as a birthday gift:

“Remember that children are men and women in miniature, and though they should be allowed to act as children, still our dealings with them should be manly and not morose.”

Remember also that men are, occasionally, children in full-size, and that the nation has now twice elected one to its highest office. Adjust your maxims accordingly.

“Never trifle with a child’s feelings when under discipline.”

Or, indeed, with a democracy’s.

“Always follow commands with a close and careful watch, until the thing is done, allowing no evasion and no modification.”

The Senate has, on two occasions, declined this instruction. We note it without further comment.

“There should never be two sets of manners, the one for home and the other for company, but a gentle behavior should be always required.”

There is, as we have established, only one set. Whether it qualifies as gentle is left as an exercise for the reader.

Happy Birthday, Mr. President. The handbook assures us that it is never too late to form good habits. We choose to believe this. It is, in the circumstances, the most charitable interpretation available to us — and the handbook, above all things, counsels charity.

It is, we note, also Father’s Day, Flag Day, and Pride Month. The handbook has opinions on fathers. The flag has opinions on pride. We leave the rest as an exercise for the reader.

On this most auspicious of days, we do not offer you the Nobel Peace Prize. We offer you, instead, a piece of advice: read the handbook. It is never too late. The Epoch Times recommends it highly.
