In September 2026, for one full week, Ukraine produced not a single tonne of steel.

According to the New York Times, that had not happened in a century. It did not happen in the chaos of 1991, nor in the first terrible year of the full-scale invasion in 2022. In September 2026 the furnaces went cold, and almost nobody outside the country noticed, because the attention of the world, and of Washington in particular, was elsewhere: on the Strait of Hormuz, on the price of diesel in Iowa, and on whether a midterm election could be rescued by a telephone call to the Kremlin.

This series begins with that cold week because it tells you what kind of war this has become. It is no longer, mainly, a war of tank columns and lines on a map. As Constant Méheut reported from Kyiv for the New York Times on October 12, 2026, the front lines have largely frozen, and Russia and Ukraine have turned the conflict into a contest of economic endurance. Each side is trying to starve the other of the money it needs to keep fighting. Russia goes after Ukraine’s ports, factories, railways and power grid. Ukraine goes after Russia’s oil refineries.

On Friday, October 9, 2026, the President of the United States picked a side in that contest. That is the subject of Part II. Here I want to do something the American coverage mostly hasn’t: look at the economic war from the Ukrainian end, and understand why the one weapon Washington now wants Kyiv to put down is the one it can least afford to lose.

The sea that was closed

Start with the ports, because everything in Ukraine’s economy eventually runs through them.

The three ports of the Odesa region — Odesa itself, Chornomorsk and Pivdennyi — carry roughly 60 per cent of the country’s total export volume, according to the Times. They are how Ukrainian grain, the product of some of the most fertile soil on earth, reaches the bakeries of Cairo, Jakarta and Lagos. They are how Ukrainian steel reaches the world. In the language of an old telecom man, they are the trunk line; everything else is a branch circuit.

For months, Russia has been methodically making those ports too dangerous to use. Not by blockade in the classical sense, with warships drawn up across the harbour mouth, but by attacking the commercial ships that call there, one at a time, until insurers and captains draw their own conclusions. The results, drawn from port data the Times obtained, are the kind of numbers that make you read them twice. In August and September 2026, traffic through the three Odesa ports was down nearly 95 per cent compared with the same months a year earlier. In September 2025, 266 ships sailed out. In September 2026, twelve did.

Twelve.

Ukraine has done what a resourceful country does when its front door is barred: it has tried the side door. Cargo has been rerouted to the ports on the Danube, which flows along the Romanian border; ships can travel down the river, out into the Black Sea and south along the coasts of Romania and Bulgaria, both NATO members, towards the Bosphorus. But the Danube ports can handle only about half of what Odesa normally moves, and in the first week of October even that route came under fire. Drones struck three ships off the coasts of Romania and Bulgaria, sinking two and killing several crew members. Every one of the three had carried Ukrainian grain at some point, according to Romanian authorities and port data. Kyiv blamed Moscow. Romania and Bulgaria, NATO allies on whose doorstep the ships went down, have so far declined to assign blame at all. Russia has not commented.

The consequence is sitting in silos across the country. Ukraine’s agriculture minister said in October 2026 that farmers had been unable to export about 30 million tonnes of agricultural products, worth roughly $30 billion. For a sense of scale, that is in the neighbourhood of an entire year’s wheat harvest for Canada’s Prairie provinces, stranded in place.

And here is the detail I keep returning to. According to estimates from the U.S. Department of Agriculture, Russia overtook Ukraine as the world’s largest exporter of sunflower oil in the 2025–26 marketing year, the first time in two decades that Ukraine has not held the top spot. The occupier is now selling to the world the product the occupied cannot get out of its own harbours.

Ukrainians do not need this pattern explained to them. Grain as a weapon is not a new idea on that soil. In 1932–33, Soviet requisitioning of Ukrainian grain produced the famine Ukrainians call the Holodomor, in which millions died. In July 2022, the United Nations and Turkey brokered the Black Sea Grain Initiative to get Ukrainian grain moving again after the invasion; Russia walked out of it a year later, in July 2023. The weapon has changed from requisition squads to drones. The target has not.

The furnaces that went cold

Now go inland, to the factories.

Metinvest is Ukraine’s largest steel manufacturer, and its story, as the Times laid it out, reads like a demonstration of how to dismantle an industrial economy one layer at a time. First, Russian strikes on the power grid left its plants in the southeast running on intermittent electricity. Then the attacks on the ports closed off the main route to foreign buyers. Then strikes on the railways left finished steel stranded inside the country with nowhere to go. Finally, in September 2026, strikes on the plants themselves forced them to shut down.

Power, then ports, then rail, then the plant. It is not random. It is a sequence.

Steel contributed more than 7 per cent of Ukraine’s economic output in 2025. Oleksandr Myronenko, Metinvest’s chief operating officer, told the Times that the collapse would carry severe economic consequences: administrative layoffs are already planned, and the towns built around the steel plants will watch their municipal budgets shrink. Anyone who grew up in an Ontario mill town, or watched Hamilton’s long relationship with its furnaces, knows what that sentence means for the people who live there. It means the arena, the library hours, the snow-clearing budget. It means the young leave.

Steel is only the most visible casualty. Russian strikes now routinely hit warehouses, power plants and data centres. Ukraine’s economy minister has put the destruction of business assets at nearly $10 billion a year. The strategy, in the Times’ assessment, is methodical: hit everything an economy needs to function, until it can’t.

The people underneath the numbers

It would be a betrayal of this series’ purpose to talk only about tonnes and dollars, so let me be plain about the human cost running alongside the economic one.

Show Image Khmelnytskyi after a Russian strike, March 2024 (Wikimedia Commons)

More than 100 Ukrainian civilians were killed in the week leading up to October 12, 2026. The United Nations has described civilian casualties as having reached what it called a “new horrifying level.” Russia is striking with a growing arsenal of increasingly powerful and precise weapons, and it is finding the gaps in Ukrainian air defences. On Saturday, October 10, 2026, rescuers in Zaporizhzhia were carrying bodies out of rubble.

And winter is coming. The fear in Kyiv, stated openly, is that if Russia succeeds in taking down Ukraine’s power and water systems before the cold sets in, the country faces a humanitarian crisis. That is not a metaphor. It is apartment blocks without heat in January, hospitals on generators, water that stops at the tap. The economic war and the war on civilians are, at this point, the same war. Zelensky’s aides expect Putin to use the winter to pound Ukraine’s cities before another lunge at Kyiv in the spring, as Jonathan Lemire and Simon Shuster reported for The Atlantic on October 12, 2026. Russia has enlarged its missile stockpiles and debuted a jet-powered drone that Ukrainian air defences have struggled to stop.

The one lever Ukraine has

Against all of this, what does Ukraine have?

It has drones, and it has learned to use them on the one part of the Russian economy that matters most: oil.

Show Image Fire at the Moscow Oil Refinery, June 18, 2026 (Wikimedia Commons)

The campaign began in 2024 with strikes on depots and refineries, and at first, by the Times’ account, it had little effect. Refineries are large, and damage can be patched. But Ukraine kept at it. It built up its fleet of long-range attack drones, and, crucially, it learned which components inside a refinery are hardest to repair or replace under sanctions, and aimed for those. The effects compounded.

The numbers now are not Ukrainian boasting; some of them come from Moscow itself. In October 2026, Vladimir Putin acknowledged that Ukrainian attacks on refineries and other businesses had cost Russia about 1 per cent of its gross domestic product. The International Energy Agency reported in September 2026 that Russian gasoline output was down about 20 per cent from 2025 levels and diesel production down nearly 30 per cent, and that Russia’s oil tax revenue from January to August 2026 ran almost 17 per cent below the same period a year earlier. By Ukrainian estimates cited by David Frum in The Atlantic on October 10, 2026, Ukraine had shut down 43 per cent of Russian refinery capacity by late summer. Russia was forced to ban diesel exports in July 2026 and extend the ban into October.

Set that beside the ledger on the other side. Russia can strike Ukraine’s ports, steel, rail, warehouses, power and data centres. Ukraine can strike Russia’s refineries, and some of its Black Sea ports. Even there the contest is lopsided: economists told the Times that Russia’s losses at its Black Sea terminals don’t compare with Ukraine’s, because Russia can still ship through the Baltic and the Caspian, which Ukrainian weapons struggle to reach. Ukraine has no Baltic. Ukraine has Odesa, and the Danube, and both are under fire.

In other words, the refinery campaign is not one weapon among many. It is the counterweight. Take it away, and the economic war becomes a one-sided demolition with a polite pause on one side and none on the other. As the Times put it plainly, should Kyiv be forced to halt the refinery strikes, Russia’s advantage in the contest to starve the other side of money will only grow.

What Kyiv has actually offered

This is the part of the story that the loudest voices in Washington have chosen not to hear, so it is worth setting down carefully.

Ukraine has not refused to stop. Ukraine has refused to stop alone.

Volodymyr Zelensky has said repeatedly that Ukraine is prepared to halt its attacks on Russian energy infrastructure if Russia halts its attacks on Ukraine’s. Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha has gone further and spelled out the terms. As Ukranews reported, Sybiha said Kyiv had received ceasefire proposals from India, Turkey, Egypt and the United States, and that Ukraine would accept an energy ceasefire together with a Black Sea ceasefire, but only as a package: a complete halt to attacks on energy facilities and on port infrastructure. He added that Kyiv was ready to consider a broader ceasefire along the front line itself.

Read that against everything above, and the logic is obvious. An energy-only truce would protect Russia’s refineries, its chief source of war revenue, while leaving Russia free to keep sinking grain ships and shutting down Ukraine’s ports, its own chief source of revenue. That is not a ceasefire. It is a handicap applied to one runner.

On Monday, October 12, 2026, Zelensky said Ukraine had refrained from striking Russian refineries overnight. That same day, entering a meeting of European foreign ministers in Luxembourg, Sybiha told reporters that Ukraine was ready for an energy ceasefire in any format that was mutual, anything that was not one-sided, and noted that Russia had attacked Ukrainian energy infrastructure on the Sunday night, just hours after the American president announced that both sides had agreed to stop.

The trade on the table

So here is the situation as it looked from Kyiv on the evening of Friday, October 9, 2026.

Ukraine’s ports were down 95 per cent. Its steel industry had gone dark for the first time in a hundred years. Thirty million tonnes of its harvest were stuck. A hundred of its civilians had been killed in a week, and winter was weeks away. Its one effective economic lever was working well enough that Putin himself had admitted the damage. And Ukraine had a standing offer on the table to give up that lever, provided Russia stopped destroying Ukraine’s ports and power in return.

Then, in a conference room at a golf club outside Miami, Ukraine’s negotiators were told that the United States would lift sanctions on Russian diesel, that the American president wanted Ukraine’s refinery strikes stopped, and that Ukraine should not expect anything in return.

That meeting, and what followed it, is the subject of the next essay. But I want to close this one by naming what was actually being proposed, because the language of “energy prices” and “ceasefires” tends to blur it. Ukraine was being asked to unilaterally disarm in the one part of the war where it was winning, in a war it is fighting for its existence, so that the price of diesel in the United States might fall a few cents before an election.

A great many things in this world can be traded. A good deal of Ukrainian history has been about larger powers deciding which of them Ukraine was. The question this series will follow, week by week, is whether that is still true, and the early evidence, from the people living it, is that they do not intend to let it be.
