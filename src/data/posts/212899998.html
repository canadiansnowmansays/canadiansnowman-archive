There is a peculiar dissonance running through the coverage of American grocery prices this year, and it is worth naming before getting into the numbers, because it explains a great deal about why this particular corner of the household ledger generates more anger, proportionally, than almost any other. The official statistics describe a story of moderation: food-at-home prices, the government’s term for what you buy at a grocery store rather than a restaurant, rose by roughly 2.7 percent over the twelve months ending in July, a rate the U.S. Department of Agriculture forecasts will land somewhere between 2.3 and 2.5 percent for 2026 as a whole — below the twenty-year historical average, and a small fraction of the 11.8 percent food inflation the country endured in 2022. By the measure that shows up in a government report, the worst of the grocery crisis is behind us.

By the measure that shows up in a shopping cart, it is not. Surveyed consumers in 2025 reported experiencing food inflation of roughly 5.4 percent — almost double the official figure for the same period — and their forecast for the year ahead runs meaningfully hotter than the government’s own projection. That is not a rounding error or a matter of consumers simply being bad at arithmetic. It is a gap wide enough, and consistent enough across surveys, that it deserves to be treated as data in its own right about how grocery inflation is actually experienced, rather than dismissed as noise around an otherwise reassuring headline number.

Where the gap comes from

Part of the explanation is compounding. The official annual figures describe a single year’s change, but households do not experience a single year in isolation — they experience the cumulative effect of several consecutive years of increases stacked on top of each other. Grocery prices have risen by roughly 25 percent since 2020 by one measure, and by nearly 37 percent since 2015 by another, using Bureau of Labor Statistics data — a compounding effect that a single year’s “only 2.7 percent” headline entirely obscures. A grocery cart that cost $150 in 2019 costs roughly $190 for the identical contents today. The average weekly grocery spend has climbed to about $170, up from $120 in 2020 — a rate of increase that itself outpaces the reported inflation figures for the same period, according to the industry’s own trade association, which tracks the discrepancy closely enough to comment on it publicly. Andrew Harig of that association, the Food Industry Association, put the psychological dimension of this plainly: even as headline concern about rising costs has ticked down somewhat, that does not mean shoppers “feel great about” the prices they are paying — resignation, not relief, is closer to the actual mood at the register.

The other part of the explanation is that the official figure is an average across categories moving in wildly different directions, and averages conceal exactly the categories a household actually notices. Beef prices rose roughly 16 percent in the twelve months to last September, and separate reporting this year put the year-over-year increase above 12 percent and climbing, with USDA modelling suggesting beef could rise as much as 9.4 percent on average in 2026 alone, with a plausible range running as high as 16.6 percent.

Coffee is up nearly 20 percent by one measure. Sugar and sweets — the category the USDA projects will rise fastest in 2026, at close to 6.7 percent — sit at the steep end of a basket where eggs and a handful of other items are actually expected to fall, and even fresh vegetables, often assumed to be a reliable budget-friendly fallback, are forecast to rise a further 2 percent this year after a brief dip, with fresh tomatoes specifically up nearly 13 percent year over year as of July. A household does not experience “the basket.” It experiences the specific handful of items it buys every week, and if those happen to be beef, coffee, and anything containing sugar, the 2.7 percent headline number will feel like a statistical trick rather than a description of anything real.

Tariffs, specifically, and why this series keeps returning to them

The previous instalment of this series traced how tariffs on steel, aluminum, and lumber translate into the cost of a home and a car. Food is a less obvious tariff casualty than a washing machine, since a great deal of American grocery spending goes toward domestically produced goods. But the exposure is real and, on the analysis available, sizeable: one estimate puts the average American household’s tariff-related cost specifically at more than $2,500 a year once food, clothing, and household supplies are counted alongside the categories examined in Part III — a figure that reflects a structural feature of tariffs worth stating plainly, because it is central to why this series keeps returning to the same mechanism. Lower- and middle-income families spend a much larger share of their income on physical goods directly exposed to tariff pressure — food, clothing, household supplies — while higher-income households spend proportionally more on services, which tariffs do not touch at all. A tariff burden of a given dollar amount is, by construction, not a flat tax. It is a tax that falls disproportionately on people with the least room to absorb it, a dynamic distinct from, but compounding, the denial pattern traced in Part II of this series. Put in concrete terms: a $2,500 annual tariff burden is a rounding error for a household earning $200,000 a year, and a genuinely painful subtraction from one earning $60,000 — the same dollar figure, landing on two entirely different scales of hardship.

Beyond tariffs, the supply side of food inflation has its own set of pressures largely outside any single policy’s control: disease and weather affecting livestock and produce, worker shortages and rising labor costs across the food supply chain, the suspension of a Mexican tomato trade agreement, and — a thread that connects this instalment back to the very first one — rising energy costs tied to the unresolved Iran conflict, which raise the fuel costs embedded in every stage of moving food from a farm to a shelf. The administration’s occasional targeted responses, such as waiving tariffs specifically on ground beef, illustrate both the mechanism and its limits: a single carve-out can ease one line item, but it does not touch the broader structure driving the rest of the cart upward, and experts tracking the full picture are candid that no broad relief at the checkout line is likely any time soon.

Who is actually absorbing this, and how unevenly

If there is a single finding from this year’s surveys and research worth holding onto, it is how unevenly the burden of even modest headline inflation is actually distributed. A LendingTree survey conducted early this year found that roughly one in five young people, families with children, and low-income households said affording food had become difficult — compared with just one in twenty baby boomers reporting the same difficulty. That gap, four to one, exists despite everyone in the survey facing the same headline grocery inflation rate. The difference is not the price tag. It is the size of the paycheck the price tag is being measured against, and the presence or absence of savings, home equity, or a fixed-rate mortgage to cushion the impact — the same distinction, in slightly different form, that ran through Part II’s point about record retirement-account gains meaning very little to a household living paycheck to paycheck.

Wages have not kept pace either, at least for the households already most exposed. Data from the Bank of America Institute found that middle- and low-income consumers’ after-tax wages have not kept pace with inflation since the start of the year — meaning the gap between what a paycheck buys and what it used to buy has been widening in real time throughout the very months the administration has been describing affordability as a solved problem. And the strain shows up in more targeted ways than the broad income-bracket data captures: a forthcoming survey cited this week found that forty-two percent of federal student loan borrowers say their monthly payments are now making it difficult to afford groceries and other basic needs — a specific, identifiable population for whom two separate cost pressures, food and debt service, are compounding at the same time.

The University of Michigan’s long-running consumer sentiment survey put a number on the cumulative psychological effect of all of this: economic optimism hit an all-time low in the survey’s history this August, with the sharpest declines concentrated among older Americans, lower-income consumers, and those without a college degree — precisely the groups least equipped to absorb the specific price increases detailed above. Survey researchers note that consumer optimism is unlikely to recover meaningfully until gas prices, in particular, begin falling — a reminder that grocery inflation does not sit in isolation from the broader cost-of-living picture this series has been tracking, but compounds with fuel and energy costs into a single, harder-to-shake sense that everything, all at once, costs more than it used to. Cornell economist Christopher Barrett’s assessment of where this leads is blunt and worth quoting directly: households facing food prices rising faster than their incomes typically respond by choosing cheaper, less nutritious diets — not a hypothetical outcome, but the documented pattern from previous inflationary periods repeating itself.

This is worth connecting back explicitly to Part II’s account of the president’s “I’ve won affordability” framing, because grocery prices are precisely the terrain on which that framing is hardest to sustain. A mortgage rate or a bond yield is abstract enough that most people experience it only secondhand, through a monthly statement that arrives once and is then filed away. A grocery bill is paid, and felt, every single week, often multiple times a week, by nearly every household in the country regardless of income or political affiliation.

It is difficult to construct a durable argument that a cost-of-living crisis has been solved when the evidence against that argument renews itself at the checkout counter on a weekly basis, for tens of millions of people simultaneously. If the affordability debate has a ground truth that no amount of rhetorical framing can fully override, the grocery aisle is very likely it.

What comes next

The next instalment turns to housing — mortgage rates, rent, and the term-premium mechanics introduced in Part I made concrete in the single largest line item on most household ledgers. Expect the same pattern to recur in sharper form: an official statistic that understates the lived experience, a cost that compounds rather than resets each year, and a burden that falls hardest on households with the least capacity to absorb it.
