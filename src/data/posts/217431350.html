At about five minutes to one on the afternoon of September 25, 2026, having viewed the Declaration of Independence, the Constitution and the Bill of Rights in the company of the General Secretary of the Chinese Communist Party, the President of the United States walked over to the reporters and explained what the two men had been discussing.

“We’re just comparing,” Trump said. “Ours goes 250 years. Which is great, and we’re proud of. Theirs goes 6,000 years. So there’s a little difference between 6,000 and 250. But we’re very proud of our 250.”

Beijing itself usually settles for five thousand years of civilisation. The American president, in a generous mood, threw in an extra millennium.

It was the last public word of a three-day state visit, delivered in the rotunda that holds the founding documents, in the very year the country marks the 250th anniversary of the first of them. Zolan Kanno-Youngs of The New York Times reported that the archivist of the United States, Bradford Wilson, conducted the tour. By 1:30, Xi Jinping and his wife, Peng Liyuan, were on their way back to China. Trump posted that the visit had been “one of Friendship, Strength, and Success,” and added: “I look forward to our next meeting!”

There will be two more this year. He will go to Shenzhen in November. Xi will come to Florida in December. That was the one firm commitment the summit produced, and it was a commitment to hold more summits.

Nice and Clean and Sparkling

Part VIII ended with Xi being shown the foundations of the ballroom on the afternoon of September 24. The evening developed the theme.

At the state dinner that night, before a room holding Jeff Bezos, Elon Musk, Jensen Huang, Tim Cook, Mark Zuckerberg and Sam Altman, a room that Shawn McCreesh of The New York Times described as full of the people “deciding the future of humanity,” Trump announced that he wished to go “off-script, just for a second.” The guests applauded. Here, at last, would be the word on artificial intelligence.

It was an invitation to tour the building site.

“I’d like to invite everyone in the room to visit through that beautiful curtain in the back,” he said. “It’s one year away from completion, the new ballroom that you’ve heard about.” The room they were sitting in held about 145, he explained; with the ballroom, “we would have had way more than a thousand people, with standing room only.” And: “Anybody who wants to walk into a construction site that’s nice and clean and sparkling, it’s our honour to have you do so. This is in honour of President Xi, Madam Peng.”

McCreesh noted that the speech did not mention artificial intelligence once. He called the president “maître d’ to the mighty,” a phrase I would have been proud to coin, since the photographs of him waving Xi towards the South Portico on the first morning had suggested much the same thing.

By Friday morning the site had become the soundtrack. As Trump welcomed Xi back for tea in the Red Room, the press pool could hear hammering and drilling from the roughly $400 million ballroom next door. On the earlier tour, according to Peter Baker of The New York Times, the president had also made sure his guest saw the photograph of an autopen he had hung in the Colonnade in place of Joe Biden’s portrait. Even Charles Cooke of National Review called that “grotesque behavior,” observing that Xi “is a communist dictator, and the head of a foreign state. Our internal divisions are not fair game in this context.”

Xi, at the tea, gave his review. “Everything is done to the highest order of perfection,” he said through an interpreter. “There is thought in every little detail and it’s greatly appreciated.” Trump, the pool reported, was visibly pleased. It was the notice he had been waiting for: a rave for the production, with not a word about the play.

Fair Coverage

At the tea, the president also thanked everyone for the “fair coverage” of the visit. The event was open to television cameras and still photographers, but not to print reporters, and nobody took questions.

The cameras were a novelty. The networks had suspended the White House television pool on September 23 and 24, after Trump barred CNN, MS NOW and Politico, and only came back on the final morning. Baker described the scene at the gate on September 24: a throng of Chinese state journalists admitted to the grounds, American reporters held on Pennsylvania Avenue. Reporters Without Borders counts at least 111 journalists and media workers imprisoned in China, which ranks 178th of 180 countries for press freedom. The United States has slipped to 64th. Shortly before the dinner, Trump posted that the networks had skipped “the arrival at the White House of the Highly Respected President of China, Xi Jinping, and his beautiful wife, Madam Peng,” an event “Actually made for Television.” “Trump doesn’t just want to meet with Xi,” Senator Chris Coons of Delaware wrote. “He wants to copy him.”

The Toasts

Trump’s toast was warm and general. “President Xi and I both understand that we represent different systems but the ties between our people endure,” he said. He had, he told the room, “never gotten along better” with Xi.

Xi’s toast was a history lesson, and a selective one. As Kanno-Youngs described it, Xi passed over the worst of the Cold War and went straight to Richard Nixon’s opening to Beijing in 1972, the same year the dinner’s Schramsberg toast was meant to evoke. “Such a friendship, forged in blood and fire,” he said, “will surely live on through the generations.” He presumably meant the wartime alliance against Japan, the one chapter of that century on which both capitals agree. The message, in Kanno-Youngs’s reading, was that Trump too could reopen the relationship, on Beijing’s terms.

Nixon went to China with a strategy and came home with pandas. Xi came to Washington with a strategy and left the pandas behind.

The Empty Chairs

One group was missing from the East Room. As Sui-Lee Wee reported for The New York Times on September 25, no prominent Chinese chief executives attended the dinner.

When Xi first came on a state visit in 2015, he brought Jack Ma of Alibaba and Pony Ma of Tencent, and American and Chinese business leaders applauded him side by side. This time, several Chinese executives were in Washington, reportedly including candidates from BYD and CATL, but none was invited. “There are a number of important Chinese C.E.O.s in Washington, waiting on the sidelines, like: ‘Do I get tapped or not?’” said Jeremy Chan of Eurasia Group. “It would be comical if it weren’t so tragic.” Emily Kilcrease of the Center for a New American Security called the absence “a rather depressing marker of how bad the relationship has gotten.”

The American executives were there in force, many of them the same ones who had stood on the steps of the Great Hall of the People in May, when Trump presented them to Xi as “the greatest businessmen” who had come “to pay respects to you and to China.” Joerg Wuttke, a partner at DGA-Albright Stonebridge Group, described the scene as American executives “kissing Xi’s ring,” and asked the question that explains the empty chairs: “Is there anybody from China interested in doing business in America? No, because it’s not possible. So why should they waste their time?”

One side sent its chief executives to pay court. The other side kept its own at home, and did not need them.

The Same Eagle

There was an exchange of gifts. China is sending two living pandas, Ping Ping and Fu Shuang, to Zoo Atlanta, on loan, and they will one day return to Sichuan, as pandas do. The United States gave Xi a large statue of a bald eagle perched on a branch. “We hope it’ll find a nice place, a wonderful place, in Beijing,” Trump said.

It will not be alone. As Claire Moses reported for The New York Times, Ronald Reagan gave Deng Xiaoping a crystal bald eagle on his state visit in 1984, and it now sits in the National Museum of China. The eagle has become Trump’s standard gift: one went to Poland’s president, Karol Nawrocki, and another to Scotland’s first minister, John Swinney, and a desk eagle was apparently prepared for Vladimir Putin in Alaska. Moses also turned up a remark by Selwa Roosevelt, Reagan’s chief of protocol, to The Times in 1984, which deserves to be engraved on the next plinth: “If someone stays in power for many years, you don’t want to keep giving him the same eagle.”

Xi has been in power since 2012 and is expected to stay on well beyond next autumn’s party congress. Beijing now holds two American eagles.

What Was Agreed

Kanno-Youngs’s tally of results by midday on September 25 can be quoted whole: the two-month extension of the trade truce, the pandas, and Xi’s apparent support for Trump’s rebranding of artificial intelligence as “super intelligence.” Trump confirmed the last himself. “That would be ‘SUPER!’” he posted. “Everyone last night agreed.”

A summit billed as an occasion for serious talk about artificial intelligence thus reached agreement on the name. Xinhua’s account at least recorded Xi saying the two sides could “continue AI dialogue” and “together guard against the misuse or malicious use of AI,” in a translation carried by CNBC. The American side offered a capital letter.

On trade, Trump said the meeting was “great for the farmers” and gave no details. The details, such as they were, came from Jamieson Greer, the U.S. trade representative, on CNBC on the morning of September 25. The two sides, he said, had agreed on a “subset” of nonsensitive goods, American farm products and medical devices and Chinese consumer goods, to be traded on better terms through the “Board of Trade” set up in Beijing in May. He named no products and no rates, and promised “a lot more details” on Monday, September 28. National security export controls, the thing Beijing wants most, were “off the table.” And he described the two-month truce as a series of “compliance periods,” in which Washington would judge whether China had kept its earlier promises on soybeans and rare earths.

It was a curious week in which to hold a compliance review. Kanno-Youngs reported that there is little sign China has made good on its May promise to sharply increase purchases of American farm goods. Greer said the goods deficit with China had fallen by nearly 40 per cent, and in the same interview acknowledged that trade had simply moved elsewhere: American imports from Mexico hit a record $60.6 billion in July. “Donald Trump is giving away the store to China,” said Senator Ron Wyden of Oregon, “and leaving American families with the bill.”

Then there was the matter nobody raised. In a prime-time address in July, Kanno-Youngs reminded readers, Trump accused China of “sinister election meddling” in 2020 and of “the largest compromise of election data in history.” In September, the same man hailed “a truly great friendship.” Asked whether the president would raise his accusation with Xi, his aides declined to comment. “He’s not a reliable narrator,” said Juliette Kayyem, a former assistant secretary of homeland security. Trump himself had already written off the grievance a few days after the speech: “They do things and we do things to them.”

What Xi Took Home

Keith Bradsher, who has covered the American-Chinese trade relationship for The New York Times since 1991, identified the real prize in a single word: time.

China needs time to manage a housing crash in which apartment prices have fallen twice as far as American prices did in 2008, wiping out household savings and starving local governments of land revenue. It needs time to prepare for next autumn’s Communist Party congress. It needs time to keep reducing its exposure to American pressure, a project already well advanced in batteries, solar panels, electric vehicles and the national grid. “They want time and stability,” Evan Medeiros of Georgetown told Bradsher. The short truce extension may reflect a calculation that time favours Beijing: if the Republicans lose badly in November, Medeiros said, “suddenly, the negotiation price changes because Trump will be on his back foot.”

Bradsher also noted the protocol ledger. When Trump landed in Beijing in May, Xi sent Vice President Han Zheng to the airport. When Xi landed in Washington, Trump was waiting at the foot of the steps himself.

Nicholas Burns, Joe Biden’s ambassador to China, called the current policy “far too weak and contrary to the views of senior leaders in both parties.”

The Scorecard

Readers of this series know the TACO framework: Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate. The final day filled in the last columns. Accommodate: a second eagle, a tea party, and an election-meddling charge quietly dropped. Obfuscate: “great for the farmers,” “tremendous things,” and details promised for Monday. Capitulate is harder to score, but the export controls came off the table without a fight, and Greer’s “compliance periods” require a willingness to find non-compliance that the week gave no sign of.

Which brings us back to the rotunda. The Declaration of Independence is, among other things, a document written by men in a hurry. They had a war to win and no time to lose, and they built a system that would renew itself every two, four and six years. Its genius and its handicap are the same: it is a republic that runs on a short clock.

On September 25, the leader of that republic stood beside its founding texts and told the man who governs by the long clock exactly how the two measures compare. Xi had come to Washington for time. Trump, in the building that houses the American proof of what can be done in 250 years, volunteered the figure himself, rounded up.

There is, as the president said, a little difference.

Until the next Opera begins.
