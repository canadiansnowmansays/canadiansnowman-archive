Around 140 interventions have landed on the public record for Broadcasting and Telecom Notice of Consultation CRTC 2026-134, as of this writing in September 2026. The sequence numbers run past 200, but gaps in the numbering mean the actual count is closer to 140, and the record is still growing. The proceeding will merge the Wireless Code, the Internet Code, the Television Service Provider Code, and the Deposit and Disconnection Code into a single Consumer Protection Code. Most of those interventions are individual Canadians, and I’ll get to them in a later instalment. This one is about the three companies that between them serve the overwhelming majority of Canadian wireless and internet subscribers, and that filed the three interventions the Commission will actually have to reckon with when the hearing opens in Gatineau on November 30: Bell, TELUS, and Rogers.

All three filed on August 14, 2026. All three support harmonisation — in the abstract. All three oppose the Commission’s actual Working Document, on the grounds that it does the opposite of what harmonisation is supposed to do. And underneath the shared framing, each company reached for a different set of tools, suited to its own position in the market. Bell defended its brand portfolio. TELUS went after the referee — a legal argument, buried in the middle of its filing, that may be the single most consequential ask in the entire proceeding, and one I’ll return to in full below. Rogers marshalled the Commission’s own research against it. None of the three said a word about advance billing.

The shared playbook

Before the divergence, the convergence, because it’s the more interesting part. Strip away each company’s house style and four asks recur, close to verbatim, across all three filings:

Federal preemption. Bell, TELUS, and Rogers all want the Commission to declare the new Code the sole, comprehensive national framework for consumer protection in telecommunications — explicitly foreclosing any provincial consumer-protection statute from applying concurrently to a federally regulated carrier’s contracts. This is the same ask in three different voices, right down to the citation to the Commission’s own 2019 finding in the Internet Code proceeding as precedent.

No more 24-month ceiling on device financing. The Wireless Code has capped early cancellation fees tied to a financed device at 24 months since 2013. All three want that ceiling gone, or at minimum turned into a floor — a mandatory 24-month option alongside whatever longer term the carrier wants to offer. The argument is identical across all three filings: device prices have risen faster than plan prices, so a longer amortisation period is what actually keeps monthly payments down. It’s a genuinely defensible economic argument. It’s also, not incidentally, an argument for locking customers into carrier financing relationships for three years instead of two.

A permanent 60-day device lock. All three support turning TELUS’s May 2026 interim-relief application — permission to lock new financed or subsidized phones to the network for 60 days after activation, to fight resale fraud — into a permanent Code provision. Rogers and TELUS both cite their own internal fraud-loss data (heavily redacted in both filings) as the evidentiary basis.

Retiring the MSRP benchmark. The manufacturer’s suggested retail price currently anchors how early cancellation fees and device subsidies are calculated. All three want it gone, on the argument that it hands pricing authority to phone manufacturers rather than carriers. Whether that’s a consumer-protective simplification or the removal of the one fixed number a customer can check their carrier’s math against depends entirely on what replaces it — and none of the three filings proposes a replacement.

Silence on advance billing. Between them, Bell, TELUS, and Rogers filed roughly 240 pages of intervention across three companies with the resources to litigate every clause of a Working Document. None of the three mentions the practice of billing customers before service is delivered — the very practice this whole newsletter exists to argue about. That’s not proof the Commission won’t touch it. It is proof that none of the three biggest carriers in the country considered it a fight worth having on the public record, which tells you something about how seriously the industry expects the issue to be pressed by anyone else.

The pattern holds together better as a table than as a paragraph:

Bell: defend the portfolio

Bell’s August 14, 2026 intervention (Consumer Intervention 200 — the Commission’s file number for Bell’s submission) reads like a filing written by people who expect to win on cost and lose on rhetoric, and are fine with that trade. Its six priority submissions are narrow and tactical:

  • Delay implementation by a minimum of two years, with an effective date no earlier than January 1, 2030, justified largely by references — mostly redacted — to an internal “System Modernization” programme. If the Commission’s final decision lands in mid-2027, as several parties expect, Bell’s own proposed date would leave the current, pre-harmonisation codes as the live rules for another two and a half years after that.

  • Declare the Code the sole national framework (the federal preemption ask, above).

  • Exempt Bell’s stop-sold brands — Distributel, Acanac, Oricom, and Bell Mobility’s prepaid line — from any new requirement entirely.

  • Refuse to extend home phone and internet rules to small business contracts.

  • Refuse to extend the Wireless Code’s $50 data-overage suspension cap to internet service, or failing that, apply it per service rather than per account.

  • Widen the Critical Information Summary’s page limit from two pages total to two pages per service.

Two adjacent, live disputes get folded in for good measure: Bell’s $40 Device Handling Fee and its 60-day-post-activation unlocking practice are both under separate CRTC review, and Bell uses this filing to pre-load its position — if it loses either fight elsewhere, it wants this proceeding on record as having already blessed the practice.

The tone throughout is heavy on confidential redaction and light on ideology. Bell isn’t trying to win an argument about what regulation should be for. It’s trying to protect specific commercial arrangements — the discount brands, the fee structures, the existing timeline — one line item at a time. Even its one procedural jab, a complaint that the Commission’s last-minute extension of the filing deadline “does not cure the underlying problem,” reads more like a lawyer noting an objection for the record than a company spoiling for a fight.

TELUS: go after the referee

TELUS’s own August 14, 2026 intervention (Consumer Intervention 197), at 82 pages plus four appendices, is a different kind of document entirely. Where Bell argues from cost, TELUS argues from nation-building. Prime Minister Carney’s Red Tape Review gets cited repeatedly. So does TELUS’s own $66-billion five-year infrastructure commitment. This is a filing written to be quoted in Cabinet as much as in front of the Commission.

Structurally, TELUS organizes its position into six focus areas, and several of them land in the same place as Bell’s asks — federal preemption, the 24-month ECF ceiling, the permanent device lock — but TELUS consistently takes the harder version of each position. On small business and television, for instance, TELUS doesn’t just resist extending select rules; it wants both television services and small business customers excluded from the harmonised Code altogether, arguing that streaming competition has already gutted the policy rationale for regulating traditional broadcast distributors at all.

On the small-ISP exemption, TELUS goes the opposite direction from Bell: no exemptions for anyone, of any size. Framed as pro-competitive fairness, the practical effect is to raise compliance costs on TELUS’s smaller wholesale-dependent rivals — the same companies that, as we’ll see in the next instalment, are fighting hard to keep the exemption Bell is happy to let them have.

And then there’s the one item that doesn’t have an analogue anywhere else in this proceeding: TELUS argues, across a full section of legal reasoning, that the CRTC has been unlawfully sub-delegating quasi-judicial authority to the CCTS for years, and that the Commission should either strip the CCTS of its role as Code administrator entirely or, at minimum, make its decisions appealable to the Commission itself. If TELUS wins that argument, the entire consumer-complaint infrastructure in this country gets rebuilt — CCTS rulings stop being final, and disputes that used to end with an ombudsman start ending up back at the regulator. It is, by a wide margin, the most consequential single ask filed by anyone in this proceeding, consumer or carrier, and it has nothing to do with any specific billing practice. It’s an argument about who gets to referee the whole system going forward.

Rogers: fight with the Commission’s own numbers

Rogers’ August 14, 2026 intervention (Consumer Intervention 176), filed by SVP of Regulatory Affairs Dean Shaikh, takes a third approach: rather than arguing from cost (Bell) or from national purpose (TELUS), Rogers argues from evidence — specifically, the Commission’s own public opinion research, turned back against the Commission’s proposed rules.

On international roaming, Rogers wants the $50 usage-notification requirement eliminated and the $100 data-roaming suspension cap narrowed to pay-per-use roaming only, exempting the daily fixed-rate plans and travel passes an increasing share of customers now use. To support this, Rogers cites the Commission’s own research finding that 81 percent of wireless subscribers reported no bill shock in the past year, that 73 percent paid no international roaming charges at all, and that 86 percent paid no wireless data overage fees. Bill shock and advance billing are not the same harm — one is about the size of a bill, the other about when you’re charged for service you haven’t received yet — but the figures are still worth flagging, because they’re real, CRTC-commissioned numbers, not carrier spin, and a genuinely different rhetorical move than anything in Bell’s or TELUS’s filings. Rogers is betting that the Commission’s own data will do more work than an appeal to cost or nationhood. On implementation timing specifically, Rogers asks for the new Code to take effect no earlier than 18 months after the Commission’s final decision — shorter than Bell’s ask, longer than what the CCTS and PIAC will separately argue is sufficient, as we’ll see in a later instalment.

Where Rogers converges with the pack — MSRP elimination, the 24-month ECF ceiling, the permanent device lock — it does so with the same arguments as Bell and TELUS. Where it diverges, it’s often in the direction of resisting change rather than requesting it: Rogers wants the current 15-day trial period held at 15 days, resisting the Working Document’s proposed uniform 30-day trial, on the grounds that a longer trial period increases exposure to fraud. And on disconnection, Rogers wants the Commission to adopt the older “$50 past due OR two months overdue” standard rather than the stricter “AND” standard the Internet Code currently uses — a change that would make it easier, not harder, to cut a customer off for non-payment.

Rogers also takes its own shot at the CCTS, though a milder one than TELUS’s: rather than attacking its legal authority, Rogers simply notes that the Commission promised a review of the CCTS’s mandate more than three years ago and never delivered it, and asks that the review happen before the new Code takes effect.

Same floor, three different levers

Put the three filings side by side and a pattern holds that’s worth naming plainly: none of Bell, TELUS, or Rogers is asking the Commission to weigh a genuinely contested question about what protects consumers. All three are asking the Commission to select, provision by provision, whichever existing rule already imposes the least obligation — the shorter cancellation window, the narrower disconnection standard, the un-capped financing term — and call the result “harmonisation.” It is the industry’s own word for the exercise, and it means something close to the opposite of what a consumer reading the word would assume.

What differs is delivery. Bell wants exceptions carved out for itself. TELUS wants no exceptions for anyone, which happens to raise costs on its smaller rivals more than on TELUS. Rogers wants the Commission’s own research cited back at it. Three companies, one target rate, three different paths to get there — and not one word, from any of them, about the practice that started this newsletter in the first place.
