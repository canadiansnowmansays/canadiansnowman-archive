At 3:36 on the afternoon of Friday, November 13, the radar feeds and radio links that hold American air traffic together began to stutter. Within half an hour the Federal Aviation Administration had done something it has done only three times this century: it stopped every departure in the country. About 4,000 aircraft were already in the air, and every one of them still had to come down. By the time the ground stop lifted at 8:15 that evening, more than 8,200 flights, nearly a third of the day’s schedule, had been cancelled, and some 1.5 million travellers had been stranded, rerouted or sent home. Only the stop after September 11, 2001 lasted longer.

For the first four hours, the official explanation was two words: “telecommunication outage.” I spent eighteen years at Rogers, most of it in the Office of the President handling the complaints that arrived after the network had let somebody down. I know what those two words mean when they are all an organisation is prepared to say. They mean the people in charge either do not yet know what broke or are not yet ready to tell you. On Friday night I decided to wait for the cause before writing a word. If it had turned out to be a foreign hack, this would have been a different essay.

It was not a hack. On Saturday morning the FAA’s administrator, Bryan Bedford, said investigators had concluded it was not a cyber issue, and that there was no evidence AI was involved either. It was the network, or more precisely, work on the network.

What failed

On Friday night, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy told reporters at the White House that old routers had become overloaded with data, according to The New York Times. He described the plant in terms any telephone technician of my generation would recognise: old switches, old routers and “1980s little copper lines.” CNN had reported earlier, citing an airline source, that the FAA told carriers the fault began with network routers at Chicago Midway and cascaded nationwide.

By Saturday morning the story had changed in an important way. Duffy told a news conference, as the Times reported in its live coverage, that the trouble began at what he called a remote facility, where the contractor L3Harris was converting a copper line to fibre optic cable. A router installed in that work malfunctioned and overloaded parts of the system that carries data to and from aircraft. Duffy said the FAA did not yet know whether the router was faulty or the installation was, and framed it as a question of incompetence. Once the site was isolated and taken offline, he said, every system came back. Bedford added that no facility went completely dark, but frequencies dropped in and out at more than ten of them, and that was when the FAA realised it faced something larger and stopped everything. By Monday, investigators had focused on a router in Arkansas installed by L3Harris and were warning that more failures could occur, according to the DealBook newsletter of The New York Times, and L3Harris shares fell after Duffy vowed to investigate whether the company’s work had caused the stop.

By Monday the FAA’s own account was less tidy than one bad router. Data had begun flooding an agency telecommunications network, it said, and the initial investigation had found two possible sources: the radio site in El Dorado, Arkansas, and a radar site in Amarillo, Texas. The investigation continues.

None of this came out of nowhere. Only last week, the FAA announced that it had begun phasing out floppy disks, and earlier this month L3Harris said on its website that about 70 percent of the copper had been converted to fibre. In September, flights around Washington were grounded when a circuit failed and its only backup, a fibre line, turned out to have been cut by a construction crew, the Times reported in its Friday coverage. In October, contractors severed fibre lines at Raleigh-Durham and, a day later, at Houston’s main airport. Bedford put the problem plainly at the time: when today’s fibre is cut there is one redundancy, and when that is lost there is none.

One detail from Friday’s news conference now reads differently. Duffy said facilities that had already been upgraded to digital systems did not suffer the overloading that brought down the others, and offered that as proof that the upgrades work. Saturday’s explanation shows the other side of it. The failure did not live in the old network or in the new one. It lived in the seam between them, at the moment one was being swapped for the other.

Two secretaries, one podium

Standing beside Duffy on Friday, Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said there was no evidence of a cyberattack. Then he offered his own explanation: the new equipment the Transportation Department had added to the old infrastructure was what had overwhelmed it. Duffy contradicted him on the spot, insisting the legacy equipment, not the upgrades, was to blame. By Saturday, Mullin was gone from the podium, replaced by Bedford and the FAA’s acting chief technology officer, Rebecca Guy, and Duffy was describing an upgrade that had gone wrong. On the facts so far, the man from Homeland Security was closer to the truth.

Anyone who has worked through a network migration could have predicted that. The most dangerous period in the life of a network is not when it is old, and not when it is new, but when it is both. Every cutover is a moment when a working circuit comes down, a new one comes up, and the two must agree with each other and with everything around them. Done well, it is invisible. Done badly, one device starts flooding its neighbours with traffic they were never sized to carry. Duffy’s own word for the current arrangement was “patchwork,” and he called it untenable. He is right, but patching is also how every network on earth gets rebuilt, one site at a time. The question is not whether you patch. It is whether a mistake at one patch can reach the whole quilt.

Red Friday

Canadians have seen this film. On July 8, 2022, Rogers went dark across the country. Wireless and internet service failed for millions of customers, some for more than a day. Interac debit payments stopped. Calls to 911 failed. Many Canadians still call it Red Friday.

I had left Rogers the year before, so I watched that one from the outside, as a customer of another carrier. But Saturday’s explanation made the parallel exact: Rogers, too, went down in the middle of an upgrade. According to the independent review the CRTC commissioned from Xona Partners and published in 2024, staff working through a phase of an upgrade to the company’s IP core removed a routing policy filter from its distribution routers. Without that filter, a flood of routing information poured into the core routers, which had not been configured with the overload protection that would have let them refuse it. The change had been rated low risk and was not lab-tested first, which the reviewers said ran against industry norms. Because staff could not reach the error logs of the failed routers, it took about 14 hours to pinpoint the root cause.

Rogers told the regulator in its own words, reported by CBC News, that the deleted filter “allowed for all possible routes to the Internet to pass through the routers.” Read that beside Saturday’s account of a single router, installed during an upgrade, overloading the system around it, and the family resemblance is obvious. In both cases a core that everything depended on was handed more than it could digest and had no way to say no.

The remedies Rogers adopted are instructive too. It did not simply buy faster routers. It tightened how changes are assessed and tested, added protection against overload, and committed to separating its wireless and wireline cores so that a failure in one could never again take down both. The lesson of Red Friday was not that old equipment is bad. It was that a single core with no circuit breaker is a national single point of failure, and that the discipline around touching it matters as much as the hardware.

Nor was Friday’s failure alone. As Steve Lohr pointed out in The New York Times on November 15, the same week brought a day-and-a-half global outage of Microsoft’s email, office and meeting services, and both meltdowns began as upgrades: new cables at the FAA, a planned increase in computing capacity at Microsoft. Two years ago, a routine software update from CrowdStrike crashed millions of Windows computers and grounded airlines around the world. Lohr likens large computing systems to geological formations, with new layers laid down on top of old ones, so that a change near the surface can shift everything beneath it. Five days later, at the DealBook Summit, Microsoft’s chief executive, Satya Nadella, called the Outlook outage a “perfect storm” that set off a latent bug, and warned that artificial intelligence now finds flaws so quickly that fixes go out before they can be properly tested. “Y2K was one bug, and one day,” he said, as The Times reported. Red Friday belongs on the same list. The pattern is not old technology wearing out. It is that the moment of change is the moment of greatest risk.

The slices that held

It would be wrong to say the system failed on Friday. Part of it held, and held well.

James Fallows, in a New York Times Magazine essay published the day before the outage, built his argument around the “Swiss cheese” model of safety that every aviator learns: each layer of protection has holes, but stack enough layers and the holes almost never line up. On Friday several slices failed together, which is rare. But the next slices held. Pilots and controllers have primary, backup and emergency frequencies. If a tower loses its radios altogether, controllers still have light guns, and pilots can acknowledge by rocking their wings. The outage was intermittent, Duffy said, not a complete blackout, so controllers could reach most aircraft most of the time.

The pilots on the other end of those frequencies back that up. The union for some 17,000 American Airlines pilots told The New York Times on Monday that it had no reports of crews losing radio contact with controllers, only the static pilots hear on any ordinary day, and Southwest’s dispatchers said their links to crews and FAA facilities never broke. The union still backed the stop. Its spokesman, a 737 captain stranded near Newark that night, said no one can know what would have happened had someone decided to take a chance. “We have plenty of knives in our boot,” he said. That does not mean pilots want to use them.

The numbers tell the story. Within three hours, the 4,000 flights aloft when the stop began had dwindled to about 550, according to Flightradar24’s count reported by The Atlantic. By 8:15, fewer than 400 remained. MIT’s R. John Hansman explained the logic to the magazine: once you stop adding aircraft, the sky gets safer by the minute, because there are fewer planes to bump into. No one was hurt. Mullin was right to stress that.

I agree that stopping was the right call. A network engineer who cannot trust his network takes it out of service. But a well-designed network should never put him in that position over a fault in one region. The ground stop was the correct response to a failure that should not have been able to spread from one router at one site to the entire country.

The second outage

There was another failure on Friday, and it lasted longer than the first. For four hours, the public got almost nothing.

The Atlantic found a passenger at Reagan National whose airline app was still blaming the weather while her pilot was telling the cabin that a friend had advised him to give up and rent a car. In Denver, much of the Democratic Party was stranded at a conference titled, of all things, “What’s Next,” and the Times found governors, senators and former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg in a hotel bar refreshing their phones, people used to being told things suddenly told nothing. In the vacuum, Americans filled the silence with whatever they already feared: DOGE cuts, Iranian hackers, rogue AI, this week’s Microsoft outage. Juliette Kayyem’s prescription, in The Atlantic, is the one every crisis manager learns, and one I watched good companies learn the hard way: say early what you know, what you do not know, and what you are doing about it.

The silence went all the way to the top. While the airports filled with stranded passengers, the president was in the Oval Office with the actor Jon Voight, Jonathan Lemire reported for The Atlantic, citing two people familiar with the meeting. Trump then phoned a string of Hollywood power players about legislation to revive the entertainment industry, and one of them asked whether his attention might be better spent on the air-travel emergency. Trump brushed the question aside. He posted about the show-business bill later that day and did not address the FAA meltdown until Sunday, two days after the ground stop began.

In my years handling escalations, the complaints that burned hottest were rarely about the outage itself. People understand that equipment fails. What they do not forgive is being left in the dark while it does. Rogers learned that in 2022, when the public anger over how little customers were told rivalled the anger over the outage. Washington learned it again on Friday. To its credit, it did better on Saturday. Bedford opened with an apology to travellers, and the explanation was specific: a site, a contractor, a device. Even “we know where to look” would have been worth saying on Friday at five o’clock.

The bill

By Friday night, the argument had moved on to money. Congress approved $12.5 billion last year to modernise air traffic facilities and networks. For months, Duffy has been asking the Republican-controlled Congress for another $30 billion. On Friday he raised the ask to $50 billion, as emergency funding, before the end of the year, which is to say before the Democrats take control of the House and the purse in January. The bills Republicans have filed to authorise $30 billion have no cosponsors. Hakeem Jeffries answered that the stop was the latest in a series of unacceptable air travel incidents under the administration, and that travellers deserve answers.

The Times added a detail that should give any lawmaker pause. Of the $30 billion Duffy had been seeking, only $10 billion was specifically for air traffic control modernisation. Another $10 billion was for terminals, towers and airport improvements, and the last $10 billion was for airport beautification. He has not said what the $50 billion would buy, and members of both parties are asking how well the first $12.5 billion has been spent.

Both can be true. The copper does need replacing, and that costs money. But Friday’s outage was not caused by a shortage of money. It was caused by money being spent: a conversion project already under way, carried out by a contractor the FAA has used for decades. Red Friday is a warning about what money alone does not buy. It buys new routers. It does not buy the change discipline that keeps a routine cutover from becoming a national outage, the overload protection that lets a core refuse traffic it cannot carry, or the technicians who were fired to save money while the agency was already short-handed. A patchwork paid for in a rush is still a patchwork.

Nor will the money do much for the people who spent Friday night on airport floors. Because an air traffic failure is not the airline’s fault, passengers are owed a refund if they choose not to fly, and little else: no guaranteed hotel, no meal vouchers. As Ian Bogost noted in The Atlantic, the Transportation Department withdrew the Biden-era plans to require compensation for airline-caused disruptions about a year ago. The safety net under the travelling public is thinner than the one under the aircraft.

Friday the 13th

Amid the chaos, one pilot remarked to a Newark controller that he had noticed the date. It is a good line, and it is the wrong lesson. Nothing about Friday was bad luck. It was a routine change at one remote site, in a network with too few ways around a single failure, overseen by an agency short of the people who maintain it, and explained to the public four hours late. Rogers could tell them how that story goes.

Monday offered no comfort to the superstitious. Three days after the stop, a “widespread system outage” held United’s departures for about an hour, ten days before Thanksgiving, as The New York Times reported. Captains told their cabins that the system for calculating weight and balance was down, and that crews were falling back on an older system or working the numbers out the old-school way. At LaGuardia, one passenger found news stories about the outage before the gate agents knew what was happening. The same afternoon, JPMorgan Chase blamed a “software bug in on-premise hardware systems” for transactions that stalled at its branches and spilled over to other banks and to Zelle, according to The Times. Nothing suggests the failures share a cause.

Tuesday brought two more: the National Security Agency’s internal communications network went offline, then Apple’s services stumbled, as The Times reported. Six failures in five days, and once again the people at the gate knew less than the news did. Senator Josh Hawley, a Missouri Republican, said that if it was all a coincidence, “it’s like the greatest coincidence in the history of the world.” That same day, in Manila, the president was asked about the air-travel meltdown and the bank outages. Such things happen, he said, when you are big and powerful and have all the money. “It’s fine,” he told reporters, as The Times reported. Marco Rubio, travelling with him, sought to reassure Americans that there was no evidence of a cyberattack, according to The New York Times. Back home, his vice president seemed less sure. On Wednesday JD Vance cancelled his interview at the DealBook Summit in New York, saying he had been called to stay in Washington for a series of meetings on the outages that had hobbled banks and airlines, according to The Times. Even Karoline Leavitt, the president’s former press secretary, went on Fox News to say that when he got home, someone at the White House should tell Americans directly what they knew.

Duffy predicted that until the upgrades are done, the system will keep failing. Saturday’s irony is that the upgrades are exactly where the next failure is likeliest to start: by L3Harris’s own count, nearly a third of the copper is still to be converted, one cutover at a time. The question is whether the next mistake stays at one site, as it should, or reaches the whole country again, and whether, when it does, anyone tells the people waiting at the gate.
