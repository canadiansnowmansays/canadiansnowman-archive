The count arrives at 1914, and I will declare the register plainly, as this series does: this is one of the serious ones — not the gravest the century will demand of me, but the one that opens the door the gravest ones walk through. It is about the only war in these files that was started, in the most literal operational sense, by schedules — by railway timetables so precise, so interlocking, and so irreversible that in the last week of July 1914 they stopped being tools of the statesmen who had commissioned them and became their masters. Eighteen hundred essays ago this series began cataloguing the ways human beings serve their own numbers. This essay is the terminal case: the summer the counts took command.

Begin in Sarajevo, June 28, 1914, with the disputed detail the veterans will be expecting. Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, survived a bomb thrown at his motorcade in the morning; his driver, hours later, took a wrong turn off the quay and stopped — by the standard account, to reverse — directly in front of a nineteen-year-old Bosnian Serb named Gavrilo Princip, who had given up on the day’s plot and happened to be standing there, and who stepped forward and fired twice. You will find, in the popular tellings, that Princip was eating a sandwich at the moment fate delivered the car to him; the tongs go to full extension: the sandwich appears in no contemporary source and has been traced by patient historians to later fiction — the most famous lunch in history never happened. The wrong turn, however, did. The twentieth century’s catastrophe was initiated by a navigation error, corrected too slowly, in front of the one man in Sarajevo still holding a pistol and a grievance. The turn is not disputed. Everything the series believes about hinges — fifteen minutes on a field, hours at a patent counter — arrives here at its darkest scale: the century turned on a driver’s mistake and the width of a street.

But a wrong turn only killed an archduke. What killed the ten million was the machinery that the death switched on, and the machinery is the essay.

Every great power of Europe had spent the previous generation solving the same problem: how to convert a nation into an army at maximum speed. The solution, everywhere, was the railway timetable — and I want you to appreciate the scale of the counting involved, because it is the most impressive and the most terrible arithmetic in these files. Germany’s mobilization plan called for the movement of some two million men, with their horses, guns, ammunition, and fodder, on the order of eleven thousand trains, across a specific sequence of days, with key bridges — the Hohenzollern Bridge at Cologne famously among them — timetabled down to intervals of minutes, train after train after train. Russia’s plan moved millions more across distances that dwarfed Germany’s, which was precisely the problem: Russian mobilization was slow, therefore it had to start early, therefore the mere act of preparing became indistinguishable from attacking. France had its Plan XVII; Austria-Hungary had its cases and variants; and each schedule had been optimized, rehearsed, and refined by general staffs for years — masterpieces of logistics, genuinely, the largest coordinated counting exercises humanity had ever designed. And every one of them shared a property that nobody had adequately reported up the chain of command: they could be started, and they could not be paused. A timetable of eleven thousand interlocking trains is not a lever you hold; it is a lever you throw. Halt it midway and you have two million men strewn along sidings, out of supply, out of sequence — not an army held in reserve but an army converted into a traffic jam, defenseless at exactly the moment your neighbour’s trains are still running.

Now watch the last week of July 1914 with that property in mind, because the diplomacy of that week — the ultimatums, the telegrams between cousins, the ministers aging years in days — was conducted by men who believed they were deciding whether, while their own machinery had quietly reduced the question to when. Austria declared war on Serbia on July 28. Russia, to back Serbia, faced its geography: mobilize now or be irrelevant. The Tsar, by the accounts of that night, ordered partial mobilization, was told by his generals that no partial plan existed — the timetable was total or it was nothing, the count had no smaller denomination — hesitated, cancelled, agonized, and on July 30 ordered general mobilization. Germany’s plan — the inherited Schlieffen concept — required, upon Russian mobilization, an immediate strike west, through Belgium, at France, because the schedule said France must fall before Russia’s slow trains finished counting. And so arrived the moment this entire essay exists to record: on August 1, the Kaiser, grasping at a British hint that France might stay neutral, asked the Chief of the General Staff, Moltke, whether the armies could be turned around — deployed east only, sparing the west.

I should enter, first, the correspondence that frames the question, because it is the most pathetic document set in these files, in the old full sense of the word. Through the final days of July, the Kaiser and the Tsar — Wilhelm and Nicholas, cousins, who holidayed together, wrote in English, and signed themselves Willy and Nicky — exchanged personal telegrams over the heads of their own governments, each begging the other to stop what was starting, each professing peace with what reads, a century on, as genuine desperation. Willy urged Nicky to halt the mobilization; Nicky replied that he wished to, that the military measures had been decided days before, that he could no more stop them than recall an avalanche he had authorized while believing it a snowbank. Two emperors, absolute in theory, autocrats on paper, cousins in fact — wiring each other in the language of their shared grandmother that the machines were running and neither hand could reach the lever. The telegrams failed. Of course they failed. They were addressed to men, and the recipients were, by then, passengers. And the Kaiser turned from the telegraph to his general with the last real question of the old world: And Moltke — by his own memoir, with the tongs applied, for later scholars have argued the railways could in fact have managed more than he admitted — replied that it could not be done: the deployment of a million men was not a thing to be improvised; to attempt it would deliver not an army but an armed rabble. The most powerful monarch in Europe asked his own machine for one degree of freedom, and the machine — through the mouth of its keeper — said no. The Kaiser reportedly answered that Moltke’s uncle, the elder Moltke, would have given him a different reply. Perhaps. The trains rolled west. Belgium was invaded on August 4, Britain came in that midnight, and the lamps, as the man said, went out.

Here is the idea at the centre of this essay, and it is the one I most need the readers of my other datelines to carry with them: a count, sufficiently optimized, becomes a decision — and its makers become its staff. Nobody in July 1914 chose the World War in the way the textbooks imply choice. Each government chose, at most, not to be late — and lateness had been defined, by their own timetables, in hours. The schedules had converted the future into a corridor: every exit that the diplomats reached for was already, by the arithmetic, a form of surrender, and so the statesmen of Europe walked single file into the abyss while genuinely believing, most of them, that they were still deliberating. This series has shown you counts that were weapons, counts that were shields, counts that were confessions. The mobilization timetables of 1914 are the count as autopilot — the first fully automated catastrophe, executed by rail, in which the human role at the end was reduced to declining to press a stop button that the designers had, in any case, mostly declined to install. I write, under other datelines, about systems being built in our own decade whose designers speak with pride of speed, of automaticity, of decisions at machine tempo with the human somewhere on the loop rather than in it. I make no comparison. I merely file the ancestor where they can find it.

What the corridor led to must be entered, though this essay is the doorway and not the tomb. Four years; the machine war; somewhere near ten million soldiers dead and the civilians beyond precise counting, as the dead of this series so often are; a generation of Europe entered into the ledger Lincoln would have recognized, blood drawn by the sword in payment for accounts nobody could later state coherently. Canada — more than six hundred thousand in uniform from a nation of fewer than eight million, some sixty thousand of them never home again — walked out of it changed, and the historians of my country date its real independence not from any statute but from a ridge in France that this series may yet reach by another number. And the peace, when it came in 1918, was signed at the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month — the war that began with a timetable ended on one, scheduled to the minute, with men dying between the signing at dawn and the appointed ceasefire because the schedule, even in armistice, could not be moved up. The count commanded to the last morning. Every November my country falls silent at 11:00, and I have come to hear that silence differently since researching this essay: it is the one minute of the year when we deliberately stop all the clocks that started it.

My own family's entry in that ledger is a man I knew as Great-Uncle Art. His name was Alexander Arthur Walters, and he married my grandfather Cameron Lawton's sister, Vera. He enlisted at Guelph on 25 March 1916, eighteen years old, giving his trade as farmer and his next of kin as a sister in Montreal. He went over with the Canadian Expeditionary Force, and somewhere in France or Flanders he was gassed — the new weapon of that war, released from cylinders and shells when the wind was right, which is to say on a schedule set by the weather and the general staff. His lungs were scarred. He survived. He came home to London, Ontario, and lived for decades at 46 Oxford Street West, just east of Wharncliffe, in a house that is a real estate office now. And he smoked, heavily, the way so many of the men who came back did, for the rest of his life. He died of lung cancer in 1971, as I was leaving my teens. My father inherited his kit: the helmet, the gas mask, what the family always called his machete, and a dress jacket in colours far too bright for anything that had happened to the man who wore it.

I think about that gas mask more than I can easily explain. It is the instrument he carried against the first count — the one the war kept, in cylinders, against his lungs. It did not entirely work. And then, for fifty-odd years, he kept a second count against the same lungs himself, a pack at a time, and that one finished the job. The armistice stopped the clocks at eleven in the morning on the eleventh of November, 1918. It did not stop the one in his chest. For men like Art, the war's arithmetic ran on, on schedule, for the rest of their lives, and the ledger of 1914 was not closed until the last of them was buried. His name, Alexander Arthur Walters, regimental number 928726, is not on any memorial. It belongs in this one.

There is no comic register available here and Dior does not appear; the veterans know the convention. I will say only that the morning I drafted the Moltke passage I took her out early along the lake, and we watched a GO train cross the trestle exactly on time, as they do, and I stood there longer than the dog thought reasonable. Punctuality is a virtue in everything that is not aimed at anyone. It took the machine age one July to learn the exception, and the tuition was ten million. That is the entry. Let it stand.

The count moves on, as it does, and I owe you the road map, because the next stretch is the hardest on the whole route. The very next landmark is essay 1,918, and it will not let the war end cleanly: in the same months the armies were counting down to the armistice, an influenza was moving through the camps and the troopships and the victory parades, and before it was done it had killed more people than the war itself — how many more, no one has ever been able to say. Further on, close together, wait 1942, 1945 and 1947, and the veterans already know what the middle of those numbers holds; I will write them the way this series writes the gravest entries, and I will not write them tired, and I may be quiet for a few numbers while I prepare. Beyond them, I promise, is the best morning of the century — a July night in 1969 when the counting ran backward, out loud, on purpose, in front of six hundred million people, toward the only deadline in these files that humanity ever set for its best self and kept. Hold on for that one. It is coming, and it is worth the road.

A wrong turn, a pistol, eleven thousand trains, and a stop button nobody installed. The summer of 1914 is what happens when the count is finished before the thinking is. Build the pause in first. It is the cheapest feature in the world, in July.

See you at 1918. Mind the schedule; it does not mind you.
