by Stephen Gordon

The count arrives at 1,517, and I want to begin with a confession: I have every sympathy for the Roman Catholic Church of the early sixteenth century, because it had done something I understand completely. It had started counting.

Not counting money — everyone counts money; there is no story in that. The Church had started counting souls. More precisely, it had started counting the time a soul owed. Purgatory, by 1517, had been fully quantified: sins carried terms, terms could be measured, and — here is where the trouble starts — measured things can be priced. An indulgence was, in its essentials, a receipt. You paid, and time came off the sentence. Yours, or your late mother’s. The Church had built the most audacious ledger in the history of accounting: a double-entry system in which one column was money and the other column was eternity.

I have written fifteen hundred essays about people who confuse the count with the thing being counted. I have never found a cleaner example. The medieval Church did not set out to sell salvation. It set out to measure it — a reasonable ambition; I measure everything, ask my dog — and then discovered, as everyone who measures eventually discovers, that a number is a handle. Once a thing has a number, it can be picked up. Once it can be picked up, it can be sold.

By 1517 the selling had become industrial. Pope Leo X needed money for the rebuilding of St. Peter’s Basilica, which was to be the grandest church in Christendom and was, like all grand projects, over budget. In the German lands, a young archbishop named Albrecht of Mainz had gone deeply into debt — to the Fugger banking house, the sixteenth century’s answer to a hedge fund — to purchase his archbishopric, and had been granted the right to sell indulgences in his territories on the understanding that the proceeds would be split: half to Rome for the basilica, half to service his loans. Salvation had not merely been priced. It had been securitized. There were bankers involved. There is always, in the end, a banker involved.

The retail arm of this operation was a Dominican friar named Johann Tetzel, a salesman of genuine gifts, who toured the German towns with a preaching style that the sources describe as vivid and a jingle that history has attributed to him — the coin rings in the coffer, the soul springs from purgatory — though whether he said it in exactly those words is disputed, and in this series we are careful about quotes, even ones from 1517. What is not disputed is the pitch: your dead were suffering, the suffering had a duration, the duration had a price, and the price was payable today.

Now. Into this arithmetic walks a thirty-three-year-old Augustinian friar and professor of theology at the small, new, not-especially-distinguished University of Wittenberg. His name was Martin Luther, and I want to be precise about what he did on October 31, 1517, because what he did is the entire reason this essay exists.

He did not lead a march. He did not burn anything — the burning came later, on all sides, and it is not the part anyone should be proud of. He did not, in 1517, even intend to leave the Church; he was proposing an academic debate, in Latin, in the ordinary way of university men, and he sent his propositions that day in a letter to Archbishop Albrecht himself, which is rather like mailing your complaint about the fire directly to the arsonist, and suggests a man who still believed the system would want to know.

What he did was make a list.

Ninety-five theses. Not “some concerns.” Not “serious questions about the direction of indulgence policy.” Ninety-five separate, numbered propositions, each one standing on its own line, each one answerable on its own terms. Number 27 took aim at the coffer-and-soul arithmetic directly. Number 82 asked the question the whole ledger could not survive: if the Pope has the power to empty purgatory, why does he do it for money rather than for love? Number 86 wondered, with a straight face I have spent a career trying to imitate, why the wealthiest man in Europe was building his basilica with the pennies of the poor rather than his own funds.

Tradition says he nailed the list to the door of the Castle Church in Wittenberg, which served the university as a notice board. I am obliged to report that historians have doubted the nailing — the story was written down decades later by a colleague who was not in Wittenberg at the time — and I am delighted to report it, because this series has a running theme — one I intend to make much of before the year is out — that the details of the great stories are always shakier than the stories. The door is disputed. The list is not. The list survives, numbered, and the numbers did the work.

Here is the idea at the center of this essay, and it is the reason 1517 belongs in the Numbers Game and not merely in a history of religion: the number was the weapon. Ninety-five is not a rhetorical count. Rhetoric rounds — a hundred grievances, a thousand injustices, we have established in this series that a war got rounded to a hundred years for the sound of it. Ninety-five is an audit count. It says: I went through the books line by line, and I found ninety-five entries that do not reconcile, and here they are, individually numbered, so that you may dispute any one of them individually. It is the least deniable format ever devised. You can wave away an angry man. You cannot wave away item forty-three without someone asking about item forty-four.

The Church had turned faith into a ledger. Luther answered the ledger as a ledger. He met the counting with a count. That is why it worked, and it is why, five hundred years later, every serious complaint in every serious institution still arrives as a numbered list — findings, counts of an indictment, articles of impeachment. The form was perfected on (or near, pending the door dispute) a church notice board in Saxony.

And then the machine got hold of it. Regular readers will remember essay 1,440, where I paid my respects to Gutenberg and claimed him, perhaps presumptuously, as the patron of everyone who publishes without permission. In 1517 the press was seventy-seven years old — old enough to be everywhere, new enough that no institution had learned to fear it properly. The theses were composed in Latin for a room of academics. Printers translated them into German and ran them off, and within weeks — one contemporary claimed a fortnight, which we may allow as the enthusiasm of a man watching his world catch fire — they were being read across the German lands by people who had never attended a disputation and never would. Luther had written a memo. The press made it a broadside. It was, I believe, the first time in history that a piece of writing went out of its author’s control at continental scale, and the author’s later career suggests he never entirely got over the discovery that publication, like an indulgence, is a transaction that cannot be refunded.

What Rome did next belongs in this essay, because Rome’s response was also a number, and it was the wrong one. The first reaction, by the accounts that have come down to us, was dismissal — a squabble among friars, the sort of thing German universities produced the way German mines produced silver, and a remark about a quarrelsome monk is attributed to Leo X himself, though we handle papal table talk in this series with the same tongs we used on Tetzel’s jingle. Dismissal failing, the Church sent debaters, and at Leipzig in the summer of 1519 the ablest of them, Johann Eck, spent days maneuvering Luther into saying aloud what the theses had only implied — that popes and councils could err — which was less a refutation than a confession extracted at chess, and Europe noticed the difference.

And then, in June 1520, Rome finally did the thing the format demanded. It answered a numbered list with a numbered list. The papal bull Exsurge Domine condemned forty-one propositions drawn from Luther’s writings — forty-one, itemized, enumerated in the grand style. And here is the detail I have been saving, the one that earns this section its place: the forty-one items were not a rebuttal. Not one of them was an answer. They were a charge sheet. The bull did not dispute Luther’s arithmetic; it disputed his standing to do arithmetic at all, and gave him sixty days — another number — to withdraw the entire ledger unaudited. On December 10, 1520, at Wittenberg, Luther burned it. I told you the burning came later.

The end of the accounting came at Worms in April 1521, before the young Emperor Charles V, where the institution performed its final act of compression: ninety-five theses, three years of pamphlets, an entire disputed ledger — reduced to two questions. Are these books yours? Will you recant? The most famous itemized complaint in history was to be settled as a single yes or no. Luther’s documented answer, stripped of the legendary flourish that was added later and that we therefore leave on the shelf, was that unless he were convinced by scripture and plain reason, he could not and would not retract. He had offered ninety-five debatable items. He was offered one box to tick. The Church lost half of Europe in the space between those two numbers, and I submit that the space between those two numbers is where institutions always lose everything: the moment they stop answering the items and start demanding the whole list back.

I am asked sometimes — by readers, and by a certain husky who audits my mornings — why I do not simply say the obvious thing here about the present day, where the posting of grievances has become the central activity of public life, up to and including its highest office. And the answer is that the contrast says it better than the comparison would. The modern grievance-post and the Wittenberg list share a door and nothing else. Luther numbered his complaints so that each one could be answered — the numbers were an invitation to be proven wrong, item by item, in public, by anyone with better arithmetic. The modern broadside carries no numbers because it contains no items. It is all column headings and no entries. It cannot be refuted line by line for the same reason a fog cannot be fenced: there are no lines. Luther, whatever else history wants to say about him — and history has a great deal to say, not all of it kind, some of it very dark indeed — put his count where it could be checked. In this series, that is the whole of the law.

Dior, for the record, was shown the ninety-five theses in summary form this morning and disputed none of them, which I initially took for agreement until I understood it was contempt for the format. She holds that a list is what you make when you lack the confidence to have one point. She has one point, and it is breakfast, and she has never needed to number it.

The essay count moves on, as it does. The next stop on this stretch of the ledger is essay 1,519, and it concerns the exact opposite of everything you have just read: the greatest counter of the age — a man who measured water, wings, faces, and the proportions of the human body across some seven thousand surviving pages — who published none of it. Luther’s list changed the world because it went out the door. The next essay is about the door that never opened.

The Church counted souls. Luther counted grievances. The press counted neither and multiplied both.

See you at 1,519, when the greatest ledger stays shut.
