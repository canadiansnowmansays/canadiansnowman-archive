This series began with a fleet.

In 1421, the largest collection of ships ever assembled left the port of Nanjing and sailed into the Indian Ocean, carrying silk and porcelain and the implicit argument that the Middle Kingdom was the centre of the world and wished the world to remember. The treasure ships were enormous, the fleet was overwhelming, and the argument was correct: China was, by any measurable standard, the most advanced civilisation on earth. The emperor who commissioned the fleet died three years later. His successors ordered the ships dismantled and the records of the voyages destroyed. China turned inward. The world changed around it.

Fifteen parts later, we arrive at the same fleet — rebuilt in different materials, sailing different waters.

The quantum communication satellites orbiting in the 141 pages are Zheng He’s treasure ships, translated into the 21st century. The Belt and Road ports at Gwadar and Hambantota and Djibouti are the tributary system, built with construction loans instead of imperial recognition. The rare earth chokehold is the silk that only China produced — except that this time, the strategy was deliberate, built over decades while the countries that needed the silk offshored its production without thinking through what they were conceding. The 141 pages are the Yongle Emperor’s commission, restated in procurement directives and investment targets and research mandates, by a leader who has spent twelve years consolidating the authority to issue them and who shows no signs of ordering the ships dismantled.

The Long Return is real. It is also unfinished. And the gap between those two facts is where the history of the next quarter-century will be written.

What this series has tried to do — across sixteen parts and roughly 35,000 words — is give the Long Return its full historical weight.

That weight is considerable. A civilisation that was the world’s most sophisticated for most of two millennia, displaced by a century of military defeat and foreign humiliation, reconstituted through revolutionary violence and catastrophic policy experiments, rebuilt through three decades of patient economic construction, and now asserting itself with explicit confidence and sophisticated strategic instruments — this is not a story that can be understood through the lens of any single decade or any single policy dispute. The trade war over semiconductors is not primarily about semiconductors. The military exercises around Taiwan are not primarily about Taiwan. The Belt and Road is not primarily about infrastructure. They are all expressions of a project whose roots run two hundred years deep and whose ambition is civilisational rather than merely strategic.

The civilisational ambition is what makes China genuinely different from the other rising powers of the post-Cold War era — from Japan in the 1980s, from the Asian tigers, from Brazil or India. Those countries were pursuing development, prosperity, and regional influence. China is pursuing something it experiences as restoration: the return to a position it occupied for most of recorded history and lost in a period it experienced as aberrant humiliation. Xi Jinping’s phrase — the Chinese Dream — is not the American Dream translated into Mandarin. It is a claim about historical trajectory: that the last two centuries were the exception, and the Long Return is the correction.

The questions the series has not answered — because they cannot yet be answered — are the ones that matter most.

The first is internal. Does the system Xi has built have the capacity to manage failure? The Great Leap Forward killed thirty million people because the system could not tell Mao his plan was failing. The Cultural Revolution destroyed a generation of expertise because the system could not tell Mao his revolution was devouring itself. Xi’s consolidation of personal authority has reconstructed, in updated form, the conditions under which those catastrophes became possible. The 141 pages may succeed — the quantum networks may be built, the rare earth dominance may be maintained, the AI investment may produce decisive advantage. But if they do not, if the plan fails in ways that require honest assessment to correct, Xi’s system currently has no institutional mechanism for delivering that assessment. The Peng Dehuai problem is unresolved. Whether this matters depends on whether the plan is correct.

The second is demographic. China’s total fertility rate of approximately 1.0 — among the lowest in the world — means the workforce that will have to execute the 141 pages is already shrinking. By 2050, China will have fewer working-age people than it had in 2000, and more elderly dependents than any society in history has managed at scale. The 141 pages invest in humanoid robotics partly as an answer to this problem — machines that can perform the labour of a workforce that no longer exists in sufficient numbers. Whether that answer is adequate, at the speed the demographic transition is moving, is genuinely uncertain.

The property market is a related and less discussed risk. At its peak, real estate and related industries accounted for roughly a quarter of China’s GDP. The Evergrande collapse and the broader property crisis that has unfolded since 2021 has produced a deflation dynamic in China’s economy that Xi’s government has struggled to address: consumers and local governments that accumulated wealth in property are reluctant to spend when their primary asset is depreciating, and the investment-led growth model that produced the factory floor miracle cannot be sustained indefinitely. The 141 pages propose a technology-led growth model as the replacement. The transition between models — from investment and manufacturing to innovation and technology — is the most difficult economic transition a large economy can attempt, and China is attempting it simultaneously with the demographic headwinds and the external technology restrictions.

The third is external. The international coalition arrayed against Chinese technological dominance — the semiconductor restrictions, the investment screening, the export controls, the security partnerships — is more coordinated and more determined than anything China has faced in the reform era. The American position has been bipartisan across two administrations. The European position has shifted from strategic naivety to strategic wariness with remarkable speed. The Indo-Pacific security architecture has deepened in ways that would have been difficult to achieve without Beijing’s Wolf Warrior assistance. Whether China can achieve the technological self-sufficiency that would render these pressures irrelevant, on the timeline the 141 pages assume, is the central open question of the strategic competition.

I have been writing about China from Toronto for most of this series — a Canadian outsider looking at a civilisation I find genuinely extraordinary and cannot fully understand.

I have visited China multiple times. The scale impresses every time. The infrastructure is real, the ambition is real, the intelligence and energy of the people building the skylines and the high-speed rail and the quantum networks are real. None of it is propaganda, in the sense of being invented. The bullet trains run on time. The ports are efficient. The factories produce things the world wants to buy. The scientists in the quantum communication laboratories are doing genuine science. The Long Return is not a fiction.

What I cannot see from Toronto, and what no outside observer can fully see, is what the project costs the people inside it — the ones who cannot speak their doubts, the ones who press green buttons every forty-eight hours in studio apartments, the ones who stopped replying to political essays from a friend in Canada. The 141 pages are China’s statement to the world about what it is building. The silence of my friend in Shenzhen is China’s statement to me about what the building costs.

Both are true. The series has tried to hold both truths simultaneously, from beginning to end — the treasure fleets and the green button, the quantum networks and the woman in Beijing looking for someone to walk with her in a park.

Canada’s particular position in this story is worth a final note. We share a continent with the United States, which means China’s relationship with Washington shapes Canada’s relationship with Beijing in ways that Ottawa cannot fully control — as the arrest of Meng Wanzhou and the detention of the Two Michaels demonstrated with painful clarity. We are a resource economy with rare earth deposits that both China and the United States want access to. We are a university research system with deep ties to Chinese academic institutions that our intelligence agencies regard with increasing concern. We are a country with the largest Chinese diaspora per capita outside Asia, whose members have complex and varied relationships with the country their families left. And we are a country that has spent most of the past decade failing to develop a coherent China policy because the political will to do so has been consistently overwhelmed by the short-term economic interests of the sectors that trade with China.

The Long Return is not a Canadian problem to solve. But it is a Canadian reality to navigate. This series, written from Toronto by a Canadian outsider who finds China genuinely extraordinary and genuinely alarming, has tried to provide some of the historical foundation that navigation requires.

Whether it succeeds is for readers to judge.

The Long Return is a series title that works in two directions.

Xi Jinping uses restoration language because he believes China is returning to its rightful place — that the two centuries of humiliation and reconstruction were a detour and that the civilisation is now resuming its proper trajectory. That is one reading of the Long Return.

The other reading is that the return is taking a very long time, is not guaranteed to succeed, and is being pursued at enormous cost to the people who live inside the project. The Century of Humiliation produced the revolution. The revolution produced the Great Leap. The Great Leap produced the Cultural Revolution. The Cultural Revolution produced the reform era. The reform era produced Xi. Xi produced the 141 pages. Each step in the chain was a response to the failures and costs of the previous step, and the chain is not finished. The Long Return describes an arc that has not yet landed.

Where it lands — whether in genuine restored centrality, in a middle power that overestimated its trajectory, in an internal crisis produced by the demographic and economic headwinds it has deferred, or in something none of these frameworks anticipates — is the story that will be written in the decades after the 141 pages were tabled.

The argument of this series ends here, at September 2026, with the plan in place and the outcome unknown. The record does not. One part remains — the bill for the growth model the 141 pages are meant to replace, presented in a courtroom this August — followed by two addenda. The treasure fleets have left the harbour. Whether they return is the question the 21st century will answer.

— Stephen Gordon, Toronto
