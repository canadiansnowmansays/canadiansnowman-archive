Ronald V. Dellums Federal Courthouse, Oakland, California

A companion series to The Asimov Gap, CanadianSnowmanSays

Sam Altman has never testified in court before Tuesday.

Elon Musk has testified in at least five trials over the past seven years. He arrived at the Oakland courthouse having already spent three days on this stand, having been cross-examined by William Savitt, having been corrected by Judge Gonzalez Rogers on basic rules of procedure, having had his wife-beating analogy cut off and his Terminator references capped. He knows courtrooms. He knows witnesses. He knows what cross-examination feels like from the inside.

Altman arrived on Tuesday morning in a dark blue suit and a purple tie. He walked quickly and occasionally locked eyes with people in the hallway. He was, by all reporter accounts, composed. He sat down in the same chair Musk had occupied for three days. He answered questions from his own lawyer first, speaking quickly, furrowing his brows slightly.

Then Musk’s lead counsel Steven Molo stood up.

“Are you completely trustworthy?”

“I believe so.”

There was an audible gasp in the courtroom. The trial has been building to this moment for ten days. The question the Farrow-Marantz investigation asked in 16,000 words — the question this series has been asking for thirty-one parts — was asked in four words in an Oakland federal courthouse, and answered in three.

What follows is what the day produced.

The Direct Examination: Musk Wanted Control

Altman’s own lawyers had one primary goal in direct examination: establish that Elon Musk — the man suing on the grounds that no one person should control AI — repeatedly tried to put one person in control of AI. That person being himself.

The record they built is substantial.

Altman testified that he and Musk and the other OpenAI founders met multiple times to discuss merging OpenAI with Tesla. At the end of 2017, Musk pushed hardest for the merger. A Tesla board seat was offered to Altman — framed partly as an incentive, partly, Altman said, as “a nascent threat.” Musk hinted he might build AI at Tesla regardless. “If you don’t take the deal, we’ll come for you” was how Mike Isaac summarized the dynamic, noting it was fairly commonplace in Silicon Valley.

Altman said a merger with Tesla would have “effectively destroyed the nonprofit in the process.”

The control question had its sharpest moment during what Altman described as a “particularly harrowing” exchange. When the co-founders asked Musk what would happen to his control of the proposed for-profit when he died, Musk replied that control would pass to his children. “I was not comfortable with that,” Altman said. The man suing on the grounds that AI should not be controlled by any one person described, under oath, his plan to pass that control to his heirs.

“I believed that AI should not be under the control of any one person,” Altman said. He said this in direct examination — but it is also the most accurate description available of why Musk left. Not because OpenAI betrayed the nonprofit mission. Because OpenAI refused to give him the control he wanted.

The 2018 Tesla visit is in the record now — “unlike a lot of other meetings with Mr. Musk, this was a good-vibes meeting,” Altman said of a conversation about the for-profit structure — because Altman and his lawyers wanted the jury to understand that Musk was aware of and supportive of the for-profit development until the moment he lost the control argument. A 2018 term sheet shown in court projected raising as much as $10 billion. Microsoft invested that amount exactly. Altman had told Musk the number. Musk had applauded the ambition.

On why he didn’t discuss the $10 billion Microsoft investment with Musk: “It would not have been appropriate,” Altman said. By late 2022, Musk was planning xAI. He had become a competitor.

On why Musk was eventually offered equity in the for-profit: he offered it on several occasions. Musk declined each time. Altman did not characterize the offers as bribes. The jury heard Musk’s characterization — “it felt like a bribe” — in the trial’s first week. Now they have heard Altman’s.

On the founding motivation: a 2015 email from Altman, shown in court, described his decision to build AI rather than try to stop it. “If it’s going to happen anyway, it seems like it would be good for someone other than Google to do it first.” Fear of Google was the founding impulse for both Musk and Altman. They agreed on the diagnosis. The dispute was always about who would control the response.

The Cross-Examination: Liar

Molo’s cross-examination had a structure that was visible from its first question. He spent nearly twenty minutes on trustworthiness before moving to anything else. The strategy was the same one Savitt used on Musk — establish the credibility problem first, then build the substantive argument on top of it.

He used the Farrow-Marantz investigation directly. He asked Altman about the New Yorker article that showed him “in an unflattering light.” He asked whether Altman was aware that employees from one of his former start-ups had tried to throw him out. He named the board members who testified under oath that Altman had not been truthful with them. He named the executives who said the same.

“Are you a person who just tells people things they want to hear whether those things are true or not?” Molo asked.

Altman’s responses were terse, quiet, and noncombative — a deliberate contrast to Musk’s combativeness. He sometimes struggled. When Molo hammered the trustworthiness theme, Metz noted from the courthouse that Altman “at times struggled to respond to repeated claims that he was a liar.” He grew more confident as the cross-examination progressed and moved to substantive territory.

On the 2023 firing: Altman said he was “flabbergasted.” He told the board it was “a terrible way to go about handling this without damaging the company.” He said: “I think you’re about to cause chaos for a thing I love very much.” He maintained, as he has maintained since the firing, that the board’s decision was wrong and that the misunderstandings were genuine — “never my intent.” He insisted he “did not force the board” to rehire him.

On his personal investments: Molo showed a list. Helion Energy — which has deals with Microsoft and OpenAI. Cerebras — a chipmaker in business with OpenAI. The argument: Altman enriches himself through self-dealing between companies in which he holds stakes and which benefit from their association with OpenAI. Altman acknowledged the investments. He did not offer a comprehensive rebuttal.

On whether he would ever fire himself — he serves both as board member and CEO: “I have no plans to do that.”

On the OpenAI Foundation: Musk’s counsel implied that its recent staffing was a trial gambit — that Altman had accelerated foundation hiring to make it look more functional before the jury. Altman acknowledged he was still staffing it. “I hope the nonprofit will have trillions of dollars,” he said. The foundation currently has few staff and an endowment potentially worth $130 billion.

On whether he could have continued to build OpenAI as a pure nonprofit: Molo pressed the point. Altman, growing firmer in his responses, said there was no way he could have built “a multitrillion dollar charity” without attaching it to a commercial company. “The only way to build such a valuable charity was to raise billions through a for-profit venture.” This became his central argument — not that the commercial turn was ideal, but that it was the only structure capable of producing the resources necessary to pursue the mission at the scale required.

What Altman Said on Redirect

Sam Altman, OpenAI’s chief executive, spent the day on the witness stand in a federal court in Oakland, Calif., on Tuesday.Credit...Jason Henry for The New York Times

Savitt returned for redirect with two questions aimed at the jury’s closing memory of Altman’s testimony.

He asked about the for-profit structure and the nonprofit’s value. Altman’s answer was the statement he will likely be remembered for from this trial: “Every step of the way, I have done my best to maximize the value of the nonprofit. I would point out that there are not a lot of historical examples of a nonprofit at this scale. And we were kind of left for dead.”

He had spent three hours on the stand — two hours of cross-examination and an hour of direct. He had been asked if he was completely trustworthy and answered that he believed so. He had been asked if he would fire himself and said he had no plans. He had grown firmer as the day progressed. He had come off the stand — in the judgment of the reporters in the room — having held his own, not having dominated.

The man who built OpenAI from a nonprofit with Google-anxiety into a company worth $730 billion left the witness stand for the last time having defended the structure he built, denied the betrayal Musk alleged, and left unresolved every question this series has been asking since Part 1.

Bret Taylor: The Final Irony

OpenAI’s board chair, Bret Taylor, also testified on Tuesday.Credit...Jason Henry for The New York Times

Before Altman took the stand, Bret Taylor completed his testimony. One exchange deserves the record.

Taylor, OpenAI’s board chairman, was asked about Musk’s $97.4 billion bid to purchase OpenAI’s assets in 2024. He said the board rejected it. His reason: “We did not feel like it was appropriate for one person to control our mission.”

The board chairman of the company being sued for abandoning its nonprofit mission rejected the plaintiff’s attempt to purchase the company on exactly the grounds the plaintiff is suing over. The man suing because no one person should control AI submitted a $97.4 billion bid to become the one person controlling AI. The board said no.

The trial’s central irony has been stated from every possible angle. It has been stated by the documentary record, by the testimony of every major witness, and now by the board chairman of the defendant in the plaintiff’s own language.

Day 12: The Jackass, the Accountant, and the Empty Chair

On Wednesday, May 13, the evidentiary portion of the trial ended. The plaintiff was not in the building. He was not in the country.

Elon Musk boarded Air Force One on Tuesday night and flew to Beijing with President Trump for the Xi Jinping summit. Judge Gonzalez Rogers had told Musk at the conclusion of his testimony in late April that he was subject to recall. “Mr. Musk, you are not excused, but you can leave for the day.” He did not seek permission from the judge before leaving. He was not recalled. Closing arguments are scheduled for Thursday. The man who filed a $134 billion lawsuit about who controls the future of artificial intelligence left his own trial to fly to China on the presidential plane.

The jury heard five witnesses in his absence.

Josh Achiam, OpenAI’s chief futurist, testified about the company’s early days — “kind of like an extension of a university,” about fifty people, with Brockman as the engineering workhorse and Sutskever as the scientific leader who “would often get up on a table and talk about silicon gladiators in a stadium.” Achiam said he saw Musk once at the beginning — “it was like watching Bigfoot through plexiglass” — and once more in 2018, when Musk announced he was leaving.

That second meeting produced the trial’s most vivid anecdote. Altman hosted a roundtable. Musk expressed strong concerns about DeepMind reaching AGI first. Several people challenged Musk’s proposal to race Google to AGI. Achiam told the court that what Musk was proposing “seemed unsafe and reckless.”

Musk snapped and called him a jackass.

“I think that he was just upset that he had been challenged.”

At the following all-hands meeting, Achiam was given a trophy — a small golden jackass. The inscription: “Never stop being a jackass for safety.”

Achiam holds an equity stake in OpenAI worth more than $50 million. Musk’s counsel surfaced tweets in which Achiam had criticized Greg Brockman. He acknowledged the criticisms but testified that he still believed Brockman was “rightly focused on safety and the mission of OpenAI.” He said he supported Altman’s return after the 2023 firing, does not share concerns about Altman’s truthfulness, and that “no question, OpenAI is still focused on safety.”

Daniel Hemel, an NYU professor and expert on nonprofits, testified that 92 percent of nonprofits use a for-profit affiliate. He gave two examples the jury could hold onto: the Hershey’s candy company and Newman’s Own — both controlled by nonprofits, both highly profitable, both well known. He testified that the OpenAI arrangement conforms to and furthers its mission, and that the nonprofit’s appointment of three board members to the partner’s two goes beyond the norm.

John Coates, deputy dean at Harvard Law School, rebutted Professor Schizer’s testimony from the previous week. He testified that the nonprofit holds roughly a $200 billion equity stake — a 25 to 29 percent share of profit — and that the intellectual property that was worth millions when the for-profit was created is now worth hundreds of billions. “This is a very good return for the nonprofit.”

The forensic accountant Louis Dudney testified that Musk contributed $37 million to OpenAI. His final donation was made in 2017 and was fully spent by November of that year. Most of the money went to compute and staff salaries. The money that built the company Musk is suing over was gone before GPT-1 existed.

Michael Wetter of Microsoft Corporate Development testified that Microsoft has spent more than $100 billion on OpenAI, including infrastructure. During its due diligence — a process involving legal, technology, tax, and finance teams — Microsoft found no documents describing any stake, obligation, or involvement attributable to Elon Musk.

The evidentiary record is now closed. Twelve days. Fourteen major witnesses. Hundreds of exhibits. One plaintiff in Beijing.

Day 13: The Bridge and the Empty Chair

Elon Musk at a state dinner in Beijing during the Trump-Xi summit, May 14, 2026. In Oakland, his lead counsel was apologizing to the jury for his absence and asking them to imagine a bridge built on Sam Altman's version of the truth. The plaintiff was 5,800 miles away, between selfies and courses. (Chinese state media)

On Thursday, May 14, closing arguments ran the full day — the longest courtroom session of the trial. The plaintiff was still in China.

Molo began by thanking the jury on behalf of Elon Musk, “who is not here.” He said his client “was called away to something at the last minute” and that he was “sorry he could not be here.” CNBC reported that Molo formally apologized for Musk’s absence. Musk’s attorneys did not respond to questions about whether they had cleared his travel with the judge. A court spokesperson said they did not know if Musk had obtained permission. Across the aisle, Altman and Brockman were both present.

Savitt, in his own closing, noted the contrast: “Mr. Musk is not here today. My clients are here.”

Molo built his case around three claims the jury must evaluate in sequence: breach of charitable trust, unjust enrichment, and aiding and abetting against Microsoft. The judge’s instructions were clear — if the jury finds Musk did not prove the first two claims, they cannot reach Microsoft.

On the charitable trust: Molo argued Musk’s $38 million created a trust with specific charitable purposes — nonprofit, safe, open-source AI for the benefit of humanity. He walked the jury through the founding blog post, the Delaware nonprofit filing, Musk’s emails to Altman about governance and safe AI. He argued that the $10 billion Microsoft deal breached that trust by enriching insiders, abandoning open sourcing, and hollowing out the nonprofit into a shell.

On credibility: Molo returned to the ground he had ploughed during Altman’s cross-examination. Five former colleagues had testified that Altman was a liar. Molo asked the jury to imagine a bridge, suspended 150 feet above a river, built on “Sam Altman’s version of the truth.” Would you walk across that bridge? “I don’t think many people would.”

On unjust enrichment: Brockman’s own diary entry — in which he wrote that it would be good to make billions of dollars — was shown to the jury. Molo called Altman’s investments in companies that do business with OpenAI self-dealing. He noted that the nonprofit had only recently hired anyone to run it, that the for-profit had absorbed virtually all employees and intellectual property, and that OpenAI had granted Microsoft exclusive use of its technology — “one company benefiting, not open source.”

Then Molo told the jury that Musk was not seeking money. The judge intervened. She had heard him say it. He said he misspoke. The judge told him to retract the statement or “drop your claim for billions of dollars.” They agreed the judge would correct the record for the jury. Musk is in fact seeking billions in disgorgement — to be paid to OpenAI’s nonprofit arm. It was a small stumble in a long day, but the judge’s terseness was noted.

Eddy, for OpenAI, spoke first in the defence’s closing. She opened with a symmetry argument: if the jury is going to evaluate Altman’s credibility, it must also evaluate Musk’s. Then she went to the documents. No quarterly reports listing specific purposes for Musk’s donations. No terms and conditions. No special charitable instructions. Several witnesses testified to this absence. “Mr. Musk has come nowhere close to making that case,” she said. And then the line that landed hardest: “Even the people who work for him, even the mother of his children can’t back his story” — a reference to Shivon Zilis, mother of four of Musk’s children, whose testimony had not supported Musk’s account of specific restrictions on his donations.

Eddy walked the jury through the 2017 negotiations. Musk had pushed for majority control of the board, initial control over the company, and offered no details on how long his proposed control would last. The co-founders wrote him a letter: “During these negotiations you’ve showed us your interest is to exert control.” Musk froze his $5 million contribution. He planned to launch his own for-profit and recruit OpenAI employees to staff it. The conversion to a for-profit entity, Eddy argued, was Musk’s idea first — and paperwork was filed to create it while he was still involved. The profit caps came after his departure. “If what Musk really believed was that AGI was necessary, wouldn’t he have launched his own nonprofit? Instead, he launched his own for-profit.”

Savitt completed OpenAI’s closing. He argued that the nonprofit remains a 501(c)(3), backed by $200 billion, the largest foundation in history. No private person or employee has received any money from the foundation. The initial $60 million in nonprofit assets has grown to $200 billion — “astronomical growth.” OpenAI consulted with two state attorneys general, including California’s, to structure the governance of the nonprofit over the for-profit entity. The nonprofit, Savitt said, is in excellent standing.

On statute of limitations: Savitt argued that Musk had notice of the for-profit conversion as early as 2018, when Altman sent him the term sheet. OpenAI posted publicly in 2020 that it would not open-source its architecture. Musk tweeted his disapproval that same year. All of this, Savitt argued, puts Musk’s claim beyond the limitations period. The judge had written in a filing that if the jury finds Musk failed to file within the statute of limitations, she will “highly likely” accept that finding and direct verdict to the defendants.

On Altman’s credibility: Savitt acknowledged the testimony about Altman’s truthfulness but argued that most OpenAI employees called for his return after the firing, that the board agreed to reinstate him, and that none of the credibility questions had anything to do with Musk’s donations, which had been fully spent years before any of those events occurred.

On unjust enrichment: Musk donated roughly $30 million. Others donated about $100 million. None of them have claimed a cut of OpenAI. Musk left the company and gave it a zero percent chance of survival. “OpenAI flourished after Musk’s departure.” The hundreds of people who stayed and did the work are responsible for its value, not Musk.

The closing arguments ran past the lunch break and into the afternoon. The judge gave the jury its final instructions and reminded them that lunch would be provided during deliberations. After three weeks of testimony about the future of artificial intelligence and $134 billion in claimed damages, the trial ended with a note about sandwiches.

What This Confirms

Cross-references to The Asimov Gap

The “are you completely trustworthy” exchange is the trial’s definitive moment. Not because Altman answered badly — “I believe so” is the most defensible answer available to a person who has been described as untrustworthy by multiple colleagues under oath. But because the question had to be asked at all. The governance architecture Altman built — the nonprofit wrapper, the capped profit structure, the board that fired and then reinstated him — produced a situation in which the most consequential question about the most consequential AI company is whether its CEO can be believed. That is the governance gap stated in four words.

Altman’s “left for dead” closing is the commercial turn’s most honest defence. He is not arguing that the for-profit structure was consistent with the founding promise. He is arguing that without it, there would be no company to have a founding promise about. That argument is coherent. It is also precisely the argument that every governance structure is supposed to prevent from becoming the only argument available — the argument that the mission required abandoning the structures built to protect it.

The self-dealing investments are now in the trial record. Helion, Cerebras — companies with OpenAI relationships in which Altman holds personal stakes. These were not rebutted comprehensively. They are in the record. Whether the judge treats them as relevant to damages or remedies is an open question.

Taylor’s rejection of Musk’s bid on Musk’s own grounds closes the loop. The trial has now produced, from the defendant’s own board chairman, the clearest possible statement of the trial’s governing irony. No appeal to the founding mission is available to the plaintiff that is not equally available to the defendant. “We did not feel it was appropriate for one person to control our mission” is OpenAI’s answer to Musk’s lawsuit, stated by the board chairman, about Musk’s attempt to buy the company.

The Wednesday testimony adds three facts that narrow the case further. Dudney’s accounting establishes that Musk’s $37 million was spent by late 2017 — years before the technology that made OpenAI valuable was built. Coates’s rebuttal establishes that the nonprofit holds a $200 billion equity stake, undermining the argument that the commercial turn looted the charity. And Achiam’s golden jackass puts the safety argument — the emotional heart of Musk’s case — in the mouth of a witness who says Musk was the one proposing to be reckless with it.

The closing arguments crystallize the statute of limitations problem. The judge has signalled that if the jury finds Musk filed too late, she will likely direct verdict for the defendants. Savitt’s argument — that Musk had notice in 2018, tweeted his objections in 2020, and waited until 2024 to sue — gives the jury a path to end the case without ever reaching the merits. That path may be the most consequential question they consider.

What Remains

The nine-person jury — six women, three men — begins deliberating Monday, May 19. Their verdict is advisory. Judge Gonzalez Rogers makes the final binding decision on liability. In an unusual procedural twist, the remedies phase also begins Monday, running in parallel: the judge will hear arguments about damages and potential relief even while the jury is deliberating. If the jury returns a no-liability verdict before the damages testimony is complete, that phase ends mid-stride.

If Musk prevails, the stakes are enormous: up to $150 billion in disgorgement redirected to the nonprofit foundation, a potential court order dismantling OpenAI’s for-profit structure, and the removal of Altman and Brockman from their roles. A Musk victory could derail OpenAI’s planned IPO — expected to be among the largest in history. Musk has said any monetary award should go to “the OpenAI charity,” though how that would work has not been explained.

The prediction markets have moved steadily against Musk since he took the stand. Kalshi traders gave him a 60 percent chance of winning when the trial opened. That number dropped to 34 percent after his testimony and sits near 40 percent as closing arguments conclude. Polymarket prices a 70 percent probability that Musk will not prevail. The market for a settlement of $10 billion or more gives Musk a 9 percent chance. The money says what the testimony suggested: the case is alive but no longer favoured.

The trial has produced a record that will outlast the verdict. Thirteen days of testimony and argument from Musk, Brockman, Birchall, Stuart Russell, Rosie Campbell, Natasha McCauley, David Schizer, Mira Murati, Shivon Zilis, Satya Nadella, Ilya Sutskever, Bret Taylor, Sam Altman, Josh Achiam, Daniel Hemel, John Coates, Louis Dudney, Kevin Scott, and Michael Wetter. Hundreds of exhibits. Text messages and emails that nobody intended to read in a federal courthouse. A bridge metaphor. A golden jackass. A plaintiff in Beijing.

The question the trial asked — whether a public promise made to build technology for humanity’s benefit can be broken without consequence — will not be answered by whatever the advisory jury recommends or the judge decides. It will be answered, if it is ever answered, by the laws that do not yet exist.

But the trial produced the record. And the record, as Max Tegmark said at the beginning: provides information that is not otherwise accessible.

The gavel has come down. The arguments are complete. The gap remains.
