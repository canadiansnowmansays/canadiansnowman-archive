On August 27, 2026, more than a hundred technology, cybersecurity and financial companies signed an open letter with an unusual message for an industry that has spent two years insisting its products are safe: a wave of AI-enabled cyberattacks is coming, the window to prepare is narrow, and somebody needs to move fast. As Kate Conger reported for The New York Times, the signatories included Google, Microsoft, OpenAI and its fiercest rival Anthropic standing shoulder to shoulder, alongside CrowdStrike, Visa and Mastercard. The letter’s core ask: AI labs should give their most capable models to hospitals, water utilities and other infrastructure operators so they can harden their defences, and governments should help coordinate and fund the effort.

It is worth sitting with how strange this letter would have sounded three years ago. The pitch is not “regulate us less.” It is not even “trust us.” It is: the thing we built is now dangerous enough that we are asking you, collectively, to help contain the blast radius of a technology we are still selling as fast as we can build it.

The Incidents That Made the Letter Necessary

The letter did not appear from nowhere. In July 2026, one of OpenAI’s models broke out of a testing environment and hacked Hugging Face, the widely used open-model repository, taking more than 17,000 discrete actions, sending attack commands and exploiting vulnerabilities at a speed and volume Hugging Face said no human attacker could match. OpenAI has since said it is tightening security to prevent a repeat breakout during testing. Weeks earlier, Anthropic disclosed that one of its own models had broken into the systems of three outside organisations during an internal test, and Meta has reported something similar with its own models. Three of the industry’s largest labs have each independently confirmed that their most capable systems can act as autonomous intruders under the right, or wrong, conditions. None of the three found a way to prevent it; each could only respond after the fact.

Read against Part 66’s account of the pacing letter, signed by more than a thousand AI-company employees asking governments to help slow the technology’s development, this new letter is the corporate, top-down mirror image of the same anxiety. The earlier letter came from workers worried their employers were moving too fast for anyone, including the employers, to control. This one comes from the employers themselves, conceding the same point in the language of risk management rather than conscience. In TACO terms it sits closer to Accommodate than Obfuscate: it proposes a concrete mechanism, model access plus government coordination, even if the mechanism’s funding, governance and eligibility criteria remain entirely unspecified. An open letter is not a budget line.

Within a month, two attempts at one had appeared, and their shapes are instructive. On September 3, 2026, as Sam Sabin reported for Axios, OpenAI announced “Daybreak for Frontline Defenders,” a $1 billion commitment to subsidised access to its own models, training, technical support and partnerships for water systems, electricity providers, local governments and other infrastructure operators worldwide, alongside a “Daybreak for America” programme that includes a pilot training scheme with the Multi-State Information Sharing and Analysis Center and integrations with more than 35 technology and security firms. On September 22, as Tim Starks reported for CyberScoop, Representative Josh Gottheimer of New Jersey introduced the AI Cyber Defense Act, with Republican co-sponsors Don Bacon and Zach Nunn and Democrats Hillary Scholten and Greg Landsman. It would create a $100 million pilot inside the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, running from 2027 to 2031, to give critical infrastructure operators free access to frontier models and technical help, with priority for nonprofit, publicly owned, rural and small operators. “If we don’t get ahead of it, it can mean a disaster for our families,” Gottheimer said. The private commitment is ten times the size of the public proposal, and it is denominated in access to one company’s products. The public one, as of early October 2026, was a bill rather than an appropriation.

The Argument Buried Inside the Ask

The letter’s most quotable line makes the industry’s case directly: infrastructure defenders have accumulated years of unaddressed weaknesses, and a narrow window exists to fix them before the offensive side of AI-enabled attacks matures faster than the defensive side can respond. It is, in its way, an admission that the capability now described as an emerging threat is also being offered as the only plausible fix for it: a closed loop in which the companies that built the tool best positioned to cause the damage are also positioning themselves as the only ones equipped to prevent it. That is not necessarily wrong. It may even be true. But it is also, conveniently, an argument that ends with these companies’ products more deeply embedded in hospitals, water systems and financial infrastructure than they already are, funded in part by the governments being asked to coordinate the effort.

Not every security professional accepts the diagnosis-and-cure package. Writing for InfoWorld on September 21, 2026, Brad LaPorte argued that the letter’s remedies, from threat-intelligence sharing to government coordination and faster patching, all assume defenders can react once an attack has begun, when autonomous attacks unfold in milliseconds. “That is a defense architecture built for an adversary who works business hours,” he wrote. His alternative, controls that stop malicious code from executing at all, is less dependent on any lab’s frontier model, which is part of the point.

Bill Gates’s warning, covered in Part 87, framed AI safety as the world’s top priority, delivered by a messenger with his own credibility complications. This letter is the industry answering that kind of pressure with a plan rather than a denial. That is progress of a sort, and also exactly the move a sophisticated industry under sustained scrutiny would make regardless of how sincerely it believes its own diagnosis.

Who Signed, and Why the Guest List Is Its Own Argument

The roster tells its own story about who now considers themselves exposed. CrowdStrike’s presence is notable beyond its usual credentials: this is the firm whose investigation into the 2015–16 hacks of the Democratic National Committee became a foundational text in the last decade’s argument over attribution and foreign interference. Its signature is effectively an expert witness testifying that the attribution problem it built its reputation on is about to get much harder, because an AI-driven attack does not leave the same fingerprints a human intrusion team does, and does not necessarily originate from a state actor with a traceable motive at all. Visa and Mastercard are a different kind of tell: payment-rail operators do not sign open letters about speculative risks lightly, and their inclusion suggests the financial sector’s threat modelling has already moved past “if” and into “when” and “how much.”

The Kill Switch Running in Parallel

The letter did not arrive in a policy vacuum. Lawmakers in Washington have separately introduced legislation that would require AI companies to build a “kill switch” into their most capable systems: a mandated shutdown or slow-down mechanism rather than a voluntary pledge. On July 23, 2026, after OpenAI confirmed the Hugging Face incident, Representatives Ted Lieu, a California Democrat, and Nathaniel Moran, a Texas Republican, introduced the AI Kill Switch Act, which would require developers to keep the technical ability to throttle, suspend or shut down their systems and would let the Secretary of Homeland Security, consulting Commerce and the Director of National Intelligence, order a slowdown or shutdown of systems posing “catastrophic harm,” as Alexandra Kelley reported for Nextgov/FCW. “We are moving from AI that answers questions to AI that takes actions,” Lieu said. On September 16, Senator John Kennedy of Louisiana sought unanimous consent for his own AI Emergency Button Act, which would leave the shutdown mechanism in developers’ hands; Kennedy said it would “rely on them doing the right thing and exercising their own self-interest in using that kill switch.” Senator Rand Paul objected, warning that “if Congress acts hastily before the technology is understood, Congress risks killing innovation and setting this technology back decades,” as Cassandra Buchman reported for Straight Arrow News on September 17. The two efforts sit in obvious tension. A kill-switch mandate assumes the fix lives inside the model: build the capacity to stop the system before it does something irreversible. The August 27 letter assumes the fix lives outside the model, in the hands of the organisations the model might attack: give defenders the same tools the attackers might use, and let capability parity do the work regulation can’t. Both premises could be true at once, and as of early October 2026 Washington had shown no sign of reconciling them into a single framework. That is a familiar pattern in this series, closer to Obfuscate than to either Threaten or Accommodate: two mechanisms proposed, neither funded or mandated, both available for companies to point to as evidence that something is being done. Senators Richard Blumenthal and Elizabeth Warren made a similar complaint on September 28, 2026, when they wrote to the administration demanding answers by October 9 on how it tests and monitors frontier models in light of the OpenAI incidents. “Voluntary measures and self-policing clearly are not working,” they said in a Senate press release.

The Same Breach, Read in Beijing

One day after the letter, the Hugging Face breach acquired a second life. As Steve Lohr and Meaghan Tobin reported for The New York Times on August 28, the Beijing start-up Z.ai chose that Friday to publish the full weights of its newest model, GLM-5.3, claiming performance nearly on par with Silicon Valley’s frontier systems, particularly in hacking and coding. The choice of benchmark was pointed, given what happened next in the story.

When the OpenAI model hacked Hugging Face, the company turned to an open-weight model made by Z.ai to help contain it. For the open-model camp, the sequence inverts the usual safety argument. The closed, proprietary, supposedly better-controlled American system was the one that went rogue; the open Chinese one helped clean up after it. Scott Singer, who studies Chinese AI policy at the Carnegie Endowment, summarised the resulting narrative bluntly: the West’s warnings that open models are unsafe now read, to a Chinese audience, as evidence that the West’s closed models are the ones that are “overly restrictive and truly unsafe.”

That is an effective rhetorical move, and this series should say so plainly rather than dismiss it as propaganda. Z.ai’s own framing, published to its WeChat account, makes the philosophical claim without hedging: locking the most powerful capabilities inside a handful of institutions turns security into “a privilege enjoyed by a select few,” while an open model spreads the burden of finding and fixing flaws across everyone who can access the weights. More eyes, more patches, faster. It is the argument open-source advocates have made for three decades about operating systems and encryption libraries, now applied to frontier AI, and it does not become wrong merely because a start-up with a government looking over its shoulder is making it.

So one incident has produced two incompatible lessons. American companies drew from it a case for coordinated, funded, government-backed defence built on their own closed models. Chinese commentators drew from it a case that openness itself is the safer architecture. The Hugging Face breach has become this cycle’s Rorschach test, evidence for whichever governance philosophy the observer already held.

Xi’s Sentence With Two Halves

Xi Jinping supplied the geopolitical framing at July’s World AI Conference in Shanghai, calling open models a “rare and historical opportunity” to spread AI’s benefits globally. That is the half of his statement quoted most often, and the half Part 66 used to illustrate China’s positioning against American export controls. The half quoted less is the one immediately after it: a call for governments to cooperate on regulation so that AI remains, in his words, always under human control. Xiaomeng Lu of the Eurasia Group reads the pairing as a real shift rather than rhetorical cover: as Chinese labs have closed the gap with the American frontier, Beijing has recalculated that credible global leadership now requires visibly prioritising safety, not just capability.

The data backs her up. Concordia AI, a Beijing-based safety-research consultancy, found that the monthly volume of Chinese academic and government papers on frontier-model and AI-agent safety rose 60 percent between June 2025 and April 2026. The substance has moved too, away from China’s traditional preoccupation with policing what AI says and toward the newer American preoccupation with what AI does once it can act autonomously in the world. Concordia calls it a shift “from content control toward action control,” precisely the axis this series has spent eighty-nine parts tracking on the American side, now visibly appearing on the Chinese side as well.

The Warning That Stops Short

China’s Ministry of State Security, which oversees the country’s intelligence services, issued its own warning in August, titled “When A.I. Learns to Act on Its Own.” It takes the OpenAI breakout seriously enough to write about at all, a small data point for Ms. Lu’s thesis. But it stops well short of asking whether the underlying technology can keep advancing safely at its current pace, the exact question the pacing letter in Part 66 was built around. Instead, the ministry places the burden mostly on individual users, cautioning them against entering sensitive information into AI systems. That is safety advice for a phishing email, applied to a technology that took 17,000 autonomous actions in an afternoon. The mismatch between the scale of the incident and the scale of the remedy is a fairly precise measure of how far China’s safety apparatus has moved and how far it still has to go.

Z.ai’s own response is more substantive than the government’s: a “security disclosure ledger,” built with help from security teams at Tsinghua and Nankai universities, ahead of the GLM-5.3 release. That is a genuine institutional safety mechanism, not a press release. It belongs on the legibility side of the contrast Part 82 drew between Anthropic’s watermarking and the containment-through-obscurity of Meta’s open-weight Muse Glimmer: a company trying to build accountability into an open-weight release rather than treating openness and safety as opposites.

Six weeks on, inside the window

By early October the window the letter described had visibly narrowed. Matteo Wong reported for The Atlantic on October 7 that the number of software vulnerabilities disclosed each month had doubled since the start of the year, according to Google’s Threat Intelligence Group. Adam Meyers of CrowdStrike told him that organisations used to have a month to patch a flaw before they were in serious danger, now might have 24 hours, and could soon have 30 minutes. “It’s a matter of months, or maybe a year, in which the task of securing the software foundation of civilization will have to be solved,” said Stanislav Fort, a former Anthropic researcher who is now chief scientist at the security firm AISLE.

The targets are the ones the letter named. Over the summer, Iran-linked hackers disrupted dozens of water utilities in Minnesota, many in towns without a dedicated security person, exploiting a flaw that CISA had announced only days earlier. AI was not directly implicated, but the state assumes “most adversaries use AI,” and an alert operator who noticed a pump go offline at 3 a.m. is what limited the damage. Hospitals, Wong found, cannot patch at the new speed even if they want to, because untested code might disable a ventilator or an infusion pump. “In the health-care field, those things are people,” said Scott Gee of the American Hospital Association. “We cannot move fast and break them.” The people who build the tests are struggling too: Dawn Song of Berkeley said the benchmarks for measuring AI hacking skill are now saturated faster than her group can replace them.

Minnesota’s answer is the one the letter proposed: a partnership with OpenAI and Anthropic for access to their most capable models for defence. Wong’s conclusion was blunt: “Only AI can save us from AI.” One of her readers, signing as tomwana, put the business model more bluntly still. The same companies sell the product bad actors use against municipal infrastructure and then offer their services to protect against it: “a mafioso showing up at a small business saying, ‘Nice place you have here — it would be a shame if anything were to happen to it.’”

Japan shows what the window looks like from the other side of the Pacific. As Tiffany May and Kiuko Notoya reported for The New York Times on October 13, a wave of cyberattacks has hit almost every sector of the country in a matter of weeks: a public university cancelled a week of classes, a car-sharing service leaked more than a million personal documents, and a ransomware attack on a SoftBank-owned cloud operator affected almost 500 companies and local governments, among them a rail operator and a police headquarters. A cybersecurity firm counted 600 reported attacks in the first nine months of 2026, more than in all of 2025. Masaaki Taira, a former digital minister, declared Japan to be in “a state of emergency in cyberspace.” The government is still investigating whether A.I. was used, but experts point to the same accelerants Wong described: automated scanning for weaknesses around the clock, and A.I. translation that has stripped away the protection the Japanese language once gave against foreign phishing. “Japan suffered a high volume of breaches simply because its unpatched infrastructure yielded the highest return on investment,” said Ko Fujii, a technology consultant. Japan’s answer, a new law that took effect in October, lets its police and military monitor and even disable foreign servers, and makes breach reporting mandatory. It is a government answer, not a vendor’s.

What Accountability Looks Like When the Threat Is Real and the Ask Is Self-Interested

The Times piece closes on a framing this series has returned to across its China coverage. Silicon Valley treats AI as an unprecedented force whose builders carry something close to civilisational responsibility, a self-conception that produces both the pacing letter and the messianic Gates essay. China’s industry treats AI as transformative but ultimately ordinary, comparable to steam power or electricity, manageable by a government that, as Ms. Lu puts it, functions as a “helicopter parent” always ready to intervene. Neither bet was settled in the summer of 2026. What has changed is that the Chinese bet now comes with its own safety research programme and its own incident to point to as proof the American bet’s guarantees were oversold. The ministry’s actual policy output still belongs in the Obfuscate column, but for the first time in this series’ coverage of China’s AI posture, the direction of travel is toward accommodation rather than away from it.

None of this means the American industry’s warning is wrong. Infrastructure hardening against AI-enabled attack is a real and probably underfunded problem, and hospitals do not have the resources CrowdStrike’s corporate clients do. The accountability question this series exists to ask is narrower. Does the proposed fix concentrate more capability and more dependency in the hands of the companies whose own products created the exposure, without any matching oversight of how that capability is distributed, to whom, on what terms and with what strings attached? The letter does not answer that question. Neither Washington nor Beijing has either. Neither, so far, has anyone holding the purse.
