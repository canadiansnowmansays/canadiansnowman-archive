Every monarchy this series has examined so far has built its legitimacy on some combination of theology, bloodline, force, or a carefully managed story about all three together. The Mughal Empire, ruling most of the Indian subcontinent from the sixteenth through the eighteenth centuries, presents this series with its clearest case yet of a ruling dynasty consciously experimenting with which legitimating strategy actually works when the population being ruled is genuinely, irreducibly diverse — a Muslim ruling house governing an overwhelmingly Hindu population, alongside Sikhs, Jains, Buddhists, and Christians, across a subcontinent with no shared language, no shared religious tradition, and centuries of prior political fragmentation. The empire’s most successful emperor solved this problem in a way no monarchy examined earlier in this series had attempted. His great-grandson unwound the solution almost entirely, and the empire never fully recovered from the reversal.

The dynasty’s founder, Babur, arrived in India as a genuinely displaced figure rather than a settled regional power expanding outward — a descendant of both Timur and Genghis Khan, driven from his ancestral territory in Central Asia by rival warlords, who turned south into the Indian subcontinent almost as a last resort after failing to hold onto his original inheritance. His decisive victory at the First Battle of Panipat in 1526, where his relatively small but tactically sophisticated force, employing artillery and matchlock techniques still unfamiliar to Indian battlefields, defeated the considerably larger army of the Delhi Sultanate’s last ruler, gave him control of northern India almost by accident of superior military technology rather than any long-planned campaign of conquest. Babur ruled only four more years before his death in 1530, leaving his son Humayun an empire that was still, in practical terms, a foreign military occupation rather than an established or accepted government — a distinction that mattered enormously for what his grandson would eventually have to solve.

Humayun’s own reign was a genuine catastrophe by most measures, losing the throne entirely to a rival Afghan noble, Sher Shah Suri, and spending over a decade in exile in Persia before managing to reclaim Delhi shortly before his death in 1556 — an inheritance he passed to his thirteen-year-old son Akbar under circumstances so precarious that few observers at the time would have predicted the Mughal dynasty’s survival, let alone its eventual status as one of the wealthiest and most populous empires on earth. It was Akbar, ruling for nearly half a century until his death in 1605, who actually solved the legitimacy problem his father and grandfather had never managed to address, and his solution deserves to be read as one of the more genuinely original pieces of political engineering examined anywhere in this series.

Akbar’s core insight was that a Muslim dynasty ruling a Hindu-majority population through pure military coercion, or through the standard tools of Islamic conquest-state legitimacy alone, would remain permanently vulnerable to the kind of rebellion that had already toppled his father once. His response was a deliberate, multi-pronged strategy of accommodation that went considerably further than any monarchy examined earlier in this series had attempted with a conquered or subject population. In 1564, he abolished the jizya, the traditional tax levied specifically on non-Muslim subjects under Islamic legal tradition, removing what had been one of the most tangible and resented markers of second-class status for his Hindu subjects. He married extensively into prominent Rajput royal families — the proud warrior clans of northwestern India who had resisted Muslim rule with particular ferocity — not as a purely symbolic gesture but as genuine political incorporation, appointing Rajput nobles and even Hindu generals, most famously Raja Man Singh, to senior positions of military and administrative authority within his own government, positions of real power rather than ceremonial inclusion. And in 1582, he went furthest of all, promulgating what he called the Din-i-Ilahi, a syncretic court philosophy drawing selectively on Islamic, Hindu, Zoroastrian, and Jain concepts, along with elements of Christianity absorbed through conversations with Jesuit missionaries he had specifically invited to his court to debate religious questions in structured public sessions — an extraordinary intellectual undertaking for any sixteenth-century ruler, let alone one governing an empire this large.

It is worth being precise about what Akbar’s syncretism actually was and was not, because later mythologizing has sometimes overstated its reach. The Din-i-Ilahi never became a mass religious movement; it functioned mainly as an elite court philosophy embraced by a relatively small circle of Akbar’s closest nobles, not as a replacement religion imposed on his broader population. What mattered more than the philosophy’s specific content was the underlying political signal it sent, consistently, across every policy this series has just described: that Akbar’s authority to rule was not contingent on his subjects sharing his own faith, and that genuine, meaningful power within his empire was available to capable men regardless of what god, or gods, they worshipped. This is a strikingly different solution to the legitimacy problem than anything this series has examined so far — not a claim to divine embodiment like Egypt’s pharaohs, not a revocable cosmic mandate like Zhou China’s emperors, but something closer to a functional bargain: loyalty and administrative competence purchased with genuine inclusion, on the explicit theory that a diverse empire’s stability depended on convincing its diverse population that the throne belonged, in some meaningful sense, to all of them rather than to one conquering faith alone.

One episode from the reign immediately following Akbar’s deserves inclusion here, because it shows a different and equally striking way legitimacy could be exercised within this same court system without ever formally sitting on the throne. Akbar’s son Jahangir, who ruled from 1605 to 1627, increasingly ceded effective day-to-day governance to his twentieth wife, Nur Jahan, whom he married in 1611 and who became, by the latter half of his reign, the empire’s genuine center of political gravity — issuing imperial orders in her own name, having coins struck bearing her own likeness and title alongside the emperor’s, and managing the empire’s affairs directly as Jahangir’s own health declined under the weight of alcoholism and opium addiction. Nur Jahan’s authority rested on no formal office and no claim to the throne in her own right, which makes her case a genuinely distinct variant from Wu Zetian’s formal usurpation in Tang China two centuries earlier — she exercised the full substance of imperial power for over a decade while never disturbing the outward form of Jahangir’s own reign, a demonstration that the gap between who holds a throne’s title and who actually exercises its authority could, in a sufficiently accommodating court culture, become almost total without ever requiring the more disruptive step of formally seizing the crown itself.

Akbar’s approach paid off in the durability of the empire he handed to his successors, and his son Jahangir and grandson Shah Jahan largely continued the broad outlines of his accommodationist policy even as they indulged, especially under Shah Jahan, in architectural extravagance on a scale this series has not encountered since the pyramid-building pharaohs of Old Kingdom Egypt. Shah Jahan’s most famous project, the Taj Mahal, built as a mausoleum for his beloved wife Mumtaz Mahal, who died in childbirth in 1631, took over a decade and reportedly some twenty thousand workers to complete, and stands today as perhaps the single most recognizable piece of architecture any monarchy examined in this entire series has ever produced. Less famous today, though it gave this essay its title, was the Peacock Throne itself, commissioned by Shah Jahan around the same period — a jewel-encrusted royal seat reportedly incorporating the Koh-i-Noor diamond among its ornamentation, its back fashioned into the spread tail of a peacock rendered in enamel, sapphires, rubies, and emeralds, valued by contemporary estimates at a sum that dwarfed the cost of the Taj Mahal itself. It was, in every sense, the physical embodiment of Mughal wealth and confidence at the empire’s peak — and it would not stay in Mughal hands for long.

The reversal that undid much of what Akbar had built came from Shah Jahan’s own son, Aurangzeb, whose path to the throne is itself worth a moment’s attention, because it shows how little the empire’s succession practices had matured even by this later, wealthier stage of Mughal history. Mughal inheritance followed no fixed rule of primogeniture; every son of a reigning emperor was, in principle, an equally legitimate claimant, which meant that nearly every transition of power this series has traced through the dynasty’s history involved some degree of armed conflict between rival brothers rather than an orderly, uncontested succession. When Shah Jahan fell seriously ill in 1657, his four sons moved almost immediately into open war against one another for the throne, and Aurangzeb, the third son and by most contemporary accounts the most militarily capable and religiously disciplined of the four, defeated and executed his brothers Dara Shikoh and Murad in succession, imprisoned his own recovering father in the Agra Fort for the remaining eight years of Shah Jahan’s life, and had himself crowned emperor in 1658 — reportedly denying his father, from that fortress imprisonment, anything more than a distant view of the Taj Mahal he had built, a detail later folklore would seize on as almost too poetically cruel to have been anything but deliberate. Aurangzeb’s nearly fifty-year reign, the longest of any Mughal emperor, represents this series’ clearest available case study in what happens when a ruler consciously reverses his predecessors’ accommodationist legitimacy strategy in favor of a narrower, more orthodox religious identity. Aurangzeb reimposed the jizya tax on non-Muslims in 1679, over a century after Akbar had abolished it, reversing one of the single most symbolically significant policies of his great-grandfather’s reign. He destroyed a number of prominent Hindu temples, particularly in regions where he judged local resistance to Mughal authority required a demonstration of religious as well as military force. And he abandoned the syncretic court culture Akbar had cultivated in favor of a considerably more austere and orthodox Islamic court identity, one considerably less interested in incorporating Hindu, Sikh, and Rajput elites into the empire’s governing structure on the generous terms Akbar had extended.

The military and political consequences of this reversal accumulated across Aurangzeb’s long reign, even as the empire reached its greatest territorial extent under his rule through relentless southward military campaigns against the Deccan sultanates. The Rajput alliance Akbar had so carefully built began fracturing, with several major Rajput states rising in open revolt against policies they experienced as a betrayal of a century-old bargain. In the Punjab, escalating conflict between the Mughal state and the Sikh community, including the execution of the ninth Sikh Guru, Tegh Bahadur, in 1675 on Aurangzeb’s orders for refusing to convert to Islam, helped transform Sikhism from a primarily religious movement into an increasingly militarized and politically organized force that would eventually carve out its own independent kingdom in the Punjab within a century of Aurangzeb’s death. And in the Deccan, the Maratha Empire, under leaders including the formidable Shivaji, grew from a regional Hindu resistance movement into a genuine rival power capable of contesting Mughal authority across large stretches of central and western India, exploiting exactly the kind of religiously-charged resentment Aurangzeb’s policies had generated among populations Akbar’s strategy had once successfully incorporated.

Aurangzeb died in 1707 having ruled the largest Mughal Empire in its history by territory, and having simultaneously exhausted its treasury, alienated substantial portions of its population, and left behind no comparably capable successor prepared to manage the fracturing alliances his own religious policies had helped produce. The century that followed his death was one of rapid and largely irreversible Mughal decline, as regional governors increasingly ruled their provinces as effectively independent states while maintaining only nominal deference to a steadily weakening emperor in Delhi. The most dramatic single blow came in 1739, when the Persian ruler Nader Shah invaded northern India, sacked Delhi itself, and departed with an almost unimaginable quantity of looted treasure — including, in the single most literal demonstration available anywhere in this series of a legitimating symbol being physically carried away from the throne it once embodied, the Peacock Throne itself, hauled back to Persia, where it was eventually broken apart, its jewels dispersed, its precise ultimate fate lost to history within a matter of decades.

What remained of Mughal authority after Nader Shah’s invasion was, for over a century, an increasingly hollow shell, with emperors ruling little more than the city of Delhi itself while the British East India Company steadily expanded its own effective control across the subcontinent through a combination of military conquest, commercial dominance, and carefully managed alliances with regional rulers — a process this series will examine in far greater detail in Part X. The formal end came only after the widespread uprising of 1857, sometimes called the Indian Rebellion or the Sepoy Mutiny, in which the aging and largely powerless last Mughal emperor, Bahadur Shah Zafar, was proclaimed a symbolic figurehead by rebel forces despite holding no meaningful independent authority of his own. When British forces suppressed the rebellion, they held Bahadur Shah personally responsible for a rebellion he had barely directed, tried him for treason, and exiled him to Rangoon in British Burma, where he died in 1862, the last of a dynastic line that had once, under Akbar, developed one of the more genuinely sophisticated legitimacy strategies this series has examined — and that had, under his own great-great-grandson Aurangzeb, thrown much of that hard-won accommodation away in exchange for a narrower and ultimately less durable claim to rule.

The lesson this series draws from the Mughal case is a sharper version of something Part I already flagged as a recurring theme: legitimacy strategies built around genuine inclusion of a diverse population tend to buy durability, while strategies that narrow the ruling group’s base of support in the name of religious or ideological purity tend to buy short-term consolidation at the cost of long-term stability. Akbar understood this and built an empire that lasted, in some meaningful form, for two more centuries after his death. Aurangzeb, ruling from a position of far greater territorial power than Akbar ever commanded, reversed course and left an empire that could not survive intact even a single further generation of weak leadership — a warning this series will find echoed, in different costume, in nearly every remaining monarchy still ahead of us.

Part X stays in India, but shifts scale entirely, from a single fallen imperial throne to the strangest monarchical patchwork this series will examine anywhere: several hundred separate princely states, ruled by everyone from the wealthiest man on earth to minor chieftains governing a few square miles, all absorbed or dissolved within a matter of months as British colonial rule finally came to an end.
