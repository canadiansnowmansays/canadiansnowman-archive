Stephen Gordon July 15, 2026

On March 5, 2026, I closed the file on Kristi Noem. I wrote that the case was not closed, even as the file was. I did not expect to be proven right this quickly, or this literally.

On the morning of July 7, an ICE officer shot and killed a man on Canal Street in Houston. His name was Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, 52, a construction business owner who had lived in the United States for 35 years and was in the process of obtaining a work permit. Acting ICE Director David Venturella said agents had stopped Araujo’s van and tried to arrest him, and that he had, in the agency’s phrase, weaponized his vehicle in an attempt to run over an officer, who fired in self-defense. Venturella offered no evidence to support the claim.

Two days later, DHS quietly added a detail that changes how that statement reads: Araujo was not the person agents were looking for. They had stopped his van searching for a Guatemalan man they believed was riding in the passenger seat. The construction worker driving three coworkers to a job site, killed by agents pursuing someone else entirely, was still fitted with the same sentence used for an intended target — as though the sentence had been drafted before anyone checked who was actually in the van.

Anyone who followed the deaths of Renee Good and Alex Pretti in January will recognize that sentence. It is not similar to the language DHS used after Good was killed in Minneapolis. It is nearly identical to it. The agency did not bother writing a new script. It reused the old one, word for word, for a new body.

That is the story of this series before I have even told you the story. The institution does not change its account when the facts change. It changes the name.

What the Witnesses Say, and What DHS Still Hasn’t Shown

I want to be careful here, because care is exactly what DHS was not. When I first drafted this piece, no video of the Houston shooting had surfaced. That has changed within days. Surveillance footage broadcast on local television shows ICE vehicles tailing Araujo’s van and appearing to cut it off — not a van bearing down on an officer, but agents boxing a vehicle in. Additional images show a man on the ground, hands behind his back, and agents standing over someone holding his abdomen.

More significant still: the three men who were riding in the van with Araujo — all three arrested during the encounter, all three now speaking from immigration detention — have disputed DHS’s account directly, through their lawyer, Hugo Balderas-Ibarra. Their claim is not a hedge or a maybe. It is a flat denial: Araujo never used the van as a weapon, and the agents were never in danger. I want to be honest about the limits of that account — these are men facing their own charges arising from the same encounter, speaking through a lawyer representing their interests, not neutral witnesses. But their statement is now also corroborated by something harder to dismiss as advocacy: the Harris County Medical Examiner has ruled Araujo’s death a homicide.

That is not proof of what happened in the way a trial produces proof. But it moves this case out of the territory Good’s and Pretti’s occupied for their first days, where the government’s word was the only word available. Here, within 72 hours, DHS’s account already has three eyewitnesses, a body of surveillance video, and a medical examiner’s ruling arrayed against it — and DHS has still not released any footage of its own, has still not explained the discrepancy between “he tried to run over an officer” and video appearing to show agents boxing his van in, and, as of this writing, has offered no evidence at all for the claim Venturella made within hours of the shooting. LULAC, FIEL Houston, and the Texas Civil Rights Project have asked for exactly what Minnesota had to sue for: preservation of body cam, dash cam, bystander footage, dispatch logs, everything. They are asking early because they have watched what happens when you ask late.

A week later, Harris County District Attorney Sean Teare made a plainer complaint: his office still does not know the identities of the ICE officers involved, or where they are. An officer-involved death without that basic information available to local prosecutors a week out is, in Teare’s words, highly unusual. “The federal government has not invited us in,” he said. “The federal government is not collaborating with us with this investigation.” That is not an investigation running slowly. That is an investigation not being permitted to happen.

The FBI, per its Houston field office, is investigating what it calls the potential assault on a federal officer. The Department of Homeland Security’s own Inspector General is nominally leading a review of the shooting itself. If that arrangement sounds familiar, it should. It is close to the arrangement DHS offered in the Pretti case, where Homeland Security Investigations — ICE’s own investigative arm — looked into a shooting carried out by ICE’s own colleagues at Customs and Border Protection, and where the two shooters were placed on the standard administrative leave that has, so far, produced no public finding of any kind after nearly six months.

The Case I Am Not Making

Before I go further, I want to be explicit about what this series is not arguing, because it would be dishonest to proceed as though the other side of this debate does not exist or has nothing to it.

There is a genuine, defensible case for aggressive interior immigration enforcement. The administration inherited what it describes as a period of record border crossings, and a considerable number of Americans, across party lines, believe a decade of weak consequences for illegal entry produced real and worsening problems that a sitting government has every right to address. Immigration agents carrying out arrests do sometimes face real resistance and real danger; officers are not fabricating that risk out of nothing, and a healthy account of these events should not pretend every encounter is a peaceful one interrupted by trigger-happy agents. None of the deaths and shootings this series will document arose in a vacuum free of any provocation whatsoever, and I am not going to claim otherwise about every single case.

That is a real debate, and reasonable people, including plenty of Americans who are horrified by what happened to Renee Good and Alex Pretti, disagree in good faith about how aggressively immigration law should be enforced and what trade-offs that enforcement should accept. This series does not attempt to settle that debate, and readers looking for an argument about immigration policy itself should look elsewhere. What this series is about is narrower and, I think, less contestable: whether the government tells the truth about what its agents did, whether it lets independent investigators test that account against evidence, and whether anyone is ever held to answer when the account turns out to be false. You can believe enforcement should be strict and still expect an honest report of what happened when an agent fires a gun. Those are different questions, and the second one is the only one this series is asking.

The Sentence That Should Not Repeat Itself

I want to sit for a moment on the specific language, because language is the tell in this story, the way it was the tell in Noem’s case. Sen. John Kennedy of Louisiana caught Noem’s fatal contradiction not by uncovering a hidden fact but by noticing what a sentence implied. The same discipline applies here.

Acting ICE Director David Venturella’s statement on Houston said Salgado Araujo “weaponized his vehicle in an attempt to run over an ICE law enforcement officer resulting in our officer firing his weapon in self-defense.” This is, functionally, a form letter. It is built to close the question before it opens: intent (weaponized), justification (self-defense), and no further elaboration required. It is the same shape of sentence DHS used for Good, whose car was parked perpendicular to traffic, not accelerating toward anyone, when federal vehicles arrived. Video proved that account wrong. The agency did not revise its template. It kept the template and waited for the next name to fill in — and within days, surveillance footage and three eyewitnesses were already pulling at the same threads.

This is what happens when an institution learns the wrong lesson from a scandal. Noem was not undone by the killings themselves. As I wrote in March, she was undone by contradicting the president under oath about an advertising budget. Nobody at DHS was fired, demoted, or publicly reprimanded for the language used to describe two dead American citizens. So the language survived her. It is doing exactly what it was built to do: buy time, shape the first news cycle, and let the eventual, quieter walk-back — if one ever comes — arrive long after the cameras have moved on to something else.

This Was Never Just Two Names

It is worth resisting the instinct to treat Good and Pretti as a contained tragedy — two names, one bad month, a department that has since moved on. By the New York Times’s count, federal agents have fired on at least 22 people since immigration enforcement operations intensified last year; six have died, three of them American citizens. Good and Pretti are two of those three. The third is Ruben Ray Martinez, killed by ICE agents during a Texas traffic stop in March 2025 — a death that stayed out of public view until it surfaced in February 2026, more than a year after it happened and weeks after Minneapolis had already made headlines impossible to ignore. Separately, in October, Silverio Villegas-Gonzalez, a Mexican immigrant, was shot and killed in Chicago as he drove away from officers. Add Keith Porter, shot dead by an off-duty ICE agent in a Los Angeles apartment complex on New Year’s Eve for the crime of firing a gun into the air to celebrate the new year, an act his family calls reckless but not remotely deserving of the response it drew. Add the six people who died in ICE detention facilities in January alone, including Geraldo Lunas Campos, whose death a medical examiner in El Paso ruled a homicide, and whose cause of death DHS has now described in at least two contradictory ways. That is nine deaths tied to immigration enforcement in a single month, in a single agency, and that is before you get to Houston — and before you get to Martinez, whose death took a year to become public at all. The sixth death in the Times’s count did not even wait for this piece to finish. It happened six days after Houston, and I get to it below.

The Sosa-Celis case belongs in this ledger too, and it deserves its own telling later in this series, because it is the closest thing we have to a preview of how these cases actually resolve. Julio Sosa-Celis was shot and wounded by an ICE agent named Christian Castro after a chase in Minnesota. Federal prosecutors initially charged the men in Sosa-Celis’s vehicle with assaulting a federal officer. A month later, the same U.S. Attorney’s Office asked a court to drop those charges with prejudice, writing that newly discovered evidence was materially inconsistent with what the government had alleged under oath at a preliminary hearing. ICE’s own director then confirmed the agency was investigating whether two of its agents had made untruthful statements about the encounter — a federal offense if proven — and placed them on leave. Hennepin County, separately, filed state criminal charges against Castro. That is a case where the government’s own account did not survive its own scrutiny, and where a state prosecutor moved to hold a federal agent accountable when the federal government would not do it for him. It is the template this whole series will keep returning to — and, as it turns out, the very case that finally forced Washington’s hand on the evidence fight. More on that below.

Six Days Later, Biddeford

I had not finished writing about Houston when the next one happened as reported by The New York Times.

On the morning of July 13, an ICE Enforcement and Removal Operations officer shot and killed a man at the intersection of Pool and Hill Streets in Biddeford, Maine. The agency’s account, relayed through Maine’s Attorney General’s office, was that the officer was executing a removal order when the subject attempted to flee in a vehicle in the direction of the officer. The victim was Joan Sebastian Guerrero, a 26-year-old Colombian man, according to a spokesman for Sen. Angus King of Maine.

I want to draw your attention to one word in that sentence, because you have now read it three times in this piece. Sen. King spoke directly with DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin in the hours after the shooting, and relayed to reporters the term the secretary used: Mullin told him the man had “weaponized” his vehicle. Not an ICE press release. Not a spokesperson’s talking point. The secretary of Homeland Security himself, on the phone with a sitting United States senator, reaching for the identical word Kristi Noem’s department used for Renee Good, and Alex Pretti, and, six days earlier, Lorenzo Salgado Araujo. Three deaths, three states, three separate encounters, and DHS cannot produce a second adjective.

It gets worse before lunch. King told reporters that Mullin initially told him the man killed in Biddeford was the target of the warrant his agents were executing. Later — the same day, not weeks later, not after video surfaced — Mullin corrected himself: the man was not the target. Compare that to Houston, where it took DHS two days to quietly concede that Salgado Araujo was not the person agents were looking for either. In Maine, the secretary of Homeland Security could not keep his own story straight for a single afternoon. And as King separately noted, the agents involved were not wearing body cameras — a detail he said concerned him, and one that means Biddeford, like Houston, like Minneapolis, will have no institutional record of the thing DHS is asking the country to take on faith. Even DHS’s own public statement could not say clearly whether Guerrero had actually been the target of the operation at all.

This is what I mean when I say the file was never closed. It is not a metaphor for slow bureaucratic drift. It is a description of an actual, ongoing rate of production. Two ICE-involved killings in six days, under a secretary who promised the department would get out of the headlines, using language his own predecessor’s department wrote before he took the job. Noem was removed for a lie about a budget. Her sentence survived her, intact, and it is now failing in real time in front of a United States senator.

Two More Things, Briefly

Two things happened this week that would not have happened without Houston and Biddeford landing six days apart. The administration has ordered ICE to halt most vehicle stops nationwide — notable, since nearly every fatal shooting this series documents began with agents stopping a vehicle, though the timing suggests the death toll becoming unmanageable mattered more than any single death on its own merits. And on Monday, the Justice Department turned over evidence to Minnesota investigators in the Good and Pretti cases after months of the exact stonewalling this series has been documenting — though, as it turns out, only as part of a trade, not a change of heart, and with Houston getting the identical treatment Minnesota just spent six months fighting. That story deserves its own telling rather than a rushed one here, and it gets it in Part V.

The Institution That Replaced Her

Markwayne Mullin was sworn in promising something different from Noem — a quieter department, warrants over sweeps, less of the theatre that defined her tenure. Some of that held; much of it didn't, and I trace that record in full in Part VII. What matters here is the short version: Noem was removed. DHS was not reformed. A department that killed two American citizens in seventeen days, that could not get its own account of either killing to survive contact with video, handed its most consequential failure of the Trump administration's second term to a successor who promised quiet and delivered a version of the same instincts with better manners. Houston and Biddeford are what that looks like in practice: the mask changed, the sentence didn't — right up until this week, when the sentence finally cost the department a policy it had defended for a year.

Where This Goes Next

I am calling this series The Blanche Files, and I will explain fully in Part II why the second name in this story belongs to Todd Blanche, the acting attorney general and Trump’s nominee for the permanent role, whose Senate confirmation hearing arrives this week. It is Blanche’s Justice Department, not Mullin’s DHS, that spent six months declining to properly investigate Good’s death, slow-walking the investigation into Pretti’s, and — this is the part that should trouble anyone who thinks institutions still check each other — obstructing Minnesota’s own investigators from getting evidence they were constitutionally entitled to pursue. That obstruction only partially broke this week, under exactly the kind of pressure most families never have the standing or the resources to generate, and it is already repeating in Houston.

That is not a Noem story. Noem is gone. That is an accountability story, and it belongs to the machinery that survives personnel change: the Justice Department, the courts, and a doctrine called supremacy-clause immunity that nobody outside a law school seminar had reason to know about eighteen months ago and that may now determine whether any American can ever hold a federal agent accountable in a state courtroom again.

The file was not closed in March. It was handed to someone else. This series is about following it there.

A Note From the Canadian Side of the Border

We do not have ICE. We do not have a supremacy clause built the way yours is, or a Department of Homeland Security assembled in the shock of a single September morning and never quite examined since. What we have is the ability to watch, from just across the water, how a democracy decides whether the people who enforce its laws answer to anyone at all.

Six months ago, one woman lost her job for embarrassing a president. Three American citizens are dead, and a fourth person died in Maine only three days ago. Their families are still waiting. The file was never closed. It only changed hands, and this week, for the first time, it moved a few inches back the other way.
