Bell, TELUS, and Rogers were never going to be the whole story. Three more filers matter in this proceeding — Cogeco, SaskTel, and Quebecor, filing on behalf of Vidéotron — and between them they cover a genuinely different set of corporate structures: a regional cableco, a provincial Crown corporation, and Quebec’s dominant vertically integrated media and telecom conglomerate. If the deregulatory push in the Big Three’s filings were really about size, scale, or shareholder pressure, these three companies should look different from Bell, TELUS, and Rogers, and from each other. Mostly, they don’t. What varies is not the destination. It’s how much consumer-facing packaging each company wraps around getting there.

Cogeco: the most ideological, and the most careful about optics

Cogeco’s August 11, 2026 intervention (Consumer Intervention 166), filed by VP of Regulatory and Corporate Affairs Paul Beaudry, is the most explicitly ideological filing in the whole proceeding. It leads with language about “one-size-fits-all” regulation imposed “from the top,” invokes the 2023 Telecommunications Policy Direction by name, and — distinctively among the filers — asks the Commission to affirm that the harmonised Code rests on Parliament’s exclusive constitutional jurisdiction over telecommunications, foreclosing any provincial consumer-protection statute from applying concurrently. Bell and TELUS want the same outcome; Cogeco is the one that frames it as a constitutional question rather than a business-cost one.

On trial periods, Cogeco goes further than anyone else in the proceeding: not just holding the line at 15 days, but arguing that television and home phone services should have no trial period at all, safety carve-outs for customers with disabilities aside. Where Rogers and SaskTel defend the status quo, Cogeco argues for less than the status quo.

And yet on device unlocking, Cogeco is the most consumer-conscious filer among the carriers covered in this series so far, and the only one to attach real conditions to the lock rather than asking for it bare. It supports the permanent 60-day lock, but only with conditions of its own devising: unlocking must be fully automated and free, restricted strictly to financed or leased devices (never ones purchased outright), and Cogeco explicitly recommends the CRTC direct the CISC — the Commission’s interconnection steering committee, the body that coordinates technical standards across carriers — to set up a working group so smaller carriers without direct manufacturer relationships can access the same anti-theft technology as the majors. It’s the same ask as Bell and TELUS, dressed in real safeguards rather than left bare.

Cogeco also wants a 24-month implementation window from the date of the Commission’s decision — the same delay window as Bell’s two-year floor, though Bell separately asks that nothing take effect before January 2030 regardless of when the decision lands, which would make Bell’s effective delay longer still. Cogeco wants annual compliance reporting scrapped in favour of a signed corporate-officer certification, a position it shares almost word for word with the Canadian Telecommunications Association’s own submission.

SaskTel: the Crown corporation making the private carriers’ argument

SaskTel’s August 14, 2026 intervention (Consumer Intervention 178), filed by Director of Regulatory Affairs Kevin Spelay, reads nothing like Cogeco’s. There’s no constitutional framing, no invocation of federal policy directives, no nation-building rhetoric. It’s plain, numbers-first, and almost entirely free of ideology — which makes it more striking, not less, that a Saskatchewan Crown corporation, wholly owned by the government of a province, lands on nearly the identical set of asks as the country’s largest private carriers.

SaskTel wants the 15-day trial period held, with the existing 30-day extension preserved for customers with disabilities. It wants the 60-day device lock made permanent, citing the same fraud rationale as everyone else. It wants the Code applied to all internet service providers regardless of size — a position that puts it alongside TELUS, PIAC, and Quebecor, and against Bell (which wants more exemptions) and TekSavvy (which wants its existing exemption preserved). And in the proceeding’s one moment of genuine cross-carrier coincidence, SaskTel independently raises the exact same objection Rogers raised: that the proposed rule letting customers pick their preferred contact method for a disconnection notice was never actually part of the scope of Telecom Regulatory Policy 2026-67, the recent decision on customer notifications, and shouldn’t be smuggled into the Code as if it were.

SaskTel’s most substantive original contribution is a full working definition of “device rental plan” — more complete and more concrete than anything TELUS or Rogers offered, spelling out upfront discounts tied to a return commitment, the trade-in-value calculation, and what happens if a customer cancels early. It’s a plainly useful piece of drafting, offered in service of the same underlying position everyone else is arguing for.

And SaskTel breaks from the pack in one place that matters: implementation timing. Where Bell wants a minimum of 24 months (and no earlier than January 2030), and Cogeco wants 24 months, SaskTel asks for just 12 — half of what the country’s two largest carriers are requesting, and closer to what the CCTS and PIAC will separately argue is actually sufficient, as we’ll see in the next instalment. A government-owned utility, arguing the industry’s substantive position with none of the industry’s urgency about delay.

Quebecor: the sharpest elbows, and the most striking numbers

Quebecor’s August 28, 2026 intervention (Consumer Intervention 192), filed in French by VP of Regulatory and Environmental Affairs Peggy Tabet on behalf of Vidéotron, is softer in tone than Bell or TELUS — Quebecor merely reserves the right to appear at the hearing rather than requesting to — but its substantive asks are, in places, the most aggressive in the entire proceeding.

On business customers, Quebecor goes further than every other filer, including TELUS. It’s not just asking that small business be excluded from the internet and home phone rules; it wants business customers of every size excluded from the entire Code, including the Wireless Code obligations Vidéotron has already been complying with for over a decade. The rationale is unusually candid: ten years of applying consumer-style wireless protections to small-business clients has, in Quebecor’s own account, been operationally difficult at every stage and has generated customer frustration rather than reduced it — an argument from lived experience rather than abstract cost-benefit.

On home phone and television, Quebecor lines up with TELUS: exclude both from the harmonised Code entirely. If home phone stays in regardless, Quebecor wants a hard line drawn between traditional analogue service and VoIP — a distinction that matters a great deal to a company whose phone business is largely VoIP-based.

Where Quebecor gets genuinely sharp is a direct, on-the-record swipe at two named competitors: it points out that Altima Telecom and Fongo — both currently exempt from the Internet Code as small non-facilities-based providers — generate more CCTS complaints than Eastlink, a company that is subject to the Code. It’s a specific, checkable claim aimed at undercutting the case for the small-ISP exemption altogether, and it puts Quebecor in the same camp as TELUS, SaskTel, and — as we’ll see next time — PIAC, all arguing from different motives that the exemption should disappear.

The empirical material throughout Quebecor’s filing is worth citing on its own merits, independent of what Quebecor wants done with it: fixed-line phone subscriptions in Canada have fallen 40 percent in twelve years, from 17.7 million to 10.7 million; CCTS phone-related complaint issues have dropped 53 percent since 2018-19, from 6,938 to 3,279; the share of households subscribing to traditional broadcast distribution has fallen 24 percentage points between 2014 and 2025, from 79 percent to 55 percent; and 2025 was the first year online broadcasting revenue in Canada overtook broadcast-distribution revenue outright, at $6.2 billion against $6.1 billion. Whatever you make of Quebecor’s argument that this decline justifies deregulating home phone and television, the numbers themselves are a genuinely useful snapshot of how fast the ground has shifted under this whole proceeding.

Quebecor also filed a second, separate document on August 11 responding directly to TELUS’s device-unlocking application — and it’s here that Quebecor makes its most technically ambitious move of the proceeding. It supports TELUS’s 60-day lock, but adds a fourth condition of its own: that the Commission require device manufacturers themselves — Apple, Samsung, Google — to make their anti-theft technology available to every Canadian carrier, not just the ones with direct manufacturer relationships. Quebecor’s argument is that a network-side lock is a partial fix at best: it only works inside Canada, it expires after 60 days, and it does nothing about theft from inventory before a device is ever activated. Apple’s own Activation Lock, by contrast, is cloud-based and survives a factory reset — the same technology, Quebecor notes, that displayed “this device has been disabled and is being tracked” on stolen phones during the 2025 Apple Tower Theatre break-in in Los Angeles. Quebecor endorses Cogeco’s CISC working-group proposal and asks that its mandate be widened to cover exactly this kind of manufacturer-side access. It also notes, pointedly, that Bell has reportedly already been running 60-day locks in practice despite the current Wireless Code prohibition — meaning the asymmetry TELUS’s application is meant to fix may already be pushing theft toward whichever carriers haven’t started bending the existing rule.

What three very different companies agree on

Strip away the constitutional framing, the plain prose, and the French-language filing, and Cogeco, SaskTel, and Quebecor land in nearly the same place as Bell, TELUS, and Rogers: permanent device locks, resistance to longer trial periods, support for excluding business customers from some or all of the Code, and — again, without exception across all six filings now read — total silence on advance billing.

What genuinely differs, and what’s worth taking seriously rather than dismissing as noise, is how much consumer protection each company is willing to build into the asks it can’t avoid making. Cogeco’s device-unlock safeguards are real. SaskTel’s shorter implementation timeline is real. Quebecor’s manufacturer-accountability proposal is real, and arguably more consumer-protective on that one point than anything the Big Three proposed. None of that changes where these six companies land collectively. It does mean the record isn’t uniform in tone, even where it’s uniform in outcome — and it’s worth remembering, when the counter-record comes in next, that the sharpest disagreements in this proceeding aren’t between the carriers and the Commission. They’re between the carriers and the people the Code is supposed to protect.
