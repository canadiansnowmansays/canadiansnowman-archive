When this series last visited James Comey’s case, in Part XXI, the charges against the former FBI director involved seashells arranged on a North Carolina beach.

That case is still pending. In April 2026, Comey was indicted for posting a photograph in which the shells spelled out “86 47” — “86” being, the Justice Department argued, military slang for eliminating someone, and “47” being Donald Trump’s presidential number. The reaction from legal observers cut across ideological lines. Jonathan Turley, the conservative legal scholar and commentator who has spent years defending executive authority and criticising prosecutorial overreach from the left, called the case unconstitutional — a judgment that carries particular weight precisely because Turley is not someone whose instincts run toward sympathy for former FBI directors who crossed Donald Trump. When critics are coming from that direction, it is usually because the case has overreached in ways that even its ideological beneficiaries cannot overlook.

In the days after the indictment, Amazon.com showed more than five hundred items for sale bearing the same inscription. The market, in its casual way, had assessed the seriousness of the charge — and had concluded that “86 47” was less a credible death threat than a commodity.

A trial was set for October 2026, in federal court in North Carolina. As of this writing, that case remains active.

Which brings us to the present moment, and to the reason this series has returned to Comey before the seashells have even gone to trial.

On October 26, 2026, the New York Times reported that federal prosecutors had, earlier that month, filed a criminal information in federal district court in Washington, D.C., charging Comey with knowingly and wilfully causing national defence information to be “communicated, delivered, and transmitted to a person not entitled to receive it.” The alleged conduct: a conversation, or a series of them, around October 4 and 5, 2016 — ten years ago — that prosecutors say ended up in a New York Times article.

Comey’s lawyers had already sought to get the criminal information dismissed, and had not succeeded. Their arguments were two: first, that the matter had previously been investigated and had not produced charges; and second, that the time to bring those charges had now passed, a statute-of-limitations argument that is, at its core, a plea for proportionality — for some recognition that a decade is a long time to decide that conduct is criminal.

The target, once again, is not persuaded he should go quietly.

“I’m still innocent. I’m still not afraid of these people,” Comey said in a video he posted online after the filing became public. The new charges, he said, “would almost be funny if it weren’t such an appalling abuse of the Department of Justice, one that we cannot become numb to.”

To understand the current moment, the full three-case timeline is necessary.

The government’s first move against Comey came before the seashells, in a false statements investigation prosecuted out of Virginia under a special prosecutor named Lindsey Halligan. That case did not survive contact with a federal judge. In November 2025, it was dismissed: Halligan had been improperly appointed to bring it, and the charges fell apart on that procedural ground before reaching the merits.

This was a clean illustration of one of the central patterns this series has tracked. The manufactured prosecution tends not to be built for durability; it is built for announcement, for pressure, for the political utility of having a case on the docket. When a judge looks hard at the architecture, it tends not to hold. The Virginia case held for less time than it took to properly appoint a prosecutor.

Then came the seashells — Part XXI of this series, April 2026, the North Carolina indictment for beach photography with an attitude. And now, while the seashells case proceeds toward trial, comes the Washington D.C. criminal information for a conversation that allegedly occurred in the autumn of Barack Obama’s final presidential term.

James Comey is, at this moment, the only person on this list — which now runs to more than a dozen tracked targets including attorneys general, senators, Federal Reserve governors, a former national security adviser, several sitting members of Congress and, as of October 2026, two former FBI intelligence officials — who faces active federal prosecution on two separate and simultaneous dockets. Three cases in total. One dismissed, two live.

The government is, in effect, running the clock on multiple fronts at once.

A criminal information is worth pausing on, because its choice as the charging vehicle here is not neutral.

An indictment requires a grand jury: a body of civilians who review the evidence, assess its sufficiency, and must agree to bring charges. A criminal information bypasses that step. The prosecutor files directly. The old joke holds that a grand jury will indict a ham sandwich if a prosecutor asks, but in Washington the joke has stopped working. In February 2026, a federal grand jury there refused to indict six Democratic lawmakers who had reminded military and intelligence personnel of their duty to refuse illegal orders. On October 27, 2026, as Michael S. Schmidt, Alan Feuer and Glenn Thrush reported for The New York Times, another grand jury in the same city returned a “no true bill” against Cassidy Hutchinson, the former White House aide whose testimony to the January 6 committee portrayed Trump as willing to do almost anything to keep power. Some career prosecutors had doubted there was a case against her at all. The investigation had been routed, unusually, to the department’s civil rights division, led by Harmeet Dhillon, who has steered that unit toward the president’s agenda. Against that record, the absence of a grand jury in the Comey case looks less like a procedural detail than a lesson learned. Neither Comey nor the two former FBI officials charged the same week was charged by a grand jury, a hurdle prosecutors will still need to clear before any of those cases can go to trial.

Sometimes a criminal information signals that a plea is in the works; a defendant who has tentatively agreed to cooperate or plead guilty is often charged by information rather than indictment, streamlining the process. Court records suggest this is not that: Comey’s lawyers are fighting the filing, not negotiating terms. The criminal information here appears to be, rather, a way of formalising charges while avoiding the grand jury stage — retaining the legal weight of a criminal charge while keeping the evidentiary review in the government’s hands.

The lawyers’ statute-of-limitations argument, if it ultimately succeeds, will end the D.C. case on procedural grounds without a trial, much as the Virginia case ended without reaching the merits. That would make it two for three on procedural dismissals. Whether that counts as a government victory depends on what one believes the government is actually trying to accomplish.

The criminal information is not the only unusual charging instrument at work in that Washington courthouse. On October 27, 2026, as Alan Feuer reported for The New York Times, Magistrate Judge Zia M. Faruqui spent a day hearing challenges to a criminal complaint against two former FBI officials, Nikki Floris and Bradley Benavides. Both served on the bureau’s Foreign Influence Task Force, the unit created after Russia’s interference in the 2016 election, which in 2020 relayed warnings about a Russian disinformation operation built around claims that Joe Biden had engaged in corrupt dealings in Ukraine. They are accused of lying to the House Judiciary Committee in 2023 about their roles in the FBI’s inquiries into Hunter Biden.

A criminal complaint, like a criminal information, keeps a grand jury out of the room. Complaints are normally reserved for cases where prosecutors must move fast or fear a defendant will flee. Judge Faruqui observed that neither condition applied: the defendants are well-respected former agents, and the underlying facts are more than three years old. Then the government went further. Late on October 26, prosecutors filed an amended complaint adding a charge of obstruction of Congress without first seeking a judge’s approval. The prosecutor, John Borchert, conceded the move was novel. The judge corrected him: “It’s never been done before.”

The more consequential disclosure came from the witness stand. Bryan Witt, the FBI agent assigned to the case, testified that it began in mid-July 2026, the day after Donald Trump used a prime-time address to attack bureau intelligence officials who had played down a disputed informant report claiming China tried to tip the 2020 election toward Biden, and to call for their prosecution. The next day, Kash Patel sent a letter to the U.S. attorney’s office in Kansas City ordering those officials investigated for obstruction and conspiracy. Witt said that in his career he had never before received instructions directly from an FBI director. He said Patel and his deputies regularly checked his progress and pressed for aggression, and that the inquiry was generally regarded in his office as a “witch hunt.” When the agents could find no evidence for the original allegations, the investigation did not close. It pivoted to three-year-old congressional testimony.

Set beside the Comey filing, the pattern is hard to miss. The president airs a grievance. An order comes down from the top. Investigators search for a crime to fit the target. Prosecutors choose a charging vehicle that keeps grand juries and, increasingly, judges at arm’s length. Judging by the order of events Witt described, the offence of Floris and Benavides was doubting a report the president wanted believed.

The same sequence played out this year on the National Mall, over something as small as pool paint. When the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool’s new “American flag blue” coating peeled in June after a rushed, no-bid repair, Trump blamed vandals, and federal prosecutors charged several people with tearing it up. Weeks later Jeanine Pirro, the U.S. attorney for the District of Columbia, asked the court to drop the charges, saying a botched repair had caused the damage; the contractor reached the same conclusion about its own work. Last month a federal judge ruled that prosecutors could never revive the one felony charge, against the former Olympian David Hearn, whose lawyers have asked internal watchdogs to examine how senior officials ignored the evidence in a rush to satisfy the president, as The New York Times reported. On Monday Trump returned to the refilled pool for his closing argument of the campaign. The day before, he was still blaming the vandals. At the pool itself, he found time to rail against James Comey.

This series established, in its twenty-sixth instalment, a four-point test for distinguishing real prosecutions from manufactured ones.

Real prosecutions share four characteristics: they predate the political grievance that would make them convenient; they are brought by career prosecutors working across administrations; they rest principally on the defendant’s own words and actions; and they tend to produce a guilty plea, because defendants facing a genuinely strong case usually accept reality.

Apply that test to the seashells case. Does it predate the grievance? No — the photograph was posted in 2025, after Comey had spent years as a public Trump critic and after Trump had spent years fixated on the man he fired and repeatedly discussed. Is it brought by career prosecutors with no political stake? The pattern of cases tracked in this series has consistently involved prosecutor and venue selections that depart from normal departmental practice. Does it rest on the defendant’s own words and actions? In the narrowest technical sense: Comey did post the image. But the interpretive leap required to read seashell typography as a federal threat is not the kind of leap a career prosecutor builds a career on. Has it produced a plea? No.

Apply the same test to the D.C. criminal information. The alleged conduct is a decade old. By the account of Comey’s own lawyers — and, importantly, by their assertion that the matter was previously investigated — there was already a moment when the government looked at this material and decided not to charge it. The decision to charge it now, in a filing made in October 2026 and surfacing eight days before midterm elections in which the administration’s prosecutorial record is itself a political issue, is a timing that strains the case for coincidence. And again: Comey is not pleading.

The Floris and Benavides case fails the test faster still, and this time the failure comes from the government’s own witness. It postdates the grievance by a single day. It was directed by the FBI director personally, which is unprecedented in the career of the veteran agent who worked it. It came to rest on the defendants’ own words only after the original allegations produced nothing. And there is no plea.

There is a dimension to this campaign that rarely receives direct discussion.

Pamela Bondi, who served as Attorney General, was removed earlier this year. Among the reasons circulated for her departure — through the customary machinery of leaks and complaints — was that she had been insufficiently aggressive in pursuing the targets on the administration’s list. The list is long. It includes people in this series and people not yet in it. Bondi’s removal was, in part, a rebuke for something that looked to her critics inside the administration like excessive restraint.

Her successor has shown no comparable inclination toward restraint. Neither, on the evidence of sworn testimony in a Washington courtroom, has the FBI director, who by one veteran agent’s account acted within a day on a grievance the president had aired on prime-time television.

And then there is Maurene Comey, James Comey’s daughter, who was a career federal prosecutor at the Southern District of New York until she was dismissed. She is now pursuing a wrongful termination claim. The reach of the campaign against her father has extended, in practice, to the professional life of a public servant who chose to work in federal law enforcement — and who worked in an office that has at various times brought cases against people close to the administration.

The logic of the list, taken to its conclusion, is not merely that the targets themselves must be pursued. It is that the people around them must understand the cost of proximity.

Comey’s response to this logic has been consistent across all three cases.

In November 2025, when the Virginia case was dismissed, he did not declare victory so much as observe that he had expected no less. After the April 2026 seashells indictment, he held a press conference. After the October 2026 criminal information, he posted a video. The substance has been the same each time: I am innocent, I am not afraid, this is an abuse, and we must not stop seeing it as one.

“One that we cannot become numb to” is the phrase worth holding onto. The concern embedded in it is not really about any individual case — neither the seashells trial nor the D.C. criminal information is likely to be remembered as a great prosecutorial success, and both face serious legal challenges. The concern is structural: that the accumulation of cases, across years and jurisdictions and charging instruments, normalises the proposition that a federal law enforcement apparatus can be deployed against private citizens for extended political purposes. That the question the public asks stops being “will this case succeed?” and becomes “which case is this again?”

The Department of Justice has now brought three separate prosecutions against a single former FBI director. The first was dismissed for improper appointment of a special prosecutor. The second charged him with arranging beach debris in a manner the government found threatening. The third reaches back a decade to conduct that was investigated and not charged the first time around. In each instance, Comey has said he is innocent and will fight. In each instance, the government has continued.

What changes is not the legal outcome, which in each case has either already gone badly for the government or shows signs of going badly. What changes, if the cases run long enough, is the atmosphere in which they operate — the degree to which this begins to seem, to some portion of the public, simply normal. Simply the way things are done now.

The list has a mirror image. On Monday, the eve of the vote, Trump told the New York Post he would pre-emptively pardon aides and allies, Elon Musk among them, if Democrats won Congress and opened investigations, as The New York Times reported. “I would not even think about it. I’d do it,” he said, citing his January 6 clemency as precedent. Prosecution for the people on the list, protection in advance for the people around the president: both halves of the machine run on the same principle, that whether the criminal law applies depends on whose side you are on.

“We cannot become numb to this,” Comey said.

He is right that it is a choice. The mechanism of normalisation does not announce itself. It operates through repetition, through the gradual conversion of the extraordinary into the routine. At some point the question is not whether these cases have merit — they demonstrably do not, or at least not enough to have survived scrutiny so far — but whether the people asking that question still number enough to matter politically. Americans vote in the midterm elections today, November 3, 2026, and that vote is, among other things, a referendum on that question.

This series began tracking these prosecutions because they represented a departure from a norm: the use of the criminal law for personal and political targeting, dressed in the language of accountability. The departure has not narrowed. It has widened, and added cases, and now runs two tracks simultaneously against its most prominent original target. Whether it represents the country’s future or a correction awaiting correction is, at this writing, still being decided.
