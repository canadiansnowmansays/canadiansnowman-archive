There was a scene playing out in Washington today that would be darkly comic if the stakes weren’t so high.

Canada’s trade minister, Dominic LeBlanc, flew in for the day. His goal — modest by any standard of serious diplomacy — was simply to leave with a date. Not a deal. Not a framework. Not a joint communiqué. Just an agreement that Canada and the United States will sit down at some point in the near future to formally discuss the renewal of the trade agreement that has governed $2.3 trillion in annual commerce between the two countries.

While LeBlanc was boarding his plane this morning, the man he needed to negotiate with posted two words about Canada on Truth Social: “51st State!”

Welcome to Part XIII of The Great Canadian Trade War. The number feels appropriate.

The Recession Gift

Let’s deal with the inconvenient mathematics first, because Trump’s post wasn’t pure trolling. He linked to a Bloomberg article reporting something real: Canada is in a technical recession.

Statistics Canada confirmed it last Friday. Real GDP fell 0.1 per cent on an annualized basis in Q1 2026, following a revised 1 per cent annualized decline in Q4 2025. Two consecutive quarters of negative growth. By the most common definition, that’s a recession — Canada’s first in years.

The numbers deserve honest accounting. Business capital investment fell 0.7 per cent in Q1, its fifth consecutive quarterly decline. TD Bank economist Marc Ercolao attributed the investment freeze largely to “uncertainty surrounding U.S. tariffs” — businesses unable to draw up spending plans without knowing what the trading relationship would look like going forward. Three of the last four quarters have posted negative real GDP growth. The Bank of Canada has held its benchmark rate at 2.25 per cent through four straight meetings, waiting for clarity on U.S. trade and the Iran war before moving.

The good news, such as it is: Capital Economics’ Bradley Saunders said the “trade-induced” technical recession is likely already over, with April showing a sharp rebound of 0.4 per cent in the mining, oil and gas sectors. Rising oil prices tied to the Iran war are actually supporting Canada’s near-term numbers.

But Trump doesn’t deal in nuance or leading indicators. He deals in ammunition. And “Canada is in recession” is exactly the kind of headline he picks up and fires across the bow of a trade delegation heading to his capital city. The “51st State!” post landed hours before LeBlanc touched down. The timing was not accidental.

There is a phrase for this in negotiation theory: softening the opponent before the meeting begins.

Dominic LeBlanc, the minister for Canada-US trade, is in Washington today for trade talks.

When LeBlanc sat down with U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer today, the asymmetry of the situation was visible in what each man did and didn’t have.

The United States and Mexico have already begun formal negotiations. USTR’s office has published a schedule of talks extending well into July. Mexico has dates, rooms, and agendas. Canada has silence. The USMCA review — which officially opens July 1 under the agreement’s own terms — is less than a month away, and Canada has not had a single formal session scheduled.

LeBlanc arrived in Washington with a letter proposing that Canada would like to see the USMCA renewed for another 16 years. The Trump administration’s response, delivered not in the letter but in years of rhetoric, is that the deal has been “overly advantageous for Canada at the expense of the United States” — a remarkable position given that the man making that argument, Greer himself, was one of the deal’s architects. And given that Trump, upon signing the same deal in January 2020, called it “the fairest, most balanced, and beneficial trade agreement we have ever signed into law.”

The math of the tariff damage is not abstract. U.S. sectoral tariffs on autos, aluminum, steel and lumber are carving real wounds into specific pockets of the Canadian economy. The business investment freeze is the invisible tariff: the cost of uncertainty that doesn’t show up in any tariff schedule but does show up in five consecutive quarters of declining capital spending.

Going in, a good outcome by Washington’s own definition of “good” was LeBlanc getting a date for formal talks. Not a deal. A date.

Meanwhile, in Brazil

Here is the thing about negotiating with a man who never gives up: he is always negotiating with someone else at the same time.

On Monday — the same day Trump posted “51st State!” about Canada — his Trade Representative proposed 25% tariffs on Brazilian goods under Section 301, citing practices that “are unreasonable and burden or restrict U.S. commerce.” The official list of complaints includes anti-corruption enforcement, intellectual property protection, ethanol market access and illegal deforestation.

These are legitimate trade grievances on some dimensions. But to understand what’s really happening, you need the full Brazil story — because it is a case study in how this administration actually operates.

In July 2025, Trump imposed 50% tariffs on Brazil. Not primarily for trade reasons. Because Jair Bolsonaro — Trump’s political ally, the “Trump of the Tropics,” a man who had plotted to overturn his own 2022 election loss — was on trial in Brazilian courts. Trump called the prosecution a “witch hunt,” posted on Truth Social that it was “an international disgrace,” and reached for the one tool he always reaches for: tariffs.

The U.S. ran a $6.8 billion trade surplus with Brazil at the time. There was no trade deficit to correct. There was no unfair trade practice to address. There was a friend in legal trouble.

The Supreme Court struck those tariffs down in February 2026. So Trump regrouped. He found new legal architecture — Section 301 — and new justifications. The 25% tariffs proposed Monday are dressed in the language of trade compliance. The hearing is set for July 6. But the underlying message is unchanged: cross this administration, and you will pay.

This is the man LeBlanc went to Washington to negotiate with.

Not a trade theorist. Not a dealmaker in the Art of the Deal sense. A man who imposed tariffs on a country because he didn’t like its court system. A man whose USTR was publicly advocating for the USMCA he helped design while privately telling Congress it was a bad deal for America. A man who calls his greatest trade achievement “overly advantageous” to the country that agreed to it.

The Brazil file is instructive for one reason above all: he came back. The courts stopped him in February. By June, he had a new instrument and a new rationale. The goal didn’t change. The method did.

The July Collision

Readers of this series will remember where we left things in Part XII: the convergence of three deadlines, all pointing at July like three fuses lit from different ends of the same room.

The first is the USMCA review itself, which opens July 1. The second was the Section 122 tariff bridge that Trump constructed after SCOTUS struck down his IEEPA authority — set to expire July 24. The third was the Section 301 forced labour investigations launched on March 13 against Canada and 60 other economies, targeted to conclude before that expiry date.

The Brazil news adds a fourth element: a living demonstration, delivered this week, that Trump treats trade law as a toolkit rather than a framework. When one instrument is taken away, he picks up another. When courts constrain him, he finds new statutory authority. When a 50% tariff gets struck down, a 25% tariff emerges with different paperwork.

Canada’s challenge in July is not purely economic. It is structural: how do you renew a trade agreement with a counterparty who doesn’t believe in the concept of agreements?

Carney’s New York speech last week struck a carefully conciliatory note — the argument that a stronger, more independent Canada would be a better partner for the United States, not a threat to it. That framing is strategically sound. It gives Trump a narrative in which helping Canada helps America. It avoids the confrontational register that provoked the 100% tariff threat after Carney’s Davos speech in January.

But it requires the other side to be listening. And today, the other side posted “51st State!” two hours before the trade minister landed.

What LeBlanc Is Actually Asking For

Strip away the diplomatic language and LeBlanc’s formal ask is almost poignant in its modesty.

The letter he delivered to USTR on Tuesday formally requests that the USMCA be renewed for another 16 years, calling the agreement “highly beneficial” to all three countries and noting Canada’s willingness to consider any proposal “beneficial to all three nations’ long-term prosperity.” It also contains a sentence that does real work: “discussions with the United States on addressing sectoral tariffs will be essential.” That’s diplomatic language for we cannot sign anything while you still have tariffs on our steel, aluminum, cars and lumber.

Carney, speaking to reporters Tuesday, said Canadian-made cars already contain roughly 50% American content on average — pre-empting one of Greer’s stated demands before negotiations have even formally begun.

All of that is reasonable. All of it is measured. None of it explains why Canada is so far behind Mexico in the queue.

Greer explained it himself, last week, with unusual candour: “Two countries in the world retaliated against us: The People’s Republic of China and Canada. So they’re just in a different spot, and it’s hard to see necessarily where that ends.”

There it is. Canada chose to fight back — retaliatory tariffs, province-level American alcohol boycotts, a prime minister who told Davos the American era was over — and the price of that choice is a seat at the back of the negotiating line. Mexico accommodated. Canada resisted. The TACO framework plays out with brutal consistency.

The conservative opposition in Ottawa is already circling. “What is the plan?” asked Conservative MP Jasraj Singh Hallan on Tuesday, calling Carney a “grand illusionist” who has failed to deliver on economic growth. The domestic pressure is building from both directions: Trump won’t schedule talks, and the opposition won’t stop asking why.

Meanwhile, Greer — the same man LeBlanc met Tuesday — just proposed 25% tariffs on Brazil using the same Section 301 mechanism currently pointed at Canada from the March investigations. The instrument, the legal structure, the timeline: it’s all the same architecture. The only variable is which country’s name appears in the filing.

The Arithmetic Hasn’t Changed

The series has always returned to one theme: the math always wins. Eventually. Sometimes it takes a year to count the damage.

Here’s where the ledger stands, roughly 16 months into The Great Canadian Trade War:

Canada’s economy is in technical recession, with business investment in five straight quarters of decline — explicitly linked by economists to tariff uncertainty. The USMCA review opens in 29 days with no formal Canada-U.S. negotiating schedule in place. If Canada, the U.S. and Mexico cannot agree to extend the USMCA by July 1, the pact reverts to annual renewal until 2036 — a cliff edge that rewards the side with less urgency, which is not Canada. The Section 301 forced labour investigation targeting Canada is on course to conclude before July 24. The tariffs on autos, aluminum, steel and lumber continue. The “51st State!” posts continue.

And the man Canada needs to negotiate with just demonstrated, again, in Brazil, that when courts take one tariff weapon away, he builds another one.

Dominic LeBlanc went to Washington today asking for a date.

He brought Canada’s Chief Trade Negotiator, Janice Charette. It was a same-day trip — fly in, meet, fly home to Ottawa. No joint readout was issued. No schedule announced. No date confirmed. Greer left for France.

Mexico, at this same moment, has bilateral rounds scheduled through July: May 28-29 in Mexico City, June 16-17 in Washington, more to follow. Room bookings. Agendas. Negotiators with calendars.

Canada has a same-day trip and a silence.

There is a word for what LeBlanc brought home from Washington. Diplomats call it “continued dialogue.” The rest of us call it nothing.

Twenty-nine days to July 1. The review clock is running. The Section 301 investigations are running. The sectoral tariffs are running. The “51st State!” posts are running.

And the man who tariffed Brazil twice — once for having a court system he didn’t like, and again after the Supreme Court took the first tariff away — just sent Canada’s trade minister home without even the courtesy of a meeting date.

Laing called it. LeBlanc understood it. The math is doing what it always does.

Adding up, in silence.
