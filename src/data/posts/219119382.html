In August 1971, a corporate lawyer in Richmond, Virginia, wrote a confidential memorandum for a friend at the United States Chamber of Commerce. Its title was “Attack on American Free Enterprise System.” The lawyer, Lewis F. Powell Jr., argued that business was losing the battle of ideas to consumer advocates, environmentalists and campus radicals, and that it had only itself to blame. He urged business to fund scholars, to monitor textbooks and television, to cultivate the courts, and to organise politically, using all the money and influence at its disposal. Two months later Nixon nominated Powell to the Supreme Court, where he served until 1987.

The Powell memo has been credited, by admirers and critics alike, with more than any single document can fairly claim. But its timing is hard to ignore. The Heritage Foundation was founded in 1973. The American Legislative Exchange Council, which writes model bills for state legislatures, was formed in the same year. The Federalist Society, which would come to shape the selection of a generation of federal judges, was founded by law students in 1982. Within a decade of Powell’s memo, the institutions he called for existed, and they have been at work ever since.

This part of the series is about where the reversals in the Reversal Ledger came from. Its answer is that most of them did not come from the president.

Who wrote the ledger

For each of its 128 items, the ledger records where the idea originated. The categories are rough, and some items could fit more than one, but the totals are clear.

The president and his appointees account for the largest single group, fifty items, most of them the tariffs, renamings, firings, treaty withdrawals and pardons that need no blueprint. Industry requests account for thirty. Project 2025 accounts for twenty-seven. The conservative legal movement, working through decades of litigation and scholarship, accounts for eleven, and Republicans in Congress, mostly through the 2025 budget law and the rescission bills, for ten.

That means seventy-eight of the reversals, three in every five, were drafted by someone other than the man who signed them.

The blueprint

Project 2025 was published in 2023 as Mandate for Leadership: The Conservative Promise. Its title echoed the Heritage Foundation’s first Mandate for Leadership, published in 1981, which the incoming Reagan administration adopted in large part. The 2025 version was longer and more specific. It proposed agency by agency what a conservative president should do, and it was accompanied by a personnel database and training programme for the people who would do it.

During the 2024 campaign, Trump said he knew nothing about it and disowned some of its proposals. After the election, his administration hired many of its authors. The ledger items that trace to it read like its table of contents. Schedule F, which became Schedule Policy/Career, stripping civil service protection from thousands of officials. The dismantling of the Department of Education. The closure of the EPA’s Office of Research and Development. The abandonment of the National Climate Assessment. The folding of USAID into the State Department. The repeal of the greenhouse gas endangerment finding. The binary definition of sex across the federal government. The restoration of the Mexico City Policy. The stopping of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s funding. The reopening of the Arctic to oil leasing.

By the first anniversary of the inauguration, according to a tracker kept by the Center for Progressive Reform, more than half of the blueprint’s domestic recommendations were under way or done. Heritage itself, as the introduction noted, has boasted to its donors of the same proportion.

The wish list

The industry requests are the most direct, and the documents that record them are sometimes startlingly plain.

The coal letter described in the introduction is the plainest example: a trade group asked the Energy Secretary in March 2025 to use an emergency power to keep old coal plants running, and two months later the department started doing it. When the EPA’s administrator, Lee Zeldin, later told coal executives that “many of the items that were on your wish list are now done,” he was describing a method, not a single favour.

The pattern repeats across the ledger. Banks and credit card companies sued to stop the late-fee cap and the medical-debt rule, and the bureau stopped defending them. Business groups sued to stop the noncompete ban, and the commission stopped defending it. Automakers lobbied against the fuel economy and emission standards, and the standards were cut and the fines removed. Mining interests lobbied to reopen the land near the Boundary Waters and around the shrunken monuments in Utah, and Congress and the president obliged. Utilities and fossil fuel companies opposed the power-plant rules, the mercury standards and the endangerment finding, and all three were repealed.

None of this is secret, and none of it is unusual in kind. Industries have always lobbied the government, under every administration. What distinguishes this period is the completeness of the response. In most of the ledger’s thirty industry items, the government’s eventual action matched the industry’s request almost exactly.

The long game

The eleven items from the legal movement are fewer, but they include the firmest locks in the ledger, and they took the longest to build.

Loper Bright, which ended judicial deference to agencies, and Slaughter, which ended the independence of regulatory commissions, were the culmination of arguments developed in law reviews, conferences and Federalist Society chapters since the 1980s. The theory behind Slaughter, sometimes called the unitary executive, was a minority view among legal scholars when it was first advanced in the Reagan Justice Department. Forty years of judicial appointments made it the law. The Title IX and Affordable Care Act rules were brought down by lawsuits filed in courts the challengers chose, before judges they expected to agree with them. The removal of the environmental review regulations rested on years of litigation arguing that the council issuing them never had the authority to do so.

This is the patience that Powell asked for in 1971. It produced, over half a century, a Supreme Court majority, a bench of appellate judges, a corps of trained lawyers and a set of legal theories ready to deploy the moment an administration wanted to use them.

The pattern that matters

Part V found that the reversals the courts stopped were disproportionately the president’s own. The ledger bears that out. Of the twelve items now stopped outright, six were his initiatives. Of the sixteen locked in at the bottom tier, six were, mostly treaty withdrawals and pardons that need no legal theory. But the reversals that survived court challenges, and that will be hardest to undo, were overwhelmingly the prepared ones: the civil service rule, the regulatory repeals, the legal doctrines.

The lesson is not that the president is weak. It is that impulse is a poor instrument of lasting change, and preparation is a very good one. The improvised reversals, announced at rallies and drafted in days, lost in court because no one had built the legal case for them. The prepared reversals had their cases built years in advance, often before the president who carried them out had decided to run.

A word in fairness

It would be wrong to present this as a conspiracy, and the ledger does not support that reading.

Everything described here was done in public. The Powell memo was leaked within a year of being written. Project 2025 was published, at great length, a year and a half before the election. The Federalist Society holds open conferences. The coal industry’s letter was obtained through a freedom of information request. The other side of American politics has its own institutions, its own think tanks, its own legal organisations and its own industry allies, and when it held power it used them too. The difference is not that one side organised and the other did not. It is that one side spent fifty years building for a moment like this one, and the other spent much of that time assuming the pendulum would do the work.

What the other side can learn

The final part of this series will set out what a serious restoration would require. This part supplies one of its main conclusions in advance.

If the reversals that lasted were those prepared years ahead, so will be any restoration that lasts. A future administration that arrives in January 2029 with a list of executive orders and good intentions will reverse the first tier and little else. One that arrives with drafted rules grounded in clear statutory language, trained appointees ready on the first day, a legal strategy for the courts it will face, legislation ready for a narrow window of congressional control, and constituencies organised to defend what it builds, might reverse a great deal more.

In Poe’s story, the blade was built by the Inquisition, and the Inquisition had all the time in the world. The prisoner had only his wits and an afternoon. That asymmetry is the subject of this part. The people who built the reversals worked as if they had all the time in the world, because in a sense they did. The people who want to undo them have been working as if they had an afternoon.

The next part looks more closely at what was undone, and at a fact the ledger revealed that this series did not expect: how much of it was never a Democratic agenda at all.
