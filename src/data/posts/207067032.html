Stephen Gordon July 22, 2026

Part II left off with a theory: that Blanche believes, and has argued to the Supreme Court, that a president is largely shielded from accountability for official acts, and that it is the president’s right and duty to direct the department’s investigative priorities. A theory is only worth this much space if you can watch it operate. This week gave us two fresh ways to watch it — one aimed at the president’s critics, one aimed at the press that covers him.

Under this administration, the Justice Department indicted former FBI Director James Comey on charges that career prosecutors had previously reviewed and declined to bring — a case built, by most outside accounts, on theories of the statute that prosecutors inside the building had already rejected once. It indicted the Southern Poverty Law Center, a left-leaning anti-extremism group, alleging its practice of paying informants inside far-right organizations amounted to donor fraud — a case former prosecutors interviewed by The Atlantic’s Quinta Jurecic described as dubious in multiple directions at once. Both cases share a signature: stretching the law itself to fit a predetermined target, rather than the older, more familiar Washington sin of stretching facts to fit an existing law. That distinction matters more than it sounds like it should. A department willing to invent a new reading of a statute to reach a president’s enemy is also, by the same instinct, a department capable of declining to read an existing statute at all when the subject is a federal agent the president would rather not see prosecuted. It is the same muscle, flexed in opposite directions.

Inside the Retribution Machine

Comey and SPLC are the prosecutions that became public. This week, 352 pages of internal Justice Department emails — obtained by the watchdog group American Oversight through a public records request and shared with the New York Times just before Blanche’s hearing — show the machinery underneath, and it is more organized, and more personally supervised by Blanche, than the public record had previously shown.

The emails cover Blanche’s stewardship of the department’s “weaponization working group,” created to carry out Trump’s Day 1 executive order on correcting what the administration calls the prior weaponization of law enforcement. Blanche did not delegate this and walk away. He built an organizational chart, assigning specific lawyers in his own office to specific investigative lanes, which had the effect of keeping the most politically sensitive work under his own direct line of sight. One lane investigated potential wrongdoing by special counsel Jack Smith, staffed by a former clerk to Judge Aileen Cannon. Another investigated alleged “coordination” between federal prosecutors and Manhattan DA Alvin Bragg, staffed by a longtime Blanche associate who had served on Trump’s own criminal defense team — she later recused herself, citing an unspecified conflict, and left the department. Other lanes pursued an FBI memo that had flagged a conservative Catholic group in Richmond, scrutiny of conservative school-board members, and a review of Biden-era FACE Act prosecutions of anti-abortion activists. One team was assigned to a single person: Tina Peters, a pro-Trump Colorado elections official convicted of state crimes and later granted clemency by the state’s Democratic governor. Another investigated President Biden’s use of an autopen.

The most interesting figure in these emails, though, is not Blanche. It is Ed Martin — a former Jan. 6 defense lawyer with no real prosecutorial experience, installed by the White House to help run this same task force, whom Blanche visibly did not trust and moved quickly to contain. “I am frustrated,” Blanche wrote to Martin less than a month into their working relationship, and the tone only sharpened from there: “This is becoming a real problem, Ed,” he wrote a few weeks later, accusing Martin of discussing grand jury material publicly for the fourth time and of letting an inflated job title go out to a reporter uncorrected. Blanche scheduled weekly check-ins to keep Martin on a leash, and in early 2026 removed him from the weaponization committee entirely, exiling him to a satellite office across town.

I want to give this its due, because fairness requires it and because it complicates the flattest version of the argument this series is making. Blanche’s defenders point to exactly this record as evidence he has been a quiet, important check on the administration’s more openly zealous actors — reining in Martin, slowing down some of the wilder impulses coming from the West Wing. There is something to that. A methodical former prosecutor keeping an inexperienced provocateur on a short leash is not nothing, and it is a genuinely different disposition than Martin’s own, which by all accounts treated senior officials’ instructions as suggestions.

But notice what that defense actually concedes: not that the retribution campaign didn’t happen, only that Blanche ran it more competently and with tighter personal control than a less disciplined lieutenant would have. Whether the investigative targets were chosen fairly is a separate question from whether the person managing them was organized about it, and the emails answer only the second question. Chioma Chukwu, American Oversight’s executive director, made the sharper point when the documents were released: senators should judge Blanche not by what he says at his hearing, but by the record he has already built. That record includes a team assigned to Jack Smith, a team assigned to Alvin Bragg, a team assigned to Tina Peters, and a team assigned to Biden’s autopen — and, so far, more than eighteen months in, no comparable team ever assigned to figuring out who killed Renee Good.

The War on the Press

That muscle has a third target, and it is worth naming plainly, because it goes to the heart of whether this department can be trusted with the discretion it holds. Over the past several months, Blanche’s Justice Department has built a pattern of using subpoenas and search warrants against journalists whose reporting has embarrassed the president, and Blanche has not merely allowed it — he has defended it publicly, on the record.

In January, while Blanche was still deputy attorney general, the FBI raided the home of a Washington Post reporter, searching for evidence in a leak investigation involving a government contractor. Two separate judges later ruled the government could not search the seized data and criticized the department’s approach in doing so. In May, DOJ subpoenaed Wall Street Journal reporters over their sourcing on the planning of Trump’s Iran war, and a second Post reporter over coverage of the Venezuela operation; those subpoenas were quietly withdrawn in June, after the fact of having been issued had already done its work. Separately, FBI Director Kash Patel has sued The Atlantic and one of its reporters over coverage of his own leadership at the bureau, and reportedly opened a criminal investigation into her as well.

The most recent episode is the clearest yet. After Air Force One’s newer, Qatari-gifted aircraft was left behind for a trip home from Turkey because it lacked adequate defensive countermeasures, the New York Times reported the reason why. Trump’s response, according to CNN’s reporting, was to write a single word — Treason — in marker on a stack of the offending articles and hand it to Blanche. Days later, DOJ issued subpoenas to four Times reporters, sending federal agents to some of their homes, seeking to compel testimony about how they learned of the security gap.

A department spokesperson’s line was that reporters are not the targets, only the people leaking classified information are. That is a distinction without a difference for anyone actually facing a subpoena: reporters who protect their sources risk fines or jail; reporters who don’t will find it far harder to report anything sensitive again. Blanche did not merely execute this campaign quietly. He defended it in public, framing the prosecutions as protecting soldiers’ lives and national secrets — rhetoric that sits uneasily next to a factual record showing the trigger, in the most recent case, was not a security breach but an article the president found embarrassing. It is difficult to square a stated devotion to protecting classified information with a track record where the specific leaks pursued are consistently the ones that make the president look bad, and not, as best as public reporting shows, the ones that put anyone in actual danger.

This is the same theory of the office running through everything else in this piece, aimed now at the institution meant to hold all the others accountable. A Justice Department willing to subpoena reporters’ phone records over an embarrassing headline is not a department that needs to be told to go easy on an ICE agent whose account doesn’t survive contact with video. It already knows which stories it wants told, and which ones it would rather nobody kept asking about.

The Librarian of Congress, Also

There is one more episode worth including here, not because it touches Minneapolis directly but because it tells you something about how this administration treats institutional guardrails generally, and Blanche’s willingness to occupy whatever seat he is handed. In May 2025, Trump fired Carla Hayden as Librarian of Congress and named Blanche her successor — while Blanche continued serving as deputy attorney general. The Library of Congress is a legislative-branch institution; its librarian has historically been nominated by the president and confirmed by the Senate, a process Trump’s move bypassed entirely. Hayden’s own staff, along with the library’s principal deputy librarian, Robert Newlen, disputed the appointment’s legality outright, arguing Newlen was the lawful successor under existing statute. The dispute was never cleanly resolved. Blanche simply held the title, disputed as it was, on top of his actual job.

I raise this not as a digression but as a data point in the same pattern this whole series is tracking: an administration that treats institutional lines — between branches, between agencies, between what a law says and what an official simply decides to do — as suggestions to be tested rather than boundaries to be respected. A deputy attorney general moonlighting as a legislative librarian whose own staff considers the appointment illegitimate is a small, almost comic version of the same instinct that produces a Justice Department unwilling to hand a state prosecutor a dead woman’s car.

Two Days on the Record

Blanche’s confirmation hearing ran two full days this week — his own testimony on Wednesday, an outside witness panel on Thursday — and it is worth walking through what actually happened rather than what anyone expected to happen, because several things landed differently than the run-up suggested they would.

The toughest questioning of the entire hearing came not from a Democrat but from Sen. John Cornyn of Texas, a lame-duck Republican with nothing left to lose politically. Cornyn spent his time methodically, almost professorially, taking apart the mechanics of the IRS settlement: Blanche’s insistence that the $1.776 billion fund was “dead,” set against the plain fact that neither Blanche nor Trump had put that in writing anywhere Trump himself couldn’t later contest it; the settlement’s release language, which reaches well past the named plaintiffs to a vaguely defined class of “affiliated individuals”; and the fact that Blanche, by his own admission, never discussed the deal with Trump until after it was already decided, which raised more questions about who actually negotiated it than it answered. Cornyn got Blanche to concede, under careful questioning, that Trump’s lawyers could still theoretically sue to enforce the settlement’s terms — meaning the fund Blanche calls dead is, legally, only dormant.

Sen. Sheldon Whitehouse spent his time on something different: the accumulating pattern of federal judges, across the political spectrum, criticizing this Justice Department’s conduct in specific, sharp language. He read some of it into the record and asked Blanche directly whether he intended to keep tolerating FBI Director Kash Patel’s conduct; Blanche called the question “extraordinarily obnoxious” and moved on without really answering it. Sen. Cory Booker pressed Blanche on the arrest of Newark Mayor Ras Baraka at Delaney Hall — detailed in Part IX — and on his July 2025 meeting with Ghislaine Maxwell, which preceded her transfer to a lower-security facility and which Blanche confirmed produced no new charges and no new investigations of anyone else. When Booker pushed on whether that looked like favorable treatment, Blanche told him, “you can ask the questions, but you cannot control my answers.”

Sen. Adam Schiff asked the sharpest personal question of the hearing, and it is worth quoting the shape of it rather than the words: what happened, he asked, to the prosecutor people in the Southern District of New York used to respect — the version of Blanche that existed before he became the president’s lawyer? Blanche, visibly rattled, insisted he was “the same exact person,” that his standard had never changed: enforce the law, do the right thing, put bad guys in jail. It was the most defensive he looked all day, and it came in response to the one question that wasn’t really about a policy or a case file at all.

And when Sen. Mazie Hirono asked him directly, again, whether he believed it was the president’s right and duty to order investigations of his perceived enemies, Blanche declined to answer yes or no — but fell back, as he has before, on Article II: the president runs the executive branch, and his answer, he said, “reflects that idea.” That is Part II’s whole argument, restated by the man himself, under oath, when given the plainest possible opportunity to disavow it.

Where It Stands

Thursday’s outside witness panel added a complication nobody had fully priced in. Former Attorney General John Ashcroft testified in support. An Epstein survivor also testified, and her account appears to have moved at least one vote: Sen. Thom Tillis, who ended Wednesday’s hearing telling Blanche “you’ve done a great job today,” said afterward that his support now depends on Blanche personally meeting with survivors — a new condition, not yet met as of this writing. Tillis is not the hard no he once looked like he might be. He is also not the settled yes his Wednesday remarks suggested.

Cornyn remains the genuine question mark. Asked after the hearing whether he’d decided, he said only, “I don’t have to make a decision now, so I’m not.” That is the most honest sentence anyone produced all week. The committee seat left vacant by Sen. Lindsey Graham’s death is still unfilled in any way that affects the math, and no vote has been scheduled.

So the hearing is over, and nothing is resolved. That is not a failure of reporting. It is the actual state of the record: two days of testimony, one senator who took the settlement apart in public and still won’t say how he’ll vote, a second whose support now has a condition attached that hasn’t been satisfied, and a nomination that remains exactly as uncertain as it was the morning this started — except that everyone involved has now said so, under oath and on the record, in their own words.

Update, July 28: Two things have moved since this piece first ran, and both belong here rather than in a separate post, since they concern the same subpoenas covered above.

First, DOJ quietly withdrew the Times subpoenas last week — not voluntarily in any meaningful sense, but after a federal judge raised the same concerns Democrats had already been pressing: whether prosecutors had actually followed the department’s own regulations governing subpoenas to journalists.

Second, Senate Judiciary Democrats, led by Sen. Adam Schiff, sent Blanche a formal letter Monday demanding answers, and the specifics in it are worse than anything this piece had on the record when it first ran. The department waited more than a week to notify the Times it had subpoenaed its reporters’ phone records, in apparent violation of its own “reasonable and timely notice” guidance. The records demanded reached back to January 1 — months before the Air Force One story even published, which is difficult to square with a rule limiting these subpoenas to “essential” information tied to an actual investigation. And the subpoenas were not confined to the reporters themselves: DOJ also sought the phone records of one reporter’s mother and two reporters’ spouses. Schiff’s letter asks directly whether Trump or any White House aide ordered the subpoenas in the first place — the question this whole series keeps arriving at from different directions.

Neither Cornyn nor Tillis has cited the subpoenas specifically as a reason for their hesitation. But the Judiciary Committee’s vote on advancing Blanche’s nomination is now scheduled for Thursday, July 30 — a firm date this piece did not have when it first ran. Whatever happens that day, it happens against a backdrop where the department has already had to unwind, under judicial pressure, conduct it was still defending on the record two weeks ago.
