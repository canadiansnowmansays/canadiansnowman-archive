A war that costs a billion dollars every few days is, among other things, an accounting exercise. Someone has to say how much, for what, and why. In the United States that someone is the Secretary of Defense, and the audience is Congress, which under Article I of the Constitution holds the power of the purse and has guarded it, with varying vigour, since 1789.

Pete Hegseth’s relationship with that power is the subject of this instalment. It can be summarised in one number: seven, the length in pages of his request for $67 billion.

The Performance

At Quantico, Hegseth called the Iran war a courageous operation “that every red-blooded American is proud of,” then announced six new initiatives, from a four-star drone command to a fifty-state competition for a new base.

He barely mentioned what any of it would cost, or who would pay. Greg Jaffe of the New York Times, in his September 30, 2026 analysis “Hegseth Talks Tough as Republican Doubts About His Leadership Grow,” observed that the Secretary scarcely spoke of the $1.5 trillion defence budget the administration had sent to Congress, a budget even Republicans concede has little chance of passing in its current form.

The omission is the performance. A man who announces a new combatant command, a new mega-base and a reorganisation of the energy supply of the entire American military is a man who has assumed that the money will arrive. On the evidence of the past year, it will not, and he has done remarkably little to change that.

The Paperwork

The request. On July 21, 2026, Hegseth told the Senate Appropriations Committee that the Iran war had cost the Department of Defense $37.5 billion so far, and asked for $67.1 billion more in emergency supplemental funding. As Patricia Kime reported for Military Times in “Cost of Iran war rises to $37.5 billion, Pentagon requests $67.1 billion more,” only about $32.7 billion of that was tied directly to Iran operations. The rest went to wider priorities: $21 billion for munitions, $12.1 billion for classified programmes, $5.1 billion for cybersecurity.

Senator Patty Murray, the committee’s senior Democrat, said Congress would not “rubber stamp a request to bankroll this war.” That might have been dismissed as partisan positioning had Republicans not been saying much the same thing in the corridors.

The seven pages. The request ran to seven pages. For comparison, the supplemental requests for the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan typically ran past one hundred, with line-by-line accounting of what the money would buy. A war supplemental is not a wedding invitation. It is meant to be read.

As the office of Senator Michael Bennet set out on September 25, 2026, in “Bennet, Slotkin, Colleagues Demand Answers from Hegseth on Cost of Iran War,” 45 Democratic senators wrote to Hegseth asking for a basic breakdown of direct and indirect war costs, a full accounting of the $67 billion request, and an explanation of how $34 billion left over from the 2025 reconciliation bill would be spent. The letter called the supplemental a thin document with only vague descriptions. Its authors also counted briefings: by their tally, Hegseth had briefed Congress seven times in six months of war, against more than twenty times in the first six months of the Iraq war under Donald Rumsfeld, who was not known for his love of congressional oversight.

There is a small, grim joke in the fact that the senators’ own letter, signed on September 17, 2026, was itself seven pages long. The Secretary asked for $67 billion in seven pages. The Senate needed the same number of pages to ask what it was for.

The running total. The war’s cost has kept climbing in the meantime. The Pentagon told Congress in September that it had passed $42 billion. The Washington Sun (formerly NOTUS) reported on September 30, 2026, in “Hegseth Is Cutting the Number of Generals and Admirals by 20%,” that U.S. Central Command put the figure at at least $43.6 billion through early September. Semafor reported in late September that Hegseth now seeks an additional $88 billion to cover the war and the restocking of weapons, on top of the $1.5 trillion.

The big number. The $1.5 trillion request for fiscal 2027 is about 40 percent more than the previous year’s budget. As Greg Hadley reported for Air & Space Forces Magazine on September 1, 2026, in “Congress Passes Short-Term Funding, Putting DOD’s $1.5T Budget on Hold,” the White House split the request in two: $1.15 trillion through ordinary appropriations and $350 billion through budget reconciliation. The House advanced $1.07 trillion in base defence spending. The Senate has not agreed a topline. Neither chamber has made meaningful progress on the reconciliation half.

What Congress did pass was a continuing resolution, a stopgap that keeps the government open at the previous year’s spending levels. The House approved it 370 to 48. It runs to December 11, 2026. Under it, by Hadley’s count, the Pentagon operates on about $838.5 billion, some 44 percent below what it asked for. (The Times put last year’s level nearer $890 billion. The two figures count different things; either way the gap is enormous.) Byron Callan, a defence budget analyst, suggested the 2027 base budget might not pass until the spring of 2027, leaving little time to spend it before the fiscal year ends.

It is the Defence Department’s 38th continuing resolution in fifty years, so stopgaps are nothing new. What is new is the distance between the request and the reality. A department that asked for a 40 percent raise is living on last year’s allowance, while fighting a war its supplemental request cannot fully explain.

What a hundred pages buys. It is worth being precise about what the missing ninety-three pages normally contain, because the complaint is easily mistaken for pedantry. A war supplemental, done properly, does not simply state a total. It breaks the request into accounts: military personnel, operations and maintenance, procurement, research, construction. It says how many of each munition the money would buy and at what unit cost. It explains how much is replacing what was used, how much is expanding stocks, and how much is for operations still to come. It separates the costs of the war from the things the department would have bought anyway.

That last distinction matters most. Of the $67.1 billion, by Military Times’s breakdown, less than half was tied directly to Iran. The rest covered munitions, classified programmes and cybersecurity, all of which may be worthwhile and none of which is obviously a war cost. Emergency supplementals are not subject to the usual budget caps. A thin request that mixes war costs with general wish-list items is, whatever its intention, a way of moving ordinary spending into an account Congress scrutinises less. Appropriators of both parties know this trick. They have watched departments try it for decades. The detail is how they tell the difference.

There is also the matter of the money already given. The senators’ letter asked how $34 billion remaining from the 2025 reconciliation bill would be spent. A department asking for $67 billion more should be able to say what it did with the last tranche. As of the end of September, it had not.

The programmes. Under a stopgap, new programmes cannot start and old ones cannot grow. Hadley reported that programmes funded through the 2025 reconciliation bill would be hit especially hard. The F-15EX fighter received about $115 million against a request of $2.65 billion. Jaffe, in the Times, reported that President Trump’s Golden Dome missile shield would likely come to a halt in the coming months.

The relationships. None of this is purely procedural. Budgets pass when a secretary has spent years cultivating the appropriators who write them. Senator Thom Tillis of North Carolina, who cast a key vote to confirm Hegseth, told the Times that if you want to sell people on your needs and priorities, you have to engage. He did not think Hegseth had developed productive relationships “even with Republican members.” Senator Joni Ernst of Iowa, a combat veteran who also voted to confirm him, said he “works very little with Republican senators” and called for more transparency for the good of the Defence Department. Senator Kevin Cramer of North Dakota told Semafor that the failure to reach out to Democrats remained a strategic error.

A budget of $1.5 trillion needs sixty votes in the Senate for its ordinary half and a disciplined majority for the reconciliation half. Hegseth has neither. He has a speech.

The Bill

The barracks. Jaffe reported that the gap between what the Pentagon asked for and what it has will force it to move money set aside for repairing barracks, training troops and buying weapons into more pressing needs, chiefly sustaining 50,000 troops in the Middle East.

The barracks were already in trouble. As Cristina Stassis reported for Military Times on August 21, 2026, in “Some military barracks ‘chronically neglected’ due to $285 billion in maintenance backlogs, GAO finds,” the Government Accountability Office found that the department funds installation maintenance at around 80 percent of estimated need against its own 90 percent target. The result is a backlog of about $285 billion. Of 31 recommendations from a 2023 GAO report on substandard barracks, only 19 had been carried out by April 2026.

The young enlisted soldiers who live in those barracks were, in effect, the audience at Quantico: the E-6s and O-3s whom Hegseth praised and led on a morning run. Their rooms are part of what pays for the war’s shortfall. The Secretary’s new base, which he promised would offer “the gold standard” in living conditions, does not yet exist. Their mould does.

Training and readiness. Training is the other quiet victim. Money moved from training to operations does not disappear. It shows up a year later in units that are less ready, pilots with fewer hours and crews with less time at sea. It does not make a good speech, which may be why it was not in one.

The taxpayer. The Democratic senators’ letter added up costs that fall outside the Pentagon’s books entirely. They estimated $100 billion in extra costs to Americans from higher petrol and diesel prices, and $60 billion more in higher prices for groceries, mortgages and air travel. Those figures are a minority party’s estimate and should be read as such. But the direction is not seriously disputed: Jaffe reported that the war and the fight over the Strait of Hormuz have pushed fuel and food prices up, and that Republican candidates in Iowa, Nebraska, Ohio and Michigan have begun questioning the case for continuing it.

The Constitution. The deepest cost is structural. Congress’s power over money is its most effective check on a war it did not declare. A secretary who asks for tens of billions in seven pages, briefs Congress seven times in six months, and leaves the base budget to a stopgap is not merely being sloppy. He is weakening that check, whether he means to or not. Lawmakers cannot judge a request they cannot read.

A Canadian minister of national defence who tabled a seven-page request for a war supplemental would be sent back to write the other ninety-three pages before Question Period ended. Washington’s procedures are more forgiving. Even so, the appropriators have now made clear they will not sign a cheque for an amount the Secretary has not explained.
