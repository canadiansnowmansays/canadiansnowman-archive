by Stephen Gordon

I count my essays. The number climbs, it lands on a year that already means something, and I stop and tell you what. This is essay 1,381, and it is about the year the ordinary people of England did the arithmetic, discovered the answer was unbearable, and marched on London to say so.

I have written, in this series, about a king forced to admit he was subject to the law. That was 1215, the Magna Carta, the barons at Runnymede with an army and a document. It is one of the most celebrated moments in the history of accountability, and rightly so. But there is a detail about it that the celebration tends to skate past, and 1381 is where that detail comes home to roost: the Magna Carta was written by and for barons. Powerful men, armed men, landowners, forcing a king to guarantee their rights. It was accountability, yes — but accountability negotiated between the top of the hierarchy and the very top. Nobody at Runnymede was asking what the peasants thought.

In 1381, the peasants asked anyway. Nobody had drafted them a charter. So they came to write their own, and they did not bring lawyers. They brought everyone.

To understand why they rose, you have to go back to a number I have already written about, because 1381 is, in the most direct way, an aftershock of 1347.

The Black Death, when it tore through England, killed something close to half the population. I wrote about the horror of that at 1347 and I will not repeat it. But there was a consequence to all that death that nobody at the time intended, and it was this: the survivors were suddenly valuable. When half the workers in a country die, the half that remain discover, for the first time in the entire history of the feudal arrangement, that they have leverage. There were fields that needed working and not enough hands to work them. A peasant who had spent his whole life being told exactly what he was worth — which was nothing, which was whatever his lord decided — could now, cautiously, ask for more. And get it, because the alternative was a rotting harvest.

For a generation after the plague, the ordinary laborer’s position quietly, genuinely improved. Wages rose. The old bonds of serfdom loosened, because a serf who was treated too harshly could simply leave and find a lord down the road who was desperate enough to pay him.

And the powerful hated this. Of course they did. So they did what the powerful always do when the market moves against them: they tried to legislate the market back. Parliament passed laws — the Statute of Labourers — freezing wages at pre-plague levels, making it a crime to ask for more, trying by decree to cancel the leverage that death had accidentally handed to the poor. For thirty years the crown pushed down on the lid while the pressure built underneath.

And then, needing money for the endless war with France — the same Hundred Years’ War I keep encountering in this window — the government reached for the crudest instrument in the box. A poll tax. A head tax. Not a tax on wealth, not a tax on land, not a tax that asked more of those who had more. A flat charge, the same sum from every single person regardless of what they had, which meant it fell hardest, by far, on those who had least. And they levied it not once but three times in four years, each one heavier, the third one triple the first.

That was the number that broke it. A flat tax, tripled, on people who had just spent a generation being told by statute that they were not allowed to earn more. The arithmetic was simply impossible, and everyone doing it arrived at the same answer at the same time.

In the summer of 1381 they rose — in Essex, in Kent, across the southeast — and they marched on London, and for a few extraordinary days the ordinary people of England held the capital.

Their leader, or the one history remembers, was a man named Wat Tyler. Their preacher was a radical priest named John Ball, who had been saying dangerous things for years, and who gave the revolt the line that has outlived every other word spoken that summer. He asked a question, and it is one of the purest distillations of the idea of equality ever put into English: When Adam delved and Eve span, who was then the gentleman? When Adam dug and Eve spun cloth — when there were only the two of them, working — where was the lord? Where was the noble? Who decided that some men are born to command and others to obey? The hierarchy that claimed to be ordained by God and the natural order — Ball pointed at the very beginning of the story and observed that it hadn’t been there at the start. Somebody had added it later. Somebody had made it up.

That is a genuinely revolutionary thought, and it was six hundred years early, and they killed him for it.

Because the revolt failed. They almost always do, at first. The young king, Richard II — fourteen years old — rode out to meet the rebels and made them promises: an end to serfdom, pardons, reforms. The crowd, astonishingly, believed him, and many went home. And then, with the pressure released and the crowds dispersing, Wat Tyler was killed in a confrontation with the king’s men, the promises were revoked, and the reprisals began. The leaders were hunted down and executed. John Ball was hanged, drawn, and quartered. The king who had made the promises reportedly told the survivors: Villeins ye are, and villeins ye shall remain. Serfs you were, and serfs you’ll stay. The whole thing was crushed inside a month.

So did it fail? On the ledger of that summer, completely. The tax was the only thing that went — quietly dropped, because even the crown could see it had been a mistake worth a rebellion. Everything else was rolled back. The leaders died horribly. Serfdom remained on the books.

But here is the longer count, and this is why 1381 belongs in a series about numbers meaning something over time. The poll tax that triggered the revolt was so thoroughly discredited that England did not seriously try another one for centuries — and when a government finally did reach for a flat head tax again, six hundred years later, in the twentieth century, it helped bring that government down too. Some numbers, it turns out, teach a lesson that lasts. And serfdom itself, though it was not abolished that summer, never recovered its grip; within a few generations it had withered away in England, killed less by any single revolt than by the same economic pressure that caused this one — the simple, unlegislatable fact that after the plague, labor was worth something, and no statute could hold that truth down forever.

The barons at Runnymede got a charter and a place in every textbook. The peasants of 1381 got a massacre and a revoked promise. But they had said the thing out loud — Ball’s question, the one nobody could un-ask once it had been asked. Who was then the gentleman? You can hang the man who asks it. You cannot hang the question. It sits there, at the beginning of the story, waiting for the next people who do the arithmetic and arrive at the same unbearable answer.

So this is essay 1,381, the aftershock of the plague and the echo, from far below, of the barons’ charter.

The arithmetic was always the engine. Death made labor scarce; scarcity made the poor valuable; the powerful tried to legislate that value away; the crown taxed the ones who had least, three times, tripling; and the sum came out to a number nobody could pay, and so they marched. It ended the way these things usually end the first time — badly, bloodily, with the promises broken the moment the danger had passed. Condemn while dangerous, I wrote at Joan’s essay; here it is again, condemn the moment the crowd disperses.

But the question survived. That is the thing about certain numbers and certain questions: you can defeat the people who raise them and still lose, slowly, over centuries, to the raising. When Adam delved and Eve span, who was then the gentleman? Six hundred years later I still don’t have a good answer, and neither, I suspect, does anyone who has ever had cause to hope you wouldn’t ask.

I will see you at the next one — a girl in France, nineteen years old, who heard voices and turned a war, and whom the machine burned and then, at its leisure, called a saint.

They taxed the poorest three times over. The poorest did the math. Sometimes that is all a revolution is: the moment enough people finish the same sum.
