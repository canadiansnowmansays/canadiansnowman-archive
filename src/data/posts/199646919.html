On September 10, 2024, during his presidential debate with Kamala Harris, Donald Trump was asked about his plan to replace the Affordable Care Act. He had been promising this plan for nine years. He had been president for four of them. He had held both chambers of Congress for two. The question was not unreasonable.

His answer: “I have concepts of a plan.”

The audience laughed. Harris laughed. The moderators paused. The phrase entered the lexicon as shorthand for a particular species of governing: announce the victory, skip the work, hope nobody checks. It was, at the time, about healthcare. Thirty-four million Americans with pre-existing conditions were being governed by concepts.

Twenty months later, those concepts have gone to war.

Today — Day 90 of the war the President launched against Iran on February 28 — the New York Times reported that “U.S. officials are closing in on a framework for negotiations with Iran that could extend the cease-fire, lead to the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz and set the table for more substantive talks.”

Read that again slowly.

They are closing in — not arrived at — a framework — not a deal — for negotiations — not an agreement — that could — not will — extend — not make permanent — the ceasefire — which is already being violated by both sides — and set the table — the diplomatic equivalent of clearing your throat before clearing your throat — for more substantive talks — meaning the current talks are, by the administration’s own admission, not yet substantive, which raises the question of what everyone has been doing for ninety days.

This is not a peace deal. This is a plan to have a plan to discuss the possibility of eventually planning a plan.

It is, in the most literal sense, concepts of a ceasefire.

The details, such as they are, deserve the same anatomical attention.

The nuclear program — the thing the administration said was the reason for the war, the existential threat, the casus belli, the justification for bombing a sovereign nation — “would be pushed to another round of negotiations.” The Trump administration had demanded that Iran disavow the program and agree to dispose of its highly enriched uranium. Instead, the fate of the nuclear program has been deferred.

So to be clear: the war was started to stop the nuclear program, and ninety days later the nuclear program will be discussed... later. This is like burning down a house to kill a spider and then agreeing to revisit the spider question in the fall.

The Strait of Hormuz — the waterway through which roughly one-fifth of the world’s oil passes, which Iran closed in retaliation for being bombed, and whose closure has caused a global energy crisis — “would be reopened under the framework.” Good. Except that on Wednesday, the same day the framework was being discussed, Iran’s state media reported that Tehran would manage the waterway in cooperation with Oman. Trump immediately said he did not want the waterway under Iranian control. Iran has already created a “Persian Gulf Strait Authority” and published graphics showing what it calls a “controlled maritime zone” spanning the waterway. So the framework includes reopening the Strait, and the two sides disagree on what “reopening” means. One man’s scissors point up. The other’s point down. The ribbon remains uncut.

Concepts.

And then there is the matter of the ceasefire itself — the one the framework would “extend.”

On Wednesday night, while the framework was being frameworked, the United States shot down four Iranian drones and struck a drone ground-control station inside Iran. The U.S. military described these as “self-defense” — the second round of such attacks that week. Hours later, Iran fired a ballistic missile at a U.S. base in Kuwait. The Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps described that as retaliation for the U.S. strikes. Kuwaiti forces intercepted the missile. The Guards issued a statement promising an even “more decisive” response to future American strikes.

The word “ceasefire” contains two words: “cease” and “fire.” The current arrangement includes neither. It is a ceasefire in the same way that “concepts of a plan” is a plan — in the sense that the word is being used, and in no other sense.

Three U.S. officials described the progress toward the framework. None were authorized to speak publicly. All were granted anonymity to discuss the U.S. view of the current negotiations. The peace deal is so promising that nobody involved in it will put their name on it.

Trump himself “has not signed off on the emerging framework.” And “it was not clear if it matched Iran’s understanding.”

So the President has not agreed to his own administration’s framework, and Iran may not agree to it either, and the three people describing it won’t say who they are. Other than that, the deal is very nearly done. This is like announcing you are “closing in” on a marriage when neither party has agreed to a date, a venue, or each other.

Meanwhile, Israel — the ally whose intelligence Trump cited to justify the war, the partner who had pressed the U.S. to go to war — widened its offensive in Lebanon on Thursday, striking Beirut for the first time in almost a month and pushing deeper into the country’s south. The strike has stoked fears that the city, which had largely been spared since the Lebanon ceasefire took effect, could be drawn back into the fighting. The ceasefire in Lebanon, like the ceasefire in Iran, appears to be a concept.

Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei, Iran’s supreme leader, issued a written statement on Thursday calling for national unity and warning against internal division. He made no mention of negotiations. He did not appear to be closing in on anything except more ammunition.

Pakistani mediators — whose army chief met with Iranian officials in Tehran last Friday — described “grounds for optimism.” Iran’s foreign ministry spokesperson said the two sides were in the “final stage” of a memorandum of understanding. But “final stage” in this conflict has meant roughly the same thing as “two weeks” has meant for every other Trump promise: a unit of time that exists only in the future tense.

Let us take inventory.

The war was launched to neutralize Iran’s nuclear program. The nuclear program has been deferred. The war was justified as a quick operation — “days, not weeks,” the President promised. It has been ninety days. A ceasefire was declared. Both sides are still shooting. A framework is being discussed. Neither side has agreed to it. The Strait of Hormuz will be reopened. Both sides disagree on what that means. The officials are optimistic. None will say so on the record.

On September 10, 2024, the concept was healthcare. On May 28, 2026, the concept is the lives of everyone in the Middle East.

The plan will be available in two weeks.

It is always available in two weeks.
