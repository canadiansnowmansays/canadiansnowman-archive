There is a building at 325 Bay Street in Toronto, Canada, that was once called the Trump International Hotel and Tower.

It is the St. Regis Toronto now.

The name came off in 2017. A private equity firm bought out the management contracts for an undisclosed sum. Trump said nothing. The letters were removed. The building was renovated. The St. Regis brand — old money, quiet luxury, the aesthetic of arrival rather than aspiration — was installed in the place where the gold letters had been. The loading dock on Bay Street remained, as loading docks tend to do, because some architectural miscalculations are easier to rename than to fix.

We began this series with the Kennedy Center and a federal judge and a ruling that landed on JFK’s birthday. We have travelled, across nine essays, through the full landscape of the naming project — the psychological wound that builds towers, the licensing model that turned the name into a product, the Canadian properties that accepted buyouts without a word, the Washington makeover that is remaking the capital in one man’s image, the Institute of Peace gutted and renamed in the same motion, the branded life of Bibles and sneakers and trading cards and illegal mock-ups of $250 bills, the brand collapse that drove the commercial project underground and the monument project above ground, the legal architecture that says only Congress can change what Congress names.

We end, as we should, with the question that all of this has been circling: what will the name be worth when the man who needed it everywhere is gone?

The question is not purely historical. It is not something to be answered by a future generation in the comfortable distance of retrospect. It is being answered now, in real time, by the courts and the Congress and the market and the republic itself — the slow, contested, imperfect process by which democratic societies negotiate their relationship to the people who hold power over them.

The courts have established that the Kennedy Center’s name belongs to Congress. That finding will stand regardless of what happens to the letters on the marble. A future Congress — of either party, in any configuration — that chose to remove Trump’s name from the Kennedy Center would be acting on solid legal ground, with a federal judge’s ninety-four-page ruling behind it and the 1964 statute in front of it. The legal architecture is in place. Whether it is used is a political question, not a legal one.

The Institute of Peace presents a harder case. The takeover has been found illegal, but the finding is stayed, the litigation continues, and the institution’s staff is gone and its budget zeroed. A future administration that chose to restore the Institute of Peace — to rehire its staff, restore its funding, return it to its statutory purpose — would be doing something the courts have already said the law requires. The building’s name, in that scenario, would sort itself out. Names come off buildings. Institutions take longer to rebuild than to destroy.

The arch and the ballroom are the most durable of the Washington makeover projects, because they are physical structures and physical structures, once built, resist removal. The arch, if it is completed before 2029, will be standing in Memorial Circle when the next president takes office. It will be there when the president after that takes office. The question of whether it should bear Trump’s name — or any name, or no name — will be a question for those administrations, those Congresses, those courts. Physical monuments outlast the controversies that attend their construction. The Arc de Triomphe is still standing, and Napoleon has been gone for two centuries.

The ballroom, similarly, will be standing when the current term ends. The White House will have a ballroom. Whether it will be known as a Trump ballroom or simply as the East Wing ballroom or the State Ballroom or something else entirely is a question that the next occupant of the building will answer, consciously or by default.

The currency is the most interesting case, because it is the most symbolic and the most reversible.

Trump’s signature is already on every dollar bill in circulation. That will change when the next Treasury Secretary decides it should change — the signature on currency reflects the current Treasurer and Treasury Secretary, and administrations change these as a matter of routine. The signature is not permanent. It is current.

The $250 bill, if the legislation passes, is a different matter. A new denomination of currency, once established, has its own inertia — new denominations are not typically abolished by incoming administrations, because abolishing currency is complicated and disruptive and the political cost typically exceeds the benefit. But a $250 bill with Trump’s face on it would be, for any subsequent administration that found the face inconvenient, a standing provocation — an object lesson, literally in people’s wallets, in what the republic had permitted during this particular administration.

There is some evidence from monetary history about the durability of faces on currency. Andrew Jackson has been on the $20 bill since 1928, and there have been sustained efforts to replace him with Harriet Tubman that have not yet produced a result, despite the fact that Jackson’s record includes the forced removal of Native American populations. Faces on currency are sticky. The $250 bill, if it exists, will likely outlast the controversy.

The historical verdict on whose face belongs on money — who has earned that form of commemoration, what it means to be on the currency of a republic — is a verdict that takes longer than a presidential term to deliver. Washington and Lincoln and Hamilton and Franklin are on the money because generations of Americans across two and a half centuries decided, collectively and through their institutions, that these individuals represented something durable about the republic’s values and achievements. That verdict was not made by the individuals themselves. It was made by everyone else.

The historical precedents for what happens to the names of leaders who inscribed themselves too aggressively on public institutions are not, as we noted in Part IX, always comforting for the inscribers.

The Roman damnatio memoriae — the formal condemnation of memory, the erasure of names from inscriptions — was applied to the worst cases. Most cases were handled differently, with the gradual attrition of public memory rather than formal erasure. Buildings got renamed. The names of their renamers were eventually replaced by the names of later benefactors or later administrators. The marble endured. The names on it changed.

In the American context, the cleanest precedent for the kind of institutional renaming currently underway is the renaming of military bases that honored Confederate generals — a process that took decades of advocacy and was finally mandated by Congress in the wake of the 2020 racial justice protests, resulting in the replacement of names like Fort Bragg with names like Fort Liberty. The Confederate generals whose names were removed had been dead for more than a century. The removal was contested and slow and complicated.

The Trump names are being added to institutions, not removed from them. The removal, when and if it comes, will face its own complications. But the Confederate base renaming establishes an important principle: the American republic, given sufficient time and sufficient consensus, is capable of deciding that names it once placed on its institutions no longer represent the values those institutions are meant to embody, and of removing them.

The question of whether Trump’s name will eventually be regarded as belonging in that category is not one this series can answer. It is a question that requires the distance of time and the perspective of consequence — the ability to see, from a vantage point not yet available, whether the naming project was an episode in the republic’s history or a transformation of it.

What this series can offer, in its closing pages, is a return to the place where we began — to the compulsion, the wound, the man who needed his name in gold.

Donald Trump is seventy-eight years old. He grew up in brick, in Queens, within sight of a skyline that did not know his name. He spent fifty years writing that name on everything he could reach — the towers, the hotels, the products, the universities, the television shows, the Bibles, the sneakers, the currency, the performing arts centres, the peace institutes, the triumphal arches. He inscribed it in gold where the surface allowed, in large letters where the surface permitted, and on things he did not own, did not build, and had no legal authority to rename where the surface existed and the institutional leverage could be found or manufactured.

The compulsion, as we have argued across this series, is not the product of simple vanity. Vain men do not need a $250 bill. Vain men do not need to rename the Kennedy Center. Vain men, in the ordinary sense of the word, are satisfied by the gold lobby and the big table and the seat at the top. What Trump has pursued goes beyond the satisfaction that vanity provides, because it is not satisfaction he is seeking. It is permanence. The name on the marble says: I cannot be erased. The name on the currency says: I am in every pocket in the republic. The name on the arch says: every person who enters the capital will pass under my name as they enter. The name on the peace institute says: even the things I destroyed will remember me.

This is the project of a man who, at some level not available to conscious examination, does not believe the name will hold on its own. The buildings were not enough. The hotels were not enough. The television show was not enough. The presidency — the first one — was not enough. The second presidency is not enough. There must be more surfaces. There must be more letters. There must be more gold.

The wound does not heal.

The towers do not fill it.

The Bay Street loading dock is still there.

The name is not.

The St. Regis is thriving. The building that failed so publicly and so comprehensively — the structural collapses, the investor fraud, the receivership, the Supreme Court of Canada, the quiet buyout and the dignified silence — has become, under a different name, a successful luxury hotel in the heart of Canada’s financial district. The architectural problem of the loading dock on the prestigious side remains, as architectural problems of that kind tend to remain, because you can rename a building but you cannot move its service entrance.

The loading dock is the most honest thing about the whole story. It is the place where the gap between the promise and the delivery is permanently visible, built into the structure, not removable by renovation or rebranding or the letters of a different name. It faces Bay Street and it will face Bay Street long after everyone involved in the Trump naming and the JCF buyout and the St. Regis renovation is gone.

History is a loading dock. It faces the prestigious side of the street. It does not move.

The names come off the buildings.

The buildings remain.

The republic remains.

And somewhere in the architecture of a country that has been arguing about self-governance for two hundred and fifty years, the principle is still load-bearing — the principle that the institutions belong to the republic, and the republic will outlast any name written on it in gold.

Only Congress can change it.

History will.

This concludes The Name Game, a ten-part series on Donald Trump’s lifelong obsession with seeing himself everywhere — and what happens when the world finally says no.

Donald Trump has spent a lifetime writing his name on things. Towers. Golf courses. Hotels. A university. A Bible. A perfume called Success. A peace institute he gutted before the paint on the letters dried. A performing arts centre dedicated to a murdered president he has spent his entire career trying to impress.

The name is always the same: gold where possible, large where mandatory, permanent where permitted, and imposed where neither permission nor permanence was ever his to grant.

This series is about what that compulsion reveals — psychologically, politically, historically, and occasionally comically. It is not a flattering portrait.

Then again, neither is a loading dock on Bay Street.
