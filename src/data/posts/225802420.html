On November 14, 2026, at a news conference in Manila after meeting President Ferdinand Marcos Jr., Mark Carney was asked whether his travels were really about getting away from the United States. He did not pretend otherwise.

“It’s part of our strategy, absolutely, 100 percent,” he said, as Ian Austen reported in The New York Times on November 16. “We’re very transparent about it. It’s a part of our strategy to develop this dense web of connections to make us resilient, to make us more independent, to make us sovereign so we can live our lives as we choose, so we’re not being told what to do by some hegemon or a hyperscaler.”

That is as plain a statement of purpose as Carney has made since Davos, and it gives this instalment its title. Earlier parts of this series looked at the words (Part I), the numbers (Part II), the tail risk (Part III), the defence plumbing (Part VI) and the officers who keep it running (Part VII). This one follows the prime minister for a week on the road, from Dublin to Manila to Shenzhen, and asks what the web actually caught.

Austen’s piece makes a striking observation along the way. A year ago, Carney was something of a curiosity at the Association of Southeast Asian Nations and the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum. Now, after Davos and after Trump added energy-security threats to his trade war with the world, he and his energy-rich country are a marquee attraction. When the leaders were introduced at the Francophonie summit in Cambodia during the same trip, Carney got noticeably more applause than anyone else.

I. Europe First, Asia Bigger

The trip began with a detour. Carney added a stop in Dublin for the European Political Community summit, where he and his European counterparts reaffirmed their support for Ukraine alongside Volodymyr Zelensky. Last month Carney announced that Canada is officially seeking to become the first “associate member” of the European Union, turning Ursula von der Leyen’s September 16 offer, covered in Part VI, into a formal bid.

Europe is the comfortable strand of the web. It shares Canada’s language of rules and rights, and an Angus Reid poll cited by Austen found that 58 percent of Canadians want closer trade ties with it. But Austen also quotes Kazuto Suzuki of the University of Tokyo, who makes the less comfortable point. Carney’s strong focus on Europe “gives an impression that he is not looking toward Asia,” Suzuki said. “Nevertheless, if Canada is to avoid dependence on the U.S. market and form a coalition of middle powers, cooperation with Asia is essential.”

The rest of the week was Carney’s answer to that.

II. Manila: Two Deals in Two Days

In Manila, Carney announced that Canada and the Philippines had concluded negotiations on a free trade agreement, with signing planned for 2027. According to The Hub, the deal covers more than 90 percent of tariff lines, and Carney says it could triple bilateral trade to more than $10 billion a year by 2035, from $3.4 billion in merchandise trade in 2025. “In each of these sectors,” he said of agriculture, aerospace and artificial intelligence, “Canadian supply is meeting Philippine demand.”

The next day, November 15, came the larger prize. At the 49th ASEAN Summit, the bloc’s chair and Canada announced the substantial conclusion of the ASEAN–Canada Free Trade Agreement, five years after talks began. The joint statement released by Global Affairs Canada says the agreement runs to 25 chapters, from goods and services to e-commerce and government procurement. Some technical work remains, with signing targeted for 2027, the 50th anniversary of ASEAN–Canada dialogue relations. It will be ASEAN’s first free trade agreement with a North American partner.

The numbers explain why Ottawa is pressing. Two-way merchandise trade with ASEAN reached $52.5 billion in 2025, up 23.7 percent in a year. Ottawa estimates the agreement could add nearly $2 billion to the Canadian economy and almost 14,000 jobs. Carney has pledged to double Canada’s non-American exports by 2035. The Hub’s figures show how far there is to go: the United States still took 71.7 percent of Canadian merchandise exports in 2025, although that was down from 75.9 percent the year before.

Seven-tenths of everything still goes south. The web is real, but it is not yet strong enough to carry much weight.

III. Shenzhen: The Xi Strand

From Manila, Carney flew to Shenzhen for the APEC summit and, on November 17, his second formal meeting of the year with Xi Jinping. The first, in Beijing in January, had produced the deal that cut Canada’s 100 percent tariff on Chinese electric vehicles to 6.1 percent for up to 49,000 cars a year, in exchange for China lowering its tariffs on Canadian canola.

This time, as Dylan Robertson of The Canadian Press reported for Global News, the two leaders added a second layer. China will drop a 25 percent tariff on Canadian frozen pork cuts and frozen cold-water shrimp, and widen relief to Greenland halibut, some molluscs and sea cucumber, covering about $400 million in Canadian exports a year. It will open its market to Canadian fava beans, chickpeas and lentils. In return, Ottawa will extend tariff remission to 32 more Chinese steel and aluminium products, 23 steel and nine aluminium, where Canadian supply falls short. Canada will send a major trade mission to China in 2027, which both sides declared a “year of e-commerce.”

“Much has been achieved since my visit,” Carney told Xi. By Robertson’s count, Canadian exports to China have risen 40 percent since January, led by oil, canola and peas, and the January deal has already been extended to late 2028. Carney said he would raise foreign interference with Xi. A Chinese spokesperson denied any had taken place.

What did not change was the most sensitive item. Since March, 16,211 Chinese electric vehicles have entered Canada under the quota, about half priced at $35,000 or less, and a second Chinese automaker has met Transport Canada’s requirements. In January, Carney predicted substantial Chinese auto investment in Canada within three years. In Manila he was more careful. He wants “a competitive Canadian auto sector that produces good-paying jobs, good union jobs for Canadian workers,” and affordable electric cars, but “it depends partially on — and importantly on — where the U.S. trading relationship settles out.”

That sentence is the web in miniature. The Chinese strand is real, and it is being held in reserve as leverage on the American one.

IV. The Photograph

Then there was Vladimir Putin. China, unlike Canada, is not a member of the International Criminal Court, which has a warrant out for his arrest, so Shenzhen was a summit he could attend. Xi had arranged a three-way meeting with Putin and Trump.

Carney settled the awkward question in Manila. Yes, he told reporters, as CBC News reported on November 14, he would stand in the leaders’ “family photo” with Putin, “out of respect for the host. We’re in Asia.” Canada’s opposition to Russia, he said, shows up in sanctions, not in photo boycotts. He noted that Russian and Belarusian teams would be barred from the World Junior Hockey Championship in Alberta at the end of the year. “Canada stands firmly with Ukraine, firmly with democracy, territorial integrity, rule of law,” he said. And on the trilateral: “The security architecture for free Europe will be determined by free Europe, for Europe and NATO, not by Russia.”

He kept his word. At the leaders’ banquet on November 18, Carney and Australia’s Anthony Albanese stood in the photo with Putin, Xi and Trump, while condemning the invasion of Ukraine, as The New Daily reported. Ukrainian Australians had urged Albanese not to normalise Putin’s presence, and some critics compared the Putin–Trump–Xi meeting to Yalta. CBC’s headline from the summit was “With Putin in the room, Carney condemns Russia’s illegal invasion of Ukraine.”

It is worth noticing where Carney drew the line. He would go to the summit, sell pork and lentils to its host, and stand in a picture with a man under an international arrest warrant. He would not let that picture say anything about Ukraine. The web is built for trade, not for absolution.

V. Twenty Minutes with Trump

The strand everyone was watching was the American one, and it got about 20 minutes. Just before the leaders’ dinner on November 18, Carney met Trump on the sidelines, with Anita Anand, Marco Rubio, Howard Lutnick, Jamieson Greer and Stephen Miller in the room. The Prime Minister’s Office said they discussed a trade agreement that creates good jobs, improves affordability and “fully respects Canada’s languages, culture and sovereignty.” Carney summed it up on X: “We agreed to continue the work and to stay in close contact.”

That is the language of a relationship on hold. Since talks collapsed in late August, Washington has imposed 50 percent tariffs on about $20 billion in U.S. dollars’ worth of Canadian goods, and Ottawa has retaliated. Asked whether the president would object to his China dealings, Carney was relaxed, according to CBC News. Canada sells China commodities, not advanced semiconductors. “I shouldn’t speak for him, but in his hierarchy of issues, it’s not the top issue at all.”

Not everyone around Trump agreed. When Carney suggested Canada could turn to other automakers if U.S. talks failed to protect an integrated North American auto industry, White House trade adviser Peter Navarro called the remarks “offensive in the extreme” and warned that China could “eat Canada alive.” It was a revealing outburst. For years, Washington has told allies that the danger lies in dependence on China. Navarro’s complaint was that Canada was becoming less dependent on the United States.

VI. The Ceiling

There is a limit to how far the Chinese strand can stretch, and it is set at home. Austen points out that Canadians’ goodwill towards China has risen since Carney restored relations, while their views of the United States have moved sharply the other way. But the same Angus Reid poll that found 58 percent wanting closer trade with Europe found only 14 percent wanting closer trade with China.

Canadians have not forgotten the two Michaels, detained for more than 1,000 days after Canada arrested a Huawei executive on an American extradition request, or the public inquiry that concluded China had tried to interfere in Canadian politics. Carney raising interference with Xi on November 17 was not a diplomatic nicety. It was the price of being allowed to sell him lentils.

That ceiling may be the most useful thing about the web. A country that wanted to swap one hegemon for another would chafe at it. Carney’s strategy, as he describes it, is to have so many partners that none of them can tell Canada what to do. On that logic, a public that keeps China at arm’s length is not a constraint on the strategy. It is the strategy working as intended.

VII. What the Web Caught

Add up the week. Two free trade agreements concluded in Manila, opening the way to ASEAN’s market of roughly 680 million people. A second tariff deal with China worth about $400 million a year, with the electric-vehicle question deliberately left open. A family photo that said nothing about Ukraine that Carney did not want it to say. Twenty minutes with Trump that produced an agreement to keep talking, and a tirade from Navarro.

None of it changes the arithmetic overnight. Seven of every ten dollars of Canadian exports still cross the southern border, and they will for years. But each strand makes the next tariff threat from Washington a little less frightening, and each one gives Canada a little more room to say no. That is what a web is for. No single strand carries the weight. Pull on one, and the others hold.

Not everyone thinks the American strand should be cut. Chrystia Freeland, Carney’s former cabinet colleague, argued in The New York Times on November 17 that after the midterms Canada should leave the door open to a renewed, more equal partnership with the United States, though not before 2028. Her case, and the airport guard who told her Canadians would be better off without America, are the subject of Part IX.
