On the morning of October 21, 2026, thirteen days before the American midterm elections, the New York Times published the kind of poll that makes Democrats sleep well and Republicans check their phones at three in the morning.

The final national New York Times/Siena College poll of the cycle, conducted October 12 to 19 among 3,494 likely voters and reported by Lisa Lerer and Ruth Igielnik, put President Trump’s approval at 36 percent, with 59 percent disapproving. That is the lowest figure the Times/Siena series has recorded for him in either term. Asked which party’s candidate they would support for Congress in their own district, likely voters chose the Democrat by 52 percent to 41, an 11-point margin. Voters trusted Democrats over Republicans on the cost of living by 15 points, and on the economy, long Mr. Trump’s strongest ground, by eight.

Nate Cohn, the paper’s chief political analyst, set the numbers in context the same morning in The Tilt, his newsletter. No party has won the national House vote by 11 points since the Democrats in 2008. And no president since Harry Truman in 1946, whose party was routed that November, has gone into a midterm with a lower approval rating.

Read quickly, it looks like a verdict. It isn’t one. It is a photograph of intentions taken over eight days in mid-October, and the result it appears to predict depends entirely on whether the people in the photograph turn up on November 3.

That is the whole argument of this essay. It is worth making carefully, because the last decade of American polling is routinely misremembered, and the misremembering leads people to exactly the wrong conclusions.

What the Polls Actually Said

The popular version of recent history runs like this: the polls said Hillary Clinton would win in 2016, the polls said Kamala Harris would win in 2024, and both times Donald Trump won. Therefore polls are biased toward Democrats, and a smart reader should mentally subtract a few points from whatever the Democrat is showing.

Almost every part of that story is wrong, and the parts that are right point somewhere more interesting.

Start with 2016. Heading into Election Day, the national surveys showed Clinton ahead by about three points on average, and she won the popular vote by 2.1 points, or roughly three million votes, as the Philadelphia Inquirer summarised the industry’s own post-mortem. By historical standards the national polls were accurate. The failure was in the states. The polls underestimated Trump in Michigan by 3.5 points and in Wisconsin by 6.5, and those were precisely the states that mattered. Many state surveys had not adjusted for the fact that college-educated voters were more likely to answer the phone, and late deciders broke heavily to Trump. He carried Michigan, Wisconsin and Pennsylvania by a combined 77,744 votes out of nearly 129 million cast nationally. That is about the population of Sarnia.

2020 was the genuinely bad year, worse than 2016, though it is less remembered because the polled favourite still won. Michael Lewis-Beck and Natalie Jackson, examining 322 national polls, found the surveys erred by almost five points in the Democratic direction on average, as Lewis-Beck later recounted in Larry Sabato’s Crystal Ball at the University of Virginia Center for Politics. Joe Biden was expected to win comfortably. He won the popular vote by about seven million, but the Electoral College told a different story. In the NPR analysis of the certified results published December 2, 2020, Domenico Montanaro observed that just 44,000 votes across Georgia, Arizona and Wisconsin stood between Biden and an Electoral College tie. A stadium crowd, scattered across three states, decided the presidency.

Then 2024, which is where the popular memory goes most astray. The polls did not show Harris winning. They showed a coin toss with a fingernail’s tilt toward her. The final national NBC News poll had the two candidates tied at 49 percent; the RealClearPolitics average gave Harris 48.7 to Trump’s 48.6. The final national Times/Siena poll of that cycle, taken October 20 to 23, 2024, was also tied, 48 to 48. Trump won the popular vote by about a point and a half. When the American Association for Public Opinion Research published its task force report on the 2024 cycle, it found national presidential polls had missed by 2.6 points on average and state polls by 3.0. That is not a scandal. That is roughly what polls do.

So what happened in 2024 was not that the polls predicted a Harris win and were humiliated. The polls predicted a race too close to call, and the race was decided by a margin smaller than their known error, in the direction they had erred twice before.

The Year the Error Ran the Other Way

If the lesson stopped there, the “subtract a few points from the Democrat” rule might hold up. It doesn’t.

In the autumn of 2022, the first midterm of the Biden presidency, the political class convinced itself a red wave was coming. The president was unpopular, inflation was high, and the party in the White House almost always loses ground in a midterm. Pundits spoke of a bloodbath and a tsunami. Republicans were going to take the House by a wide margin and probably the Senate.

It didn’t happen. Democrats held the Senate and kept their House losses narrow. The interesting point is who got it wrong. When FiveThirtyEight reviewed its own forecasts afterward, it concluded that the aggregate polls had one of their most accurate cycles in recent memory, and that where they missed, they had slightly underestimated Democrats. The red wave was a story the commentators told, not a finding the data supported. Derek Thompson put it neatly in The Ringer the day after the vote: the polls were right and the vibes were wrong. He also noted that this was the second consecutive midterm in which the polling error had leaned against Republicans rather than Democrats. Even that needs a footnote. Cohn points out that in 2018, Trump’s first midterm, the polls held up nationally but underestimated Republicans in the red states that decided the Senate, Tennessee, Missouri and Indiana among them.

Put the four cycles side by side and the “polls favour Democrats” rule falls apart. In 2016 and 2020 the error ran toward the Democrats. In 2022 it ran, modestly, the other way. In 2024 it ran toward the Democrats again, but by an amount the pollsters would describe as ordinary. The direction of polling error is not a law of nature. Pollsters adjust after each miss, and the adjustments sometimes overcorrect.

An honest reader of the October 21 poll should therefore hold two thoughts at once. The 11-point Democratic lead may be overstated. It may also be understated. Nobody, including the pollsters, knows which. Cohn offers the reassuring version: the lead is wide enough that Democrats would still win decisively even if this poll missed by as much as the polls did in 2016 or 2020. That is true, but only if the electorate the poll modelled actually turns up.

What a Poll Is

The most useful single fact about the Times/Siena survey is printed in its methodology note, the part almost nobody reads. The margin of sampling error is plus or minus 1.9 points, and the Times adds, with admirable candour, that many other sources of error exist beyond sampling. The people who answered were then weighted to stand in for the people who did not.

In other words, a poll is a carefully built model of an electorate that does not yet exist. The “likely voter” in a likely-voter poll is a statistical construction: a person who, on the evidence of their voting history and their own stated intentions, is expected to cast a ballot. The 52-to-41 result is a statement about what will happen if those people vote as they say they will.

This is where the poll stops being a forecast and becomes a dependency. Every likely Democratic voter who reads the headline, concludes the thing is in the bag, and decides that Tuesday, November 3, is a good day to clean the gutters instead, is a small hole in the model. The poll is not wrong when that happens. The voter has made it wrong.

This poll shows exactly how much rides on that assumption. Times/Siena decides who counts as a likely voter using two measures: whether people have voted before, and whether they say they will vote this time. In this election, Cohn reports, the two measures point to quite different results. Among registered voters with a record of voting in a past midterm or primary, Democrats lead by nine. Among those who say they are at least “very likely” to vote, or have already voted, they lead by 14. The five-point gap between those numbers is the one question no poll can answer: who actually comes.

There is a sardonic coda. The only group in the poll that Republicans lead, Cohn notes, is the voters who said they were not at all likely to vote. Those people are planning to stay home. The danger is a Democrat who joins them.

The Warnings Inside the Good News

The same October 21 poll that delivers the 11-point headline also contains several reasons not to trust it as a promise.

The first is the base. Eighty-two percent of Republicans still approve of the president’s job performance, and most of them still support his signature policies, the Iran war and the tariffs included. Among white voters without a college degree, the group that powered his victories in 2016 and 2024, approval is split almost evenly, 48 to 46. That is a sharp decline, but it is not a collapse. A motivated base that turns out reliably in a low-turnout midterm can outperform a larger but lukewarm majority.

The second is the enthusiasm gap running in the wrong direction. Only 28 percent of voters said they were satisfied with the Democratic Party, eight points lower than the share satisfied with the Republican Party. The unhappiness comes largely from Democrats themselves: 43 percent of Democrats said they were dissatisfied with their own party, compared with 16 percent of Republicans who felt that way about theirs. A voter who prefers your party but does not much like it is exactly the voter most likely to skip a midterm.

The third is late movement. Immigration has re-emerged as a defining concern. Ten percent of voters now name it as their top issue, up from 6 percent in September, and among Republicans the figure has nearly doubled to almost 20 percent. The Democratic edge on immigration has narrowed to four points, 50 to 46. A poll that closed its field period on October 19 cannot see what happens in the final two weeks, and the 2016 post-mortems showed how heavily late deciders can break in one direction.

The fourth is arithmetic that no national number captures. A generic-ballot lead is a national figure, but the House is elected district by district, and the districts were drawn by people who knew exactly what they were doing. Democrats’ votes are concentrated in cities, which means a national lead converts into seats less efficiently than the headline suggests. The Senate is harder still. As the Times notes, Democrats are trying to flip seats in Texas, Iowa, Alaska and Ohio, all states Mr. Trump won by double digits in 2024, and other polls show several of those contests locked in a tie. A national 11-point lead tells you very little about whether a few thousand voters in Des Moines or San Antonio will turn up.

The Generation the Poll Is Counting On

The day after the national numbers landed, the Times published a companion analysis by Claire Cain Miller, with graphics by Rachel Buigas-Lopez, breaking the same poll down by age. It is the clearest illustration I have seen of the argument in this essay, because it shows where the Democratic lead is concentrated and whom it depends on.

Among likely voters aged 18 to 29, 70 percent now identify as Democrats or lean that way. That is 15 points higher than in the final Times/Siena poll before the 2024 election, and roughly where the youth vote stood in 2008, when young voters helped carry Barack Obama into the White House. On the congressional ballot, young voters favour the Democrat in their district by 44 points. Two years ago the margin was 10. Only 16 percent approve of the president’s performance, at least 11 points below any other age group. Eighty-seven percent disapprove of the Iran war, and ninety-four percent describe the economy as poor or only fair.

The discontent runs deeper than one administration. In the September poll, 53 percent of young likely voters said they feared the United States could fail as a nation, up from 38 percent two years earlier. A generation that believes the house might fall down is not, on the face of it, a generation inclined to stay home.

But here is the catch, and it is the oldest catch in American politics. No age group has a worse record of showing up for a midterm. According to the Center for Information and Research on Civic Learning and Engagement at Tufts University, about 31 percent of eligible Americans under 30 voted in 2018, the high-water mark for a midterm in decades, and roughly 27 percent voted in 2022. Historically the figure has hovered around one in five. Even in a good year, most young Americans sit out the midterm.

That is why the likely-voter question matters more here than anywhere else in the poll. Young voters have, almost by definition, the thinnest voting records. The screen that leans on past behaviour therefore has the least to go on, and the screen that leans on stated intention relies on the group whose intentions have historically been least reliable. The 18-to-29 sample is also only a fraction of the 3,494 respondents, so its margin of error is considerably wider than the headline 1.9 points.

The people the Times interviewed make the dependency concrete. A 21-year-old medical assistant in Athens, Georgia, earning $17 an hour, did not vote in 2024 and says she will vote Democratic this time. A 27-year-old truck driver in Phelan, California, voted for Mr. Trump expecting a strong economy. He now says he cannot think why he did, pointing to the cost of fuel and to people close to him who have had to flee immigration agents. A 23-year-old store associate in McKinney, Texas, put the generational case bluntly: “We are the generation that will change things, because we can’t afford anything.” Each of them has told an interviewer what they intend to do. None of them has yet done it.

The enthusiasm problem from the previous section applies here with particular force. Young voters prefer Democrats on every issue the Times asked about, but they are hardly smitten with the party: 41 percent say it is focused on the wrong things. A 20-year-old AutoZone employee in Spokane, Washington, who calls himself a gun-loving environmentalist, said the party’s infighting makes it hard to commit to. Their verdict on Republicans is harsher still, with 77 percent saying that party has the wrong priorities. Disliking the other side, however, is a reason to vote, not a guarantee of it.

If the young voters in this poll turn out in 2018 numbers, the 11-point lead may prove conservative. If they turn out the way young Americans usually do in a midterm, a good part of that lead will evaporate between the interview and the ballot box. The poll has counted them. November 3 will decide whether they count themselves.

The Brookings Warning That Came True

On September 4, 2024, when Kamala Harris had just taken over the Democratic ticket, analysts at Brookings cautioned against concluding that she enjoyed a clear lead. They pointed out that differences between final polling averages and results in recent presidential elections had mostly reflected underestimates of Trump’s vote, and that in the Blue Wall states the FiveThirtyEight average had underestimated him by an average of 4.3 points. Their conclusion was that 2024 was likely to resemble 2016 and 2020. It did.

The people who studied the polls most closely were the least confident in them. The overconfidence came from readers, and campaigns, who treated a narrow lead as a settled outcome. The Clinton campaign in 2016 did not begin airing television ads in Wisconsin until October 28 and did not announce a Michigan television campaign until November 1, as Rolling Stone reported on November 9, 2016. Those states were lost by 22,748 and 10,704 votes respectively.

A Canadian Footnote

Watching all of this from Toronto, I am struck by how familiar it looks. Ontario’s 2022 provincial election drew well under half of eligible voters, and the result was a comfortable majority built on the votes of fewer than one eligible voter in five. When turnout falls that low, the “majority” in a poll can be a minority at the ballot box, and the people who stay home hand the decision to the people who don’t. Stephen Leacock, who had a fine nose for the gap between what a town says it will do and what it does, would have recognised the pattern. In Mariposa, the whole town was devoted to the new church until the bills came due.

Americans face a different system with higher stakes, but the principle is identical. The poll measures who people prefer. Only the vote measures who they choose.

What to Do With a Good Poll

If you are an American voter who found the October 21 poll encouraging, here is the honest way to read it.

It tells you the national mood is unfavourable to the party in power, that the trend has run in one direction since September, and that Democrats have regained ground among men, young voters and Hispanic voters. These are real findings, and they are not trivial.

It does not tell you who will win your district. It does not tell you who will control the Senate. And it most certainly does not tell you that your vote is unnecessary. The poll assumed you would vote. That is how you got counted in the 52 percent.

The four most recent national elections were decided by about 77,000 votes in three states, about 44,000 votes in three states, a red wave that never came because the people who were supposed to sit it out did not, and a margin smaller than the polls’ own error. Every one of those outcomes was settled by people who showed up and people who didn’t.

On November 3, the poll will stop mattering. It will have been accurate, or not, depending largely on whether the people who told the interviewer they would vote actually do. The poll is counting on you. So, for that matter, is everyone else.
