Geoffrey Hinton, in the interview that anchored Part 118, offered a note of cautious optimism about the one problem that overshadows all the others: the race between the United States and China. Many of China’s leaders are engineers, he said, and he is fairly sure Xi Jinping understands the risks very well. Since neither superpower benefits from a technology that wipes out humanity, a rational American leadership could eventually strike an agreement with Beijing, much as Washington and Moscow did on nuclear weapons.

I want to take that hope seriously, and then take it apart. Because on the same morning Hinton’s interview appeared, The New York Times published a guest essay by John Garnaut — a former China correspondent for The Sydney Morning Herald and a onetime adviser to an Australian prime minister — that suggests Xi understands a risk very clearly indeed. Just not the same one Hinton means.

The essay’s title says it plainly: A.I. safety means something different in China. The argument is that when Chinese developers and officials talk about making artificial intelligence “safe,” they are having a conversation that barely overlaps with the one happening in Western capitals, and that the gap between the two is why the talks then under way between Trump and Xi were unlikely to produce anything real.

The man at the frontier

Garnaut builds his case around Tang Jie, the soft-spoken data engineer who co-founded, serves as chief scientist of, and holds the largest individual stake in Z.ai, China’s leading A.I. lab. Readers of this series met Z.ai in Part 90, when its GLM-5.3 model arrived as a cheap, open-weight cybersecurity tool that China’s boosters read as vindication after Hugging Face. Tang is the man behind it, and on the surface his rhetoric could pass for Dario Amodei’s.

In a letter to staff leaked to Chinese media in July, Tang wrote that once a technology becomes powerful enough to alter the course of civilisation, safety is no longer an accessory but the precondition for that technology being permitted to exist at all. Strip the names off that sentence and it could sit comfortably inside one of Amodei’s essays, the corpus surveyed back in Part 99.

But Garnaut’s point is that the words carry a different cargo. To understand what Tang means by safety, you have to read it through Xi Jinping’s vocabulary, in which security and control ultimately refer to one thing: the survival of the Chinese Communist Party. Where the Western debate asks what A.I. might do to society, the animating fear in China is what it might do to the state.

The translation tell

The most forensic passage in Garnaut’s essay concerns a mistranslation, and it is the kind of detail that rewards the close attention this series tries to bring to primary sources.

Six days after Tang’s letter, Xi convened the World Artificial Intelligence Conference in Shanghai on July 17. State media rendered his remarks into English as a reassuring pledge that China places great emphasis on A.I. safety and security and is committed to keeping it safe, secure and controllable. Ahead of talks with Washington, that reads like an olive branch.

What Xi actually said in Chinese, Garnaut reports, was that China’s emphasis is on “development and security,” and on making sure A.I. is “secure, reliable and controllable.” The shift looks minor. It is not. In Xi’s lexicon, security and control are terms of art that point to the political security of the Party. State media routinely softens his language for foreign audiences precisely so that the political meaning does not travel. The English-speaking world is handed a word about machine safety; the Chinese-speaking world is handed a word about regime stability. Same speech, two audiences, two entirely different promises.

This is exactly the danger Hinton gestured at, without quite naming, when he told Hanna Rosin that value alignment is naïve because different people hold different values. Here is that abstraction made concrete at the scale of a superpower. The two sides are not disagreeing about how to reach a shared definition of safety. They are using the same English word for two unrelated things.

Constitution versus catechism

Garnaut draws the contrast through the men doing the building. Today’s Silicon Valley founders grew up in a liberal democracy, marinated in science fiction about machines that promise utopia and threaten annihilation. Amodei’s January essay opened with a scene from the film Contact. His method, constitutional A.I., tries to bake principles into the model at the level of character, drawing on the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and weighting heavily toward access to information, rule of law, political freedom and individual liberty. Demis Hassabis of Google DeepMind has anchored his own A.I. principles explicitly in liberal-democratic values.

China’s new generation of technologists was raised in a different world, one where liberal values are labelled hostile and technology is developed for the Party’s benefit. Xi’s minister for state security, Chen Yixin — a name that appeared in The Diplomat’s Ceiling — published an essay in September that made no mention at all of the existential fear that preoccupies American labs, the machines turning on their makers. Instead he warned of hostile human forces using A.I. for a direct assault on China’s political, institutional and ideological security. Safety, in Chen’s telling, means pushing Chinese A.I. forward fast enough that it does not fall behind the United States, since falling behind is itself the danger.

Tang has gone further than rhetoric. In his July letter he rejected bolt-on safety patches and argued that behavioural principles, including Chinese national laws, strategy and security concerns, must be written into the foundations of the models. In August he published a commentary arguing for laws to impose ideological conformity on the entire Chinese A.I. industry. Amodei wants to teach a model what it means to be a good A.I. Tang wants to teach a model what it means to be a loyal one. The methods rhyme. The catechism does not.

The values travel with the tool

Here Garnaut’s argument turns from the philosophical to the practical, and it is the part that should worry readers who do not much care about the theology of alignment.

Chinese open-weight models are being adopted around the world. GLM-5.3, as Part 90 noted, is cheap, capable and downloadable. The values embedded in such models — the way they interpret events, what they will and will not say — travel with them wherever they are installed. As Chinese A.I. spreads globally, Garnaut warns, the Party’s worldview travels quietly with it, far beyond China’s borders.

And there is a sharper edge. Chinese citizens and companies are bound by a National Intelligence Law obliging them to support state intelligence work when asked. Z.ai’s own financial filings state that vulnerabilities its models uncover are submitted to Chinese authorities, including a national vulnerability database managed by Chen Yixin’s Ministry of State Security. Which raises the question Garnaut poses and lets hang: when one of these models finds a dangerous security flaw, who hears about it first — the company that needs to fix it, or the Chinese state that might prefer to keep it?

This connects to the sleeper-agent problem. Garnaut notes that researchers at leading American labs and allied governments are increasingly alarmed about malicious hidden instructions planted in models and left dormant until triggered, a danger amplified by exactly the kind of autonomous, system-accessing agents that Part 119 watched break into an Australian health portal. An open-weight model whose deep behaviours are hard to strip out, built by a company legally obliged to assist state intelligence, is a different kind of object from a piece of neutral software.

Where this leaves Hinton’s hope

Garnaut’s conclusion is bleak in a specific and useful way. The Communist Party’s fixation on out-competing the United States and preserving the regime, he argues, leaves little room for agreement on the catastrophic risks that keep American researchers awake. And Trump, for his own reasons, seems to feel that strategic competition outweighs the danger of unchecked development, having dismissed the need for regulation in exactly the terms Part 118 recorded.

So set the two September 24 conversations side by side. Hinton believes that once America has a rational leader, Washington and Beijing can recognise a shared existential threat and act on it together, as the Cold War superpowers did. Garnaut believes the two capitals are not looking at the same threat at all, and that the word they would use to describe their agreement would mean one thing in Washington and another in Beijing.

The summit ended the next day, and its one A.I. outcome reads, in hindsight, like a footnote to Garnaut’s essay. According to the White House fact sheet of September 25, the two countries established a “U.S.-China Super Intelligence (SI) Dialogue to exchange views on risks and benefits related to SI,” agreed to set up “a bilateral communication channel for SI incidents,” and said the next exchange “will occur by November 2026.” An incident channel is a real, if modest, thing, and Hinton would be entitled to count it. But the same document records a second agreement: “The two leaders agreed to use the term ‘super intelligence’ rather than ‘artificial intelligence’ to describe the applicable emerging technologies.” Four days later, Trump signed an executive order requiring every federal agency to do the same, on the grounds that the new name better captures the technology’s “promise, potential, and rapidly advancing capabilities.” So the two capitals did agree on a word. It was a new one, chosen for what it promises rather than for what it warns about, and nothing in Washington’s account of the summit says what either side will mean by it.

The first test of that dialogue comes this week. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the first meeting of the Super Intelligence Dialogue will take place in Shenzhen on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit on November 18 and 19, where Trump is due to meet Xi, bdnews24.com reported. “We also expect to exchange best practices for evaluating and testing advanced models before their release,” Bessent said, and he confirmed that the two sides had agreed to a direct channel for alerting each other to serious incidents. Testing models before release is the closest the two governments have come to the kind of safety Hinton means. Whether the Chinese side hears it as a promise about machines or a promise about the Party is the question Garnaut’s essay leaves hanging over Shenzhen.

The eve of Shenzhen

On the eve of the summit, the prospects look thinner than Bessent’s account suggests. The two governments have made little if any progress on A.I. safety or development guidelines, current and former American officials told Edward Wong and Dustin Volz of The New York Times. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said last week that the conversations had happened “sort of at the technical level.” “Obviously, we’ve heard their perspective, they’ve heard ours, but that’s still a work in progress,” he said. The dialogue is run by the Treasury, and critics say it barely exists. Bessent insists it will happen.

The officials’ explanation for the stall is Garnaut’s argument in plain terms. In the United States, officials, executives and researchers worry about A.I. slipping out of human control. China shares that worry to a degree, but its government appears more concerned about how people could use A.I. to undermine the Communist Party. The body that oversees A.I. in China is the Cyberspace Administration, the agency that turned a once freewheeling Chinese internet into a controlled space that security officials use for surveillance. Chen Yixin’s September essay, Wong and Volz note, called for more Party control of A.I. to prevent political instability and to compete militarily with the United States.

The history of these talks is not encouraging either. When Joe Biden’s administration opened an A.I. dialogue with Beijing in Geneva in May 2024, his aides hoped China would send officials from the agencies that actually monitor A.I., including the Ministry of State Security. China sent foreign ministry officials, and the talks went nowhere. The one concrete result came that November in Lima, when Biden and Xi agreed that humans, not A.I., must keep control of their countries’ nuclear weapons. And even if Beijing signed a broader safety agreement, a senior American intelligence official said, Washington would doubt it would be kept: in 2015 China pledged to the Obama administration not to hack American companies to steal their intellectual property, and American intelligence agencies quickly concluded it was breaking the pledge. “The Chinese view any type of outreach that we are doing as a means to really keep them down,” said Gen. Paul M. Nakasone, the retired head of the National Security Agency.

Spying now hangs over the summit as well. Last week Jensen Huang was forced to resign as Nvidia’s chief executive after The Times reported American intelligence suggesting that a Chinese businesswoman in Singapore close to him might have been working with, or in contact with, the Ministry of State Security; Huang has admitted an affair, and Beijing calls the espionage concerns a “fabrication.” That story belongs to The Shovel Seller, and this series leaves it there. But it is not the only one. Last month the cybersecurity firm Proofpoint reported that China-aligned hackers had targeted A.I. experts at American research institutes, universities and law firms, some of whom work on regulation and export controls, using emails that impersonated A.I. policymakers, including a senior one at the White House. On Monday the administration said it would go ahead with a rule limiting China’s access to advanced chips through data centres in other countries.

An incident channel depends on trust about what counts as an incident and confidence that the other side will report one. Each government, the officials told Wong and Volz, believes the other is spying to learn its A.I. secrets and get ahead faster. Garnaut warned that the two sides do not mean the same thing by safety. Shenzhen will add a second problem to the first: neither side believes the other will keep its word.

They cannot both be fully right. My own reading, for what a Canadian outsider’s reading is worth, is that Hinton is describing the destination and Garnaut is describing the terrain, and that the terrain is where the journey actually happens. It is entirely possible that a machine-safety catastrophe is a shared human risk, exactly as Hinton says, and that this shared risk is nonetheless unreachable through diplomacy because each side has quietly redefined the key word before sitting down.

Hinton reached, in the end, for the nuclear analogy and its hopeful lesson: mutual survival is a powerful motive. Garnaut would point out that the arms-control agreements worked because both sides meant the same thing by a warhead. Nobody in Moscow translated “missile” into a word that also meant “the security of the Party.”

The race, Garnaut writes in his closing, is no longer only about technological superiority. It is a contest over whose concepts of freedom, security and the role of the state get embedded in the systems that will increasingly shape how whole societies think and decide. That is a larger and stranger competition than the chip war, and it is the one Trump’s free-market shrug misses entirely.

Two conversations, then, published the same morning, with a Pacific between them and no agreed dictionary to cross it.
