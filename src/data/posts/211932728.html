by Stephen Gordon

Essay 1,522 is about the most honourable counting error ever made. I say that at the top because this series has spent fifteen hundred entries cataloguing the other kind — the inflated crowd, the rounded war, the ledger of souls — and I would not want you to brace for another one. The men in this essay counted perfectly. They wrote it all down, every single day, for three years, through mutiny and scurvy and the death of nearly everyone they sailed with. And when they got home, their books were wrong by one day.

It was the best mistake anyone has ever made. Let me tell you how they made it.

On September 20, 1519, five ships under the command of Ferdinand Magellan cleared the bar at Sanlúcar de Barrameda in Spain and turned west. The stated purpose was commerce — a westward route to the Spice Islands, whose cloves and nutmeg were worth roughly their weight in dignity. Aboard were somewhere in the neighbourhood of 270 men, and I say “in the neighbourhood” because the muster rolls disagree with one another, which is the sixteenth century’s way of reminding us that even the starting number of the greatest voyage in history is a little soft. In this series we have learned to expect that.

Among the 270 was a young Italian gentleman-adventurer named Antonio Pigafetta, who had talked his way aboard as a supernumerary and who appointed himself, informally, the expedition’s memory. Pigafetta kept a journal. Not a captain’s log of headings and soundings — a daily record, kept without fail, of everything: the weather, the coastlines, the languages of the peoples they met, the deaths, the food, and above all the days themselves, each one entered in its turn, none skipped, none doubled. If this series has a patron saint of counting, it is not the monk from the Christmas story yet to come, and it is not Luther with his ninety-five line items. It is Pigafetta, who counted every day of the first trip around the world and got every single one of them right, and was still wrong, and it wasn’t his fault, and that is the whole point of this essay.

First, though, the subtraction. Because before this becomes a story about one day, it is a story about losing nearly everything else.

Five ships left Spain. One was wrecked surveying the Patagonian coast. One deserted during the winter and slunk home. One was burned off the Philippines because there were no longer enough living men to sail her. One, the Trinidad, attempted to return east across the Pacific, failed, and fell to the Portuguese. The arithmetic of the men is worse. They wintered in Patagonia through a mutiny that ended with executions. They crossed the Pacific — which no European had done, and which Magellan, finding it calm, named “peaceful,” an act of labelling optimism this series files alongside the Hundred Years’ War — for three months and twenty days without fresh food. And here Pigafetta’s counting turns into something harder than record-keeping, because he inventoried the starvation itself, item by item, like a quartermaster of the apocalypse. The biscuit, he recorded, had broken down to a powder swarming with worms and fouled by rats, and they ate it. The drinking water had gone yellow and putrid, and they drank it. They ate sawdust. They ate the ox-hides that wrapped the mainyard rigging — leather cured stiff by years of sun and salt, which they softened by trailing it in the sea for days before grilling it. The going price aboard for a rat was half a ducat, and there were buyers, and Pigafetta noted the price the way he noted everything, because a price is a number and numbers were his office. He counted the scurvy dead of the crossing too — nineteen, by his tally — and the count is somehow worse than the description, which is a lesson this series keeps arriving at from different directions: the inventory outlasts the adjectives. Four hundred years later we know what that ocean cost because one man wrote down what a rat sold for.

And on April 27, 1521, on the island of Mactan in the Philippines, Magellan himself was subtracted — killed in the surf in a fight he did not need to pick, against a local ruler named Lapu-Lapu, who is a national hero in the Philippines to this day and is entitled to be. The expedition that history files under Magellan’s name was finished by other men. It is worth a line in this series, which has covered a president who claims credit for sunrises, that the most famous circumnavigation in history belongs, by name, to a man who made it slightly less than halfway.

The man who brought them home was a Basque named Juan Sebastián Elcano — mutineer, pardoned, promoted by attrition — and on September 6, 1522, he sailed the last ship, the Victoria, leaking and crewed by ghosts, back over the bar at Sanlúcar. Eighteen men. Eighteen, of the 270. The cargo of cloves in her hold was reportedly rich enough to turn a profit on the entire catastrophe, which tells you everything you need to know about the spice trade and most of what you need to know about accounting.

One more entry in the credit column before we get to the day, because it is the finest piece of credit-accounting a monarch ever performed, and this series does not get many chances to praise the bookkeeping of kings. The young Emperor Charles V — the same Charles, readers of essay 1,517 will note, who five hundred words of history earlier was presiding over Worms; the sixteenth century was a small room — received Elcano, heard the account, and granted him a coat of arms. The arms showed a globe. The motto beneath it read Primus circumdedisti me: “You went around me first.” Note the pronoun. The Earth itself is the speaker, addressing the sailor, conceding the record. The crown, in other words, posted the credit to the correct ledger — to Elcano, the man who actually closed the circle. And then posterity, which does its accounting by fame rather than by entry, filed the whole voyage under Magellan anyway, and Elcano’s globe sits in the footnotes. I record the correction here, four essays past number 1,500, on behalf of the audit that history never runs: the strait is Magellan’s, fairly. The circle is Elcano’s. The books should say so.

Now the day.

Two months earlier, in July 1522, the Victoria had put in at the Cape Verde Islands, Portuguese territory, to buy food. The shore party came back with rice, and with a piece of information that no amount of hunger could make sense of: ashore, it was Thursday.

Aboard, it was Wednesday.

Pigafetta’s reaction is one of my favourite moments in the whole literature of counting. He did not shrug. He audited. He had kept the journal himself, every day, without illness or interruption, and he went back through it and confirmed what he already knew: there was no missing entry. The count was perfect. And the count was wrong. The ship had somehow arrived one full day behind the rest of Christendom, carrying a flawless record of a calendar that no longer matched the world.

The explanation, when the scholars worked it through — and it genuinely puzzled learned Europe for a while, the matter being put to savants who confirmed the sailors had done nothing wrong — is the kind of thing that is obvious only afterward. The Victoria had sailed west, chasing the sun. Every day of the voyage, the ship gained a little on the sunset, and so every shipboard day was a sliver longer than a day for a man standing still in Seville. Three years of slivers add up to exactly one day. The men had lived 1,081 days by their own reckoning while Spain lived 1,082. Nobody miscounted. The travellers and the stay-at-homes had simply been keeping books in two different currencies of time, and the difference only came due when the accounts were reunited.

Here is the idea at the centre of this essay: it was the first number the Earth itself ever corrected. Every counting error before 1522 — and this series is a museum of them — was a human error: vanity, laziness, fraud, a monk’s slipped arithmetic. This one was different in kind. The books were flawless and the total was wrong, because the counters had failed to account for one thing only: their own motion. They were not standing outside the system, tallying it. They were part of it, moving with it, and the count bent around them like light around a mass. It took another three and a half centuries for the world to formalize the fix — a line drawn down the Pacific where the calendar is allowed to break, so that everyone else’s books can balance — which means the international arrangement of time itself is, in the end, a patch issued for a bug that eighteen starving men discovered by accident and reported honestly.

And that is the distinction I want to leave on the table, because the contrast does the work better than any comparison could. The age I write about produces counting errors daily, and they share a signature: they are made without moving. The crowd inflated from a podium, the ballots recounted from a golf cart, the map redrawn from a chair — errors of men who never left the room, correcting the world to match themselves. The error of 1522 is the exact inverse. It was made by men who went all the way around — who did the entire thing, completely, at a cost of five ships and 252 lives — and whose only mistake was to assume the world would hold still while they measured it. You could not earn their error without earning their voyage. It is the only counting mistake I have ever wanted to make.

Dior was briefed on the missing day this morning, on the lakeshore, and was unmoved, as I should have predicted. Dior has never lost a day in her life, because her days are not numbered but identical — breakfast, ride, patrol, sleep — and a thing that repeats perfectly cannot drift. She is, I realized somewhere around kilometre eleven, the stationary observer in this experiment. I circle; she abides; and every morning when I return to where she is, I am a sliver older than she thinks I am. She has decided not to invoice me for the difference. It is the nearest thing to grace I am offered on a weekday.

The count moves on, as it does, and the next landmark on this stretch is essay 1,543 — the year a Polish canon, dying, was handed a printed book of tables that quietly removed the Earth from the centre of the universe, and us with it. The men of 1522 discovered that the counter moves. The man of 1543 discovered that the counting-house does too. It gets worse before it gets better, if by worse you mean truer, which by now, fifteen hundred essays in, I do.

The log was perfect. The planet kept its own. Both were right, and only one of them was ours.

See you at 1,543, when we lose the centre.
