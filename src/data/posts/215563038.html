There’s a particular kind of battle that ends one era and begins another so cleanly that later generations treat it less like an event and more like a hinge. Bach Dang, 938 CE, is that kind of battle for Vietnam. Everything before it belongs to the thousand years of Chinese administrative rule I described in Part I. Everything after it — nearly a millennium of independent Vietnamese dynasties, interrupted but never permanently ended — flows from what happened on a tidal river outside what is now Hai Phong, when a regional military commander named Ngo Quyen turned Chinese naval superiority into Chinese naval catastrophe using nothing more sophisticated than iron-tipped wooden stakes and an intimate knowledge of when the tide would turn.

I want to tell this story carefully, because it’s done a huge amount of cultural work in Vietnam across the centuries since, and understanding how it’s been used matters as much as understanding what happened.

The Chaos That Made the Opportunity

To understand why 938 was the moment Chinese rule finally broke, you have to look north, at what was happening to the empire that had governed the Red River Delta as a matter of course for the better part of a millennium. The Tang dynasty, which had ruled China since 618 and which governed the Vietnamese region through an administrative structure called the Protectorate of Annam, collapsed formally in 907. What followed is remembered in Chinese historiography as the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period — a fifty-year stretch of fragmentation in which no single authority controlled all of China, and regional military governors across the old Tang empire’s periphery took the opportunity to carve out independent or semi-independent domains for themselves.

The Red River Delta, at the empire’s southern edge, was exactly the kind of periphery where this pattern played out. Local Vietnamese elites — men who had, in many cases, spent generations operating within the Chinese administrative and scholarly system, exactly the kind of partially assimilated aristocracy I described in Part I — found themselves with an opening that hadn’t existed in living memory. Central Chinese authority wasn’t just weak; for stretches, it was functionally absent. A leader named Khuc Thua Du seized administrative control of the protectorate in 905, and though he nominally continued to acknowledge Chinese suzerainty, the practical reality was Vietnamese self-governance in everything but name. His son and successor, Khuc Hao, continued this arrangement into the 910s and 920s.

Real Chinese military pressure returned in the form of the Southern Han, one of the “Ten Kingdoms,” which controlled the Canton region and had ambitions to reassert direct control over the Delta. In 930, Southern Han forces invaded and captured Khuc Thua My, ending the Khuc family’s period of quasi-autonomous rule and restoring direct — if contested — Chinese authority over the region.

This is where Duong Dinh Nghe enters the story. A local military leader, likely a former subordinate of the Khuc administration, Duong Dinh Nghe organized and led a successful uprising against the Southern Han occupation in 931, driving Chinese forces out and reestablishing Vietnamese self-rule under his own leadership. For six years, Duong Dinh Nghe governed the Delta as its de facto ruler, still nominally deferential to Chinese court forms in the way regional Vietnamese leaders had learned to be for centuries, but functionally independent.

Then, in 937, one of his own subordinates — a man named Kieu Cong Tien — murdered him and seized power, evidently hoping either to rule in his place or to use the assassination to curry favour with the Southern Han court by presenting himself as a more pliable client than his predecessor had been. This is the moment Ngo Quyen enters, and the way he enters matters: not as an abstract nationalist liberator, but as an aggrieved son-in-law and loyal subordinate seeking to avenge his patron and reclaim the position that assassination had stolen from Duong Dinh Nghe’s faction.

Ngo Quyen’s Position

Ngo Quyen was a military commander from Duong Xa, in what is now Hanoi’s outskirts, and by the time of Duong Dinh Nghe’s murder he had already built a substantial reputation and power base as one of Duong Dinh Nghe’s most capable generals — married to Duong Dinh Nghe’s daughter, which placed him inside the ruling family’s inner circle by marriage as well as by military service. When news of the assassination reached him, he moved quickly, mustering forces and marching on the seat of power to confront Kieu Cong Tien directly.

Recognizing he couldn’t defeat Ngo Quyen through his own resources alone, Kieu Cong Tien did what regional Vietnamese leaders under pressure had periodically done for centuries: he appealed to the Chinese court for military assistance, offering nominal submission in exchange for troops to secure his position. The Southern Han ruler, Liu Yan (also referred to in some sources as Liu Gong), saw an opportunity not merely to prop up a client but to reassert full and direct Southern Han control over the Delta — the ambition the Southern Han had been pursuing intermittently since their 930 invasion. He dispatched a fleet under the command of his son, Liu Hongcao, to sail up the coast and put down what he understood as an internal Vietnamese power struggle, with the deeper aim of reestablishing Chinese sovereignty over the whole region.

Before that fleet arrived, however, Ngo Quyen had already defeated and killed Kieu Cong Tien — removing the pretext, though not the ambition, for Chinese intervention. The Southern Han fleet sailed anyway. Ngo Quyen would have to face it not as an internal rival but as a foreign invading force, arriving at exactly the moment he had just consolidated power over the Delta.

The Bach Dang River

The battle that followed is where the story moves from political intrigue into genuinely inventive military engineering, and it’s worth walking through the mechanics in some detail because the tactic itself is what made this battle mythic rather than merely consequential.

The Bach Dang River, near modern Hai Phong in northern Vietnam, is a tidal estuary — meaning its water level and current direction change dramatically and predictably with the tide, a feature of coastal river systems that anyone who has spent time on tidal water understands intuitively but that a naval force unfamiliar with local conditions might badly underestimate. Ngo Quyen, whose forces knew this stretch of water intimately, ordered his men to plant a field of large wooden stakes, tipped with iron, into the riverbed at a point where the Southern Han fleet would need to pass. Crucially, the stakes were positioned and sized so that at high tide, they would be fully submerged and invisible beneath the water’s surface — a trap the incoming fleet would have no way of detecting from the surface.

Ngo Quyen’s plan then required a further layer of tactical patience: he needed to lure the Southern Han fleet into the trapped stretch of river specifically at high tide, and needed the battle to still be underway — or at least the fleet to still be in position over the stakes — when the tide began to recede. He sent a small vanguard force to provoke the Southern Han fleet into engagement and then feign retreat, drawing the pursuing Chinese ships upriver, over the submerged stake field, deeper into the trap.

The Southern Han commander, Liu Hongcao, took the bait — pursuing what he read as a fleeing, weaker force deeper into the estuary. As the engagement continued and the tide began to turn, the water level began to drop, and the previously submerged stakes began to rise toward the surface, impaling and holing the hulls of the Southern Han ships from below, immobilizing and sinking vessels that only shortly before had been sailing freely over open water. With the Chinese fleet suddenly disabled, grounded, and in disarray, Ngo Quyen’s main force — which had been holding back — attacked in force. The battle turned into a rout. Liu Hongcao himself was killed in the fighting, and the Southern Han fleet was effectively destroyed as a fighting force.

What Bach Dang Actually Ended

It’s worth being precise about what this victory did and didn’t accomplish, because the mythologized version of Vietnamese history sometimes flattens the aftermath into something cleaner than it was. Bach Dang did not end Chinese interest in the Delta — Southern Han and later Chinese dynasties would periodically probe, threaten, and in a few cases briefly reoccupy parts of Vietnamese territory over the following centuries, most significantly the Ming dynasty’s occupation in the early fifteenth century, which I’ll cover later in this series when the arc reaches Le Loi and the Lam Son uprising. What Bach Dang did accomplish was the permanent end of the specific administrative structure — direct Chinese governance of the Red River Delta as an integrated province of a Chinese empire — that had defined the region for roughly a thousand years. After 938, Vietnam would be periodically invaded, periodically threatened, and occasionally briefly occupied by Chinese forces, but it would never again be simply administered as Chinese territory in the way it had been continuously from 111 BCE onward.

Following the victory, Ngo Quyen declared himself king in 939, establishing what historians generally treat as the first fully independent Vietnamese dynasty of the post-Bắc thuộc era, and setting a precedent — Vietnamese self-rule under a Vietnamese monarch, however contested and however frequently challenged by subsequent internal power struggles and external pressure — that would continue, with interruptions, until the French colonial conquest nearly a thousand years later.

His reign itself was brief; he died in 944, and the Ngo dynasty he founded fragmented quickly afterward into a period of internal fighting among rival warlords known as the Anarchy of the Twelve Warlords, which wouldn’t be resolved until Dinh Bo Linh’s unification of the country and founding of the Dinh dynasty in 968. I mention this not to undercut the significance of Bach Dang but to be honest about the fact that independence, once won, did not immediately produce stability. Vietnamese history from this point forward is not a smooth ascent but a recurring pattern: external threat repelled, followed by internal fragmentation, followed by consolidation under a new dynasty, followed eventually by a new external threat. That pattern will recur across the Ly, Tran, Le, and Nguyen dynasties this series will move through in coming installments, and it recurs again, in a very different register, in the twentieth century.

The Battle as Myth, and Why the Myth Mattered Later

I noted in Part I that the emphasis on a thousand years of continuous Vietnamese resistance to Chinese rule is, in real part, a retrospective nationalist construction rather than a straightforward historical fact — and Bach Dang is precisely the kind of event that construction was built around. The battle did happen. The tactical details — the stakes, the tide, the lured pursuit — are reasonably well attested across multiple traditional sources, even if some of the more dramatic flourishes in later retellings likely accumulated over centuries of retelling rather than reflecting contemporary eyewitness precision. But the meaning assigned to Bach Dang — as the definitive, climactic end of a thousand years of oppression, the moment the Vietnamese nation as such threw off its chains — is a meaning constructed by later generations of Vietnamese historians, court chroniclers, and eventually twentieth-century nationalist leaders who needed exactly this kind of founding story.

And it’s worth noting: the same tactic, at the same river, would be used again. In 981, Le Hoan used a similar stake-and-tide trap on the Bach Dang to defeat another Chinese invasion force from the Song dynasty. And in 1288, Tran Hung Dao would use an even larger-scale version of the identical tactic on the same river to destroy an invading Mongol fleet — a battle I’ll cover later in this series, and one of the most celebrated military victories in all of Vietnamese history. Three times, across three and a half centuries, at the same stretch of tidal water, Vietnamese commanders defeated numerically superior naval invasions using local knowledge of tide and terrain against opponents who had underestimated exactly that kind of knowledge. That’s not myth-making — that’s a real, repeated pattern of asymmetric tactical genius exploiting geography. But the fact that Vietnamese historical memory treats these three battles as a connected lineage, a doctrine passed down and reapplied by successive generations of commanders defending the same stretch of river against successive waves of larger invaders, is itself part of how Vietnam constructed a national identity organized around the idea that small, patient, geographically knowledgeable forces reliably outlast larger, better-resourced invaders.

That idea — tested and seemingly reconfirmed across a thousand years of pre-modern history — would resurface with enormous consequence in the twentieth century, when Vietnamese communist leadership faced first a French colonial army and then the military of the United States. Ho Chi Minh and Vo Nguyen Giap both explicitly invoked this deep well of historical memory — Bach Dang, the Trung sisters, Tran Hung Dao against the Mongols — in framing their own resistance as merely the next iteration of an ancient and reliable pattern. Whether or not that framing accurately described the full complexity of Vietnam’s pre-modern relationship with Chinese civilization, it proved to be an extraordinarily effective piece of strategic and psychological infrastructure. A leadership convinced that history was on its side, and that patience plus local knowledge had always eventually defeated larger invading powers, fought differently — and asked its population to sacrifice differently — than one without that conviction.

We’re a long way from Dien Bien Phu and Tet in this series still. But the river doctrine that will define both of those later battles — small forces, superior local knowledge, patience against a larger and more heavily resourced opponent — has its origin, in Vietnamese historical memory at least, right here, on a stretch of tidal water outside Hai Phong, in 938.
