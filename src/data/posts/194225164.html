Part of the series: Israel/Palestine: The Original Sin and Its Compound Interest

By Stephen Gordon | CanadianSnowmanSays

Yasser Arafat and Yitzhak Rabin shake hands on the White House South Lawn, September 13, 1993, with President Bill Clinton between them.

On September 13, 1993, on the South Lawn of the White House, Yasser Arafat and Yitzhak Rabin shook hands.

Bill Clinton stood behind them, arms spread wide, the benevolent broker of what the world was being invited to believe was the beginning of the end of the longest running conflict of the 20th century. The photograph of that handshake — Rabin visibly reluctant, Arafat beaming, Clinton expansive — became one of the defining images of the post-Cold War moment of optimism, the sense that the world’s intractable problems were finally yielding to the forces of reason and negotiation.

Twenty-five months later, Yitzhak Rabin was shot twice in the back at a peace rally in Tel Aviv. He died on the operating table at 11:10 p.m. on November 4, 1995. His killer was a 25-year-old Israeli law student named Yigal Amir, a religious nationalist who believed that Rabin’s willingness to trade land for peace was a betrayal of God’s covenant with the Jewish people and that Jewish law permitted — required — his death.

The Oslo peace process did not die with Rabin. It continued, in increasingly desperate form, for another seven years. But it never recovered the political momentum that Rabin’s leadership had given it, and with the benefit of hindsight it is possible to say that the peace died on that operating table in Tel Aviv, even if the formal declaration of death came later.

Oslo was not simply a failed negotiation. It was a peace process that was systematically undermined, from multiple directions simultaneously, by actors who had more interest in its failure than its success. Understanding who those actors were, and why they preferred failure, is essential to understanding why there is no peace process today and why the prospect of one has become almost impossible to discuss seriously.

What Oslo Actually Was

The Oslo Accords — formally the Declaration of Principles on Interim Self-Government Arrangements — were the product of secret negotiations conducted in Oslo, Norway, between Israeli officials and PLO representatives, facilitated by Norwegian diplomats, over many months in 1992 and 1993. They were secret because both parties needed political cover: the Israeli government was legally prohibited from negotiating with the PLO, which it had designated a terrorist organization, and the PLO’s negotiators needed to reach an agreement before presenting it to their own constituencies.

Israeli settlement construction in the West Bank during the Oslo years. While negotiators discussed future borders, settlement population roughly doubled between 1993 and 2000 — creating irreversible facts on the ground that eroded the possibility of a viable Palestinian state.

What Oslo established was not a peace treaty. It was a framework for a process that was intended to lead to a peace treaty. The distinction matters enormously and is routinely collapsed in popular discussion.

Oslo established the Palestinian Authority as an interim self-governing body in parts of the West Bank and Gaza. It established a five-year negotiating timetable during which the permanent status issues — Jerusalem, refugees, borders, settlements — would be resolved. It did not resolve any of those issues. It deferred them all to final status negotiations that were supposed to begin within two years and conclude within five.

The permanent status negotiations never concluded. The deferred issues — Jerusalem, refugees, borders, settlements — remain deferred today, thirty years later, along with all the compound interest that thirty years of deferral generates.

This is the central structural problem of Oslo that its architects either did not see or chose not to acknowledge: an agreement that deferred the hardest questions to a later negotiation assumed that the political will to resolve those questions would exist later, even though the act of deferral gave every opponent of resolution additional time to create facts on the ground that made resolution harder.

The settlements grew throughout the Oslo process. While Israeli and Palestinian negotiators were meeting in Washington and Taba and Camp David to discuss the future of the West Bank, Israeli construction crews were building the infrastructure of permanent Israeli presence in the West Bank. Between 1993 and 2000 — the entire Oslo period — the settler population in the West Bank grew by approximately 50 percent. The land that was supposed to become a Palestinian state was being settled faster during the peace process than before it.

This was not a secret. It was visible, documented, and protested by Palestinian negotiators at every stage. It was allowed to continue by Israeli governments that were negotiating in one room while their housing ministries were building in another.

Rabin’s Murder and What It Meant

Yitzhak Rabin addressing a peace rally in Tel Aviv shortly before his assassination on November 4, 1995. The former general who concluded that security required territorial compromise was killed by a religious nationalist who saw Oslo as betrayal.

The assassination of Yitzhak Rabin on November 4, 1995 is the most underweighted event in the history of the Oslo process, and its underweighting is itself politically significant.

Rabin was not a dove. He was a former military chief of staff, a general who had commanded Israeli forces in 1967, a man whose entire political identity was rooted in security rather than ideology. When he concluded that a negotiated settlement with the Palestinians was Israel’s best security option, his conclusion carried a weight that no left-wing Israeli politician’s conclusion could carry. He could not be dismissed as naive about security threats. He had spent his life managing them.

His willingness to shake Arafat’s hand, to recognize the PLO, to begin the process of trading occupied territory for peace — these were decisions made by a man who understood the military balance better than almost anyone in the country. The Israeli right understood that Rabin’s credibility on security was the Oslo process’s most important asset, which is precisely why Yigal Amir killed him.

The assassination was not the act of a lone fanatic, though Amir pulled the trigger alone. It was the product of a political and religious culture within the Israeli right that had spent months delegitimizing Rabin in terms that made violence feel justified to those operating within that culture. Rabin had been depicted in demonstrations wearing a Nazi uniform and an SS officer’s cap. Rabbis had issued legal opinions — din rodef, din moser — characterizing Rabin’s actions as religiously actionable offences that could, in extreme interpretations of Jewish law, warrant the death of the offender. The political culture had prepared the ground. Amir planted the seed.

Benjamin Netanyahu attended many of the demonstrations at which Rabin was depicted in Nazi imagery. He did not produce the imagery. He did not incite the murder. He has never been accused of complicity in the assassination. What is documented is that he led a political movement that systematically delegitimized the peace process and the man leading it, and that the political environment created by that movement produced the assassination.

Netanyahu became Prime Minister for the first time in May 1996, six months after Rabin’s murder, defeating Shimon Peres — Rabin’s successor and peace partner — by less than one percentage point. His first government proceeded to slow-walk the Oslo implementation, expand settlements, and treat the peace process as a negotiating obligation to be managed rather than an opportunity to be pursued.

The trajectory from November 4, 1995 to the current government of Ben Gvir and Smotrich is not a straight line. But it is a discernible one. And Netanyahu himself has now provided its most honest summary. Speaking recently, he said: “There will be no Palestinian state to the West of the Jordan River. For years, I have prevented the creation of that terror state against tremendous pressure, both domestic and from abroad. We have done this with determination and with astute statesmanship. Moreover, we have doubled the Jewish settlement in Judea and Samaria, and we will continue on this path.”

Read that statement carefully. It does not describe a failed peace process. It describes the successful execution of a deliberate counter-project. Oslo, in Netanyahu’s own framing, was not a negotiation he participated in and watched collapse. It was an obstacle he managed and ultimately defeated. The distinction between a failed peace process and the successful destruction of one is not semantic. It determines who bears accountability for the world we are living in. Netanyahu has removed any ambiguity about where that accountability lies.

Camp David 2000: The Myth of the Generous Offer

Ehud Barak, Bill Clinton, and Yasser Arafat at Camp David, July 2000. The collapse of these final-status negotiations — after years of settlement growth — marked a point of no return for the Oslo process.

In July 2000, Bill Clinton brought Ehud Barak and Yasser Arafat to Camp David for two weeks of final status negotiations. The talks collapsed. Arafat left without an agreement. Clinton publicly blamed Arafat. Barak’s offer was characterized in Western media — and has been in countless subsequent accounts — as extraordinarily generous, the best deal the Palestinians could ever hope for, proof that Palestinian leadership was constitutionally incapable of accepting peace.

This narrative is false. Not entirely false — Arafat did make serious negotiating errors at Camp David, and the Palestinian leadership’s handling of the final status talks has been reasonably criticized by serious analysts including Palestinian ones. But the “generous offer” narrative, as it has been transmitted in Western popular discourse, is a politically constructed myth that requires correction.

What Barak actually offered at Camp David — based on the maps and documents that have since been made public and analyzed by scholars including Robert Malley, who was a member of the US negotiating team and subsequently wrote a detailed account for the New York Review of Books — was considerably less generous than the public narrative suggested.

The offer proposed a Palestinian state in approximately 91 percent of the West Bank, with the remaining 9 percent annexed to Israel to incorporate the major settlement blocs. The Palestinian state would not have been contiguous — it would have been divided into cantons by Israeli roads and settlement blocs that cut through its territory. Israeli forces would retain a military presence in the Jordan Valley for an extended period. Jerusalem would be divided in a complex arrangement that gave the Palestinians administrative control over Arab neighbourhoods and Muslim holy sites but left the question of sovereignty deliberately ambiguous. The refugee right of return would be limited to a small symbolic number — Israel was not willing to accept any arrangement that changed the demographic character of the Jewish state.

These were not unreasonable Israeli positions, given Israeli security concerns and political constraints. They were also not the transformative offer that the “most generous in history” characterization implied. They left a Palestinian state that was non-contiguous, partially demilitarized, surrounded by Israeli military presence, and unable to control its own borders. Whether Arafat should have accepted this framework as a starting point for further negotiation — rather than the final offer it was presented as — is a genuinely contested question. What is not contested is that the offer was not what the Western public was told it was.

Arafat’s failure to make a counter-proposal — his departure from Camp David without presenting an alternative framework — was a catastrophic negotiating error that Clinton was entitled to criticize. It gave Israel the diplomatic victory of appearing more flexible than it was. It damaged Palestinian credibility internationally at a critical moment. It contributed directly to the Second Intifada that followed, and to the collapse of the Israeli political centre that had supported the peace process.

But the collapse of Camp David was not simply Arafat’s fault. It was the product of a negotiating process that had allowed settlement expansion to continue for seven years, that presented the Palestinians with a take-it-or-leave-it final offer after a framework of ongoing deferral had eroded their negotiating position, and that gave them seventy-two hours to accept or reject terms that affected the national aspirations of five million people.

The Second Intifada and the Death of the Israeli Left

Scenes from the Second Intifada (2000–2005). Palestinian suicide bombings destroyed the Israeli political centre that had supported Oslo, while the ensuing cycle of violence radicalized both sides and buried the peace process.

In September 2000, Ariel Sharon — then leader of the Likud opposition — walked onto the Temple Mount with a large security escort in what was understood by Palestinians and by most Israeli security officials as a deliberate provocation. The Second Intifada erupted the following day.

The causal relationship between Sharon’s visit and the Intifada is contested. Palestinian violence had been escalating in the weeks before the visit, and Israeli intelligence assessments from the period suggest that Palestinian militant organizations were prepared for a confrontation regardless of the specific trigger. But Sharon’s visit provided the trigger, and his advisers understood that it would. It was, in the most precise sense, a political act dressed as a religious one — the assertion of Israeli sovereignty over the most sensitive piece of real estate in the world, at the most sensitive moment in the peace process, by the man who had spent his career opposing that peace process.

The Second Intifada lasted from 2000 to approximately 2005 and killed over 1,000 Israelis and over 3,000 Palestinians. The suicide bombings that Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad carried out against Israeli civilians — on buses, in restaurants, at a Passover seder — were war crimes by any legal definition and were experienced by Israeli society as an existential threat. They destroyed the Israeli political centre.

The Israeli voters who had supported Oslo — who had believed that a negotiated solution was achievable, that Palestinian partners existed, that the risk of territorial withdrawal was manageable — watched the peace process produce a wave of suicide bombings that killed their neighbours on the Number 18 bus in Jerusalem. The political conclusion they drew was the one that Sharon and the Israeli right had always argued: there was no partner, there was no peace, there was only security, and security required force.

The Israeli peace movement — which had been a significant political force in the 1990s, capable of putting hundreds of thousands of people in the streets in support of Oslo — never recovered from the Second Intifada. The political parties that had carried the peace process — Labour, Meretz — were progressively marginalized in subsequent elections. The centre of Israeli politics moved rightward and has continued moving rightward ever since.

This is the political consequence of a peace process that was undermined by settlement expansion, assassinated in the form of its most credible champion, presented with a final offer that was less generous than advertised, and then used as the justification for a campaign of suicide bombing that destroyed the Israeli constituency that might have sustained it.

Oslo did not fail because peace was impossible. It failed because specific actors made specific decisions that ensured its failure. The settlers who expanded their presence throughout the process. The rabbis who issued religious opinions that delegitimized its architects. The man who pulled the trigger in Tel Aviv. The Palestinian militant organizations whose suicide bombers killed the Israeli political centre. The negotiators who presented a flawed offer as a final one. The political leaders on both sides who found the continuation of conflict more useful than its resolution.

These are not abstract forces. They are people, institutions, and decisions. They have names.

What Died With Oslo

Oslo’s death left a specific political landscape that has defined everything since.

On the Israeli side: a political culture in which the peace camp is electorally marginal, in which security maximalism is the default political position, in which the settlement enterprise has so deeply embedded itself in Israeli society — economically, institutionally, ideologically — that no government can dismantle it without triggering a domestic political crisis of potentially revolutionary proportions.

On the Palestinian side: a fractured political movement in which the PA, discredited by its corruption and its failure to deliver a state, governs the West Bank with dwindling legitimacy, while Hamas — which won the 2006 Palestinian legislative elections on an anti-corruption platform and was then internationally boycotted for winning them — controls Gaza with an armed resistance theology that has made its people the hostages of its strategic choices.

On the international side: a peace process framework that everyone nominally supports and nobody seriously pursues, in which the two-state solution is invoked ritually at every diplomatic occasion and undermined practically by the settlement expansion that every invocation fails to stop.

This is the world that Oslo’s murder created. It is the world in which October 7th happened. It is the world in which Gaza is rubble and Lebanon is targeted and the question of whether any political solution exists has become genuinely difficult to answer in the affirmative.
