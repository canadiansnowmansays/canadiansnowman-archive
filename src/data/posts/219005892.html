On Wednesday, September 23, 2026, the head of sustainability at the most valuable company in the world walked onto a stage in Manhattan and made a promise with no date attached to it.

Josh Parker, who runs Nvidia’s sustainability programme, was a guest at The New York Times’ annual Climate Forward event, in a session called “Building the A.I. Economy.” The question the organisers put to him was a fair one: what can be done to keep the AI boom from increasing emissions and further warming the planet? His answer, as The New York Times’ Claire Brown reported the same afternoon, was that artificial intelligence has “huge potential to drive net emissions reductions.”

He did not say how. He did not say when. Brown was polite enough to note both omissions in her second sentence.

Readers of this series will recognise the shape of the argument, because it is the same shape as nearly every argument examined here since Part I. The cost is real, measurable, and arriving now. The benefit is enormous, speculative, and scheduled for later. The trick is to put both in the same column and call the result a net.

The Word Doing the Work

Everything in Parker’s pitch hangs on one small word. “What’s really important,” he said, “is what is the net impact of the technology.” In his telling, the right way to judge AI is not by its direct emissions but by what remains after you subtract the energy it will someday save in manufacturing, buildings, and transportation, plus whatever it might contribute to what he called “moonshots,” such as nuclear fusion.

In principle, there is nothing wrong with net accounting. Every business case ever written weighs costs against benefits. The problem is not the arithmetic. It is the timing and the evidence on each side of the equals sign.

A company’s own books do not work this way. Nvidia does not report next decade’s hoped-for revenue in this quarter’s earnings and then invite analysts to judge its performance on the “net.” Securities regulators would have views on that. Costs are recorded when they are incurred. Revenue is recognised when it is earned. That discipline is the whole point of an accounting standard: it stops a company from paying today’s bills with tomorrow’s optimism.

Parker’s framing asks the public to accept, on the carbon ledger, a method that no auditor would allow on the financial one. The emissions are booked now. The offsets are booked on faith.

What the Ledger Actually Says

Here is what can be counted.

The data-centre boom has pushed U.S. electricity demand upward for the first time in decades. That demand is being met, in large part, by new natural gas turbines and diesel backup generators, and Brown’s summary of the outlook was blunt: there is no end in sight.

Part XVII of this series traced where some of that power is coming from. The Environmental Integrity Project counted 82 gas plants tied to data centres nationwide, 39 of them in Texas. Amazon’s proposed plant in Pecos County would emit 33 million tons of carbon dioxide a year, more than twice the largest existing U.S. plant, in open contradiction of the company’s own Climate Pledge. Those are not projections of what AI might do. They are permits.

And then there is Nvidia’s own disclosure. By the company’s fiscal 2026 sustainability report, its supply-chain emissions nearly tripled in two years. Greenpeace East Asia’s reading of the report, published on June 12, 2026, put the figures side by side: about 3.6 million tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent in fiscal 2024, about 10.7 million tonnes in fiscal 2026. Those indirect, or Scope 3, emissions make up more than 99 per cent of the company’s total footprint. Several other large technology companies have reported similar surges over the same period. The numbers are not in dispute. They come from the companies themselves.

Two days before Parker took the stage, a coalition of eight groups including Greenpeace International, Friends of the Earth and Stand.earth stretched the comparison back further. Euronews reported on September 21, 2026 that the coalition’s report found Nvidia’s supply-chain emissions had risen 725 per cent since 2020, from 1.3 million tonnes to 10.7 million, and estimated that running the Nvidia chips sold since 2022 could have generated between 4 and 21 million tonnes in 2025 alone. That range is wide, which is itself a finding: nobody, including the seller, publishes the number that would narrow it.

Nvidia’s answer on this front is that it purchased or generated enough clean electricity in fiscal 2026 to match all of its global electricity use, as The American Bazaar noted in its September 29, 2026 report. That is a real commitment. It also covers only the electricity Nvidia itself buys, which belongs to the slice of the footprint that sits under 1 per cent. The other 99 per cent lives mostly in the supply chain, above all the factories that make the chips.

So the gross figure is known, sourced, and rising. The net figure is a sentence spoken on a stage.

Fusion, One Year Sooner

Parker’s most striking claim was also his most revealing. Even if AI only speeds up commercially viable fusion, or advanced fission, by a single year, he argued, the resulting carbon savings, energy abundance, and affordability would be “really, really dramatic.”

It is worth sitting with the structure of that argument, because it cannot lose.

Fusion has been thirty years away for roughly seventy years. No one can say when a commercial plant will come online, which means no one can say when it would have come online without AI. The counterfactual is unknowable. If fusion arrives in 2045, the claim is that AI moved it up from 2046. If it arrives in 2060, the claim is that AI moved it up from 2061. If it never arrives, the claim was only ever about “potential.” There is no outcome that could prove the statement false, and a statement that cannot be proven false is not an accounting entry. It is a sermon.

This series met the same move in Part XIII, when Elon Musk suggested that AI revenue from his orbital compute venture could eventually exceed everything else his companies earn combined. The verdict then was that the claim could not be tested until real unit economics existed. The verdict on fusion-by-a-year is the same. A benefit whose size depends on an unknowable date cannot be subtracted from a cost with a known one.

Parker returned to the theme on October 5, 2026, in an essay for Fortune headlined “NVIDIA Head of Sustainability: how to accelerate America’s nuclear energy renaissance”. The word “net” does not appear in it. What does appear is the commercial logic underneath: “AI is now accelerating interest by creating a massive, well-capitalized customer base that is willing to invest in clean power and next-generation nuclear technologies.” He offered two examples of AI speeding the work along. “Commonwealth Fusion Systems is using Omniverse libraries and OpenUSD to compress years of fusion experimentation into weeks,” he wrote, and TerraPower is using “an NVIDIA Omniverse-powered platform” to model advanced nuclear siting and design. Omniverse is an Nvidia product. The fusion acceleration on which the net-impact argument leans is, in the examples its sustainability chief chose, also a sale.

To be fair to the utopian case, it is not pure vapour. The Asimov Gap, the governance series under the same umbrella as this one, recorded in Part 99 a real instance of AI doing science: a Stanford “virtual lab” of AI agents that helped redesign a vaccine protein and flagged a lung-cancer target later corroborated by a drug trial, for about $46 in computing costs. Evidence of that kind is worth having. But it is evidence that AI can accelerate particular pieces of research. It is not evidence of how many tons of carbon those pieces of research will eventually displace, or when. An example is not an offset.

The Company on Both Sides of the Table

It matters who is making the argument.

Nvidia is a $5 trillion company. On August 26, 2026, it reported $59.7 billion in quarterly net income on $96.2 billion in revenue, more than double the prior year, with data-centre sales making up 92 per cent of the total. It controls an estimated 90 per cent of the U.S. market for the advanced chips that power artificial intelligence. And, as Brown noted, its fortunes are closely intertwined with those of its data-centre customers, some of which it also finances.

That last detail is the one this series keeps returning to. Part III examined the circular financing between Nvidia and OpenAI, in which money flows out as investment and back in as chip orders. Part V examined Nvidia’s $105 billion guarantee behind the Piketon, Ohio data-centre project, and Jensen Huang’s insistence that the arrangement was not circular, allegedly. The pattern in both cases was a company standing on both sides of the transaction, funding the demand for its own product.

The net-impact argument extends that pattern from the balance sheet to the atmosphere. Every data centre that gets built is a customer for Nvidia. Every data centre that gets blocked is a lost sale. When the company’s sustainability chief tells a climate audience that the true measure of AI is its net effect, he is not a neutral party offering a methodology. He is the seller explaining why the purchase is good for you.

That does not make him wrong. It makes him an interested party, and interested parties should be expected to show their work. Parker did not.

The Local Timber Problem

Parker did offer specifics on one front. Nvidia, he said, is working to make data centres more energy-efficient, and he cited the company’s efforts to build facilities using local timber. “Data centers absolutely can be sustainable,” he said.

Efficiency is a genuine good, and building with timber rather than concrete and steel does reduce a structure’s embodied carbon. Nobody should sneer at it. But efficiency has a well-known habit of disappointing the people who expect it to reduce total consumption.

The Asimov Gap explored this in Part 19, through Annie Lowrey’s reporting on the Jevons paradox. The nineteenth-century economist William Stanley Jevons observed that as steam engines burned coal more efficiently, Britain burned more coal, not less. Cheaper work meant more work got done. The same logic applies to computation. Every efficiency gain in a data centre lowers the cost of a unit of AI, and a lower cost of AI invites more AI. Parts VII and XVI of this series documented where that leads: companies tracking their token spend the way they once tracked oil, and “tokenmaxxing” leaderboards ranking employees by how much AI they consume.

A more efficient data centre built from local timber is still a data centre. If it makes AI cheap enough that ten more are built, the timber is a rounding error.

What an Honest Ledger Would Look Like

None of this means AI’s climate case is hopeless, or that Parker’s hopes are insincere. It means the case is being made in the wrong currency. If the industry believes its net impact will be positive, there is a straightforward way to demonstrate it, and it would not take a moonshot.

First, report the gross. Direct emissions, supply-chain emissions, and the emissions of the power plants built to serve the load, published together and plainly, before any offsets are mentioned. Nvidia has already started on this in its sustainability report. The industry as a whole has not.

Second, date the benefits. A claimed emissions saving in manufacturing or buildings should come with a year attached and a way to check it when that year arrives. A saving without a date is a hope.

Third, attribute them honestly. If AI-optimised building controls cut a factory’s energy use, some of that saving belongs to the controls, some to the engineers, some to the rising electricity prices that made the factory care in the first place. Claiming the whole of it for AI is the carbon equivalent of the depreciation problem Michael Burry flagged in Part III: the numbers look better because the assumptions were chosen to make them look better.

A company that did those three things could make its net-impact argument with a straight face. Until then, the argument is a promissory note written by the party that collects on it.

The Same Stage, Earlier the Same Day

The most eloquent reply to Parker came before he ever walked on stage.

At 12:15 that afternoon, Virginia Governor Abigail Spanberger had described residents near data centres in her state, which has more data centres than any other state, putting mattresses against their windows to muffle the noise of the cooling systems. She described diesel backup generators worsening childhood asthma. Earlier still, Erin Brockovich and Tennessee state representative Justin Pearson had talked about pollution from gas turbines and diesel generators near data centres, and about estimates that it could kill dozens of people a year.

Parker heard a blunter version of the same reply in person. At a Climate Week NYC panel on A.I. and climate that same day, September 23, student protesters organised with the Sunrise Movement interrupted him, The Business Standard reported on September 29, 2026, accusing the company of greenwashing and criticising the organisers for inviting A.I. executives to speak on climate at all. One student, according to The American Bazaar, shouted: “Parker, shame on you! My family in India are dying from drought because of you.” A single drought cannot be traced to a single company’s chips, and the student’s arithmetic would not survive an audit any better than Parker’s. But the two claims share a structure. Each assigns a global effect to a particular cause. Only one of them was made by someone with a sustainability report to back it up, and that report shows the emissions going the wrong way.

Those people are living in the gross. The mattress is not waiting for fusion. The child with asthma is not waiting for the net.

That is the quiet dishonesty of the net-impact frame, however sincerely it is offered. It takes costs that fall on specific people in specific places now, in Loudoun County and Memphis and Pecos County, and averages them against benefits that will fall, if they fall at all, on everyone everywhere later. The arithmetic may even balance someday. But the people paying the cost and the people receiving the benefit are not the same people, and they are not paying and receiving at the same time.

A ledger that nets those two columns together has not measured anything. It has only hidden who is paying.
