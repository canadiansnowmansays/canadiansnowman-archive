On November 27, 2026, the Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunications Commission did something it does not do often enough. It looked at a fee, called it by its proper name, and told the three largest carriers in the country to stop charging it.

As Jeff Lagerquist reported for CBC News, the Commission ruled that Bell’s cellphone handling charge, Rogers’ cellphone setup charge and Telus’s SIM and eSIM provisioning fee are all prohibited under the Telecommunications Act. The carriers have until December 27 to stop collecting them. In the same announcement the regulator told Bell it is considering a fine of up to $10 million for selling phones locked to its network, and it formally refused Telus’s request to begin locking phones for 60 days.

That is a good day’s work, and I want to give the Commission its due before I complain. Regular readers will know that I am about to complain.

What was decided

The story starts in June. Under Telecom Regulatory Policy 2026-43, which took effect on June 12, 2026, the CRTC banned fees for activating, changing or cancelling a phone or internet plan. The connection fee most Canadians have paid at one time or another, anywhere from $30 to $80, was gone.

Then it came back under other names. Bell had already replaced its connection fee with a $40 device handling charge in May. Telus replaced its own with a $15 SIM charge, which, as MobileSyrup noted at the time, applies at Koodo as well. Rogers followed within days of the ban taking effect: a $40 device setup charge, a $25 shipping charge for phones ordered online, and a SIM fee it never put a number on, according to the Commission’s Telecom Notice of Consultation CRTC 2026-155.

On November 27 the Commission sorted those charges into two piles. Bell’s handling charge, Rogers’ setup charge and Telus’s SIM and eSIM fee are prohibited. Rogers’ $25 shipping charge is allowed, and so are the fees Rogers and Telus charge to replace a SIM card that has been lost or broken.

The line is a sensible one. A carrier cannot charge you for the thing you need to get service in the first place. It can charge you for an optional convenience, like having a phone mailed to your door rather than picking it up in a store, and it can charge you for replacing something you lost. Keldon Bester of the Canadian Anti-Monopoly Project made the point plainly to CBC: nobody can use a mobile network without a SIM card, so pricing the card separately works against the Commission’s whole aim of making it easy to change carriers.

Six months of a prohibited fee

The decision is right. The timetable is the problem.

The carriers were not taken by surprise. Commission staff had warned all three in writing, as the charges appeared in May and June, that they looked to be activation fees in disguise. The carriers kept charging them and argued they were optional. Sheehan Carter, the CRTC’s acting vice-president of consumer analytics and strategy, told reporters on November 27 that because the companies chose to keep collecting the fees and to argue they were allowed, the Commission moved to settle the question.

Settling it took a show-cause proceeding, and the carriers did not make it quick. Telus asked the Commission to split the case in two and to remove staff it said had already made up their minds. The Commission rejected that on August 14, as iPhone in Canada reported. The ruling came on November 27, and the deadline to comply is December 27.

From the day the ban took effect to the day the carriers must stop is 198 days. For Bell, whose handling charge predates the ban, the run is longer still. Nothing in the November 27 reporting says anyone gets a refund.

Matt Hatfield of OpenMedia gave CBC the line I have been trying to write since Whack-a-Mole in June. He called the ruling “just one round of whack-a-mole in a game customers always lose,” and added that the carriers profited from these fees while they lasted and will now go looking for the next ones.

He is right, and I would put it in accounting terms. A fee that brings in money for six months and then has to be dropped, with nothing paid back, is not a loss for the company. It is a six-month product.

“Real costs”

Rogers told CBC it disagrees with the Commission on the setup charge, and said its charges help recover real costs. It welcomed the parts of the ruling that let it keep the shipping and SIM replacement fees. Telus said every SIM and eSIM comes with real costs, and that those costs should be reflected in what the product sells for, rather than carriers being made to hand it over free.

I spent 18 years in the Office of the President at Rogers, handling the complaints that had escalated past every other level: from the front lines, from regulators, from the media and from the executive floor. I left in 2021, and I will not pretend to know what was said in the rooms where these particular fees were designed. But I know the sentence. I heard it many times.

“Recovers real costs” is true of every charge any company has ever made, including the monthly price of the plan. That is where the cost of a SIM card has always lived. A carrier that needs to recover more for SIM cards can raise the price of its plans. What it should not do, and what the Commission has now said it cannot do, is take that cost out of the advertised price and present it at the counter, where it is harder to see and harder to compare.

The complaints that reached my desk were seldom about how big a charge was. They were about the surprise.

The structure of the market lets this happen. CBC cited a CRTC report released in February: as of 2024, Bell, Telus and Rogers together had nearly 90 per cent of mobile wireless sales and about 86 per cent of subscribers. When three companies hold that much of a market and all three bring in matching fees within weeks of one another, the customer has nowhere to go to escape them. Competition is supposed to punish that kind of fee. In Canadian wireless, only the regulator can, and it takes six months.

Three and a half hours

The phone-locking finding tells the same story more starkly.

The CRTC banned locked phones, and the fees carriers charged to unlock them, in 2017. The point was to let people take their phone to whichever carrier gave them a better deal. Bell began locking new phones for 60 days in April 2025, telling the Commission it was trying to cut theft and fraud at its stores. The Commission ordered it to stop in November 2025, in a letter from Secretary General Marc Morin. The period now under examination runs from April 23, 2025 to August 14, 2026, according to iPhone in Canada. That is roughly 16 months of a practice the Wireless Code did not allow, including more than eight months after the regulator told Bell in writing to stop.

In August the Commission temporarily relaxed the rule, after Bell and Telus argued that unlocked phones were drawing thieves and fraudsters. Carriers may now lock a phone for up to two business days, after which it unlocks itself, and a customer can ask for an immediate unlock at no charge. On November 27 it denied Telus’s separate application to lock phones for 60 days. Carter said the Commission had seen no evidence that Telus had actually sold locked phones, only that it had planned to, so no fine was warranted.

For Bell, Carter said the Commission is considering a penalty of up to $10 million, at the upper end of what the Telecommunications Act permits. It is worth setting that beside a number CBC printed in the same story. In early November, BCE, Bell’s parent, reported $6.18 billion in operating revenue for the three months ending September 30. Spread evenly over that quarter’s 92 days, that is about $2.8 million an hour. The largest fine the Commission is considering comes to roughly three and a half hours of Bell’s revenue.

I am not criticizing the Commission for that. It can only impose the penalties Parliament has given it. But a fine that size is a bill, not a deterrent, and a company that weighs a bill against 16 months of locked-in customers can do the arithmetic as well as I can.

My own carrier

I am a Koodo customer, which makes me a Telus customer, which means the $15 SIM charge was aimed at people like me.

Regular readers know how this series began: my partner’s move from Fido, a Rogers brand, to Koodo in December 2025, four days into a billing cycle, and a bill for the full month. Had that move happened this summer, it would have cost twice. The Koodo end would have carried a $15 SIM charge, a fee for the way in, and the CRTC has now ruled it prohibited. The Fido end carried more than $38 for days of service my partner would never use, a fee for the way out. Nobody has ruled on that one yet.

The fee on the way out

Advance billing works like this. Canadian carriers charge for each month of service at the start of the month. If you cancel or switch partway through, you have already paid for days you will not use, and you rarely see that money again. The longer you have left in the cycle, the more it costs you to leave.

Call it what you like. In practice it is a charge for cancelling, and Telecom Regulatory Policy 2026-43 bans fees for cancelling.

When I first raised this with the Commission in January, its director wrote back in February calling advance billing “a common industry practice” and “a business decision.” That is essentially the defence the carriers made for their SIM and setup charges: that these were ordinary commercial choices, priced to recover real costs. On November 27 the Commission rejected that defence for the way in. On November 30 it has a chance to reject it for the way out.

November 30 in Gatineau

On Monday, November 30, the Commission opens its public hearing in Gatineau on Notice of Consultation 2026-134, the proceeding to merge Canada’s separate consumer protection codes into one. My intervention, filed in July as number 14 on the record, asks the Commission to prohibit advance billing and require carriers to bill for service already provided. Failing that, it asks for an up-to-date balance at the moment of cancellation and late fees calculated only on what is actually owed. In July the Commission confirmed that advance billing falls within the scope of the proceeding.

The record has been instructive. The Public Interest Advocacy Centre quoted my Fido story in its own filing, along with other individual consumers who raised the same complaint, but it did not adopt a ban as one of its formal recommendations. None of the industry filings touch the subject. Of the individual interventions I have read that deal with billing practices, well over sixty support ending advance billing in one form or another. I found one clear dissent.

Fill in the holes

Whack-a-mole is what you get when you regulate fees by name. Ban the “activation fee” and you get a “device handling charge.” Ban that and something else will turn up in spring with a new name and a cost-recovery rationale already written.

The way out of the game is to regulate by principle. You pay for service you can use, at a price you were shown, and you stop paying when you stop receiving it. That one rule covers the SIM fee, the setup charge and the month you paid for after you left. It does not need updating every time a marketing department finds a new noun.

The November 27 decision shows the Commission can see a disguised fee when it looks hard enough. The hearing that opens on November 30 is its chance to stop waiting for the next disguise.

The carriers have until December 27 to comply, two days after Christmas. For once, the present is ours.
