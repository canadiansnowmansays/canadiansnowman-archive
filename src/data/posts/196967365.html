Twenty-two essays into this series, I have documented solar, wind, nuclear, fusion, batteries, sodium-ion, space-based systems, EVs, EREVs, policy failures, political buyouts, and the smell of natural gas turbines in a Memphis neighbourhood where the cancer rate is four times the national average.

I have not, until today, written about the energy source that sits underneath all of us, requires no fuel, produces virtually no emissions, runs twenty-four hours a day regardless of weather, occupies a footprint smaller than a suburban parking lot, and — as of this spring — can also produce the lithium the battery revolution needs without opening a single new mine.

I should have written this essay months ago. The technology got ahead of me.

A Hole in Cornwall That Changes the Conversation

United Downs geothermal plant in Cornwall – the exact 1.5-acre site producing both 3 MW power and lithium carbonate.

In southwest England, near Redruth in Cornwall, a company called Geothermal Engineering Limited has done something the United Kingdom has never done before at commercial scale. At a site called United Downs, they drilled more than five kilometres into granite, hit temperatures exceeding 190°C, pulled up mineral-rich brine, harvested the heat to generate electricity, extracted battery-grade lithium carbonate from the same fluid, and sent the cooled water back underground in a closed loop.

Two outputs from one hole. Clean electricity and critical minerals. From 1.5 acres of land — roughly the size of a small retail lot.

The power plant uses a binary system — an Organic Rankine Cycle that transfers heat from the brine into a secondary fluid that vaporises more easily, spinning a turbine without venting anything to the atmosphere. The headline numbers are modest: 3 megawatts, enough for about 10,000 homes, at a cost of approximately $68 million. The lithium output is about 100 metric tons of lithium carbonate equivalent per year at current scale.

Those numbers are small. But what they represent is not. United Downs is a proof of concept that clean baseload electricity and critical mineral extraction can happen simultaneously, from the same infrastructure, on a footprint that would fit inside a parking garage. Octopus Energy founder Greg Jackson put the value proposition simply: “Bills are still too high, and the answer is more homegrown, renewable energy.”

Geothermal is the quietest revolution in the energy transition. And it’s about to get very loud.

Fervo Energy and the American Breakthrough

While Cornwall was drilling into granite, a Houston-based startup called Fervo Energy was doing something that may prove even more consequential in the American Southwest.

Fervo’s Cape Station project in Beaver County, Utah is expected to deliver first power in late 2026, reaching 100 megawatts by early 2027 and scaling to 500 megawatts by 2028. The company holds permits to develop an additional 1.5 gigawatts at the same site, with total capacity potential estimated at 4.3 gigawatts. Fervo raised over $1 billion between 2022 and 2025, including a $462 million Series E in December 2025. It recently filed for an IPO.

But here’s the detail that connects this essay to everything I’ve written about data centres and the AI energy crisis: Fervo has signed a 3-gigawatt framework agreement with Google to advance potential power offtake for current and planned data centres. Google has also signed a 115-megawatt enhanced geothermal deal through Nevada’s NV Energy, and a separate 150-megawatt conventional geothermal agreement with Ormat Technologies. Meta has signed 150-megawatt deals with both XGS Energy and Sage Geosystems. Microsoft has linked 120 megawatts of geothermal power to data centres in Iceland through ENEL.

In 2025 alone, tech giants signed 14 geothermal power purchase agreements totalling 635 megawatts — three times the 2024 figure. Data centres now drive 60 percent of new geothermal development. The same companies that signed Trump’s voluntary ratepayer protection pledge in March are quietly building the actual solution underneath the desert.

The numbers that should make every energy policymaker in North America pay attention: a 2025 study published in PNAS calculated that next-generation geothermal, deployed at depths down to five kilometres across the contiguous United States, could produce more than 5 terawatts of power. That is roughly four times America’s total generating capacity today. Stanford researchers found that almost 90 percent of the United States could access geothermal energy at less than $80 per megawatt-hour — competitive with nuclear and biomass. Fervo’s Cape Station is already demonstrating commercial viability at $79 per megawatt-hour without subsidy.

The U.S. Geological Survey estimates 135 to 150 gigawatts of enhanced geothermal potential in the Great Basin alone. Geothermal investment in North America soared 85 percent in the first quarter of 2025, reaching $1.7 billion in public funding. The Department of Energy announced $171.5 million for next-generation geothermal field tests in February 2026.

Wood Mackenzie’s assessment is worth quoting: “Next-generation geothermal technologies are fundamentally changing the energy landscape by making this reliable, 24/7 clean power source available almost anywhere.”

Almost anywhere. Remember that phrase.

The Technology That Borrowed From Fracking

Here’s the part of the geothermal story that should appeal to anyone who appreciates irony.

Enhanced geothermal systems — the technology driving Fervo and its competitors — use horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing techniques adapted directly from the oil and gas industry. The same technology that unlocked the shale gas revolution is now being repurposed to access clean, inexhaustible heat from deep rock formations.

The concept dates to the early 1970s, when researchers at Fenton Hill, New Mexico drilled into hot, impermeable rock and showed that forcing high-pressure fluid into the formation could create permeability — opening existing cracks or creating new ones through which water could circulate to draw heat to the surface. For fifty years, it remained a laboratory curiosity. What changed was the drilling technology developed by the oil and gas industry: horizontal wells, multi-stage completions, advanced sensors, and the operational expertise to drill deep, fast, and precisely.

Fervo has achieved drilling rates of 30 metres per hour in crystalline rock. Nature Reviews Clean Technology reported in January 2026 that enhanced geothermal systems advanced from demonstration to commercial-scale deployment in 2025, with new pilots exceeding 330°C enabling breakthroughs toward accessing supercritical resources. Power densities of approximately 30 megawatts per square kilometre are now being achieved — which means enormous output from tiny footprints.

For a writer who has spent considerable time documenting the fossil fuel industry’s resistance to change, there is something deeply satisfying about watching its own technology become the instrument of its succession.

Lithium Without a Mine

The dual-output model that Cornwall is proving — electricity plus lithium from the same well — deserves its own section, because it connects directly to everything this series has covered about the battery supply chain.

The International Energy Agency expects lithium demand to grow approximately fivefold by 2040. As I documented in earlier essays, the battery revolution depends on securing lithium supply without replicating the environmental damage of traditional mining. Direct lithium extraction from geothermal brines offers precisely that.

The U.S. Department of Energy has committed a $1.4 billion loan to EnergySource Minerals for a direct lithium extraction plant near the Salton Sea in California — one of the richest geothermal brine deposits in the world. Trial operations are slated for 2026, with full production expected by end of 2027. The DOE’s own analysis found that direct lithium extraction takes less time, has a higher recovery rate, has a smaller footprint, and requires 99 percent less water per ton of lithium than other mining processes.

In April 2026, the DOE announced up to $69 million specifically for technologies that advance mineral extraction combined with geothermal energy. Controlled Thermal Resources is developing a 600-megawatt complex in California’s Imperial County that will co-produce geothermal power and lithium, along with potentially rare earth elements, potassium, zinc, and manganese.

Cornwall’s United Downs is producing 100 metric tons of lithium carbonate equivalent per year at proof-of-concept scale. That’s a modest number. But scale it across the geothermal brines of the Salton Sea, the Upper Rhine Graben in Germany, the Western Canadian Sedimentary Basin, and suddenly you have a distributed, low-impact lithium supply chain that doesn’t require strip-mining the Atacama Desert or negotiating with the DRC.

Two outputs. One hole. No mine. The elegance of it is almost unreasonable.

And Now: Canada

DEEP Earth Energy drilling site near Estevan, Saskatchewan – real oilfield-style rig repurposed for geothermal.

This is where I’ve been heading.

The Cascade Institute published research in October 2025 providing a techno-economic analysis of enhanced geothermal systems in western and northwestern Canada. In June 2025, they released a report identifying best practices to help Canadian provinces establish effective geothermal regulations. Their conclusion: Canada can and should become a global leader in ultra-deep geothermal electricity production.

The raw potential is staggering. Canada’s geothermal resource potential has been estimated at 5,000 megawatts using currently available technology — enough to support 8,500 permanent jobs and 20,000 construction jobs. The Western Canadian Sedimentary Basin, stretching beneath Alberta, Saskatchewan, and British Columbia, contains extensive hot sedimentary aquifers that are ideal for geothermal development. Recent research on Baker Lake, Nunavut revealed that previously dismissed regions of the Canadian Shield may hold viable deep geothermal resources, with measured gradients significantly higher than earlier national estimates.

DEEP Earth Energy, based in Saskatoon, has partnered with oilfield services company SLB to develop Canada’s first commercial-scale geothermal power facility near Estevan, on the Saskatchewan-North Dakota border. Phase 1 involves drilling two wells; Phase 2 could scale to 18 wells producing 30 megawatts. The project leverages exactly the infrastructure, expertise, and workforce that the oil and gas industry already has in place.

That last point is critical, and it is one that should resonate with every Canadian policymaker and petroleum worker reading this. The skills required for enhanced geothermal — drilling, well completion, reservoir management, fluid handling, subsurface modelling — are precisely the skills that hundreds of thousands of Canadian oil and gas workers already possess. This is not a transition that requires retraining a workforce from scratch. It requires redirecting one.

And here’s the timing: the World Geothermal Congress is coming to Calgary in June 2026. Alberta, the province that built its economy on pulling hydrocarbons from underground, is about to host the global conversation about pulling clean energy from the same formations.

Only Alberta, British Columbia, and Nova Scotia have created regulatory frameworks for geothermal development so far. There is no national strategy, no coordinated R&D agenda, and insufficient financial tools to de-risk early-stage projects. RBC’s Climate Action Institute called this out directly in February 2026, noting that Canada’s geothermal industry is less developed than in comparable countries — despite having some of the best geological conditions on earth.

From the porch up here, the picture is frustratingly clear. Canada is sitting on an energy resource that could provide baseload clean power to remote northern communities, decarbonise heating across the prairies, leverage the existing oil and gas workforce, and potentially co-produce critical minerals from the same wells. We have the geology. We have the workforce. We have the technology — it was literally developed by our own industry for other purposes.

What we don’t have is a plan.

The Source Nobody’s Talking About

U.S. geothermal resources map (NREL) – shows why the Great Basin and Southwest are so promising for EGS.

Let me bring this back to where the series stands.

In Essays #19 through #21, I documented an administration that blocked offshore wind, staged an unenforceable pledge, and paid $928 million to a French company to kill clean energy projects. In Essay #22, I documented an automobile industry selling gasoline-powered electric cars because the political environment made the real thing commercially unviable.

Meanwhile, underneath the noise, Google and Meta have been quietly signing gigawatts of geothermal power purchase agreements. Fervo Energy is building a project in Utah that could reach 4.3 gigawatts from a single site. Cornwall is pulling electricity and lithium from the same hole in the ground. The DOE is funding the technology even as the administration above it sabotages wind farms. And Canada is about to host the world’s geothermal congress while lacking a national strategy to develop the resource sitting beneath its own feet.

Geothermal is not a silver bullet. Induced seismicity is a real risk that requires careful monitoring. Not every geology is suitable. Drilling costs remain high and uncertain. The lithium extraction technology is still scaling from proof-of-concept to commercial production.

But as a complement to solar, wind, nuclear, and storage — as the baseload backbone that runs when the sun doesn’t shine and the wind doesn’t blow and the nuclear plant is still 650 days from restart — enhanced geothermal may be the most underappreciated clean energy technology on earth.

It’s always on. It’s nearly everywhere. It produces no emissions. It occupies almost no land. It can co-produce critical minerals. And the workforce to build it already exists.

The energy source nobody’s talking about is the one we’re standing on.
