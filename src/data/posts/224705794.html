Every American health-care reform eventually meets the same unglamorous enemy: the form. Not the doctor, not the hospital, not even the insurer, but the piece of paper someone has to find, fill in and send back before a deadline they may not know exists.

On 1 January 2027, that enemy gets a promotion. Hospitals across the United States are mobilising, as Christopher Rowland reported for The New York Times on 11 November 2026, to keep roughly 19 million patients from losing their coverage under new Medicaid work rules. The rules require able-bodied adults without young children to work, volunteer or attend school or job training for at least 80 hours a month, and to prove it.

The same week, Rogé Karma reported in The Atlantic that the cost of employer-provided coverage, the kind 160 million Americans rely on, is expected to rise by as much as 11 per cent next year, roughly double the average annual increase of the past decade. “These are the highest numbers we’ve ever seen,” Ellen Kelsay of the Business Group on Health told him. “And the current projections are likely an understatement.”

Two stories, two sets of people, one month. This series spent ten parts in December 2025 arguing that America’s health-care problem is, at bottom, a problem of prices and paperwork. January 2027 is about to supply a demonstration.

The rule

The work requirements were written into the tax-and-spending package Republicans passed in 2025, alongside nearly $1 trillion in Medicaid cuts. They apply to the states that expanded Medicaid under the Affordable Care Act, which allowed childless adults earning up to 138 per cent of the poverty line to enrol. The government estimates they will save $317 billion over ten years.

The argument for them is not frivolous, and it deserves a fair statement. Many Republican lawmakers have long held that health insurance paid for by taxpayers should be earned, that a job or training is the surest route out of poverty, and that the programme has been too loose with eligibility. Drew Gonshorowski, who stepped down as Nebraska’s Medicaid director in August and supports the rules, told Rowland that checks happen on the anniversary of enrolment, which spreads the burden across the year. “Regardless of the views on the specific policy,” he said, “there is opportunity to do what Medicaid programs do really well: extend a hand to the most vulnerable.”

The trouble is not the principle. It is how the savings are produced.

Who falls off

By 2034, the Congressional Budget Office expects more than five million low-income people in 44 states and the District of Columbia to lose Medicaid because of the rules. Many, Rowland reports, will lose it not because they fail the test but because they miss a deadline or get lost in the paperwork.

That is not speculation. It has been tried.

In 2018, Arkansas became the first state to impose work requirements on Medicaid. About 18,000 people lost their insurance before a federal judge suspended the programme. A survey published in The New England Journal of Medicine found the programme “plagued by confusion,” and that 92 per cent of the people who lost coverage had actually met the work requirement or should have been exempt.

Ninety-two per cent. Read that figure as an accountant would. A programme designed to remove people who will not work removed, overwhelmingly, people who already did. The savings were real. They came from eligible people who could not get the form back in time.

Nebraska and Montana imposed their rules early, in 2026, and they offer a preview. After Nebraska began on 1 May, waits at its Medicaid call centre rose from under 30 minutes to as long as two hours. Monthly approvals fell 25 per cent, nearly 1,400 people, over May, June and July, according to The Hilltop Institute at the University of Maryland, Baltimore County. The state says it denied 1,773 people through September on the work rules alone, and attributes other denials to “seasonal fluctuations.” It also says it prepared carefully, sending more than 75,000 notices, 38,000 text messages and 10,000 emails.

Jeremy Nordquist, who runs the Nebraska Hospital Association, gave Rowland the number that matters: since May, 90 per cent of the state’s Medicaid denials have been for incomplete or improperly filed paperwork. The state’s own Medicaid agency says 83 per cent of denials on new applications were for people who did not answer requests for information.

The people most exposed are not shirkers. They are part-time retail workers, seasonal farm hands and gig drivers, people whose hours change week to week and who must now hunt down pay stubs and meet recurring deadlines. “This is a little like having a D.M.V. appointment to try to prove you’re meeting your work requirements every month,” said Shelly Schlenker of CommonSpirit Health, which runs hospitals in 24 states.

Kuru

Then there are the exemptions. A person too sick to work is supposed to be excused. Deciding who is too sick has turned out to be its own small bureaucracy, and every state is building a different one.

Nebraska exempts rheumatoid arthritis. Montana does not. To qualify for a medical exemption in Montana, a person must choose an ailment from a list of hundreds of permissible diagnoses. That list includes kuru, a neurodegenerative disease from the South Pacific that is virtually extinct. A scientific paper Rowland cites explains why: “The end of cannibalism has put an end to this disease.”

Stephen Leacock could not have improved on it. A Montanan with crippling arthritis may have to work 80 hours a month; a Montanan who has contracted a disease last seen among people who ate their dead relatives is excused. Montana’s Medicaid director, Rebecca de Camara, defended her state’s approach at a legislative hearing in June: Nebraska’s list was “overly inclusive,” she said, and Montana wanted a narrow one that “really constitutes medical frailty.” Montana started applying its rules in October, and experts told Rowland that confusion is widespread, with notification letters giving conflicting deadlines.

In October, a federal judge in Boston blocked a federal requirement that would have made it harder for people too sick to work to seek an exemption. Dr. Mehmet Oz, who runs the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, says the ruling will be appealed.

Where the bill goes

The savings do not disappear. They move.

More than a third of the nearly $1 trillion spent on Medicaid each year goes to hospitals. A patient who loses coverage does not stop getting sick. She arrives in the emergency room uninsured, and the hospital absorbs the cost as charity care or an unpaid bill, then recovers what it can from employers and patients with commercial insurance. Small rural hospitals, already running on thin margins, may shed jobs and close services such as maternity wards. Roseland Community Hospital, a safety-net hospital in Chicago, cited the looming cuts among the reasons for its bankruptcy filing this month.

“There’s going to be people who fall through the cracks,” said Nicole Harper, chief financial officer of Eskenazi Health in Indianapolis, which is sending teams into low-income neighbourhoods to help patients keep their coverage. “We’re bracing for impact.”

The administrative cost lands on the states too. Indiana, which adopted tougher procedures than Congress required, including quarterly compliance checks, is hiring 400 workers as part of $43 million a year in new spending to run the rules. Hospitals are hiring navigators. Sanford Health, the largest rural system in the country, is looking for volunteer posts such as valet parking in its hospitals and nursing homes so that patients can log their hours. Henry Ford Health in Michigan gave a four-person team a list of 1,400 Medicaid recipients to phone. “I tell them the process is still very new and we don’t have all the information,” said Raquel Fenderson, one of its navigators.

“There really wasn’t enough time to roll out something that was this complex,” said Matthew Salo, a past president of the National Association of Medicaid Administrators. Sara Singer, a Stanford health-policy professor, was blunter. “It’s going to be a big train wreck.”

Meanwhile, the people with jobs

Here is where the second story begins, and where it meets the first.

The Americans who are not on Medicaid, the ones with employer plans, are about to pay more for what they have. A 2026 KFF survey put the average annual cost of a family plan at about $28,300, of which employees paid about $7,400. Over the past decade, Karma notes, that cost rose 50 per cent, far faster than inflation, for reasons long understood: an ageing workforce, more chronic illness and the consolidation of hospitals.

Next year adds three new pressures.

The first is GLP-1 drugs for weight loss, whose use nearly quadrupled between 2024 and 2026. Even after discounts, they cost an estimated $7,000 to $9,000 per patient per year. Projections from the consulting firm Marsh imply they could account for up to a quarter of next year’s extra increase.

The second is artificial intelligence. Doctors increasingly use AI tools that record and transcribe appointments. The selling point is less paperwork. The business model is more billing. Ambience Healthcare urges doctors to use its product to “capture every dollar earned—automatically.” Another company, Suki, says its scribes generate up to $6,566 a year in extra revenue per clinician by moving visits into higher billing codes. Analyses suggest this, too, could explain up to a quarter of next year’s extra rise. A machine sold as a cure for paperwork turns out to be very good at producing invoices.

The third is the most infuriating, and it began as a reform.

The reform that ate the savings

Before 2022, a patient treated at an in-network hospital by an out-of-network doctor, an anaesthetist or a surgical assistant she never chose, could receive a surprise bill for the difference. The No Surprises Act, passed in 2020 and in force from 2022, banned that. The patient pays only her in-network share. The provider and the insurer settle the rest between themselves, and if they cannot agree, each submits a number to an independent arbitrator, who picks one.

Government agencies expected about 17,000 such disputes a year. In 2025, providers filed 2.6 million.

Half of them came from just two practices, according to Georgetown University’s Center on Health Insurance Reforms: Radiology Partners, majority-owned by private equity and venture capital, and TeamHealth, owned by the private-equity giant Blackstone. Another 27 per cent came from HaloMD, a company founded in 2022, as Stat has reported, specifically to help providers win these disputes. “This really isn’t about your average doctor trying to get a fair rate,” said Jack Hoadley, the Georgetown professor emeritus who co-wrote the report. “It’s an active strategy by a few companies with a lot of money trying to game the system.”

Providers have won about 85 per cent of disputes. In 2025, the median award was more than four times the typical in-network price. In one case reviewed by The New York Times, a Dallas surgeon who accepted a patient’s insurance was paid $1,843 for removing a prostate. His assistant, who did not accept it, went to arbitration and was paid $50,456 for the same operation.

Why do providers win so often? Health economists point to the arbitrators. They are not public servants. They work for a handful of companies and non-profits paid per claim, and providers can often choose which arbitration firm hears their case. “The more these arbitrators rule on behalf of providers, the more money they ultimately make,” Christopher Whaley, a Brown University health economist, told Karma. “So what you end up with is a competition between arbitrators to be the most provider-friendly.” In the first half of 2024, the busiest arbitration company decided more than 90 per cent of its cases for providers. Some private-equity firms, according to the Private Equity Stakeholder Project, have invested both in arbitration companies and in the practices bringing the claims.

Fairness requires the other side. The providers say insurers refuse to negotiate in good faith, and they have evidence: in 8 per cent of disputes insurers have offered $1 or less, and in about a quarter they submitted no offer at all. Hoadley’s forthcoming analysis finds that 90 per cent of insurer offers are at or above the benchmark price, and that missing offers probably reflect volume. One large insurer says it has more than 400 people working on these disputes alone.

The cost has gone from about $1.6 billion a year in 2022 to 2024 to $16.6 billion in 2025, by Hoadley’s estimate with Kennah Watts, and consulting firms project it could account for 25 to 40 per cent of next year’s extra premium increase. New York’s state employee plan blamed more than $200 million in arbitration payments for a nearly 10 per cent premium rise in 2025. “This is a tidal wave that could totally wipe out health-care affordability if it isn’t fixed,” Whaley said.

Employers are already cutting. Disney is dropping coverage for working spouses who can get their own. Starbucks is ending coverage of GLP-1s for weight loss. Deloitte is rolling back parental-leave and IVF benefits for some staff. Family premiums for employees rose 9 per cent in 2026, the fastest since 2010.

The same squeeze

Put the two stories side by side and they describe one system.

At the bottom, the government saves money by making coverage harder to keep, and the people who lose it are mostly eligible. Their care becomes uncompensated hospital costs. At the top, hospitals and private-equity-owned practices recover money from commercial insurers, sometimes through a loophole worth billions, and insurers pass it on in premiums. Working families pay twice: once in their premiums and once in their taxes for the hospitals that treat the people who were dropped.

Part 3 of this series, in December 2025, was titled “It’s the Prices, Stupid.” Its argument was that America does not spend more on health care because Americans use more of it, but because each unit costs more and the administrative machinery around it is enormous. The work rules add administrative machinery to save money. The arbitration loophole is administrative machinery that costs money. Neither makes anyone healthier.

From Toronto, the comparison is hard to resist, though it should be made carefully. Canada has its own long waits and its own shortages. But nobody in Ontario fills out a monthly form to prove they deserve a doctor, and nobody’s surgical assistant can send an invoice twenty-seven times larger than the surgeon’s. The American system is not short of money. It is short of simplicity.

What January allows

The new Democratic Congress, which takes office on 3 January 2027, two days after the work rules start, cannot simply undo them. Democrats do not have the votes to override a veto, and Rowland notes that without a deal with Republicans, a rollback is uncertain. Hospitals, which depend on Medicaid and know what is coming, are preparing to lobby for one anyway. Delaying the rules, funding state systems that can verify work automatically from tax and payroll data, and protecting rural hospitals are all things that could ride on a spending bill the president needs to sign.

The Democrats are not waiting to say what they want. On 17 November, the day before House Democrats were due to nominate him for Speaker, Hakeem Jeffries told reporters at the Capitol, as Annie Karni reported for The New York Times, that Medicare eligibility should be lowered, that vision, hearing and dental care should be covered, and that every child in the country should be insured. He called these steps on “our march toward universal health coverage.” Before the election he had promised only to reverse the Medicaid cuts and restore the Affordable Care Act tax credits Republicans let expire. Senator Bernie Sanders, in line to chair the Senate health committee, wants to move a similar package next year.

The most telling item on the list is the smallest. One proposal Jeffries is considering, a bill from Senator Andy Kim of New Jersey, would automatically enrol every child born in the United States in an extension of Medicaid, with coverage until age 26. Automatically. No form, no deadline, no two-hour queue. It is the mirror image of the machinery that switches on in January, and the contrast makes the point plainly: paperwork is a policy choice. Add it and enrolment falls. Take it away and enrolment rises.

None of it will reach a signature soon. The expansion would cost hundreds of billions of dollars, and Jared Moskowitz, a centrist Democrat from Florida, put the arithmetic bluntly: the progressives’ bills lack 60 Senate votes, “and the president won’t sign it,” so the work is to pass the pieces that can reach his desk. That is the case for starting with the repairs that follow.

The arbitration loophole is easier. Karma points out that, unlike the long-term forces behind rising costs, this one is susceptible to a legislative fix. One House bill would scrap the arbitration process and tie payments to existing in-network rates. A patient who did not choose an out-of-network provider would stay protected, and the provider would be paid what the network pays. It is the kind of narrow, bipartisan repair this series argued for when it scaled back its own Code Blue bill: fix the specific thing that is broken rather than waiting for a grand reform that never comes.

Congress made the arbitration problem. It can unmake it in a single bill. The paperwork problem it made in 2025 will take longer, and it will be paid for, in the meantime, by people standing in a two-hour phone queue in Lincoln, Nebraska, trying to prove they did what they were already doing.
