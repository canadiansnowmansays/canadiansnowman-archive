Chang’an in the eighth century was, by most reasonable estimates, the largest city on earth. Its walled rectangle enclosed something on the order of eighty square kilometres, laid out on a rigid grid of walled wards that were locked at curfew each night, with a population within the walls and immediate suburbs plausibly approaching a million people. It was also, and this is the more remarkable fact, a city where a visitor from Samarkand, Persia, India, Korea, Japan, or the Arabian peninsula could find countrymen, coreligionists, a place to worship, and in many cases a functioning legal framework that recognized foreign merchants as a category with defined rights. The Tang Dynasty, which ruled from 618 to 907 CE, presided over what remains the most genuinely cosmopolitan period in premodern Chinese history, and it did so for reasons that had a good deal more to do with the Silk Road’s commercial logic than with any principled Tang commitment to openness — a distinction that matters enormously, because it explains why the openness proved so reversible.

The Tang inherited a China recently reunified after nearly four centuries of division following the Han collapse, and they inherited it from the short-lived Sui Dynasty, which had done the difficult and unpopular work of reunification and canal-building before exhausting itself. What the Tang added was reach. Tang military campaigns in the seventh century pushed Chinese control deep into Central Asia, establishing protectorates over the Tarim Basin oasis kingdoms and extending Tang authority further west than any Chinese dynasty had managed since the Han. This mattered commercially in the obvious way: the stretch of the Silk Road running from Chang’an through the Hexi Corridor to Kashgar was, for much of the seventh and eighth centuries, under a single administration capable of garrisoning it, and the volume of trade moving along it responded accordingly. It also mattered demographically, because military control over Central Asia meant sustained contact with Central Asian populations, and a substantial number of them ended up living in China.

The foreign communities in Tang cities were not a marginal curiosity. Sogdian merchants — the same commercial network Part III of this series described operating out of Samarkand — maintained substantial resident populations in Chang’an, Luoyang, and the Hexi Corridor towns, organized under community leaders whom the Tang state recognized as intermediaries with defined administrative functions. Persian refugees arrived in numbers after the Sassanid Empire fell to the Arab conquests in the mid-seventh century, including, remarkably, members of the deposed Sassanid royal family who sought Tang protection and were granted honorary titles at the Tang court. Arab, Indian, Korean, Japanese, and Turkic populations were all present. Guangzhou, on the southern coast, hosted an enormous foreign merchant community arriving by sea rather than overland, sufficiently large that Arab geographers wrote about it and that the Tang state appointed a dedicated official to supervise maritime trade there from 714 CE onward.

The cultural consequences show up everywhere in Tang material culture, and they are not subtle. Tang tomb figurines — the glazed sancai ceramics that fill museum collections today — depict Central Asian grooms with prominent noses and beards, camels laden with goods, foreign musicians, and dancers performing what Tang sources identify as Sogdian dances. Central Asian music was so fashionable at the Tang court that the imperial music bureau maintained ensembles categorized by region of origin. Polo, imported from Persia, became an aristocratic obsession, played by emperors and reportedly by court women. Grape wine, still a foreign novelty, appears repeatedly in Tang poetry. Foreign clothing styles cycled in and out of fashion among the Chang’an elite in a manner that contemporary Chinese commentators sometimes found alarming, in exactly the register Seneca had used about silk seven centuries earlier and in almost exactly the same terms — the complaint that a great civilization was adopting the dress of peoples it considered its inferiors is apparently one of the more durable genres of cultural commentary.

The religious openness was equally striking and, as Part IV of this series described, equally consequential. Tang Chang’an contained Buddhist monasteries in enormous numbers, Daoist temples, Zoroastrian fire temples serving the Sogdian and Persian communities, Nestorian Christian churches whose presence the 781 CE stele documents in detail, Manichaean establishments, and eventually mosques. The Tang state’s characteristic posture toward all of this was regulatory rather than doctrinal: foreign religions required registration and operated under state supervision, but they were not, for most of the dynasty’s first two centuries, suppressed on the grounds of being foreign or wrong.

The legal position of foreigners under the Tang deserves specific attention, because the Tang Code — the comprehensive legal compilation that would serve as a model for legal systems in Japan, Korea, and Vietnam for centuries — addressed the question directly and with a pragmatism that is genuinely striking for the period. The code stipulated that disputes between foreigners of the same origin were to be settled according to that group’s own customary law, while disputes between foreigners of different origins fell under Tang law. This is, functionally, a legal-pluralism provision written into a seventh-century imperial statute, and it existed not out of any theory about the rights of resident aliens but because a state administering a capital city full of Sogdians, Persians, Koreans, and Arabs needed a workable rule for the cases that would inevitably arise, and adjudicating internal Sogdian commercial disputes according to Chinese principles would have generated more friction than it resolved. Foreign merchants could own property, marry locally, and in a substantial number of documented cases sit for the imperial examinations and enter the Tang bureaucracy — the Japanese scholar Abe no Nakamaro, who arrived as a student in 717 CE, passed the examinations, served in the Tang administration for decades, became a friend of the poet Li Bai, and died in China in 770 having never managed to get home despite trying, is the best-known individual case, but he was not an isolated one.

The Tang also produced woodblock printing, in use by at least the eighth century for Buddhist texts and charms and responsible for the dated Diamond Sutra of 868 CE that Part VI found in the Dunhuang Library Cave — a technology whose consequences for the wider world would take another six centuries to arrive, for reasons this series takes up when it reaches the westward journey of paper.

The end of Tang power in Central Asia came in two blows four years apart. The first was the Battle of Talas in 751 CE, where an Abbasid army defeated a Tang force under Gao Xianzhi near the Talas river, checking Chinese westward expansion — a battle whose broader significance a later installment examines. The second, and by far the more consequential for China itself, arrived in 755.

It is worth being precise about what this tolerance actually was, because it is easy to project modern categories onto it and get the mechanism wrong. Tang openness was not pluralism as a value. No Tang emperor articulated a principle that religious diversity was good in itself or that foreigners had inherent rights. What the Tang had instead was a combination of self-confidence, commercial interest, and administrative pragmatism. A dynasty that controlled Central Asia militarily and drew enormous revenue from Central Asian trade had every practical reason to accommodate the merchants who generated that revenue, and a court secure in its own cultural pre-eminence had little reason to feel threatened by a Zoroastrian fire temple in a designated ward. The Tang imperial family’s own partly Turkic ancestry — the Li clan had steppe connections through marriage that later Chinese historiography sometimes handled awkwardly — likely contributed to a court less invested in ethnic purity as an organizing idea than some of its successors would be. This was tolerance as a byproduct of strength and profit, which is a real thing and produced real benefits for real people, but it is not the same as tolerance as a commitment, and the difference became brutally apparent the moment the strength and the profit went away.

An Lushan was a Tang military commander of Sogdian and Turkic parentage who had risen very high in the frontier command structure — precisely the sort of career the Tang system’s openness to foreign talent made possible — and who by the early 750s held simultaneous command of three northern military regions, an accumulation of authority that reflected both his competence and the court’s confidence in him. He was a favourite of Emperor Xuanzong, whose long reign had begun with vigorous administration and had settled, by its fifth decade, into something closer to distracted indulgence, and he was on famously good terms with the emperor’s celebrated consort Yang Guifei, whose kinsman Yang Guozhong served as chief minister and became An Lushan’s principal rival at court. The rebellion, when it came in December 755, was framed by An Lushan as a campaign against Yang Guozhong rather than against the emperor, which is the traditional courtesy of such affairs and fooled nobody.

What followed nearly destroyed the dynasty. An Lushan’s forces took Luoyang within a month and Chang’an by mid-756. Xuanzong fled southwest toward Sichuan, and en route, at the Mawei post station, his own escort troops mutinied and demanded the execution of Yang Guozhong and Yang Guifei — a demand the emperor, having no alternative, granted. The death of Yang Guifei became one of the most enduring subjects in Chinese poetry, most famously in Bai Juyi’s Song of Everlasting Sorrow, and the episode has been retold as romantic tragedy for twelve centuries; the underlying political fact is that an emperor was forced by his own bodyguard to kill his consort on the roadside while fleeing his burning capital.

The Tang eventually recovered control, after eight years, but only by making enormous concessions to regional military governors whose autonomy the central government never fully reversed, and by inviting Uyghur allies to intervene militarily at a price that included permitting them to plunder recaptured cities. An Lushan himself did not survive to see the outcome; he was murdered in 757 by his own son, in the first of several succession killings among the rebel leadership that did as much to end the revolt as Tang arms did. Tang census figures before and after the rebellion show a population collapse so severe — from roughly fifty-three million registered persons to under seventeen million — that historians debate at length how much reflects actual mortality and how much reflects the state’s loss of the administrative capacity to count anyone. Both readings point the same direction: whatever the true death toll, the machinery of the Tang state had been broken.

The consequences for foreigners in China were direct. The rebellion’s leader had been a Sogdian-descended general, and the association was not lost on anyone. Sogdian communities in Chinese cities faced suspicion and violence in the rebellion’s aftermath, and some appear to have concealed their origins in official records. Tang control over Central Asia collapsed during the rebellion and was never restored — Tibetan forces seized the Hexi Corridor and held Dunhuang for decades, cutting the overland route’s Chinese section entirely. The commercial logic that had underwritten the openness disappeared along with the trade.

The formal end came in 845 CE, when Emperor Wuzong ordered the great suppression of Buddhism, and with it, foreign religions generally. The stated motives mixed Daoist doctrinal preference with fiscal calculation: Buddhist monasteries had accumulated enormous tax-exempt landholdings and precious-metal reserves, and monks and nuns represented a large population outside the tax and corvée systems. The edicts ordered the destruction of thousands of monasteries and tens of thousands of smaller shrines, the forcible laicization of hundreds of thousands of monks and nuns, and the confiscation of monastic land and metal. Nestorian Christianity, Manichaeism, and Zoroastrianism were swept up in the same suppression, and the Nestorian church in China — which the 781 stele had proudly documented as a thriving two-century-old institution — essentially ceased to exist as an organized presence. The Japanese monk Ennin, who was in China at the time and kept a diary, recorded the suppression from the perspective of someone directly caught in it.

Buddhism in China survived, weakened but permanently rooted; it had been there long enough and had penetrated deeply enough into ordinary religious life that no edict could remove it. The other foreign religions largely did not, and Tang cosmopolitanism as a general condition did not either. Later Chinese dynasties would trade extensively with the outside world, sometimes on an enormous scale, but the specific arrangement Chang’an had embodied in the eighth century — a capital city where foreign communities lived openly, worshipped in their own establishments, and shaped elite fashion, music, and taste — was not reconstituted on the same terms.

The lesson this offers a series about the Silk Road is not a cheerful one. Tang China represents the route’s cultural high-water mark, the period when the exchange of goods produced the fullest exchange of everything else, and it happened because a confident empire found openness profitable. When the profit and the confidence collapsed together in the middle of the eighth century, the openness collapsed with them within a generation, and the institutional structures that had housed it — the churches, the fire temples, the recognized merchant communities — proved to have no independent foundation of their own. Tolerance that rests on convenience lasts precisely as long as the convenience, which is a durable enough observation that it has outlasted the empire that demonstrated it by rather a long way.
