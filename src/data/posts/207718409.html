By 1911, Winston Churchill had already concluded that the Dardanelles could not be forced. As First Lord of the Admiralty, he wrote plainly that it was “no longer possible” to push a fleet through the strait separating Europe from Asia Minor, and a 1906 study by the Committee of Imperial Defence had reached the same conclusion years earlier. This is worth stating first, before the campaign itself, because the popular version of the Gallipoli disaster tends to treat it as a reckless gamble dreamed up on the spot by an impulsive minister. It was not. It was a considered strategic option that its own principal architect had, on the record, already rejected once — and then reversed himself on, under the pressure of a war that by early 1915 had already produced a stalemate on the Western Front horrifying enough to make almost any alternative look attractive by comparison.

The alternative Churchill settled on was a naval assault on the Dardanelles, intended to force the strait, threaten Constantinople, knock Ottoman Turkey out of the war, and open a supply route to Russia — a single stroke that promised to relieve the pressure of trench deadlock in France without adding to the casualty lists there. The idea found support across the War Council, including from Admiral Lord Fisher, the elderly First Sea Lord whom Churchill had personally recalled to the post and who initially backed the plan before turning against it with mounting alarm as the operation’s difficulties multiplied.

The naval attack on the Turkish forts began on February 19, 1915, and made slow, uneven progress against coastal batteries defending the strait’s entrance — an opening effort Churchill himself later criticized as “utterly without vigour” on the part of the fleet’s initial commander, Vice Admiral Sackville Carden, who collapsed from nervous exhaustion just before the campaign’s decisive push and had to be replaced.

That decisive push came on March 18, 1915, and it is worth walking through in specific detail, because the single afternoon captures the entire operation’s character in miniature. Ten Allied battleships advanced in three lines toward the Narrows, trading fire with Turkish shore batteries that, unknown to the Allied commanders, were nearly out of ammunition and close to capitulating outright. Then, turning back through a bay the Allies believed had already been swept clear of mines, the French battleship Bouvet struck an uncharted mine laid ten days earlier by a single Ottoman minelayer, the Nusret, and capsized within two minutes, taking roughly six hundred and thirty of her crew down with her. Within hours, HMS Irresistible and HMS Ocean struck mines from the same field and were lost as well; HMS Inflexible was badly damaged but survived. More than eight hundred Allied sailors died in a single afternoon, and Admiral John de Robeck, who had taken command after Carden’s collapse, broke off the naval campaign rather than risk the rest of the fleet — a decision made without confirming that the Turkish guns his ships had spent the day fighting were, by the Turks’ own later admission, nearly spent. The naval option to force the Dardanelles ended not because it had definitively failed, but because a single night’s minelaying by one small Ottoman ship had made continuing the attempt look too costly to a commander who had no way of knowing how close the other side actually was to running out of the means to resist. It is the kind of margin — one minelayer, one afternoon, twenty-six mines laid in the dark — on which entire military and political reputations subsequently turned.

Troop landings followed at Gallipoli on April 25, 1915, an amphibious assault intended to seize the high ground the failed naval attack had been unable to clear from the sea, and what had been conceived as a swift, decisive stroke collapsed almost immediately into exactly the kind of grinding, static warfare the whole plan had been designed to avoid. The campaign ran for nine months before the final evacuation in January 1916. Each side sustained roughly a quarter of a million casualties. Forty-six thousand Allied troops died on a peninsula that never yielded the strategic breakthrough the operation had promised.

The political consequences arrived faster than the military ones resolved. Fisher, increasingly convinced the operation was unwinnable and increasingly at odds with Churchill over how to proceed, resigned as First Sea Lord in May 1915, precipitating a full-blown government crisis. Prime Minister Asquith, needing to shore up his Liberal government’s position, was forced into a coalition with the Conservative opposition — and the Conservatives, still nursing a decade-old grudge against Churchill for having defected from their party to the Liberals in 1904, made his removal from the Admiralty a condition of their support. Asquith obliged. Churchill was demoted to Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster, a position so ceremonial that its principal remaining function, by his own later account, was the appointment of rural judges. He held it, in his own words, as “a spectator of the tragedy, placed cruelly in a front seat” — able to watch the Gallipoli campaign continue to consume men and matériel, unable to influence its conduct in any material way.

Clementine’s account of this period, recorded decades later by their daughter Mary Soames, is worth quoting for what it reveals about the private cost of the public catastrophe: “When he left the Admiralty he thought he was finished... I thought he would never get over the Dardanelles; I thought he would die of grief.” This is not the language of a wife managing an ordinary political setback. It is the language of someone watching a spouse experience something close to a genuine breakdown, and it deserves to sit alongside the more famous, more literary account Churchill himself gave of the same months in “The World Crisis” — comparing himself to “a sea-beast fished up from the depths, or a diver too suddenly hoisted,” his own veins threatening to burst from the sudden fall in pressure after years at the absolute center of wartime decision-making. Both descriptions are describing the same six months. Neither reads like a man confident that a comeback was coming.

He held the meaningless Duchy of Lancaster post from the end of May until November 12, 1915, when — unable, in his own words, to accept cabinet responsibility for what he had come to regard as “a wholly erroneous conception of the war” — he resigned from the government entirely. What he did next is the detail that separates Churchill’s fall from his father’s, and it is worth dwelling on, because Part 4 of this series left Randolph limping through eight years of diminished relevance after his own resignation, watching from the backbenches as his career quietly expired. Winston did not choose the backbenches. He was a serving major in the Queen’s Own Oxfordshire Hussars, a territorial commission he had held since 1902, and rather than nurse his grievance in Parliament, he asked to be posted to active service in France. “I thought it necessary to quit their counsels,” he wrote, “and betook myself to the Armies.” The man who had just presided, at the highest level, over the catastrophic failure of a plan to spare British troops the horror of trench warfare in Flanders was now volunteering to go and share that horror personally.

He arrived in France in November 1915 hoping for a brigade command and had to settle, initially, for a spell as a major with the 2nd Battalion, Grenadier Guards, before being appointed Lieutenant-Colonel commanding the 6th Battalion, Royal Scots Fusiliers in January 1916 — a battalion of under a thousand men, a considerable demotion in institutional terms from First Lord of the Admiralty, and one that political friends and enemies alike expected to be the last chapter of his public life. The battalion took over roughly a thousand yards of front line at Ploegsteert, Belgium — universally known to the troops as “Plug Street” — rotating six days in the trenches with six in immediate reserve. Churchill proved, by the near-unanimous account of the men who served under him, an unexpectedly effective and genuinely well-liked commanding officer: he relaxed some of the army’s harsher disciplinary conventions, organized entertainments for his troops to break the monotony of trench life, and regularly crossed into no-man’s-land himself to inspect forward positions at close range, at least once coming under direct machine-gun fire. One shell struck the farmhouse he was using as a command post while he sat inside it, a piece of shrapnel striking the battery holder of a lamp he happened to be holding at the time — the kind of near-miss that, six months earlier, would have generated headlines across the empire, and which now passed almost unremarked, one more incident in an unglamorous command nobody in London was paying particularly close attention to.

It is worth pausing on the asymmetry this represents, because it is the clearest illustration in the whole series so far of the gap between personal risk and institutional risk introduced at the end of Part 5. The younger Churchill of the 1890s had risked his own body in Cuba, on the Northwest Frontier, at Omdurman, in the Boer War — genuine physical courage, but courage exercised entirely at his own expense, with nobody’s safety on the line but his own. At the Admiralty in 1915, he had risked something categorically different: institutional authority over other men’s lives, exercised at a geographic and hierarchical remove from the consequences, with forty-six thousand Allied dead as the eventual accounting. And now, at Plug Street, he had returned to the first kind of risk — his own body, in a trench, exposed to the same shellfire as the men under his command — as though some part of him understood, without necessarily articulating it, that this was the only kind of danger he still had the standing to volunteer for. The Admiralty had cost other men’s lives on his judgment. The trenches could only cost his own.

Churchill’s battalion command lasted until May 1916, when the 6th Royal Scots Fusiliers were merged with another battalion depleted by losses and he returned to England and, before long, to Parliament — a serving MP throughout his time in France, having never actually vacated his seat, which allowed him to resume his political life immediately rather than fight a by-election to reclaim it. The political rehabilitation that followed was neither immediate nor complete. He remained, for the better part of two years, the minister most closely associated in the public mind with the single worst military catastrophe of a war already catastrophically expensive in lives, and “What about the Dardanelles?” became, in the words of one of his biographers, the recurring taunt he would hear for the rest of the interwar period whenever his judgment on any subject was questioned. The Dardanelles Commission, established to investigate the campaign’s failures, published an interim report in 1917 that distributed responsibility considerably more broadly than the popular narrative had — implicating Asquith, Kitchener, Fisher, and the War Council collectively, rather than resting the blame on Churchill alone — and it was only after that report’s publication that Prime Minister David Lloyd George, over the loud objections of Conservative MPs and a press that had not forgotten Gallipoli, appointed Churchill Minister of Munitions in July 1917, restoring him to cabinet rank for the first time since his November 1915 resignation.

The two years in between are the actual substance of what this part of the series is named for: not a single fall, but the first demonstration, at full institutional scale, of the recovery template this series has been tracing since Leonard Jerome’s Wall Street ruins and Winston’s own boyhood misadventures. Randolph’s fall in 1886 had no war to hide inside; his miscalculation played out against the ordinary rhythm of Victorian domestic politics, and there was no larger crisis available to absorb it, dilute it, or eventually make it look like one difficult chapter in a longer story rather than the whole story. Winston’s fall in 1915 had exactly that kind of larger crisis to hide inside — a four-year war whose eventual outcome would retroactively reshape how every individual decision within it got remembered, Gallipoli included. This is not offered as an excuse for the catastrophe itself, which cost tens of thousands of lives on a strategic gamble that several of Churchill’s own colleagues had warned against at the time. It is offered as an explanation for why this fall, unlike his father’s, did not end his career. Randolph overreached during peacetime and had nowhere to hide. Winston overreached during the deadliest war in human history to that date, and the war itself — vast, chaotic, and eventually victorious for Britain regardless of any single commander’s individual record — gave him room that peacetime politics never would have.

There is a final irony worth noting before this part closes, because it connects directly back to the family pattern this series opened with. Churchill’s own account of these years, written and rewritten across multiple volumes of memoir in the decades that followed, is itself an exercise in exactly the kind of selective narrative-shaping this series has been trying to resist. “The World Crisis” gives the Dardanelles plan a sympathetic, carefully argued defense; it gives considerably less space to the forty-six thousand Allied dead than to the strategic logic that, in Churchill’s telling, very nearly worked. He was, by the time he came to write his own history of the war, doing to his own record precisely what Leonard Jerome’s Wall Street admirers had done to Jerome’s — praising the net result of a career built on catastrophic risk while compressing the actual cost of the catastrophes into a paragraph or two, on the way to the recovery that made the whole story worth telling. The pattern holds across three generations now: risk, catastrophe, recovery, and a retrospective account that remembers the recovery in far more detail than the catastrophe. Part 7 turns to the empire this pattern was built inside of, and to the ledger entries — India, Bengal, the years between the wars — that this series cannot keep deferring if the audit is to mean anything at all.
