On May 22, 2026, Judge Waverly Crenshaw dismissed the indictment against Kilmar Abrego Garcia. As NPR reported, the dismissal was based on vindictive and selective prosecution in violation of the Fifth Amendment’s due process clause. The DOJ immediately announced it would appeal, calling the ruling “wrong and dangerous.”

It is worth pausing to understand how extraordinary this is. Judges almost never dismiss criminal cases on vindictive prosecution grounds. The bar is among the highest in criminal law — a defendant must first demonstrate a realistic likelihood of vindictiveness, which shifts the burden to the government to rebut the presumption. It is, by design, extremely difficult to meet. The government usually wins these motions simply by showing up and offering any plausible explanation.

Judge Crenshaw found that the government could not clear even this generous bar. Its explanations did not engage with the evidence. As a matter of logic, the judge wrote, “the government cannot rebut a presumption when it does not address the evidence supporting the presumption.” As CNN reported, the opinion cited Todd Blanche’s public statements as direct evidence of the tainted investigation.

The opinion calls out Todd Blanche — now the acting Attorney General of the United States — by name, multiple times. It finds that his Fox News statements “directly confirm that the Executive Branch reopened the criminal investigation because the Judicial Branch required the Executive Branch to facilitate Abrego’s return from El Salvador.” It characterizes the investigation he set in motion as “tainted.”

And it closes with the line that, in any functioning republic, would end careers: “The evidence before this court sadly reflects an abuse of prosecuting power.”

So what does the story of the pupusa maker’s son tell us? It tells us two things — one about the machinery, and one about the man who operates it.

The Machinery

In my series The Enemies List, I documented the Trump administration’s systematic prosecution of political opponents through the Justice Department — James Comey, Letitia James, John Bolton. Those cases followed a pattern: Trump publicly demanded prosecution. Resistant prosecutors were removed. Charges were brought despite insufficient evidence. The pattern was, and remains, operational.

Abrego Garcia is not on any enemies list. He is not a former FBI director. He is not a state attorney general. He is not a national security advisor who wrote a tell-all memoir. He is a construction worker from Maryland who sold pupusas as a child in San Salvador and wore a Chicago Bulls hat to a Home Depot parking lot. He became a target not because he opposed the president, but because he embarrassed the president — because the courts said the administration broke the law, and the administration’s response was to try to turn the victim into a criminal.

Glenn Kirschner, who spent thirty years as a federal prosecutor before becoming one of the most prominent legal analysts of this era, placed the Abrego Garcia case in a catalogue on Justice Matters that is growing too long to ignore. The original Comey prosecution, which collapsed. The Letitia James prosecution, which collapsed. The Jerome Powell criminal investigation, which collapsed. The attempted indictment of six members of Congress for a public service announcement reminding military members of their duty to disobey unlawful orders. And now Abrego Garcia.

Kirschner draws a direct line between Blanche’s Fox News comments that tanked the Abrego Garcia case and Blanche’s separate Fox News comments about the Comey “8647” prosecution — where Blanche essentially admitted that thousands of people have displayed the same four numbers without prosecution, handing Comey’s lawyers a selective prosecution defence on a silver platter. “Todd Blanch opens his mouth only to change feet,” Kirschner says.

The pattern Kirschner identifies — and that this series has documented from its origins in a San Salvador pupusa shop to a federal courtroom in Tennessee — is the pattern of political persecution. Pick the person. Find the crime. Weaponize the DOJ. And when the courts push back, double down.

The Enemies List explains who gets targeted. The Abrego Garcia case explains why the targeting never stops.

The Man

Consider the decision tree a rational administration would have followed. March 2025: we deported someone in violation of a court order. It is an error. We bring him back, we acknowledge the mistake, we fix the internal process that allowed it, and the story dies in forty-eight hours. The political cost would have been negligible — one man, one mistake, corrected. That is what a government concerned with governance does.

But that option was never available to this administration, because admitting the error and accepting the correction means losing. And Donald Trump cannot lose.

This is not speculation. This is pattern recognition, documented across hundreds of data points and dozens of essays in this publication. Trump has told us who he is. He has told us that winning is everything, that losers are contemptible, that strength is measured by dominance and weakness is measured by concession. He told us during the 2016 campaign. He told us during the first administration. He tells us now, daily, from the Oval Office and from Truth Social.

The Abrego Garcia case is arguably the purest expression of this psychology applied to governance. The man himself was irrelevant. He was nobody — a sheet metal apprentice with a fragile legal status and a family in Prince George’s County. But his case had become visible. The courts had weighed in. The Supreme Court had ruled unanimously. CNN had his name in a chyron. And at that point, the calculus shifted from the rational — fix the error, move on — to the pathological (if this man can beat me, anyone can beat me, and the strongman mystique collapses.

So every judicial loss produced an escalation rather than a retreat. That is the signature. Not strategy — compulsion.

The Supreme Court says facilitate his return. The administration spends two months arguing about what “facilitate” means. He comes back. They indict him on the tarmac. He beats the indictment on bail. ICE is waiting at the jail door within minutes. He won’t take the plea. Costa Rica becomes Uganda becomes Liberia. A judge dismisses the case. They announce an appeal within hours.

Readers of this publication will recognise the TACO framework — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — that I have applied across foreign policy and accountability series throughout this past year. I introduced this in The Enemies List. The Abrego Garcia case follows the pattern precisely: the administration Threatened (the deportation itself, the MS-13 branding, the CECOT imprisonment), was forced to Accommodate (the Supreme Court order), then Obfuscated (the “administrative error” framing, the “facilitate doesn’t mean we have to do anything” argument, the DHS “bombshell” press release about a traffic stop that produced no charges). But when none of that worked, the cycle did not end. It looped back to Threaten — the indictment, the plea coercion, the parade of African countries offered as exile.

In a normal government, the cycle breaks when the cost of continuing exceeds the cost of conceding. In this government, that calculation never arrives, because the cost of conceding is existential to the man at the top. Losing to a federal judge is intolerable. Losing to the ACLU is intolerable. And losing to a Salvadoran construction worker whose lawyers had to explain to a court that their client was identified as a gang member because of a baseball cap — that is the most intolerable of all, because the asymmetry is the point. If this man can beat you, then the apparatus of intimidation is revealed as theatre. And theatre only works if the audience believes it is real.

This is why the DOJ announced its appeal within hours of Judge Crenshaw’s ruling. Not because the legal merits support an appeal — the opinion is thirty-two pages of meticulously documented vindictiveness, built on the government’s own admissions and Todd Blanche’s own words. They will appeal because they cannot not appeal. The psychology demands it. Every instrument of the state must be deployed to prevent the loss from becoming final, even when deploying those instruments only deepens the loss.

And this is where the Abrego Garcia case becomes something larger and more frightening than a single man’s legal odyssey. Because the same psychology that cannot accept being beaten by a pupusa maker’s son is the same psychology that is, at this moment, conducting foreign policy. If Trump cannot accept a loss to Kilmar Abrego Garcia, what does he do when facing a stalemate with Iran? The answer — and readers of my Iran Crisis series know this — is that he cannot accept the stalemate. Escalation becomes the only psychologically available option, regardless of whether it is strategically rational. The distance between the pupusa shop in San Salvador and the federal courthouse in Tennessee is roughly 2,500 miles. The distance between a president who cannot lose and a government that has lost its way is much shorter.

The Constitution

But the Abrego Garcia case reveals something the Enemies List cases do not, and it is the thing worth holding onto.

Comey, James, Bolton — they have platforms, resources, elite legal representation, and public constituencies. They can fight back. Abrego Garcia is a man who arrived in this country at sixteen with nothing, who married his wife through prison glass, whose children have disabilities, whose legal status hung on a single court order issued during the first Trump administration that the government didn’t even bother to appeal. He had no platform. No constituency. No power beyond the Constitution itself.

And yet he won. Trial court. Court of Appeals. Supreme Court. Criminal court. Four for four. Judicial clean sweep.

In his Lincoln quote — “Be sure to put your feet in the right place, then stand firm” — Kirschner captures what Abrego Garcia did from a prison cell in El Salvador. He planted his feet in the rule of law. And the rule of law held.

It held against a president who cannot tolerate losing. It held against an Attorney General and a Deputy Attorney General who weaponized the DOJ on national television and then couldn’t explain why they’d done it. It held against an immigration apparatus that proposed deporting one man to six different countries across three continents rather than accept a single judicial ruling. It held against the full weight of the federal government, deployed against one person, for fifteen months, at a cost in taxpayer money and institutional credibility that no one in this administration will ever calculate because calculating it would mean acknowledging the loss.

The rule of law held this time. Whether it holds next time — in the next vindictive prosecution, in the next confrontation between a president who cannot lose and a judiciary that insists on the law — depends on whether the pattern of political persecution is recognised for what it is, named for what it is, and stopped. Judge Crenshaw named it. This series has tried to document it. The rest — as it always is, in a democracy — is up to us. “The evidence before this court sadly reflects an abuse of prosecuting power.”

Sadly. That word carries the weight of the whole opinion. Not angrily. Not defiantly. Sadly. A federal judge looking at the Department of Justice and finding not an adversary to be defeated but an institution to be mourned. A judge who opened his opinion with Robert Jackson’s warning about prosecutors who pick the person first and the crime second, and who closed it with the quiet acknowledgment that the warning had gone unheeded.

The pupusa maker’s son beat them all. He beat them because the framers of the Constitution built something strong enough to withstand even a president who treats the machinery of justice as a personal instrument of dominance. The question — the only question that matters now — is whether the institution that persecuted him can be rebuilt into something worth saving, or whether the pattern of political persecution has become the pattern itself, self-sustaining, feeding on its own compulsions, unable to stop because stopping would mean admitting that it was wrong.

And admitting that it was wrong would mean losing. And losing, for this president, is the one thing the Constitution cannot require of him — because it is the one thing he will never, under any circumstances, do voluntarily.

Kilmar Abrego Garcia, the son of a pupusa maker from San Salvador, did not have that problem. He knew how to lose — his country, his freedom, his weight in a prison cell, thirty-one pounds in two weeks. And because he knew how to lose, he knew how to fight. And because he knew how to fight, he won.

The president of the United States, who has never lost anything he was willing to acknowledge, does not know how to fight. He only knows how to punish. And punishment, as Judge Crenshaw reminded us, is not prosecution. It is an abuse of power. And it failed.

It failed this time. As I write this, the DOJ has announced its appeal. The Liberia deportation effort remains alive in Judge Xinis’s courtroom. The machinery has not stopped — it cannot stop, for the reasons this essay has tried to explain. Kilmar Abrego Garcia is home in Prince George’s County with his wife and children, but he is not free of this. He may never be, so long as the man who cannot lose remains in the Oval Office.

The triumph is real. It is also provisional. That is what it means to live under the rule of law in a country where the rule of law must be defended every single day, by anyone willing to plant their feet and stand firm — including, perhaps especially, the son of a pupusa maker from San Salvador who had nothing on his side but the Constitution, and found that it was enough.
