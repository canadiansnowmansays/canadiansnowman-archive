One week. That’s all it took.

On May 19, Donald Trump endorsed Ken Paxton over John Cornyn. On May 26, Republican voters did as they were told. Paxton defeated the four-term senator 63 to 37, in a result so decisive that the twenty-three-year career of one of the most accomplished legislators in the United States Senate was ended by a social media post made seven days before the vote.

The Associated Press called the race at 9:00 p.m. Eastern with 58 percent of the vote counted. There was no drama. Cornyn’s watch party had no supporters, no food, no music — just reporters and a podium. The campaign had known.

Cornyn was trailing in nearly all of Texas’s 254 counties. He conceded in an Austin hotel meeting room that was empty except for his immediate family and the assembled press. He did not mention Paxton by name. “My hope is to keep my party in power for generations,” he said — a gracious exit from a man whose party had just told him he was no longer needed.

In March, these same voters had split 42-41. The only variable that changed between March and May was Trump’s preference. A 27-point swing in one week. That is not a primary electorate making a judgment. That is a primary electorate following an instruction.

The Loyalty Dynamic

That word — instruction — deserves examination. Because Cornyn is clearly the better candidate by any conventional measure, and 63 percent of Republican primary voters chose the worse one. Why?

Three things are happening simultaneously, and separating them matters.

The first is the cult dynamic, and it is real. The 27-point swing is not the product of voters evaluating Paxton’s record against Cornyn’s and concluding that the indicted, impeached, divorced attorney general is the stronger public servant. It is the product of voters receiving a directive and complying. Trump said Paxton. They voted Paxton. The evaluation was outsourced to one man, and the electorate accepted the outsourcing.

This is not to say that all 63 percent were following instructions. Paxton has a genuine constituency — built over a decade of suing the Biden administration, fighting on the border, and waging cultural warfare from the attorney general’s office. A significant share of the Republican base views his scandals as persecution and his combativeness as proof of fitness. That constituency existed before the endorsement and would have delivered him a competitive race regardless. What the endorsement added was the remaining 20-odd points — the voters who were not Paxton supporters but Trump followers, for whom the directive was sufficient and the evaluation unnecessary.

The second is the social pressure of visible dissent. In a closed primary with low turnout, your political choices feel less anonymous than they do in a general election where millions participate. In a party where Trump has demonstrated — publicly, repeatedly, this month — that he will destroy officeholders who cross him, the atmosphere trickles down. Neighbours know you voted. Social media makes dissent public. The retribution visited on Cassidy, Massie, and the Indiana legislators is not abstract — it is a demonstrated pattern, visible to every Republican who pays attention. The rational calculation for a primary voter in that environment is not “which candidate is better?” It is “which vote carries less social risk?” That is not ballot intimidation. It is something subtler and, in its way, more corrosive: a political culture in which the secret ballot is technically intact but the social consequences of the wrong choice are openly advertised.

The third is the information architecture. These voters are not watching the New York Times‘ county-by-county tracker. They are inside a media ecosystem where Paxton’s scandals are framed as persecution and Cornyn’s 99 percent loyalty record is framed as betrayal. If the only information you receive tells you that Cornyn is a RINO who stabbed the president in the back, then voting against him is not irrational — it is the logical conclusion of corrupted inputs.

A lemming analogy is tempting. But what is happening is more precise than lemmings. Lemmings do not know the cliff is there. These voters have been told the cliff is a bridge.

I am aware that the language of cults is not language anyone welcomes. The word carries a stigma that makes the person hearing it stop listening. But there is a closer parallel that may be more useful — and more compassionate. Consider addiction. Nobody becomes an addict overnight. It happens incrementally: one drink, then another, then a pattern, then a dependence. Each step feels like a free choice. The person inside the pattern is the last to recognise the pattern. And the defining feature of addiction is that the diagnosis is rejected precisely because accepting it would require dismantling an identity built around the behaviour.

The trajectory from 2015 to 2026 has the same architecture. The voter who thought “this guy is shaking things up” is not the same person as the voter who chose an indicted, impeached attorney general over a 23-year senator because a social media post told them to. But the path between those two points was never marked with a sign that said you have crossed a line. One rally. One endorsement. One loyalty test. Each one reasonable in the moment. And now, a 27-point swing in a week — and no one inside the movement sees it as compliance, because compliance is invisible from the inside.

No Republican primary voter in Texas believes they voted for Paxton because they were told to. They believe they chose him. That is what makes the dynamic so durable — and so difficult to break. Someone has to shake them into the understanding that this is what has happened, and the shaking itself is the thing they are most prepared to resist.

What Was Lost

Senator Cornyn, a four-term incumbent, lost decisively. Credit...Ilana Panich-Linsman for The New York Times

Let’s Talk Texas has documented Cornyn’s record before, but the scale of what was discarded on Tuesday night deserves one more accounting.

A senator who was the primary sponsor of 119 bills signed into law. The author of the Justice for Victims of Trafficking Act, which passed unanimously. The co-author of the bipartisan gun safety bill after Uvalde — twenty-one people dead in his own state, most of them children. A former Republican Whip and NRSC chair. A member of Finance, Judiciary, Intelligence, Foreign Relations, and Budget.

He voted with Trump more than 99 percent of the time.

But the base did not count the 99 percent. It counted the 1 percent: the bipartisan gun bill after Uvalde, which the activist wing booed him for at the state convention. The vote to certify the 2020 election results. And the electability quote — the single sentence that became the entire case against him:

“I don’t think President Trump understands that when you run in a general election, you have to appeal to voters beyond your base.”

He was right. The polls prove it. And being right was the thing that ended him.

What Was Chosen

Ken Paxton defeated Senator John Cornyn in a runoff for the Republican nomination on Tuesday, setting up a marquee race against James Talarico, the Democrat.Credit...Desiree Rios for The New York Times

Ken Paxton has been indicted on felony securities fraud charges. He was impeached by his own party’s legislature for corruption and bribery. His wife of 38 years filed for divorce “on biblical grounds.” His former aides filed whistleblower complaints that triggered a federal investigation. He tried to overturn the 2020 election results in four states he did not represent.

On Tuesday, she endorsed in three other runoffs. She did not endorse in his.

He has never lost an election. He has now won the Republican nomination for the United States Senate from Texas. He had already shifted to the general election before the polls closed — a super PAC supporting him began running ads against Talarico while votes were still being counted. In his victory speech, he debuted the nicknames: “Tofu Talarico” and “six-gender Jimmy James.” A group affiliated with the Club for Growth offered another: “Woke weirdo for Senate.”

Senator Thom Tillis of North Carolina, a retiring Republican who has grown increasingly unshackled, offered his own assessment of the nominee on CNN: “To call Paxton ethically challenged is to call Jeffrey Dahmer suffering from an eating disorder. This guy is an empty suit.”

And there is one more detail. The Paxton divorce trial is scheduled for late June — if the couple cannot reach an agreement, the breakdown of the marriage will be aired publicly in the midst of a general election campaign. A seminarian running on faith and family values against a man whose wife divorced him on biblical grounds, with the trial potentially underway during the campaign. Republican strategists are already reaching for something stronger than coffee.

Trump called him “a Fighter” who “knows how to WIN.” Sixty-three percent of Republican primary voters agreed.

The $128 Million Primary

This was the most expensive Senate primary in the United States since at least 2018. Total advertising expenditure: approximately $128 million. Cornyn and his allies spent at least $92 million — roughly four times what Paxton’s side spent.

It did not matter. Money, it turns out, is not the currency of the Republican primary electorate. Loyalty is. And loyalty is measured in a single denomination: did the president tell you to vote for this person, or didn’t he?

Cornyn’s final ad, seen more than 89 million times, featured clips of Trump expressing support and claimed Cornyn voted with the president “99 percent of the time.” It was an ad begging voters to believe that the man the president had just publicly rejected was actually the president’s man. The premise was impossible, and the voters knew it.

The Menefee Result

The other result from Texas on Tuesday belongs in this essay because it is the human cost of the redistricting war that started in this state.

Representative Christian Menefee, 38, defeated Representative Al Green, 78, in the Democratic primary for the redrawn 18th Congressional District. An eleven-term incumbent, one of the most senior Black members of Congress, lost his seat because Republican mapmakers eliminated his district and forced him into someone else’s. It was the first time a Democratic incumbent lost a primary in 2026. The crypto industry’s Fairshake super PAC spent $6.5 million across both rounds to take down Green, a critic of the industry — their biggest House investment of the cycle. A late ad from the group featured Representative Jasmine Crockett arguing for a “new generation of fighters.” Green campaigned against the industry on the House floor, calling himself “unbought” and attacking those who side with crypto as “cryptocrats.” The industry bought his seat anyway.

“This is the president’s heavy hand,” Green said on primary day. “He demanded this.”

Adam Fontenette, a Houston voter, said what the redistricting scorecards cannot: “No matter who wins, we lose. They’re both too good. No matter who wins, we lose.”

Green’s defeat is the first time a Democratic incumbent has lost a primary in 2026. It happened because Texas Republicans drew the lines to make it happen. The same redistricting that was designed to lock in Republican advantage produced this as a side effect: two good public servants forced to destroy each other’s careers.

The Rest of the Ballot

The other results from Tuesday confirmed what the Senate race suggested: Trump’s grip on the Texas Republican Party is now total, and the redistricting it produced is reshaping both parties from the inside.

Attorney General: Mayes Middleton, who branded himself “MAGA Mayes,” defeated Representative Chip Roy. The office Paxton weaponised for a decade will be occupied by another Trump-aligned conservative. Roy, a hard-liner who had at times broken with Trump — including after January 6 — could not overcome that single deviation. The pattern holds.

TX-33 (Dallas): Colin Allred defeated Representative Julie Johnson to reclaim the House seat he vacated when he ran against Ted Cruz in 2024. Allred is the general-election favourite in a heavily Democratic district that was actually strengthened by the Republican gerrymander — one of the unintended consequences of consolidating Democratic voters.

TX-35 (San Antonio): Johnny Garcia, the sheriff’s deputy backed by national Democrats, defeated Maureen Galindo, the sex therapist whose call for a “prison for American Zionists” Democrats had denounced as “straight out of the Nazi playbook.” A secretive super PAC with Republican ties had spent nearly $900,000 boosting Galindo — an apparent gambit to nominate an unelectable opponent in a district Republicans had gerrymandered for their party. The gambit failed.

TX-9 (Houston): Alex Mealer, backed by Governor Abbott, defeated Briscoe Cain, backed by Trump, in a blowout. This was the one race where the Abbott candidate prevailed over the Trump candidate — in a newly created district drawn to elect a Republican from Green’s dismantled old seat.

The name-calling: The overnight reporting revealed something uglier than the usual primary rhetoric. Paxton attacked Cornyn as “Caliphate Cornyn.” Middleton became “Mecca Mayes” in opposition mailers. The incumbent railroad commissioner was branded “Jihadi Jim.” Chad Wilbanks, a Republican lobbyist and former executive director of the state party, called it “so beneath Republican politics. So disheartening.” The party that claims to represent patriotic values ran a primary season laced with anti-Muslim slurs.

The TACO Framework: The Verdict

In the previous instalment of this series, the TACO framework was applied to the Paxton endorsement. Here is how it resolved.

Threaten. Trump spent months signalling. The endorsement was the threat made real — not just to Cornyn, but to every Republican senator watching. Lisa Murkowski said the mood was “pretty sour.” Carl Hulse reported that the Senate caucus was “boiling mad.”

Accommodate. Cornyn tried. He ran an ad saying he voted with Trump 99 percent of the time. He did not criticise the president by name during the campaign. He argued electability, not ideology. The accommodation was total — and it was insufficient.

Capitulate. The Senate caucus partially capitulated on the ballroom funding and the reconciliation bill. It was not fast enough. Partial capitulation bought no goodwill. After the endorsement, Senate Republicans pushed back on the anti-weaponisation fund and the January 6 defendant payments — the first time the caucus has openly defied Trump’s legislative priorities. The punishment preceded the rebellion, and the rebellion followed the punishment.

Obfuscate. The framing held. Paxton the “Fighter.” Cornyn the disloyal establishment figure. A man who has been indicted, impeached, and divorced was presented as a victim-hero. A man who authored 119 laws and voted with the president 99 percent of the time was presented as a traitor. The obfuscation was the endorsement itself — a punitive act against the Senate majority leader, dressed as populist empowerment. And 63 percent of Republican primary voters accepted the framing without question.

Cook Shifts the Rating

James Talarico, the Democratic nominee, cast Mr. Paxton, who has been indicted and impeached, as corrupt.Credit...Lexi Parra for The New York Times

Before the polls closed, the Cook Political Report had already signalled that a Paxton win would shift the Texas Senate race from Likely Republican to Lean Republican. The NRSC issued a statement on the race that did not mention Paxton’s name — saying only that “a state President Trump won by nearly 14 points isn’t going to elect James Talarico.” The party’s own campaign arm could not bring itself to congratulate its nominee.

This means Texas is now on the competitive Senate map for the first time since 1988 — the last time a Democrat won statewide. That does not mean Texas will flip. The structural Republican advantage remains enormous: Trump carried the state by nearly 14 points, and Democrats have not won statewide in over thirty years. But Cook’s shift to Lean Republican is not a prediction of a Democratic win. It is a prediction that Republicans will have to spend money and attention in a state they have taken for granted for a generation — money and attention that will not be available in Alaska, Maine, North Carolina, and Ohio, where Democrats are already competing.

James Talarico, the Democratic nominee, has $40 million. He won his primary outright. On election night, moments before Paxton took the stage, Talarico’s campaign released a video calling Paxton “the most corrupt politician in America.” His statement landed the framing: “For 50 years, megadonors and their puppet politicians like Ken Paxton have stolen from us with their bribes, bailouts, and billionaire tax breaks. That ends this year. In this state. In this race.”

Talarico is running against the system — which means Paxton walks into a framing that was built before he arrived.

Republican operatives told the New York Times that defending the seat against Talarico could cost the party $100 million or more — on top of the $128 million already spent in the primary. Cornyn himself, on primary day, warned: “Several hundred millions of dollars.” Notably, the main Senate Republican super PAC has left Texas entirely out of its fall advertising reservations. Senate officials are hoping Trump and his $350 million super PAC decide to fill the gap. The party’s nominee cannot fund himself. Its institutional donors are looking the other way. The only question is whether the man who created this problem will spend his own war chest to fix it.

The party that built the gerrymander may now have to spend hundreds of millions defending a seat it held safely for thirty years — because the president endorsed a candidate whose scandals make the seat competitive.

The Gerrymander Meets Its Accelerant

In the previous instalment of this series, I wrote: “The redistricting we documented in ‘When Democracy Fights Back’ — the lines drawn to lock in Republican advantage for a decade — could be undermined by the party’s own presidential endorsement. The gerrymander, designed to be fireproof, may have met its accelerant.”

Tuesday confirmed it.

If Paxton drags down the Republican ticket in November — and his unfavourables among suburban women and independents suggest he will — the very congressional districts Texas Republicans redrew last summer to guarantee five additional seats could become competitive. The redistricting assumed a normal Republican nominee at the top of the ticket. Paxton is not a normal nominee. He is an accelerant.

Doug Deason, one of Paxton’s few major Republican donors, argued that the Democratic money flooding into Texas would be “redirected from states where they actually have a chance.” He may be right. He may also be wrong — because the money isn’t coming from a fixed pool. Talarico’s $40 million is new money, small-dollar money, enthusiasm money. It doesn’t replace anything. It adds.

The Cassidy Parallel

Bill Cassidy finished third in Louisiana on May 16. John Cornyn was defeated on May 26. Ten days apart. Two sitting senators removed by Trump endorsements in a single month.

The parallels are precise and the differences are instructive.

Cassidy’s offence was voting to convict Trump after January 6 — an act of constitutional conscience. Cornyn’s offence was a single sentence questioning Trump’s electability — an act of political analysis. Cassidy crossed a bright line. Cornyn barely scuffed one. Both were destroyed.

The lesson is not that there is a line. The lesson is that there is no threshold. Ninety-nine percent is a failing grade. A single moment of candour, spoken two years before the primary, is enough. The loyalty economy does not have a minimum balance. It has a maximum — total — and anything less is insufficient.

Cassidy, freed by his defeat, has now voted with Democrats on Iran war powers. Cornyn, if the pattern holds, will spend his remaining months in the Senate liberated in the same way. The purge has a side effect: it creates lame-duck senators with nothing left to lose.

Lamar Alexander described this dynamic from retirement. Cassidy is living it in real time. Cornyn may be next.

What Comes Next

The general election is November 3. Between now and then, both sides face problems they have built for themselves.

The Republican unity problem. Governor Abbott called for unity within hours of the result. Paxton thanked Cornyn in his victory speech — a courtesy Cornyn did not reciprocate, never mentioning Paxton by name in his concession. Chad Wilbanks, the Republican lobbyist, warned that with Paxton at the top of the ticket, Republicans would need to spend more and campaign harder across the board. “You have a lot of independents and women that, quite candidly, just do not like him,” Wilbanks said. “Republicans won’t vote for James Talarico. But they may not vote in that race at all.”

Matt Mackowiak, Cornyn’s senior adviser, was more direct about the general election: “Paxton doesn’t know how to broaden his appeal. He runs generals like they’re primaries. I don’t know that he’s run in an environment like this.”

Talarico’s outreach. While Republicans bled through their runoff, Talarico spent three months unifying Democrats. He had tacos in Austin with Barack Obama. He gave the commencement address at Paul Quinn College, a historically Black college in Dallas. He met with Opal Lee, the 99-year-old voting rights activist who led the movement to make Juneteenth a federal holiday. On Tuesday night, he thanked Cornyn for his service and told the senator’s supporters they “have a place” in his campaign.

But Jasmine Crockett, who lost the Democratic primary to Talarico in March, offered a warning: “He is struggling with some of our consistent Black voters. I am pretty doubtful that infrequent Black voters will be motivated, as I’m not convinced that they are a target. The target seemingly has been disaffected MAGA.” If Talarico is counting on Cornyn’s moderate Republicans to cross over, Crockett is asking who is counting on the Black voters who stayed home.

The Republican attack. Republicans have already begun positioning Talarico as culturally alien to Texas. They are circulating clips of him using the story of Christ’s conception to defend abortion rights, saying “God is nonbinary,” supporting transgender rights, and warning about “radicalized white men.” The Club for Growth branded him a “woke weirdo.” Paxton debuted “Tofu Talarico” and “six-gender Jimmy James” in his victory speech. The effort is designed to cast a seminarian who has made faith the centrepiece of his campaign as too liberal for a state where evangelical voters hold considerable sway.

Talarico has stood by his comments while acknowledging he might phrase some differently now. “Those are certainly principles and values that I still hold, and that stem directly from my faith,” he said. “But I think I probably would have said them differently now.”

Beto O’Rourke, who came within three points of knocking out Ted Cruz in 2018, predicted Paxton’s appeal will not translate to the general electorate: “He’s too extreme and he’s too tied to Trump, whose popularity continues to decline.”

Texas has not elected a Democrat statewide since 1994. But Texas has also never nominated a candidate like Paxton for statewide office — a man whose own party impeached him, whose own wife divorced him, whose own former aides filed whistleblower complaints against him, and whose divorce trial is scheduled for late June, potentially airing the details of his marriage’s collapse in the middle of the campaign.

The question is not whether Talarico can win. The question is whether the Republican Party can hold a seat it has owned for thirty years with a nominee who makes holding it an act of endurance rather than an act of governance.

Everything’s bigger in Texas, including the consequences.
