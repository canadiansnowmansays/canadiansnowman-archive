Stephen Gordon May 8, 2026

TThe Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002 was signed into law on March 27th of that year by a president who had opposed it, in a ceremony so deliberately low-key that it bordered on insulting. George W. Bush signed it in private, without fanfare, without the customary pen presentation, without the Rose Garden gathering that legislation of this significance would normally receive. He signed it, his press secretary explained, because it had passed Congress and he respected the legislative process, not because he believed it was good policy.

This is worth noting, because the manner of a bill’s signing sometimes tells you more about its future than its contents do. A president who signs legislation he opposes, without ceremony, in private, has sent a signal to everyone in his party who also opposes it: I did what I had to do. Now it’s your turn.

Bush signed the bill and left for a two-day fundraising trip for congressional Republicans. The irony was not subtle and was not intended to be. The president of the United States signed the most significant campaign finance reform in a generation and then immediately went out to raise money under the rules that still applied — a gesture that communicated, to anyone paying attention, that the old system would adapt faster than the new law could constrain it.

For McCain, whose road from the Keating Five to this moment had consumed more than a decade of legislative trench warfare, the signing was not a celebration. It was a delivery receipt. He and Feingold had outmaneuvered a Senate leadership that had filibustered the bill repeatedly. The Enron scandal in 2001 had provided the final catalyst, exposing the scale at which corporate money had bankrolled legislators in both parties and giving the reformers the public momentum they needed to force a House vote through a rare discharge petition — only the second successful use of that parliamentary maneuver since the 1980s.

The bill passed the House 240 to 189. It passed the Senate 60 to 40. The votes were not close. The enthusiasm was. Of the 240 House votes in favour, 198 came from Democrats and 41 from Republicans. Of the 189 votes against, 176 came from Republicans. The bipartisan label on the bill was technically accurate and practically misleading. This was a reform that one party wanted and the other party tolerated, signed by a president who opposed it, in a room with no cameras. The ceremony told the truth that the vote tallies obscured.

Mitch McConnell was already in federal court before the ink was dry.

Senators John McCain (R-AZ) and Russ Feingold (D-WI), architects of the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002. Seven years of filibusters, one reluctant presidential signature, eight years of operation before the Supreme Court finished what Mitch McConnell started

What McCain-Feingold actually did was, at its core, two things.

First, it banned soft money contributions to national political party committees. The unlimited contributions from corporations, unions, and wealthy individuals that had grown from $19 million in 1984 to $496 million in 2002 could no longer flow to the national parties. The tap, specifically, was turned off. State parties retained some ability to raise and spend soft money on state and local activity, with restrictions, but the national apparatus that had become the primary vehicle for soft money in presidential and congressional elections was subject to hard limits for the first time.

Second, it regulated “electioneering communications” — a new category of political advertising defined as broadcast advertisements that mentioned a federal candidate within sixty days of a general election or thirty days of a primary. Corporations and unions could not use their treasury funds to pay for such advertisements. The rule was designed to close a loophole that had been growing since the mid-1990s: organizations that wanted to influence elections without formally “advocating” for or against a candidate had discovered that advertising that mentioned the candidate by name and ran in the weeks before an election had essentially the same effect as explicit advocacy, while being technically exempt from campaign finance rules because it stopped short of the magic words “vote for” or “vote against.”

These were real reforms. They addressed real problems. The soft money ban in particular represented a genuine structural change to how American campaigns were financed, and its passage after seven years of effort by McCain and Feingold represented one of the last moments in recent American political history when a bipartisan coalition successfully overcame the financial interests that benefited from the status quo.

The opposition understood exactly what had been taken from them. They moved immediately to get it back.

Soft money contributions to national party committees, 1984–2002. From $19 million to $496 million in eighteen years. McCain-Feingold turned off the tap. The system found another one within a single election cycle.

Mitch McConnell’s lawsuit — McConnell v. FEC — argued that the Act violated the First Amendment. The soft money ban infringed on political parties’ right to raise and spend money for political activity. The electioneering communications restriction infringed on corporations’ and unions’ right to run issue advertisements. The government had no sufficient interest that justified these restrictions. The Court should strike the Act down.

McConnell was not alone. Within days of the signing, eighty-five plaintiffs — including the National Rifle Association, the Republican National Committee, the California Democratic Party, and the American Civil Liberties Union — had filed suit. The coalition was as ideologically improbable as the one that had challenged Buckley in 1975, and unified by the same principle: strange bedfellows whose shared interest in the outcome was stronger than their disagreement about everything else.

The Supreme Court interrupted its summer recess to hear four hours of oral argument in a special September session — a procedural signal that the justices understood the stakes. The decision, issued on December 10, 2003, ran to 272 pages. By a 5-4 vote — Justice Sandra Day O’Connor joining the four more liberal justices — the Court upheld the Act’s core provisions. The majority opinion, co-authored by Justices Stevens and O’Connor, accepted the government’s central argument: that the flow of massive, unregulated soft money contributions gave rise to corruption, or at minimum the appearance of corruption, and that Congress had both the authority and the evidence to act. The record before the Court included extensive documentation that large donors had been rewarded with special access to legislators — not the explicit exchange of money for votes that the opposition insisted was the only form of corruption the Constitution permitted Congress to address, but something close enough that five justices found it sufficient.

The four dissenters were not quiet about their disagreement. Scalia called the decision a “sad day for the freedom of speech” and argued the law was incumbency protection dressed in reformist language. Kennedy, in a separate sixty-eight-page dissent, accused the majority of adopting a reasoning that was “profoundly paternalistic” and argued the Court was silencing speakers in an attempt to level a playing field the Constitution did not require to be level. The government, Kennedy wrote, should not be in the business of deciding how much political speech was too much.

That dissent — Kennedy’s dissent — would become the intellectual foundation of his majority opinion in Citizens United seven years later. The blueprint for the reversal was written into the decision that the reformers believed they had won.

McConnell lost. The Act stood.

The vote was 5-4. That number matters. It means the decision was one justice away from going the other way. It means that the constitutionality of the most significant campaign finance reform in a generation rested on the continued presence of Sandra Day O’Connor on the Supreme Court. O’Connor retired in 2005. President Bush replaced her with Samuel Alito.

The math changed.

Justice Sandra Day O’Connor, whose vote upheld the constitutionality of McCain-Feingold by a single margin. She retired in 2005. President Bush replaced her with Samuel Alito. The math changed — and with it, the future of American campaign finance law.

The opposition to McCain-Feingold came from two directions that were theoretically distinct and practically aligned.

The first was the political direction: the Republican Party establishment, led by McConnell, which had correctly calculated that soft money was flowing more generously to Republicans than to Democrats and saw the ban as a partisan attack dressed in reformist clothing. McConnell was not wrong that campaign finance regulation can serve incumbency protection — rules written by incumbents tend, not coincidentally, to advantage incumbents. He was not wrong that there are genuine First Amendment concerns about government regulation of political speech. He was wrong, or at least strategically selective, in failing to apply those concerns symmetrically to the soft money system that he was benefiting from.

The second direction was the ideological one: a loose coalition of libertarian and conservative legal thinkers who had been developing, since the 1970s, a comprehensive theory of the First Amendment that treated virtually all campaign finance regulation as unconstitutional. This was not purely a cover for financial interests, though it served financial interests well. Some of its proponents were genuine First Amendment absolutists who would have taken the same position regardless of which party benefited. Others were less pure. The coalition held together because the legal theory and the financial interest pointed in the same direction, and coalitions that serve multiple interests simultaneously tend to be durable.

The legal theory went like this: the First Amendment protects political speech. Money spent on political activity is a form of political speech. The government’s only legitimate interest in restricting such speech is preventing quid pro quo corruption — the explicit exchange of money for political favors. Independent spending — money that does not go directly to a candidate — cannot constitute quid pro quo corruption because no direct transaction occurs. Therefore the government has no sufficient interest in limiting independent spending. Therefore independent spending cannot be regulated. Therefore any law that attempts to regulate it is unconstitutional.

This was Buckley’s logic, stated more completely. It was the logic that Citizens United would eventually apply to finish the job that McConnell v. FEC had temporarily interrupted.

The years between the McConnell decision in 2003 and the Citizens United decision in 2010 were years of progressive erosion. The Act had been upheld, but it had been upheld by a coalition that was one justice away from reversal. The lawyers who had opposed it understood this. They began looking for the right case.

The right case, it turned out, involved a documentary film about Hillary Clinton.

But before we get to that, it is worth recording what McCain-Feingold accomplished in its brief operational lifetime. The 2004 election cycle was the first conducted under the soft money ban. The national parties could no longer raise unlimited soft money contributions. The electioneering communications restriction was in effect. Something would have to change.

What changed was the emergence of a new vehicle: the Section 527 organization, named for the provision of the Internal Revenue Code that governed it. Section 527 organizations were tax-exempt political organizations that could raise and spend unlimited money for political activity, provided they did not explicitly advocate for or against specific candidates. They were, in other words, a new workaround — the latest iteration of the principle that Sidney Hillman had established in 1943, updated for the post-McCain-Feingold landscape.

In the 2004 cycle, Section 527 organizations spent approximately $424 million. The most prominent were the Swift Boat Veterans for Truth, which ran advertising attacking Democratic presidential candidate John Kerry’s Vietnam War record, and America Coming Together, a Democratic organization that spent heavily on voter mobilization. Both were funded by large donors — Texas oilman T. Boone Pickens gave $4.5 million to the Swift Boat group; liberal financier George Soros gave $23 million to various Democratic 527s.

The arithmetic was brutal and immediate. McCain-Feingold had turned off a soft money tap that had reached $496 million in the 2002 cycle. In the very next presidential election, 527 organizations spent $424 million — eighty-five cents on the dollar, restored through a different pipe within twenty-four months. The reform had not reduced the amount of unregulated money in American elections. It had changed the return address. The national parties no longer controlled the flow. Instead, the money moved to organizations that were accountable to their donors rather than to party leadership, less transparent in their operations, and more extreme in their messaging — because organizations funded by a single billionaire or a small cluster of ideological donors have no institutional incentive toward moderation. The soft money system had been bad. What replaced it, in at least one respect, was worse: the same volume of money, with less accountability and less restraint.

The soft money ban had eliminated one vehicle. The system had produced another within a single election cycle. The maze had reset.

A television advertisement from Swift Boat Veterans for Truth, 2004 — the most prominent of the new Section 527 organizations that filled the vacuum McCain-Feingold created. Funded by $4.5 million from Texas oilman T. Boone Pickens. The soft money ban closed a $496 million tap. Section 527s opened a $424 million tap in the next room.

McCain watched this happen with the frustration of a man who had spent seven years building something and watched it begin to leak before the paint was dry. He continued to argue for reform. He pushed for stronger regulation of 527 organizations. He found less traction than he had found for the original Act, partly because the energy of reform had dissipated and partly because both parties were now using 527s and neither was eager to eliminate a tool it was actively using.

This is the reformer’s recurring dilemma in American campaign finance: by the time a reform is implemented, the system has already adapted around it, and the adaptation has been adopted by both sides, and neither side is willing to give up what the adaptation has given them. The reform becomes the floor, not the ceiling. The workaround becomes the new normal.

McCain-Feingold was the last successful major campaign finance reform in American history. It has not been followed by anything comparable. What followed it, in 2010, was not reform but reversal — a Supreme Court decision that did not merely open a new loophole but declared that the entire framework of loophole-closing was constitutionally suspect.

We are still living in the aftermath of that decision. We will be living in it for some time.

One legacy of McCain-Feingold that Americans encounter every election cycle: the “stand by your ad” provision requiring candidates to personally state “I approve this message.” It was designed to discourage attack ads. It became a punchline.
