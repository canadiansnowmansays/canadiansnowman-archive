The maze resets. Every election cycle, the dots reappear, the ghosts return, and the game begins again — unless we decide to rewrite the code.

We have spent nine essays following the money. We have watched it move from union halls to corporate boardrooms, from soft money accounts to super PACs, from disclosed contributions to invisible donor-advised funds layered through 501(c)(4) organizations on their way to purchasing congressional seats. We have watched the reforms come — the Tillman Act, the Federal Election Campaign Act, McCain-Feingold — and we have watched them be dismantled, one court case at a time, until what remains is a system that calls itself democratic while functioning as something considerably closer to plutocratic.

This essay is about what could be done instead.

I want to be clear about the nature of that question. I am not asking what could be done in a world without constraints — a world where the Supreme Court’s Citizens United decision could be waved away, where Mitch McConnell’s decades of opposition to campaign finance reform had not happened, where the donor class that benefits from the current system had no political influence. I am asking what could be done in the actual world, with the actual constraints that exist, by people who actually want to make American democracy function as advertised.

The answer is: more than the current discourse suggests. Considerably more.

The first thing worth establishing is that the Citizens United problem, while real and significant, is not the whole problem. Campaign finance reformers have sometimes made the mistake of treating Citizens United as the original sin — the moment when everything went wrong — in ways that obscure the longer history this series has tried to tell. Buckley v. Valeo established the foundational premise in 1976. The soft money machine of the 1980s and 1990s demonstrated what that premise enabled before Citizens United accelerated it. The FEC’s structural dysfunction has been a feature of the regulatory landscape for decades. Citizens United made things significantly worse, but it did not create the problem from nothing.

This matters for reform strategy, because it means that overturning Citizens United — while necessary — would not, by itself, restore a well-functioning campaign finance system. It would restore the 2009 landscape, which was already significantly compromised. Comprehensive reform requires addressing the full architecture: the super PAC, the dark money nonprofit, the FEC deadlock, the disclosure gaps, the structural advantages that concentrated wealth enjoys regardless of any specific court ruling.

That is a larger project. It is not an impossible one.

The constitutional amendment path is the most discussed and the most difficult.

Twenty-five states have now passed resolutions calling for a constitutional amendment to overturn Citizens United — or more precisely, to establish that constitutional rights belong to natural persons rather than corporations, and that Congress and the states may regulate the raising and spending of money to influence elections. The movement has genuine breadth: it includes red states and blue states, legislative bodies dominated by both parties, and polling that consistently shows overwhelming bipartisan public support for limits on campaign spending.

The difficulty is arithmetic. A constitutional amendment requires two-thirds of both houses of Congress and ratification by three-quarters of the states — thirty-eight of fifty. In the current political environment, with a Republican Party that has largely concluded that the post-Citizens United landscape serves its interests, achieving two-thirds of both chambers is effectively impossible without a dramatic shift in the political balance of power. The amendment path is a long-term project, not a near-term solution.

It is, however, worth pursuing on its own terms, because the process of pursuing it is itself politically useful. Every state that passes a resolution is a demonstration that public opinion on this issue is not aligned with the Supreme Court’s jurisprudence. Every congressional debate on the amendment is an opportunity to make the case for reform to audiences that might not otherwise engage with it. The amendment will not pass in the next Congress. It might pass in a Congress twenty years from now, if the public pressure is sustained.

Long-term projects require long-term thinking. Campaign finance reform has a long-term problem. It requires long-term thinking.

The disclosure path is simultaneously the most achievable and the most consistently blocked.

Super PACs and dark money groups act as the relentless ghosts in the American political maze — well-funded, hard to escape, and always in pursuit.

The DISCLOSE Act — Democracy Is Strengthened by Casting Light on Spending in Elections — has been introduced in Congress multiple times since Citizens United. Its core requirement is simple: organizations that spend money to influence federal elections must disclose their donors, above a threshold amount, to the public. Super PACs already do this. The Act would extend the requirement to the 501(c)(4) organizations that currently serve as the primary dark money vehicles.

The DISCLOSE Act would not eliminate dark money. It would illuminate it. Donors who currently enjoy the anonymity of 501(c)(4) status would, above the threshold, have their identities disclosed. This would not prevent them from spending. It would give voters the information they need to evaluate the messages they receive.

This is not a radical proposal. The Supreme Court has consistently held that disclosure requirements are constitutional — even Citizens United acknowledged the importance of transparency. The Act does not restrict spending. It requires transparency about who is spending. It is the narrowest possible reform consistent with the problem it addresses.

It has been filibustered in the Senate every time it has reached the floor.

Some critics of disclosure requirements — not all of them self-interested — argue that publicizing donor identities can expose individuals to harassment, particularly those supporting unpopular or minority causes. That concern is real in specific contexts. The answer to it is better harassment law, not permanent donor anonymity for political spending at scale. When a single individual spends $5 million in the final week of a state referendum, the public’s interest in knowing who that person is substantially outweighs any chilling concern.

The filibuster is the primary mechanism blocking disclosure reform. The senators who block the DISCLOSE Act represent states where dark money has been deployed to their benefit. Their opposition is rational, in the narrow sense of serving their interests. It is also, in any broader sense, indefensible.

Small donor public matching programs aim to do this: amplify the voice of ordinary citizens so that elected officials answer first to voters, not to the largest check-writers.

The small donor matching model is the most empirically validated reform available, and the one that has received the least attention in national debates dominated by the super PAC question.

New York City has operated a small donor matching program since 1988. Under the current version, small donations — contributions of $250 or less — are matched at a rate of 8-to-1 with public funds, for candidates who agree to spending limits and meet qualifying thresholds. A $100 donation from a constituent becomes an $800 donation for the candidate, effectively amplifying the political voice of small donors relative to large ones.

The results, documented over more than three decades, are striking. Candidates who participate in the matching program raise a significantly higher proportion of their funds from small donors in their own districts. They spend less time with major donors and more time with constituents. They are more responsive, by measurable metrics, to the preferences of ordinary voters. The program has not eliminated the influence of large donors in New York City politics — nothing short of comprehensive reform could do that — but it has meaningfully altered the incentive structure for participating candidates.

New York State adopted a version of the program in 2020. Connecticut, Hawaii, Maine, and Minnesota have similar programs at the state level. The political logic is sound and the empirical record is positive. The obstacle to federal adoption is not evidence — it is the opposition of incumbents who benefit from the current system and are disinclined to change it.

Public financing of federal elections — the model established by the post-Watergate reforms and steadily eroded since — deserves serious reconsideration.

The presidential public financing system created in 1974 worked reasonably well for several decades. Candidates who accepted public funds agreed to spending limits and were freed from the constant pressure to raise private money. The system began breaking down when the spending limits failed to keep pace with the actual cost of running a presidential campaign, and collapsed entirely in 2008 when Barack Obama — calculating correctly that he could raise far more through small donor online fundraising than the public financing limits allowed — became the first major-party presidential candidate to opt out of the system entirely.

A modernized public financing system — with limits set at realistic levels, adjusted for inflation, paired with small donor matching to amplify citizen participation — would not solve every problem in American campaign finance. It would address the most important one: the relentless financial dependence of candidates on large donors, which is the mechanism through which the new nobility’s preferences are transmitted into policy outcomes.

The cost would be real. Public financing of elections is not free. The Brennan Center has estimated that a comprehensive small donor matching program for federal elections would cost approximately $3 billion per election cycle — roughly the amount that super PACs alone spent in 2020. Whether that is a reasonable expenditure of public funds to ensure that elected officials are primarily accountable to their constituents rather than their donors is a values question. The evidence suggests it is.

Disclosure, public financing, and small donor matching all share one goal: making sure the votes that matter most are the ones cast by citizens — not the ones purchased by invisible money.

Let me say something, now, from the Canadian side of the border.

Canada has stricter campaign finance rules than the United States. Political donations are capped at approximately $1,700 per year to each registered party. Corporations and unions cannot donate to federal parties or candidates at all — a prohibition that has survived without the constitutional challenges that equivalent American restrictions have faced, because Canada’s constitutional framework does not treat money as speech in the way that Buckley established. Independent spending is permitted but subject to limits. The Elections Act requires disclosure of all contributions above a modest threshold.

The Canadian system is not perfect. It has its own money-in-politics problems, its own influence-peddling scandals, its own mechanisms by which wealthy interests seek to shape political outcomes. Party financing rules can also concentrate influence within party structures rather than eliminating it. It does not have, because its legal framework does not permit, the systematic architecture of dark money and super PACs that defines the American landscape.

The British system caps total campaign spending — for parties, candidates, and third parties combined — at levels that make American election costs appear from another planet. The German system provides public funding to parties based on their share of the vote, supplemented by private donations with strict limits and full disclosure. Neither system is beyond criticism. Both produce politicians who are, by empirical measures, more responsive to ordinary constituents and less responsive to the donor class than their American counterparts.

The United States chose differently. It chose, through Buckley and Citizens United and McCutcheon and a regulatory apparatus designed not to regulate, a system in which concentrated wealth is translated into concentrated political power with minimal legal friction. This was not the inevitable result of the First Amendment. Canada and Britain and Germany all have free speech. None of them have Peter Thiel. The American system produced the outcomes it was designed to produce, and it was designed — through the accumulated choices of lawyers, judges, legislators, and political operatives over eight decades — to produce them.

Choices can be unmade. Not easily. Not quickly. Not without sustained political effort against well-resourced opposition. But unmade.

The maze resets at the end of every game. The dots reappear. The character starts again from the same position, eating the same dots, pursued by the same ghosts, in the same maze.

But games can be redesigned. The code can be rewritten. The ghosts can be given different names — or removed from the board entirely, if the people who play the game decide that the current version is rigged in ways they are no longer willing to accept.

The American campaign finance system is not a law of nature. It is a set of choices, made by human beings, that other human beings can unmake. The twenty-five states that have called for a constitutional amendment have made a choice. The cities and states that have adopted small donor matching programs have made a choice. The campaign finance lawyers who file complaint after complaint with a deadlocked FEC, knowing the complaints will be dismissed, and file them again — they are making a choice too.

Sidney Hillman made a choice in 1943. He solved the problem in front of him and accidentally created a larger one. John McCain made a choice in the 1990s. He spent seven years building something and watched it be dismantled. Justice Stevens made a choice in 2010. He wrote ninety pages that described exactly what was coming, and it came.

The choice available now is whether to accept the maze as fixed — as a permanent feature of the American political landscape that cannot be altered because the Court has blessed it and the beneficiaries have protected it — or to treat it as what it actually is: a system built by people, sustained by choices, and capable of being changed by different people making different choices.

The maze resets. That much is true.

What happens next is not determined by the game. It is determined by the people who decide to keep playing — and the people who decide, finally, to change the rules.

Super PACman is an eleven part history of money in American democracy. The series began with the original sin and ends with the question that every generation of American reformers has faced and none has finally answered: does the promise mean what it says?
