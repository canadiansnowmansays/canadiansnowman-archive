I should begin by stating my qualifications.

I spent thirty-one years as a simultaneous interpreter at the highest levels of international diplomacy. I have sat in the glass booth at the United Nations and rendered eleven languages in real time while heads of state said things that made me briefly question my career. I have interpreted at G7 summits, NATO councils, bilateral meetings of considerable sensitivity, and one APEC dinner in which three world leaders simultaneously tried to explain their positions on fisheries quotas and I was asked to render all three at once, which I did, though I have not fully recovered.

I was not at the Beijing summit.

I want to be clear about that.

I was at home in Toronto, watching the coverage, drinking tea, and making notes in the margin of my notepad in the professional shorthand I have never been able to stop using even in retirement, because thirty-one years of training does not simply switch off because a man has reached a certain age and his knees have begun to offer opinions about stairs.

What follows is my professional reconstruction of what was said in Beijing, based on the public statements made before, during, and after the summit by both sides. It is not a transcript. It is, I believe, more accurate than a transcript, because a transcript records what people said, whereas what I have produced records what they meant, what they heard, and the distance between those two things, which in this case is considerable.

I offer it in the public interest and in the spirit of professional obligation.

On the Opening Pleasantries

All summits begin with pleasantries. This is not merely courtesy — it is intelligence. An experienced interpreter listens to the pleasantries very carefully, because they establish the frame within which everything that follows will be understood.

President Xi Jinping welcomed President Trump to Beijing with what the Chinese readout described as “warm and friendly” remarks. Xi noted the importance of the bilateral relationship, the shared interest in stability, and the necessity of managing differences through dialogue. He spoke, I am told, with characteristic precision — each sentence a closed system, each pause deliberate.

President Trump, according to his own subsequent account, told Xi that he had “always liked him” and that Xi was “a very good man, very strong.” He said the room was “beautiful” and that the Great Hall of the People was “very big, much bigger than people think.” He said he had had a “great” flight.

The Chinese interpreter rendered all of this faithfully.

The Chinese delegation, I am reliably informed by my professional network, made extensive notes.

Trump and Xi Jinping during the Beijing summit (Great Hall of the People). Trump touted new agricultural purchases, including soybeans, as a major win for U.S. farmers

On Trade

The trade discussion produced, by the end of two days, the following confirmed deliverables:

China will purchase $17 billion per year in American agricultural products for 2026, 2027, and 2028. This is in addition to previous soybean commitments, which are also ongoing, and which have been announced at sufficient summits that the American soybean has become, in my professional assessment, the most diplomatically significant legume in the history of international relations.

China has approved an initial purchase of 200 Boeing aircraft.

China has taken steps to open market access for American beef and poultry.

What the United States offered China in return has not been disclosed.

I want to sit with that sentence for a moment, because in thirty-one years of diplomatic interpretation, I have never encountered a joint communiqué in which one side’s concessions were enumerated in detail and the other side’s were described as forthcoming. This is not how trade agreements work. This is how one party to a trade agreement describes the agreement when it is not yet certain what it agreed to.

Jamieson Greer, the U.S. Trade Representative, said on Sunday that more details would be available “in the coming days.”

In interpreting, we have a term for a statement that acknowledges a question without answering it. We call it a non-response. Mr. Greer’s statement was a masterpiece of the form.

The Boeing aircraft are locked in, per Mr. Greer.

They were also locked in after the 2017 summit, when $250 billion in deals were announced, including an $83.7 billion memorandum of understanding with the state of West Virginia that exceeded West Virginia’s entire GDP and was never heard from again.

West Virginia has sent a representative to inquire about the aircraft.

The representative has not yet received a response.¹

¹ I note, as a professional courtesy, that the 200 Boeing aircraft represent approximately two years of Boeing’s current production capacity. I further note that Boeing has, in recent years, experienced certain quality control difficulties that have been documented extensively in the press and that I mention only to observe that “initial purchase” and “delivered aircraft” are phrases that mean different things in different timeframes. The Chinese delegation is aware of this distinction. They studied it carefully after 2017.

On Taiwan

This is the section I have been most professionally reluctant to write.

Before the summit, President Trump described potential American weapons sales to Taiwan as a “negotiating chip” with Beijing. He said this publicly. On camera. In a way that was subsequently played, I am told, on a continuous loop in certain offices in Taipei.

After the summit, Mr. Greer said there had been “no change in American policy on Taiwan.”

I have been an interpreter for thirty-one years. I have spent those years in the precise business of determining what words mean. I want to offer the following professional assessment of these two statements:

They cannot both be true.

A “negotiating chip” is, by definition, something offered in exchange for something else. If Taiwan’s weapons deal is a negotiating chip, then its status is subject to negotiation. If there is no change in American policy on Taiwan, then there is nothing to negotiate. These positions are not in tension. They are mutually exclusive. One of them is what was said in the room in Beijing. The other is what was said on American television on Sunday morning.

My professional experience tells me which is which.

Taiwan’s President Lai Ching-te, who also was not in the room, posted on social media that Taiwan “will not provoke or escalate conflicts, but we will not, under pressure, relinquish our national sovereignty and dignity.”

This is an extremely precise statement. It is the kind of statement produced by people who have read a transcript very carefully and chosen every word in response to what they found there.

Mr. Greer said U.S. support for Taiwan remained unchanged.

President Lai said Taiwan would not relinquish its sovereignty under pressure.

These are not the same reassurance.

In Helsinki, in 2018, there was a private meeting between Trump and Putin. An interpreter was present. What was said in that room was never disclosed. The world spent weeks trying to reconstruct it from subsequent statements, policy shifts, and the expressions on the faces of people who had been briefed.

I spent those weeks making notes.

I am making notes again.²

² The Russian President visited Beijing the week after President Trump departed. This was described by the Kremlin as a separate visit with a separate agenda. Xi Jinping met Putin three days after meeting Trump. I am a retired interpreter. I note things. I note, for instance, that the two leaders who most benefit from American uncertainty about Taiwan met in the same city in the same week, and that one of them had just spent two days with the American president discussing what, exactly, the United States was prepared to offer in exchange for soybeans and Boeing aircraft. I note this. I do not draw conclusions. I am an interpreter, not an analyst. I note things and other people draw conclusions and then wonder why they didn’t see it sooner.

On What Was Agreed

The White House released details on Sunday of what China agreed to.

It has not yet released details of what America agreed to.

Mr. Greer said more information would be available in coming days.

I have noted this.

On the Soybeans

The soybeans were confirmed.

Both sides confirmed them.

They are the one point of complete bilateral consensus from the Beijing summit of May 14-15, 2026.

I have been an interpreter for thirty-one years. I have sat in rooms while men in suits negotiated the architecture of the world. I have rendered their words from one language to another, trying always to carry not just the meaning but the weight — the things behind the words, the things the words were meant to conceal, the distances between what was said and what was meant and what was heard.

I have never before written a professional assessment of a bilateral summit in which the most unambiguous deliverable was a legume.

I record this not as a criticism but as an observation.

The soybeans are confirmed.

Everything else is in the coming days.³

³ I should note, for completeness, that the summit also produced a “Board of Trade,” which is a new bilateral institution with no staff, no budget, no fixed meeting schedule, and no agenda beyond “trade matters.” I was briefly asked, in retirement, whether I would be available to interpret at its first meeting. I said I would need to check my calendar. My calendar is empty. I have not called back. Some institutions are better understood from a distance, and I have reached an age where I am permitted to say so.

A Final Note on the Craft

Simultaneous interpretation is, at its best, an act of complete presence. You are in the room. You are in both languages at once. You carry the words from one world to another and you try — you always try — to carry what the words actually mean, not just what they say.

What they actually mean is not always what the communiqué records.

What was agreed in Beijing may become clearer in coming days, as Mr. Greer promises. It may become clearer when the weapons deal is either completed or quietly shelved. It may become clearer when the Boeing aircraft are either delivered or become the subject of a subsequent summit. It may become clearer when the Board of Trade convenes, if it convenes, and produces minutes, if it produces minutes.

It may become clearest of all in Taipei, where a president who was not in the room has read the transcript very carefully and chosen every word of his response with the precision of someone who understands exactly what was said in Beijing and exactly what it means for a small democracy that has been described, in public, by the leader of its most important ally, as a negotiating chip.

I was not in the room.

I am making notes.

The soybeans, at least, are confirmed.

The author is a retired Canadian writer, not a retired interpreter. He was also not in the room. He is also making notes. The soybeans are also confirmed from where he sits.
