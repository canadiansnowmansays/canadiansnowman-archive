James Talarico on the trail in Texas — tied at forty-seven in a state Trump carried by fourteen, with the best net favorability of any candidate in six states.

There is a comfortable story making its way through American political coverage this summer, and it goes like this: the environment has turned against the Republicans, Donald Trump is underwater on the economy, and a Democratic wave is building toward November. The topline of the new Times/Siena battleground survey obligingly supplies the numbers. Across six states Trump carried by an average of eight points two years ago, the Senate races now average a dead tie — forty-seven percent for each party. Trump’s approval sits at forty-three. Only a little over a third of voters approve of how he has handled the cost of living, and among independents that figure collapses to twenty-four.

A wave, then. Except waves are supposed to lift everything at once, and this one keeps setting boats down in different places for reasons that have nothing to do with the tide.

Look at what the same poll actually found, state by state, and the tidy wave narrative starts to come apart in the most instructive way. It comes apart because the individual candidate — that supposedly obsolete variable in an age of nationalized, straight-ticket politics — turns out to be doing most of the work.

Two Men, One Environment, Opposite Results

Ken Paxton at CPAC — minus twelve net favorability, the worst number posted by any candidate, in either party, anywhere in the survey.

Start with the two candidates who bracket the entire field.

In Texas — a state Trump won by fourteen points — James Talarico is tied with Ken Paxton at forty-seven apiece. His net favorability is plus six. Paxton’s is minus twelve, which happens to be the worst net favorability of any candidate, in either party, in any of the six states surveyed. Talarico is not merely surviving the Texas environment; he is running ahead of his own party’s brand, pulling white college graduates that neither Joe Biden nor Kamala Harris could reach, and doing it in a state where Democrats have not won a Senate seat in a generation.

Now travel to Maine, which is nearly the mirror image of Texas — a New England state that has not gone Republican at the presidential level since the elder Bush. On paper, this should be the easiest Democratic pickup on the board. Instead it produces the one Democrat in all six states with negative net favorability. Graham Platner, the oyster farmer and combat veteran, carries minus five, trailing his own damaged Republican opponent, Susan Collins, on the likability measure that every other Democrat in the survey wins. The Reddit history, the questions about a tattoo’s symbolism, the reporting on his treatment of women — these are candidate problems, not environmental ones, and no favorable national mood has been able to paper over them.

Graham Platner — the perfect résumé on paper, and the only Democrat in six states voters like less than his opponent

Same year. Same anti-Trump environment. Same generic Democratic tailwind. One candidate is dragging his party uphill in hostile territory; the other is being dragged down by himself in friendly territory. If the environment were the whole story, these two results could not coexist. They coexist because the candidate is the story.

The State That Proves the Rule

For the cleanest demonstration, though, look at Ohio — because Ohio is the control group.

Sherrod Brown is not a flawed candidate or an untested newcomer. He is a former three-term senator running to reclaim the seat voters took from him just two years ago, in the friendliest environment his party has enjoyed in years. Every tailwind blowing through this poll is at his back. And he is polling forty-seven to Jon Husted’s fifty — which is, almost to the decimal, exactly where he stood when he lost in 2024.

Sit with that for a moment, because it is the single most clarifying number in the survey. The environment improved dramatically. Brown did not move. The wave arrived and lifted him precisely nowhere. Whatever the electorate had already decided about Sherrod Brown, a better national mood did not un-decide it.

Sherrod Brown — polling forty-seven to fifty, almost exactly where Ohio left him on election night 2024.

If a friendly year automatically rescued Democrats, Brown — an incumbent-in-exile with universal name recognition and a friendly climate — should be the surest bet in the field. He is instead the surest illustration that the tide does not lift a boat voters have already dry-docked.

The Brand and the Person

There is a fourth data point, and it explains why the candidate matters so much this year: the Democratic Party’s national brand has become an anchor its individual candidates have to actively cut loose.

Iowa makes the gap almost comically visible. As the New York Times reported, fifty-seven percent of Iowa voters say the Democratic Party is too far to the left. Only twenty-five percent say the same about Josh Turek — the Democratic nominee, a state legislator and two-time Paralympic gold medalist in wheelchair basketball. That is a thirty-two-point chasm between how voters see the party and how they see the person carrying its banner. Turek is running competitively not because voters have warmed to the Democratic label but because he has managed to stand apart from it.

Josh Turek — two Paralympic golds, one statehouse seat, and a thirty-two-point gap between how Iowa sees him and how it sees his party.

This is the mechanism underneath the whole poll. In a nationalized era, we were told, the individual no longer matters — voters pick a jersey, not a player. But when the jersey itself is a liability, the player who can make voters forget the jersey becomes the entire ballgame. Talarico can. Turek can. Brown, for reasons already litigated in 2024, cannot. Platner is actively worse than his jersey.

The Governors Say It Twice

Lest anyone think this is a quirk of Senate contests, the companion polling on the governor’s races runs the same experiment and gets the same answer — in the very same states.

In Iowa, Democrat Rob Sand leads forty-eight to forty-seven in the open race to replace a retiring Republican governor, running his broad, deliberately bipartisan pitch a step ahead of the environment. In Ohio, Dr. Amy Acton is tied at forty-seven with Vivek Ramaswamy — a self-funding celebrity who has already poured twenty-five million dollars of his own money into the race and still cannot pull clear. Two more deep-red states, two more Democrats outrunning what the partisan lean says they should be able to do, two more contests where the specific human being on the ballot is bending a result the fundamentals said was settled.

Dr. Amy Acton — tied at forty-seven with an opponent who has already spent twenty-five million dollars of his own money trying to pull away.

What This Means for November

The wave narrative is not wrong, exactly. The environment has turned. Trump is a drag. The door to the Senate majority, bolted shut two years ago, is genuinely ajar.

But an environment only opens doors. It does not walk through them. What this poll shows, race by race, is that whether a given door actually swings depends overwhelmingly on who is standing in front of it — and that the party has assembled, more or less by accident, a field of candidates ranging from the extraordinary to the self-sabotaging.

So here is the honest forecast, the one the topline tie obscures. Democrats will not win or lose this Senate on the strength of the national mood. They will win or lose it one candidate at a time — a teacher-turned-seminarian in Texas, a wheelchair-basketball champion in Iowa, a returning exile in Ohio who cannot escape his own past, an oyster farmer in Maine who cannot escape his own Reddit account. The tide came in. Now we find out which of them can swim.

Everyone spent the spring arguing about the wave. It turns out the more useful question was always the older one, the one nationalized politics was supposed to have retired: who, exactly, is on the ballot?

This piece draws on the New York Times/Siena battleground polls conducted June 15–29, 2026, surveying 3,659 likely voters across Alaska, Iowa, Maine, North Carolina, Ohio, and Texas, with companion gubernatorial polling in Iowa, Ohio, Maine, and Texas.
