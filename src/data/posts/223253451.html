Part 100 of this series covered AI quietly reshaping Medicare through pilot programs few patients ever voted on. This entry covers two more domains where the same pattern holds — AI deployed into systems that already carry real human stakes, built and shipped with minimal public visibility until an investigation forces the visibility after the fact.

The algorithm built to find people who would lose the most

As The New York Times’ Alex Klavens, Walt Bogdanich, and Jenny Vrentas reported on September 19, 2026, following interviews with more than 40 former DraftKings employees and internal documents obtained by the paper, the online sports-betting giant built a machine-learning model in 2023 specifically to identify which customers were most likely to respond to promotional incentives by gambling — and losing — more. The model scored users on what the company calls “elasticity”: the higher the score, the more money a promotion was likely to extract from that particular gambler. Jayden Butts, the data analyst who tested the model on live customers, described the core question in terms that need no further interpretation: “Is this person going to give us more than we’re giving them? And if the answer is yes, open the floodgates.” Butts, who grew up watching his own grandfather’s gambling addiction and later developed his own gambling problem with online slots, told the Times that his supervisors made clear the company wasn’t trying to save money by avoiding unprofitable customers — it was trying to redirect promotional spending toward the customers likely to lose the most.

The detail that turns this from an aggressive marketing story into something closer to this series’ own account of deliberate risk-blindness is what DraftKings did with the mirror-image version of the same technology. A separate team, led by data scientist Nestor Hernandez, built a model using the identical betting-record data to predict which customers were heading toward a gambling crisis — the same signal, pointed at protection instead of extraction. Jake Shannin, who continued the project after Hernandez left the company, said the team concluded that “even with simple rules” the model could produce a better-than-guessing sense of who needed intervention. The presentation to company leadership, including Lori Kalani, DraftKings’s chief responsible gaming officer, was cancelled the day it was scheduled. Two other attempts by DraftKings employees to build similar protective models have also been shelved. Kalani’s own explanation to the Times was that the company made a “collective decision” the approach “wasn’t evidence-based” — a rationale that sits uneasily next to the company’s simultaneous, continued investment in the promotional-targeting version of the same underlying technique, and against competitors including PrizePicks, whose responsible-gaming director told the Times his company has a “moral obligation” to use exactly this kind of predictive tool: “Frankly, without technology like this, I can’t look you in the eye and say, ‘We’re doing our best.’”

The human cost the Times reporting attaches to this isn’t abstract. Bryan Biehl lost nearly $70,000 gambling online, more than half of it at DraftKings; during a period when he’d begun therapy for his addiction, he received 40 separate promotions from the company in the first two weeks of December alone. “I would get flooded with bonuses and deposits,” he told the Times. “If you are in addiction, you are not going to say no.” He relapsed once more on Christmas Day before placing himself on a self-exclusion list. DraftKings disputes former employees’ characterisation of its practices and says promotions are directed at “customers who demonstrate sustained, engaged use of our platform, not toward customers based on their losses” — a defence that does not address why the company built, tested, and then declined to deploy the specific model capable of distinguishing engaged use from a developing crisis.

The Times investigation also lands on a company already fighting local officials over precisely this question. In April 2025 the City of Baltimore sued DraftKings and FanDuel under its consumer protection ordinance, alleging, among other things, that the companies used “data analytics to keep problem gamblers making wagers,” as Adam Thompson and Kelsey Kushner reported for CBS News Baltimore; Mayor Brandon Scott said the two firms had “specifically targeted our most vulnerable residents — including those struggling with gambling disorders.” In Philadelphia, the city issued a subpoena on April 24, 2026 seeking DraftKings records on “wagering revenue, customer targeting, promotions, VIP programmes and responsible gambling practices.” DraftKings’ response, reported by Charles Shephardson for GamblingNews on July 18, 2026, was to file a federal lawsuit to stop the investigation, arguing that Pennsylvania’s Gaming Control Board holds “general and sole authority over gaming and related activities across the state” and that the city has no business asking. The argument may well succeed on the law. It also means the records that would show how customer targeting actually works are, for now, the subject of litigation over who is allowed to look at them, which is the same visibility problem this essay is about.

The tool nobody who flies through Washington got a vote on

The New York Times’ Aishvarya Kavi and Karoun Demirjian, reporting September 18, 2026, cover a different domain with a similar shape: the Federal Aviation Administration’s rollout, announced on September 18, of an AI tool called SMART — Strategic Management of Airspace, Routing and Trajectories — designed to analyse scheduling and weather data and recommend routes that avoid compound flight delays at three of the country’s busiest and most scrutinized airports: Reagan National, Dulles, and Baltimore-Washington. The $875 million system’s initial deployment lands squarely in the airspace already under particular scrutiny following the January 2025 midair collision between a commercial jet and an Army Black Hawk helicopter that killed 67 people — a crash the National Transportation Safety Board attributed partly to FAA failures. The NTSB’s final report, released on February 17, 2026 after a public board meeting on January 27, was specific about which failures: the FAA had placed a helicopter route in close proximity to a runway approach path, and “did not regularly evaluate route safety data and did not implement recommendations proposed to reduce risk of midair collision.” On the night of the crash a single controller was handling both the helicopter and the airliner, an arrangement the investigation described as not normal for that time of day. The agency now deploying an advisory algorithm into that airspace is, in other words, the agency the safety board found had failed to act on the data it already had. Transportation officials are explicit that SMART will only recommend routes to human controllers, who remain free to ignore its suggestions, and that the tool will start conservatively, addressing only same-day weather delays before scaling toward its full month-ahead predictive scope.

The rollout proceeds against a backdrop this series’ own healthcare coverage in Part 100 would recognise immediately: a federal agency moving to deploy AI faster than the underlying institutional capacity to support it. The FAA is simultaneously requesting billions more from Congress to modernize air traffic infrastructure generally, while managing a chronic controller shortage the agency’s own numbers illustrate starkly — a 2025 report found the FAA needed 14,633 controllers to be fully staffed; a revised estimate in the spring of 2026 cut that figure by more than 2,000 positions, citing more efficient scheduling as an offset, even as the agency reported having barely more than 11,000 fully certified controllers as of April 2026. The National Air Traffic Controllers Association, in its own statement, made explicit what this essay’s DraftKings reporting already illustrated in a different domain: the union “had not been part of the design or testing process of the system” it will now be asked to work alongside, and insists any new technology should “complement and not replace” controllers’ own professional judgment rather than substitute for it. Robert Mann, a former airline executive turned aviation consultant, offers the most grounded skepticism in either piece of reporting: SMART may suggest better flight plans, but delays typically compound after a plan is already filed, meaning the tool addresses a narrower slice of the actual delay problem than its billing suggests — and airlines that cooperate with the system initially, he predicts, may come to resent it once its schedule recommendations start costing them the carefully planned routes they’d rather keep controlling themselves.

Two domains, one pattern

Neither story involves an agent going rogue, a company issuing an extinction warning, or a summit between heads of state — the register this series has spent most of its run documenting. Both involve something closer to this series’ own account of the HHS medical-AI rollout in Part 100: a system with real, differential power over vulnerable people, built and deployed with the public informed only after the fact, by journalists rather than by the companies or agencies doing the deploying. DraftKings built the tool that could have protected Bryan Biehl and chose not to ship it. The FAA is deploying a tool into the exact airspace where the cost of getting it wrong has already been measured in bodies, without the controllers who’ll actually use it having had a hand in designing it. Neither decision required an agent capable of anything approaching the autonomy this series has spent its Hugging Face coverage documenting. Both required only the same choice this series keeps finding underneath its most consequential stories: someone with the power to build the safer version, and a business or institutional reason not to.
