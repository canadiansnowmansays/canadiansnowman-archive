On the morning of February 24, 2022, Russian missiles struck Kyiv.

For Ukrainians, it was the beginning of the worst ordeal their country had faced since the Second World War. For the post-war international order, it was something else: an unexpected demonstration that the institutional framework assembled over seventy years still worked — that when a major power committed naked aggression against a smaller neighbor, the Western alliance could mount a coherent, sustained, and surprisingly effective response.

Surprising because the priors were not favorable. The Trump presidency had spent four years undermining allied confidence. The Afghanistan withdrawal of August 2021 — the chaotic, humiliating scenes at Kabul airport, the hasty American departure that abandoned interpreters, allies, and a country to the Taliban — had raised fundamental questions about American reliability. Putin had calculated, on the basis of these priors, that NATO would not respond effectively to an invasion that he expected to complete in three days.

He was wrong. And being wrong cost him, and Russia, more than he had imagined possible.

The Response That Worked

The Western response to the Ukraine invasion was not, by any objective measure, adequate to prevent enormous suffering. Hundreds of thousands of people died. Millions were displaced. Ukrainian cities — Mariupol, Bakhmut, Avdiivka — were reduced to rubble in battles of attrition that recalled the Western Front of the First World War rather than the swift maneuver operations of the post-Cold War era.

But the response demonstrated something that had been in doubt: the capacity for collective action.

The sanctions package assembled within days of the invasion was unprecedented in scope and speed. Russia was cut off from SWIFT. The assets of the Russian central bank, held in Western financial institutions, were frozen — approximately $300 billion, a sum that provided the Western alliance with extraordinary leverage. Russian oligarchs’ yachts were seized in European harbors. Russia’s economy, which Putin had deliberately structured for sanctions resilience, contracted sharply.

The military assistance was transformative. The Javelin anti-tank missiles that arrived in the invasion’s first weeks allowed Ukrainian ground forces to destroy Russian armor at a rate that shattered the myth of Russian military invincibility. The HIMARS rocket systems enabled Ukraine to strike Russian logistics depots and command nodes far behind the front lines, disrupting the supply chains on which the Russian advance depended. The intelligence sharing — American, British, and allied intelligence agencies providing near-real-time information about Russian troop movements — was an asymmetric advantage that Ukraine’s defenders exploited to devastating effect.

Ramstein Ukraine Defense Contact Group — defense ministers coordinating aid from 40+ nations

The coordination was sustained through the Ramstein contact group — the gathering of defense ministers from more than forty nations that met monthly to coordinate assistance, share intelligence, and maintain the political will necessary for a sustained effort. It was the most significant institutionalization of collective military assistance since the Second World War — and it was built, largely improvised, in the first months of the invasion.

Ukraine’s battlefield performance surprised everyone — including, it must be said, many Ukrainians who had assumed that Russian military power would be more effective. The Ukrainian military that had been trained, equipped, and reorganized through the eight years since Russia’s seizure of Crimea in 2014 proved capable of stopping, and in some cases reversing, Russian advances that had been assumed to be unstoppable.

This was not merely a military story. It was an institutional story. The post-war order had created, over seventy years, a framework of military standardization, intelligence sharing, logistical cooperation, and political coordination that could be rapidly activated in a genuine emergency. When the emergency came, the framework worked.

The Limits

But the response also revealed the limits that would eventually doom its sustainability.

The first limit was the hesitation ladder. Every escalation rung — the provision of anti-tank missiles, the HIMARS systems, the Patriot air defense, the tanks, the long-range missiles, the fighter jets — required months of political negotiation within the Western alliance before it was implemented. Each step was delayed by the fear of “provoking” Russia, a fear that proved consistently overestimated — Russia’s response to each escalation rung was to threaten nuclear war and then do nothing — but that was politically powerful enough to slow the assistance to a pace that was adequate to prevent Ukrainian defeat but insufficient to produce decisive victory.

Ukrainian forces with HIMARS — the transformative weapon that helped stop the Russian advance

The second limit was the uneven commitment. The United States provided the largest share of military assistance and the most capable weapons systems. European countries provided significant economic support and some military assistance, but their defense industrial capacity had been systematically run down over three decades of post-Cold War complacency. Germany, nominally Europe’s largest economy and most capable military power, struggled to supply basic ammunition stocks that its own army had allowed to run low.

The third limit was the political sustainability question. Ukraine’s defense was costing the United States and its allies real money — approximately $100 billion from the United States alone in the first two years — and producing no rapid resolution. Public support, while initially strong in most Western countries, showed signs of fatigue as the war extended without a decisive outcome.

And the fourth limit was the one that ultimately proved fatal: the American political cycle.

The Second Trump Term and Ukraine’s Abandonment

Donald Trump returned to office in January 2025 having campaigned, among other things, on ending the Ukraine war “in 24 hours” — a promise that was operationally meaningless but politically effective, capturing the fatigue of an electorate that had been paying for a war for three years without visible resolution.

The second Trump administration’s approach to Ukraine was not the immediate withdrawal of support that some had feared. It was something more insidious: the gradual erosion of support combined with the systematic pressure on Ukraine to accept terms that would reward Russian aggression.

Military assistance was not immediately cut off. But it was reduced. Intelligence sharing became more selective. The Ramstein contact group continued to meet, but American participation became less reliable. And publicly, administration officials began describing the situation in terms that suggested American patience was limited — that Ukraine would need to make concessions, that a negotiated settlement was necessary even if it meant accepting Russian territorial gains.

The Oval Office Showdown

The diplomatic pressure on Ukraine to negotiate on Russian terms was accompanied by the lifting of sanctions on Russian oil — the gesture to Moscow that Robert Kagan noted would provide Putin with a $38 billion windfall even if the Iran war ended in April 2026. The message to Russia was unmistakable: the sanctions regime that had been assembled at such effort and sustained at such cost was negotiable, and the price of negotiation was manageable.

For Ukraine, the second Trump term’s approach was a demonstration of exactly what Zelensky had warned about during the first Trump term: that American support was conditional, temporary, and ultimately subordinate to American political calculations in which Ukrainian survival was not a primary consideration.

For the post-war order, it was the definitive answer to the question that Ukraine’s initial defense had raised. The order had worked — for a while, at a cost, against a specific adversary, in a specific context. When the political conditions that had made it work changed, it stopped working. The order was not, it turned out, self-sustaining. It was American political will made institutional. And American political will was not, it turned out, reliably available.

The Iran War’s Shadow Over Ukraine

The Iran war that began on February 28, 2026 cast a specific shadow over Ukraine that has received insufficient attention.

The war consumed American military resources — munitions, air defense interceptors, logistics capacity, intelligence attention — that had previously been available for Ukraine. The depletion of Patriot missile interceptors by the Gulf War’s air defense demands was particularly consequential: Ukraine depends on Patriot systems to defend its cities against Russian missile strikes, and the systems that would have resupplied Ukraine’s stocks were diverted to the Gulf.

The war also consumed European political attention at precisely the moment when European strategic autonomy — the project that Trump’s first term had made necessary — was making real progress. The European defense spending increases of 2023-2025, the joint procurement initiatives, the gradual development of a European security capacity that did not depend on American leadership — all of this was complicated by the Iran war’s economic disruption, which strained European government budgets and created pressure to choose between energy relief for citizens and defense investment.

And the war demonstrated to Putin what he had long suspected: that American strategic attention was not reliably focused on Europe, that the Middle East remained capable of diverting American resources and political will, and that the window of maximum Western support for Ukraine was closing.

Russia has not moved to exploit this window — yet. The grinding attrition of the Ukraine war has constrained Russian options in ways that the invasion’s architects did not anticipate. But the conditions for a Russian move to consolidate its position — to push for a settlement on terms that reflect the current lines of contact rather than the internationally recognized borders — are being created by the Iran war and by the second Trump administration’s evident preference for a settlement that ends the financial and political cost of Ukraine support.

What that settlement looks like — whether it is a genuine peace or a frozen conflict that gives Russia time to rebuild for the next round — is the question that will shape European security for the next generation.

Iran, 2026: No allies consulted, no UN authorization, no multilateral burden-sharing — and an adversary that has closed the world’s most important energy chokepoint while the countries most dependent on it watch from the sidelines.

Ukraine, 2022: Forty-plus nations, transformative weapons systems, the post-war framework activated at speed.

The Lesson of 2022-2025

The Ukraine experience carries one lesson of overriding importance for understanding the post-war order’s trajectory.

The order can work when all of the following conditions are present simultaneously: a genuine and immediate threat that mobilizes political will; an adversary sufficiently limited in capability that conventional military assistance can offset its advantages; an American administration willing to provide the coordination, intelligence, and military systems that only the United States can provide; and a European alliance with sufficient remaining institutional coherence to act collectively.

None of these conditions can be taken for granted. Each is contingent on specific political, economic, and strategic circumstances that may not recur. The Ukraine response was not a demonstration that the post-war order is robust. It was a demonstration that the order can function under optimal conditions — and that those conditions are increasingly rare.

The Iran war demonstrates what happens when those conditions are absent: when the American administration acts unilaterally rather than within a multilateral framework, when the adversary is capable enough to impose real costs on American power, when the alliance is not mobilized but rather alienated, and when the institutional frameworks of the post-war order are bypassed rather than activated.

The post-war order was built knowing that its sustainability depended on consistent maintenance — that the institutions, alliances, and norms created after 1945 were not self-reinforcing but required active investment by their most important member. Keenleyside understood this from the Canadian side of the table: the medium power next to the superpower always knows that the framework holds only as long as the superpower chooses to honour it. Power, to be legitimate, requires not just capability but commitment: the sustained, demonstrable, costly commitment that tells smaller and weaker partners that their security is taken seriously.

That commitment has been withdrawn. The consequences are now visible, in the Strait of Hormuz and in the front lines of Ukraine, simultaneously.

The last alliance held in 2022. In 2026, it is watching from the sidelines as the reluctant empire becomes the rogue superpower — and wondering what comes next.
