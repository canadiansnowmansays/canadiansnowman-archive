On August 19, 2026, America’s gross national debt crossed $40 trillion. I want to sit with that sentence for a moment, because the series this essay belongs to has now been outrun by its own title twice. When I began writing about the $37 trillion question in July 2025, the number felt like an argument in itself — a threshold worth naming, a figure large enough to force a conversation. By March 2026 it was $38.86 trillion and I was already noting, in the Epilogue, that the title had become a historical artifact. Five months later it is $40 trillion, and I find myself less interested in the number than in the shape of the excuses now being offered for it, because the excuses are, for the first time in this series, coming directly from the people who made the promises.

As Alan Rappeport reported for the New York Times, the United States is on track to borrow more than $2 trillion this year alone, and roughly half of that new borrowing is simply interest on money already spent — the debt now feeding itself in the way Lloyd Blankfein described to David Frum back in the spring, the compound spiral where higher rates produce larger interest payments which produce larger deficits which produce higher rates. Blankfein called it a grain silo slowly emptying. Five months later, the silo has emptied by another trillion dollars, and the mechanism he described in the abstract has acquired a name, a face, and — this is the part worth dwelling on — a spokesman willing to explain it on cable news.

The Confession on Newsmax

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent set a goal, early in this administration, of reducing the federal deficit to 3 percent of GDP by 2028, down from over 6 percent when Donald Trump took office. That goal now reads like the Trump phone’s “Made in USA” promise — a commitment that existed sincerely at the moment it was made and evaporated the moment it met arithmetic. Bessent acknowledged as much last week, telling Newsmax that deficits are moving in the wrong direction, and offering three specific reasons that deserve to be catalogued, because they constitute the administration’s own after-action report on its own failure.

First, the war in Iran. Military spending associated with the conflict has pushed costs higher, and the resulting rise in energy prices has acted as a drag on economic growth, undercutting the tax revenue the administration hoped a stronger economy would generate. Second, the tariff refunds. When the Supreme Court ruled earlier this year that a significant portion of the administration’s signature tariff programme was illegal, the federal government was forced to refund more than $160 billion to the companies that had paid the import duties — money that had been counted, however provisionally, as progress toward Bessent’s deficit target, and that vanished the moment the Court spoke. Third, the One Big Beautiful Bill’s factory-expensing provision, which allows businesses to immediately deduct the cost of new construction and equipment. The Joint Committee on Taxation estimates this could cost $100 billion this year alone. Bessent’s defence of that last item is worth quoting for its imagery rather than its economics: the deduction, he said, is “a hit now to the deficit, but we are creating productive assets for future growth which will be paying taxes down the line” — like pulling back a slingshot and storing potential energy that becomes kinetic later.

It is a lovely metaphor. It is also, notably, a promise about the future made to explain a failure in the present, which is the defining rhetorical move of this entire series. The OBBBA was sold as fiscal responsibility that would pay off later. DOGE was sold as savings that would materialize later. The tariffs were sold as revenue that would arrive later. Later has now arrived three separate times in this series, and on each occasion it has turned out to be a different later, still ahead, still promised, still — like the Trump phone’s delivery date — sliding one more season down the calendar.

DOGE: The Epitaph, Finally Written

I devoted a substantial portion of the March Epilogue to the Department of Government Efficiency’s collapsing credibility — the arc from $55 billion claimed, to $16.5 billion documented, to the discovery that half of that was a single data-entry error mistaking $8 billion for $8 million, to a Politico analysis finding only $1.4 billion in actual verified cuts. Five months later, the number has moved again, and moved in the direction you would expect: DOGE now claims just over $200 billion in total savings against its original $1 trillion promise, and the Government Accountability Office has formally concluded this month that even that reduced figure lacks reliability and transparency. The agency tasked with auditing the government’s books cannot audit its own auditor’s math.

I want to resist the temptation to treat this as merely comic, because the comedy obscures the structural point. The federal budget is approximately $7.4 trillion. Roughly 64 percent of that goes to Social Security, Medicare, health programmes, income security, and veterans’ benefits — spending that is legally and politically untouchable through the kind of unilateral executive action DOGE was designed to exercise. Add defence at 14 percent and interest on the debt, now consuming a growing share on its own, and what remains for discretionary cutting is a sliver of the whole. DOGE was never going to find $1 trillion because $1 trillion was never there to find, not through the mechanism DOGE was given. This was arithmetic that any competent civil servant could have explained on day one. Nobody in a position to say so was in a position to be heard.

The Bond Market Starts Talking Back

The most consequential development since the Addendum is not political — it is happening in the Treasury market itself, and it is the part of this story that finally makes the abstraction concrete for people who do not read Congressional Budget Office reports. Thirty-year Treasury yields hit their highest level in nearly two decades this week. In the Addendum, published in May, I noted that yields had reached 5.12 percent, the highest since 2007. They have climbed further since, and each increment translates directly into higher mortgage rates, higher borrowing costs for businesses, and higher costs for the government to finance the very deficits driving the yields upward in the first place — Blankfein’s virtuous cycle turned vicious, now visibly compounding in real time rather than as a projection on a chart.

Two responses from Washington this week deserve attention, because they represent the first moments in this series where the administration has visibly, publicly, moved to manage the mechanics of the crisis rather than simply deny its existence. First, Bessent made a rare direct intervention in currency markets to prop up the weakening Japanese yen — a move intended, as the New York Times reported, specifically to discourage Japan from selling its substantial holdings of U.S. Treasuries to defend its own currency. This is worth sitting with. The United States is now actively working the phones, so to speak, to keep one of its largest creditors from cashing out. It is the debtor managing the creditor’s behaviour, which is not usually how healthy debtor-creditor relationships function. Second, the Treasury announced it will double the amount of its own debt it is permitted to buy back from investors, an attempt to contain borrowing costs by having the government purchase its own paper — a move that reads, charitably, as liquidity management, and less charitably as a controlled test of exactly how much of its own debt the market will let the debtor absorb before the exercise stops working.

Warsh and the Fed’s Quiet Reckoning

I noted in the March Epilogue that Janet Yellen had warned of “fiscal dominance” — the point at which a central bank is forced to finance government deficits because private markets will not. That warning now has an institutional address. Kevin Warsh, who took over as Federal Reserve chairman in May 2026, has made it a top priority to shrink the Fed’s $6.8 trillion portfolio of government bonds and mortgage-backed securities, holdings accumulated primarily as emergency measures during past crises. He has not yet detailed a specific plan and is expected to wait for a task force reviewing the balance sheet to report by year’s end.

This is the thread worth watching most closely into 2027, because it sits directly at the intersection of everything this series has documented. If the Fed shrinks its holdings gradually, by shifting the composition toward shorter-term notes, the market impact may be modest. If it attempts outright sales at any scale, it does so into a Treasury market already absorbing near-record issuance — competing, as Apollo’s chief economist noted regarding a separate but related pressure, with an estimated $2.25 trillion in corporate bond issuance in 2026, driven substantially by AI infrastructure spending. The Fed, in other words, may soon be asking the same buyers who are already being asked to absorb record government debt and record corporate debt to absorb a third wave of supply as the central bank unwinds its own crisis-era holdings. Warsh inherits a balance sheet built to calm markets during emergencies, and he is contemplating unwinding it during what the administration insists, publicly, is not one.

The Household Mirror, Updated

The Epilogue drew a parallel between the sovereign debt spiral and the quiet replication of that logic in American households — $18.8 trillion in total household debt, credit card balances at record highs, a growing share of that debt now financing groceries and utilities rather than discretionary spending. Nothing in the NYT’s August 19th reporting on the $40 trillion milestone addresses household debt directly, which is itself worth noting: the story of the sovereign debt and the story of the household debt continue to be told in separate sections of separate publications, by separate reporters, as though a government running structural deficits and a population running structural deficits are unrelated phenomena occurring in the same country at the same time by coincidence. They are not. Margaret Spellings of the Bipartisan Policy Center offered the closing line for this instalment’s Washington coverage that I could not improve on: “We’re speeding toward a cliff and refusing to turn the wheel.” She was describing the federal budget. She could equally have been describing the average credit card statement.

Slowly, Still

Marc Goldwein of the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget told the Times this week that the debt spiral is beginning, pointing specifically to the compounding weight of interest payments. This is not a new observation from Goldwein’s organization — it is the same observation Maya MacGuineas made in March, the same one Blankfein made in May, the same one Bernstein confessed to in his own Atlantic mea culpa that same month. What is new is the frequency with which serious, institutionally credentialed, non-alarmist people are making it, and the shortening interval between milestones large enough to make them say it publicly. Thirty-seven trillion in July. Thirty-eight point eight six in March. Forty in August. The gap between Epilogue and Addendum was two months. The gap between Addendum and this instalment is three. The number is not merely growing. The rate at which it forces me back to the keyboard is accelerating, which is its own kind of data.

Blankfein, in his conversation with Frum, ended on institutional optimism: America deals with its problems, eventually, imperfectly, but it deals with them. I noted in May that there is a difference between optimism and complacency, and that the distance between them is measured in the gap between recognizing a problem and acting on it. Five months later, the recognizing has become almost universal — Bessent recognizes it on Newsmax, Warsh recognizes it at the Fed, Goldwein and MacGuineas and Bernstein recognize it from three different points on the ideological compass. The acting remains exactly where it was. Nobody picked up the grain silo. Nobody turned the wheel. The number is forty trillion now. From Toronto, I will keep counting, because someone, apparently, has to.
