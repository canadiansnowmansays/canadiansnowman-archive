The Texas State Capitol in Austin — where local dynamics are quietly defying national polling narratives on white voters.

Every few weeks this year, a poll lands somewhere in America and the same sentence appears in the writeup: Democrats are struggling to make inroads with white voters. It has shown up in the national surveys. It showed up in our poll out of Maine, one of the whitest states in the country, where a Democratic Senate hopeful could not move the needle with the demographic that should, in theory, be easiest to find on a New England doorstep.

Then you get to Texas, and someone forgot to send James Talarico the memo.

The Lone Star Flag — a reminder that Texas often writes its own political rules.

The same Times/Siena poll that produced the tied Senate race buried a smaller finding that deserves its own conversation: Talarico is running meaningfully ahead of where both Joe Biden and Kamala Harris stood with white Texas voters, and he is doing it while leading outright among white college graduates. In a state Donald Trump carried by 14 points two years ago. While his own party’s national brand is, by most measures, losing ground with the very voters Talarico is gaining.

This is the part of the poll that should make pollsters everywhere a little uncomfortable, because it does not fit the story they have been telling all year.

A Different Kind of Swing

We have spent a great deal of this series on the Hispanic vote, and rightly so — a 61-29 swing in Talarico’s favor is the loudest number in the poll, and it comes with an obvious explanation attached: Trump’s approval among Hispanic voters in Texas has collapsed to 27 percent, dragged down by the economy and immigration, and that collapse is large enough to survive even after you account for irregular 2024 voters staying home.

The white-voter story doesn’t have that same tidy mechanism sitting next to it. The poll tells us the result without telling us the reason, and that absence is worth sitting with rather than papering over. It would be easy to assume this is simply Paxton's unfavorables bleeding into every demographic uniformly — recall the divorce trial, the impeachment that came from inside his own party, and a net favorability of minus twelve that is, remarkably, the worst of any candidate in either party across all six states Times/Siena surveyed. A bad enough candidate drags down support across the board, white suburban voters included.

But “uniformly bad candidate” is a national-environment explanation, and the national environment isn’t producing this result anywhere else. Maine has its own deeply damaged incumbent dynamics and still couldn’t move white voters. Something in Texas specifically — the candidate, the matchup, possibly the particular flavor of college-educated suburb that ring cities like Austin, Dallas, and Houston — is doing work that the generic anti-incumbent mood isn’t doing elsewhere.

The Suburb That Built the Trap

Modern Texas suburbs — home to the college-educated white voters where Talarico is outperforming recent Democratic nominees.

This is worth connecting back to the Farm Team Problem, because it complicates the structural story we told there. The argument then was that Republicans have only held Texas by letting Hispanic gains cancel out white suburban weakness — a trade that worked as long as both sides of the ledger stayed roughly where they were supposed to.

What the new poll suggests is that the ledger may be breaking in two places at once, not one. If Hispanic support is genuinely cratering and white college graduates are moving toward a Democrat in numbers Biden and Harris never touched, that is not a single crack in the Republican coalition. That is two separate failures occurring at the same time, for what appear to be two separate reasons — and a party doesn’t usually get to solve both with the same ad campaign.

It is also, not incidentally, exactly the coalition national Democratic strategists have spent a decade chasing and mostly failing to assemble: working-class and Hispanic voters energized by economic message, suburban college-educated voters reassured by tone and temperament. Talarico, a former middle school teacher turned seminary student who has built his entire campaign on reclaiming the language of decency from the people currently shouting about it, may simply be a more credible messenger to both audiences than anyone Texas Democrats have fielded in a generation. That is a candidate-specific explanation rather than a structural one, and candidate-specific explanations have an expiration date the moment a candidate leaves the ballot.

What Comes Next

James Talarico campaigning — a candidate who appears to be moving white college graduates in ways Biden and Harris did not.

Here is the honest caveat, and it belongs at the end of this one rather than buried in the middle: this finding is a headline number without much underneath it yet. We don’t have the crosstabs on which suburbs, which counties, or which issues are moving these voters specifically. Until that detail surfaces — in a county-level breakdown, a follow-up survey, or reporting that gets inside an actual living room in Collin County — this remains an observation rather than an explanation.

But it is an observation worth banking, because if it holds through the fall, it changes the shape of what a Talarico win would mean. A win built only on Hispanic disaffection with Trump would be a referendum on this president. A win built on both that and a defection among white college graduates would be something closer to a referendum on the modern Republican Party itself — in the one state where that party has never had to answer for it.

Everything’s bigger in Texas. Apparently that now includes the list of people the Republican Party needs to win back.
