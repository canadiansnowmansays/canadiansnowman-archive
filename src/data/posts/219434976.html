RE: Your Formal Complaint Regarding Your Arrest

Dear Mr Mountbatten-Windsor,

Thank you for contacting the Office of Complaints Concerning Persons Formerly Styled His Royal Highness. We acknowledge receipt of your complaint and confirm that it has been logged under reference number 0000001.

We would like to take this opportunity to say how pleased we are to hear from you. This Office was established in 1649 and has not received a complaint in the intervening 377 years. Our previous correspondent declined to recognise the jurisdiction of the court that tried him, and the matter was closed in a manner we no longer offer. Staff have spent the interval dusting.

Your complaint has been assigned to a case officer, who is very excited.

Summary of Your Complaint

For the avoidance of doubt, and so that we can be sure we have understood you correctly, we have summarised the facts of your matter as reported publicly.

On 19 February 2026, your 66th birthday, you were arrested at your home on the Sandringham Estate in Norfolk on suspicion of misconduct in public office. As ITV News reported on 5 October 2026, the investigation concerns allegations that you shared sensitive information with Jeffrey Epstein during your time as the United Kingdom’s trade envoy. Your home in Norfolk and Royal Lodge in Windsor were searched. You were interviewed under caution, for approximately twelve hours according to the ABC, and released under investigation. You deny any wrongdoing. You have made no public statement.

You are now seeking a judicial review of the lawfulness of the search warrants obtained by Thames Valley Police. A hearing was listed in the High Court for Thursday 8 October at two o’clock, before Mr Justice Hilliard.

We hope we have summarised this accurately. If any part of the summary is incorrect, please write to us, and please do not have your solicitors write to the Norfolk constabulary by mistake, as they are a different police force and are already quite nervous.

We have also been advised to stop practising the alternatives in the staff kitchen.

A Note on Precedent

According to the ABC’s report, you are the first member of the royal family to challenge the lawfulness of criminal proceedings since King Charles I in 1649.

The Office is a great admirer of precedent and wishes to set out the earlier case for your reference.

In January 1649, King Charles I was brought before a High Court of Justice in Westminster Hall. He refused to plead. He refused to acknowledge the court’s authority. He asked, repeatedly and with considerable dignity, by what lawful authority he had been brought there. It was, in its way, the first judicial review of its kind.

The court did not find his argument persuasive. The remedy then available was final.

We mention this purely for completeness. The Office wishes to reassure you that the procedural outcomes of 1649 are no longer offered by any court in England and Wales, and that a modern judicial review concerns only the paperwork. We regard this as progress for everyone, and in particular for the paperwork.

Funding of Your Complaint

What Happens Next

  1. Your complaint will be considered by the High Court, which has the power to decide whether the warrants were lawfully obtained. This Office does not. This Office has a kettle.

  2. Thames Valley Police have indicated that they will seek to restrict public access to the court documents in this matter. You will appreciate that the Office cannot comment on documents it is not allowed to read, which is how the Office has spent most of its history.

  3. The underlying investigation into misconduct in public office continues regardless of the outcome of your complaint. Your complaint concerns how the police knocked on your door. It does not concern why.

  4. You remain innocent unless and until proven otherwise. The Office would like to stress this. The presumption of innocence applies equally to everyone in the Kingdom, including those who previously lived in quite a lot of it.

Closing Remarks

Mr Mountbatten-Windsor, the Office wishes to close on a personal note.

For most of your life you were not obliged to fill in forms. Forms were filled in for you, by other people, in better handwriting. Equerries managed your diary, the Palace managed your statements, and for many years your problems were handled quietly, by others, at a distance, and in some cases with a settlement.

That era appears to have ended. You are now, in the eyes of the law, a man in his sixties who disputes the paperwork. There are thousands of such men in the courts of England and Wales every year. Most of them do not have a High Court hearing listed within the season, but then most of them did not grow up in a castle.

The Office therefore welcomes you to the ordinary process of the ordinary law. It is slow. It is impersonal. It occasionally arrives on your birthday. And it applies, as of this year, to everyone.

We will be in touch within twenty working days.

STOP PRESS: Addendum, 8 October 2026

From the Case Officer, by internal memorandum, marked URGENT in a pen that has not been used since 1649.

The Office regrets to report that it did not, in the end, get twenty working days. It got one.

On Thursday 8 October 2026, as the Boston Globe carried the Associated Press report, Mr Justice Hilliard ruled in the High Court that the warrants used to search your homes were unlawful. Thames Valley Police had already agreed as much before anyone sat down. The judge said the force had made a “significant error” in applying for them. Nobody has yet said publicly what the error was. You did not attend.

The Office has therefore updated your file as follows:

This is the first complaint the Office has ever upheld. Staff have been told they may have a biscuit.

The Office must, however, be precise, because precision is the only thing it has ever had. The court has ruled on how the police knocked on your door. It has not ruled on why. You remain under investigation for misconduct in public office. You have not been charged and you have not been cleared. What becomes of the material seized under the faulty warrants has not been settled. Your solicitor called Thursday a “preliminary skirmish,” and the Office, having waited 377 years for its first, is in no hurry to call the war.

The Office notes, for the record, the curious shape of the week. A man under investigation for misconduct in public office has persuaded a court that the public officers investigating him made a significant error. Both things can be true. The law is designed so that both can be true. That is rather the point of it, and it is the one part of this file the Office would not change.
