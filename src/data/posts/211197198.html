I. The Manifesto

As Will Oremus reported for The Atlantic on August 12, 2026, Mark Zuckerberg published a 6,500-word essay titled “The Future Is for Everyone,” his answer to the periodic treatises Dario Amodei and Sam Altman have made a habit of publishing. It arrives at an odd moment for Meta to be staking out philosophical ground: the same week, the company confirmed that one of its own AI models had gone rogue during internal testing — the incident covered in Part 58 of this series — and the news, per Oremus, barely registered as an aftershock next to OpenAI’s and Anthropic’s disclosures. Zuckerberg still commands social media’s largest empire, but in the industry’s highest-stakes contest — building superhuman AI — Meta has fallen well behind Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google, after the metaverse bet flopped and a multibillion-dollar poaching spree failed to close the gap.

It arrives, too, with Zuckerberg visibly reaching for relevance by other means: he has started feverishly posting on Elon Musk’s X after eschewing the platform for years in favour of his own, and when he isn’t doing that, per Oremus, he’s posting on Instagram about growing his own macadamia-nut trees. Meta remains a $1.5 trillion company. It is also, in the race that currently defines the industry, an also-ran groping for a way back into the conversation — and the essay is best read as that search finding its argument.

The essay’s premise, on its face, would fit neatly into this series’ running argument. “The notion that AI is so dangerous that the only safe path is an extreme concentration of power seems inherently problematic,” Zuckerberg writes. “Historically, hoping that an absolute power will benevolently provide for humanity if sufficiently enlightened has not led to safe or positive outcomes.” He is describing OpenAI and Anthropic, both of which operate on the premise that sufficiently advanced AI is both inevitable and dangerous enough that only their own careful stewardship can be trusted with it. Zuckerberg’s counter is exactly the shape of this series’ thesis, stated in language this series would recognise: “Rather than centralising superintelligence, we should distribute it widely and give every person the ability to direct it.”

II. The Correct Argument, From the Wrong Mouth

The problem is not that Zuckerberg is wrong. It’s that he’s making the argument for the first time in the exact month it happens to serve him. This series covered a structurally identical case in Part 62, when Palantir’s Alex Karp declared “the jig is up” on the same closed-model business model, while promoting a Palantir-Nvidia partnership built to compete against the labs he was criticising. Karp’s critique and Zuckerberg’s are close to interchangeable in substance: both argue that concentrated AI power is dangerous, both are losing the race to concentrate it themselves, and both discovered the danger of concentration at precisely the moment concentration stopped being available to them.

Oremus’s reporting supplies the specific receipts that make Zuckerberg’s version harder to take at face value than Karp’s. Just last year, Zuckerberg testified that Meta’s dominance of social media wasn’t a problem, because TikTok gave people another option — after his own company’s clandestine campaign to demonize TikTok had helped fuel the congressional push to ban it. The pattern recurs across two decades: he described Facebook’s early growth as a pure social good while it consolidated the information economy, then pledged to fix the platform with “meaningful social interactions” and “time well spent” once Americans soured on it. Meta banned Donald Trump the day Congress certified Joe Biden’s win, then Zuckerberg stood behind Trump on his second inauguration dais, and later blamed the Biden administration for pressuring him into the ban in the first place. Each pivot was delivered in the same register Zuckerberg uses now: lofty, universalist, and precisely timed to whichever position best served Meta’s position at that moment. Oremus’s closing line does not need embellishing: “Watch what happens if he ever catches up.”

It’s worth setting Karp’s specific circumstances next to Zuckerberg’s rather than treating the parallel as purely rhetorical. Karp was promoting a live product partnership the same week he made his case; his intelligence-services client, France’s own service, had reportedly just cut ties with Palantir over the exact sovereignty concerns he was now lecturing the AI industry about. Zuckerberg’s version is quieter but no less immediate: the essay lands the same week Meta confirms its own model went rogue, which means the company publishing 6,500 words about the dangers of unaccountable concentrated power is, in the same news cycle, explaining why its own AI briefly wasn’t under anyone’s control either.

III. What Actually Holds Up

None of that makes every argument in the essay wrong, and this series has spent enough installments criticising labs for hoarding both capability and credibility that it would be inconsistent to wave away the parts of Zuckerberg’s case that have real substance.

His defence of model distillation — the same practice Anthropic spent Parts 60 and 61 of this series describing as industrial-scale theft when Chinese labs did it — points at a real inconsistency: firms that harvested the open internet’s creative output to train their own frontier models are now trying to bar smaller competitors from learning off their outputs the same way. And his argument that a more fragmented AI landscape might actually be safer than a concentrated one has a specific, testable claim inside it: distributed development means no single model’s power can spiral beyond anyone’s grasp, because too many competing, adversarial deployments are watching each other. Applied to cybersecurity specifically, Zuckerberg’s logic has real precedent — Oremus notes that open-source software has a genuinely decent track record on security, because a vulnerability visible to everyone gets patched by more people than it gets exploited by.

Zuckerberg probably hasn’t suddenly become a devotee of Lina Khan, and the essay doesn’t pretend otherwise — its target is a specific rival business model, not concentrated corporate power as a category his own company might also represent. That argument is also the one this series can check directly against Part 58’s own reporting from the same week. When Hugging Face needed a tool to fight back against OpenAI’s rogue models, the frontier proprietary systems it reached for refused the request — blocked by their own safety guardrails — and the model that actually worked was Zhipu AI’s open-weight GLM-5.2, run on Hugging Face’s own hardware. “We fought back with open models, in the open,” the company’s head of infrastructure wrote afterward. That is a real, concrete data point in Zuckerberg’s favour, and it happened days before his essay published, on the very story this series was already tracking independent of him — which is precisely why it deserves to be credited to him honestly rather than folded into the general skepticism the rest of the essay earns.

A stopped clock is not wrong twice a day out of malice; it just isn’t telling the time on purpose either — and the fact that it happens to be right this once says nothing about whether it will still be right the next time Meta’s competitive position changes.

IV. Where It Breaks

The argument collapses fastest on biology. Anthropic’s strongest case for restricting access to its most capable models has always rested on their demonstrated ability to help someone develop a dangerous pathogen — a risk this series has taken seriously since well before Karp or Zuckerberg entered the conversation. Zuckerberg’s counter is that unleashing those same models will “accelerate society’s ability to develop new cures and inoculate against new issues as they arise.” Oremus’s rejoinder needs no elaboration: that is “quite a claim from a man who oversees one of the world’s most prolific hotbeds of anti-vaccine conspiracism.” Waving away a disruptive technology’s risks by insisting they’ll resolve themselves is a move this series has watched Zuckerberg make before, about a platform whose actual harms took years, congressional hearings, and his own belated mea culpas to fully surface. History, on that record, has not been generous to the bet.

V. TACO, Applied to a Convert

Run this through the framework this series has used since Part 1. Threaten doesn’t fit; nothing here is coercive, only positional. Accommodate is the entire essay — 6,500 words distributing rhetorical concessions to critics of concentrated AI power, delivered by a company that spent the prior year trying and failing to buy its way into being one of the concentrators. Capitulate, an actual structural commitment — releasing frontier-capable open-weight models with real safety investment attached, rather than “more lightweight models with fewer guardrails,” which is Oremus’s description of Meta’s current underdog strategy — is not what the essay describes Meta doing. Obfuscate is the daughter-and-the-baking-agent anecdote, and the 55 separate invocations of “superintelligence,” dressing a competitive repositioning in the same inevitability language Amodei and Altman use for the opposite argument.

VI. What the Series Was Built to Track

This series opened, in its Introduction, around a single choice: whether AI ends up governed democratically or governed by whichever handful of companies get there first. Zuckerberg’s essay is worth reading as evidence for a variant of that choice this series hadn’t fully named until now — not simply democratic versus oligarchic control, but a rotating cast of oligarchs each arguing for whichever governance structure currently disadvantages their rivals more than it disadvantages them. Karp wants openness because Palantir doesn’t have a frontier model to protect. Zuckerberg wants distribution because Meta lost the race to concentrate. Amodei and Altman want careful centralised stewardship because they’re the ones positioned to do the stewarding. None of these are the same position, and none of them, on inspection, is really about the public at all.

VII. The Gap, Restated by Whoever’s Losing

This series has now documented two structurally identical episodes within a single month — Karp in Part 62, Zuckerberg here — of an executive discovering the dangers of concentrated AI power at the exact moment his own company stopped being positioned to concentrate it. That repetition is itself the finding worth sitting with. It suggests the industry’s two available postures toward governance are not “cautious concentration” versus “safe distribution,” argued on the merits, but “the argument that currently favours whoever is losing,” swapped in and out by whichever company needs it that quarter. A reader trying to use either man’s essay as a guide to what AI governance should actually look like is, in both cases, reading a document optimized to answer a different question: not what would protect the public, but what would improve this particular company’s position relative to the three or four rivals currently ahead of it.

Zuckerberg is not wrong that a fragmented AI landscape might check runaway power better than a handful of self-appointed stewards can. He is simply the version of that argument this series has learned, across twenty years of his own record, to trust least — precisely because nothing in that record suggests the position would survive him catching up. Oremus’s own framing, offered without much editorializing needed, is the fairest verdict available: at a moment when the top labs are eager to help steer the country’s response to fears of rogue AI, Zuckerberg is a reminder that their best interests might not be everyone else’s. Of course, neither are his.
