The Hermit Kingdom Ascendant traces North Korea from its ancient origins to its emergence as a nuclear power. Parts I and II established the peninsula’s geopolitical logic and the Joseon dynasty’s fateful experiment with isolation. Part III arrives at the wound that made everything after it possible — thirty-five years of Japanese colonial rule that are still raw, still present, and still the furnace in which the Kim dynasty’s ideology was forged.

The Japan–Korea Annexation Treaty of 1910. The document that extinguished Korean sovereignty and began thirty-five years of colonial rule.

On August 22, 1910, the last emperor of Korea signed the document that extinguished his country. The Japan-Korea Annexation Treaty was brief, clinical, and total. In its pages, the Korean Empire ceded all sovereign rights to Japan “completely and permanently.” Five centuries of the Joseon Dynasty, fourteen centuries of unified Korean statehood, four thousand years of continuous civilization on the peninsula — all of it reduced to a few paragraphs of diplomatic prose and a signature obtained, as historians have since established, under conditions of coercion that made the treaty’s legal validity questionable at best.

Japan did not particularly care about the legal debate. It had what it wanted: a peninsula.

What followed over the next thirty-five years was not merely political subjugation but a systematic attempt to erase a people’s sense of who they were. Japan aimed, in its own formal policy language, at Nai-Sen ittai — “Japan and Korea as one body.” The phrase had the clean euphemism of all imperial projects. What it meant in practice was the dismantling, piece by piece, of every marker of Korean identity that could be found and destroyed.

It left a wound. The wound has not closed. Everything that North Korea is — its ideology, its governing mythology, its nuclear programme, its entire reason for being — is built, in part, around that wound and the determination that it will never be inflicted again.

The Architecture of Erasure

Japanese colonial administration or public ceremony in Korea. Policies included banning Korean language in schools, forcing Japanese names, and destroying historical records.

Japan set up a government in Korea with the governor-generalship filled by generals or admirals appointed by the Japanese emperor. The Koreans were immediately deprived of freedom of assembly, association, press, and speech. Many private schools were closed. But the deepest assault was not political. It was cultural.

Schools and universities forbade speaking Korean and emphasized manual labour and loyalty to the Emperor. Public places adopted Japanese. Teaching history from non-approved texts became a crime. Authorities burned over 200,000 Korean historical documents — an act of calculated amnesia, eliminating the written record of a civilization so that its children would grow up knowing only what Japan chose to tell them about their past. The Hangul alphabet that Sejong the Great had spent his reign creating and defending — the alphabet that was itself an act of cultural independence — was suppressed in favour of Japanese.

Most devastating of all, Koreans were forced to abandon their own names and take Japanese ones. This was not a suggestion. The sōshi-kaimei policy, implemented in full force by 1940, required Korean families to register Japanese-style surnames and given names. Names are not incidental to identity. They carry lineage, ancestry, the accumulated meaning of generations. To strip a people of their names is to sever them from their own history at the most intimate possible level. The colonial authorities understood this perfectly. That was precisely the point.

It became a crime to teach history from non-approved texts. It became a crime to publish in Korean. It became, in the most comprehensive sense, a crime to be Korean in any way that Japanese imperial policy had not sanctioned.

The Land and the Labour

Colonial-era factory or industrial scene in Korea. Japan transformed the northern peninsula into a resource and munitions base — infrastructure later inherited by the DPRK.

Cultural erasure was the ideological dimension of colonialism. The economic dimension was equally brutal, and in some ways more immediately devastating to the lives of ordinary people.

Japan implemented a sweeping land survey between 1910 and 1918, ostensibly to modernize land ownership. The practical effect was the transfer of enormous amounts of Korean agricultural land to Japanese ownership. Korean farmers who had cultivated their fields for generations found themselves reclassified as tenant farmers on land that now legally belonged to Japanese corporations or the colonial state. The great zaibatsu conglomerates — Japan’s industrial monopolies — moved into Korea and took control of key economic sectors. Korean rice production was reorganized to feed Japan, creating food shortages on the very peninsula that produced the grain.

As Japan’s imperial ambitions expanded into Manchuria and then into broader war in the Pacific, Korea became an industrial supply depot and a reservoir of forced labour. Korean workers were conscripted to work in mines and factories in Japan and Manchuria under conditions that amounted to slavery. In the final years of the colonial period, over 700,000 Koreans were forcibly mobilized for labour. Hundreds of thousands more were displaced, relocated, scattered across an empire that had no interest in their welfare.

There is one further dimension of the economic legacy that bears particular attention for this series. In the 1930s, Japan developed the northern part of the peninsula — what would become North Korea — as its industrial heartland. Mines, hydroelectric dams, steel mills, and munitions factories were built across the northern provinces, transforming what had been a predominantly agricultural region into an industrial base. The Korean working class expanded rapidly during these years, and many Koreans moved to Manchuria to work in Japanese-owned industries. This industrialization was extractive and exploitative in every sense — the profits flowed to Tokyo, the workers had no rights, and the infrastructure was designed to serve Japanese imperial expansion, not Korean welfare. But it left behind factories, mines, and power-generating capacity. When the peninsula was divided in 1945 and Kim Il-sung was installed in the North, he inherited this industrial infrastructure. In the immediate postwar years, North Korea was actually the more developed half of the divided peninsula — a fact that makes the South’s subsequent transformation and the North’s subsequent collapse all the more historically vertiginous.

And then there were the comfort women.

The euphemism itself is an obscenity. Korean women and girls — many of them teenagers, some as young as fourteen — were taken from their families under false pretences, deceived with promises of work, and forced into sexual slavery serving the Japanese military. Estimates of the total number of women subjected to this system range from tens of thousands to over two hundred thousand. Most were Korean. They were held at military “comfort stations” across the Japanese empire, forced to service dozens of soldiers a day, deprived of medical care, subjected to violence, and in many cases killed before they could bear witness to what had been done to them. Those who survived returned to societies that often could not bring themselves to acknowledge what had happened. Many suffered in silence for decades before the first survivor, Kim Hak-Sun, gave public testimony in 1991 — eighty-one years after the annexation, forty-six years after liberation.

Korean women subjected to the Japanese military’s “comfort women” system. Tens to hundreds of thousands were forced into sexual slavery — one of the deepest and most enduring wounds of the colonial period.

The comfort women issue remains an open wound in regional politics to this day, unresolved between Japan and both Koreas, a site of historical controversy that implicates questions of legal accountability, national honour, and the meaning of an apology. Japan has issued multiple statements of remorse. Korea — both Koreas — has consistently found them insufficient. The disagreement is not merely diplomatic. It is about whether the crime has ever been fully named, and whether naming it matters if the naming is never accompanied by consequences.

The March and Its Meaning

On March 1, 1919, thirty-three Korean nationalist leaders gathered in a restaurant in Seoul and read aloud a Declaration of Independence. They had drawn on the language of Woodrow Wilson’s Fourteen Points, with their promise of national self-determination — a promise that, as colonized peoples across the world were discovering simultaneously, was intended for European nations and not for the subjects of empires that happened to be American allies.

Crowds during the March 1, 1919 independence movement. Approximately two million Koreans took to the streets in peaceful protests — one of the largest non-violent demonstrations in history — only to face brutal suppression.

What happened next was extraordinary. Without organized military capacity, without foreign support, without any realistic prospect of success, approximately two million Koreans — one in ten of the entire population — took to the streets across more than 1,500 demonstrations in the weeks and months that followed. They waved the Korean flag. They chanted Daehan dongnip manse — “Long live Korean independence.” They faced Japanese police and soldiers with their bodies and their voices, and in most cases nothing else.

Japan’s response was systematic violence. By the time the movement was suppressed, approximately 7,500 Koreans had been killed, 16,000 wounded, and nearly 47,000 arrested. In the village of Jeam-ri, Japanese soldiers locked Korean independence demonstrators inside a church and burned it down. Across the country, private homes, schools, and churches were destroyed. A sixteen-year-old student named Yu Gwan-sun, who had helped organize demonstrations in her home province after her school was closed, was arrested, tortured, and died in prison in 1920 from injuries sustained under interrogation. She became one of the most enduring symbols of Korean resistance — celebrated in the South, appropriated in diluted form in the North, known by every Korean child on both sides of the 38th parallel.

The March First Movement did not achieve independence. It was not designed to achieve independence in any military sense. What it achieved was something harder to quantify but more durable: it demonstrated that colonial erasure had failed. After nine years of systematic suppression of Korean culture, language, and identity, two million people still knew they were Korean. They still wanted to be Korean. The identity that Japan had tried to burn out of them with 200,000 destroyed documents and compulsory Japanese names was still there, still fierce, still capable of filling the streets.

The movement also fractured the Korean independence movement along lines that would prove fateful. In its aftermath, a schism opened between moderates willing to work within the limited cultural freedoms Japan subsequently offered, and socialist radicals who rejected any accommodation entirely and went to Manchuria and Siberia to fight. It was from that second stream — the uncompromising, anti-Japanese, Marxist-inflected guerrilla tradition — that Kim Il-sung would emerge.

The Boy from Pyongyang

Young Kim Il-sung (or guerrilla fighters in Manchuria). His formative years in the anti-Japanese resistance became central to the founding mythology of North Korea.

Kim Il-sung was born Kim Song-ju on April 15, 1912, two years after the annexation, in a village near Pyongyang. His father, Kim Hyong-jik, was a schoolteacher associated with anti-Japanese activism. His mother, Kang Pan-sok, was the daughter of a Protestant minister. When Kim was a child his father was imprisoned by the Japanese — the young Kim reportedly visited him and saw the wounds on his body, an encounter his later memoirs would describe as the moment he resolved, at age five, to dedicate his life to destroying Japanese imperialism.

The family fled to Manchuria in the early 1920s as colonial rule grew harsher. Kim spent his formative years there, educated in Chinese schools, exposed to Marxist literature, radicalized by the grinding reality of Korean dispossession. At seventeen he was arrested for belonging to a communist youth organization. By his mid-twenties he had become a guerrilla commander fighting Japanese forces in Manchuria, wanted badly enough that by 1939 the Japanese had placed a 200,000-yen bounty on his head — a sum that tells you more about his operational effectiveness than any biography could.

In the hagiography that North Korea would eventually construct around him, Kim Il-sung became not merely a guerrilla leader but the liberator of Korea — the man who defeated Japan through the force of his will and the righteousness of his cause. The historical record is more complicated. Kim’s guerrilla operations, while genuine and dangerous, were one strand of resistance among many, and Japan was ultimately defeated not by Korean guerrillas but by American atomic bombs and Soviet military action. Kim returned to Korea in September 1945 not as a conquering hero but in the uniform of a Soviet army captain, arriving by ship with sixty-six other Korean officers who had spent the war years training in the USSR.

But the mythology was more powerful than the biography, and Kim understood this with the instinct of genius. He had something real to build on: a life genuinely shaped by colonial violence, a family genuinely involved in resistance, a people genuinely hungry for a narrative of defiance that ended in liberation rather than humiliation. He took that material and made it the foundation of a state.

The Furnace

To understand North Korea — not merely as a political curiosity or a security problem but as a human project with its own internal logic — you have to sit with what colonial rule actually felt like from inside it. Not the diplomatic abstractions, not the treaty language, not the policy acronyms of forced assimilation. The actual experience of watching your language become illegal. Of being ordered to abandon the name your parents gave you. Of having your history burned in front of you. Of watching 200,000 documents — the accumulated memory of your civilization — reduced to ash by bureaucrats following orders.

That experience produced a specific form of fury. It produced a nationalism so intense that it could sustain two million unarmed people in the streets against armed soldiers. It produced a generation of exiles in Manchuria and Siberia who were willing to spend their lives in guerrilla warfare against an empire for the theoretical possibility of a Korea they might never live to see. And it produced, in the political culture that Kim Il-sung constructed from those materials, an ideology centred on one proposition above all others: never again.

Never again would Korea be absorbed. Never again would a foreign power dictate Korean names, Korean language, Korean history. Never again would the peninsula be a corridor for someone else’s ambitions or a supply depot for someone else’s wars. The juche ideology of self-reliance, the military-first songun politics, the nuclear weapons programme — all of it flows, in a direct and traceable line, from the furnace of those thirty-five years.

This is not a defence of Kim Jong-un or his grandfather. The regime that weaponized the colonial wound is itself a system of extraordinary cruelty — a state that has inflicted on its own people something in many ways comparable to what Japan inflicted on Korea: the destruction of free expression, the forced adoption of prescribed identities, the punishment of dissent with imprisonment and death, the systematic erasure of any history that does not serve power. The irony is as savage as any in modern political history. The state built to prevent colonial subjugation became, for its own people, a form of it.

The story of Kang Chol-hwan makes the circularity of this process concrete. His grandfather was a Korean-Japanese — one of the tens of thousands of ethnic Koreans who had lived in Japan during the colonial period and who, after liberation, chose to repatriate to North Korea, the homeland they had been separated from. Many brought their savings, their skills, and a genuine belief in Kim Il-sung’s socialist project. Kang’s grandfather donated his family fortune to the state on arrival — an act of revolutionary commitment that the family assumed would guarantee their security and status in the new Korea.

It did not. In 1977, when Kang Chol-hwan was nine years old, his grandfather was arrested on suspicion of counter-revolutionary tendencies — the precise charge was never clearly specified, as it rarely was — and the entire family was transported without trial to the Yodok concentration camp, one of North Korea’s political prison colonies. Kang spent ten years there, learning to trap rats and catch frogs to supplement the near-starvation rations. He eventually escaped to South Korea and wrote a memoir, The Aquariums of Pyongyang, that became one of the first systematic accounts of the camp system to reach Western readers.

The family’s trajectory — colonial subject, Japanese resident, true believer, donor, prisoner — is the colonial wound and its exploitation compressed into a single lifetime. Japan made them second-class citizens. The liberation promised them dignity. The regime took their money, then took their freedom. The ideology that claimed to have been built against precisely this kind of abuse reproduced it on its own people with a thoroughness that the Japanese colonial authorities would have recognized immediately.

But the wound was real before it was exploited. Japan inflicted it. The world that emerged from it — divided, armed, ideologically rigid, and fiercely committed to sovereignty at any cost — is the world Kim Jong-un inherited. To understand why he behaves as he does, you have to understand what was done to the peninsula before any of the Kims were born, and how deeply the memory of it runs in the people they have governed.

The wound is still there. It still bleeds. And in Pyongyang, it has never been allowed to heal — because a wound, for a regime built on grievance, is not a liability. It is the source of power.
