There is a particular kind of organizational failure that only reveals itself when the people inside it start describing it to reporters. Not the failure itself, which has usually been visible for months to anyone paying attention, but the texture of it: who shouted at whom, which furniture got moved, who was at lunch when the phone rang.

On October 9, 2026, Alan Feuer and Glenn Thrush of the New York Times supplied that texture for the Justice Department’s so-called grand conspiracy case, the sprawling Fort Pierce inquiry that this series last visited in Part XXIX, when the subpoena to Daniel Richman marked the third attempt to build a case around James Comey. The Times reporters spoke to more than 20 current and former officials. What emerges from their account is not a prosecution in trouble. It is a prosecution that was never quite a prosecution at all, but a political promise wearing a prosecutor’s borrowed suit.

The scene they open with deserves to be set down plainly, because no satirist would dare invent it.

Lunch in Fort Pierce

In September 2026, Joseph diGenova, the 81-year-old lawyer whom President Trump had personally summoned out of retirement in April to run the case, was leaving a seafood restaurant near the beach in Fort Pierce, Florida, and walking to his Tesla when his phone rang. On the line was Attorney General Todd Blanche, and according to people briefed on the exchange, he was furious.

The cause was diGenova’s treatment of two prosecutors Blanche had sent down from Washington in early September to move the case along. During a heated session, the visitors had questioned diGenova’s staff about their grand jury work. When diGenova learned that the upshot was that Washington would now be running things, he reportedly declared he would have U.S. marshals physically remove the two Washington prosecutors from the grand jury room if they turned up to interfere.

One pauses over that. A special prosecutor appointed to prove that the federal government conspired against Donald Trump was threatening to use federal marshals against federal prosecutors sent by Donald Trump’s own attorney general. If the grand conspiracy theory holds that the Justice Department is a nest of saboteurs working against the president, the people running the investigation have done a great deal to supply evidence for it, if only against themselves.

Blanche ordered diGenova to report to his office on September 11. Expecting to be fired, diGenova quit first. A person close to Blanche later told the Times that the attorney general had wanted only to air his grievances in person and never intended to fire him. We will have to take that on trust, which is a commodity in short supply in this story.

Shortly afterward, diGenova’s top deputy, Kurt Olsen, also departed. Two people familiar with the matter told the Times that Olsen had failed a polygraph examination concerning his contacts with foreigners, which put his security clearance at risk. Olsen told the paper he had complied with every requirement on foreign contacts and had not resigned over his clearance, and declined to elaborate. Olsen, it should be noted, is a longtime election denier who, per the Times, had no experience working criminal cases before joining a criminal investigation of former intelligence chiefs.

So: an investigation premised on the idea that Trump’s enemies colluded with foreign powers has lost its second-in-command amid questions about his own foreign contacts. A Leacock character could not have walked into a rake with greater precision.

The theory, and the problem with it

The grand conspiracy case rests on a single proposition that has been an article of faith in Trump’s political movement since roughly 2017: that every investigation of him, from the Russia inquiry of 2016 and 2017 through Jack Smith’s two special-counsel prosecutions over the classified documents and the 2020 election, was one continuous plot to destroy him.

As a political narrative, it is tidy. As a legal theory, it runs into problems that no amount of loyalty can wish away, and the Times account lists them with almost clinical calm.

The clock. The Russia investigation concluded in 2019. Any crime arising from it falls outside the five-year federal statute of limitations. The only way around that is to argue that the Russia inquiry was merely the opening chapter of an ongoing conspiracy that continued into later years, which is precisely why the theory needs to be “grand.” The size of the conspiracy is not a finding. It is a workaround.

The venue. None of the Russia-era conduct has any obvious connection to South Florida. The case was steered there, by the Times account, partly at the urging of Mike Davis, the conservative lawyer and social-media combatant who has campaigned loudly for prosecutions reaching as far as Barack Obama and Hillary Clinton. The appeal was transparent: a grand jury in the Fort Pierce courthouse, where the only sitting judge is Aileen Cannon, the Trump appointee who handed the president several favourable rulings in his classified documents case. The bridge from Washington to Florida was supposed to be the August 2022 FBI search of Mar-a-Lago.

The bridge collapsed. Here is where the story turns almost comic. Jack Eckenrode, an FBI veteran on a special squad assembled by FBI Director Kash Patel and someone who had himself pursued Comey and Brennan during the Russia inquiry, reminded diGenova that Judge Cannon had already ruled the Mar-a-Lago search lawful. A lawful, court-approved search is a poor foundation for a criminal conspiracy linking Washington to Florida. DiGenova thought the point legally irrelevant. He called Patel, and Patel removed Eckenrode from the case.

That sequence is worth holding up to the light. The objection did not come from a career holdout or a Comey loyalist. It came from a member of Patel’s handpicked team, a man with his own history of investigating these same targets. The investigation’s answer to a sound legal objection from a friendly source was to take the source off the case.

The prosecutor who said no

The Times account also fills in a chapter this series had only glimpsed.

The case began, by the Times reckoning, in July 2025, when CIA Director John Ratcliffe sent a criminal referral to the FBI accusing former CIA Director John Brennan of lying to Congress about the early-2017 intelligence assessment that Russia had interfered in the 2016 election. The referral went first to David Metcalf, the Trump-appointed U.S. attorney in Philadelphia. It stalled there, and career prosecutors threatened to revolt if the office pressed ahead. In the autumn of 2025, Blanche, then deputy attorney general, moved it to Jason Reding Quiñones, the U.S. attorney in Miami.

By early 2026 it sat with Maria Medetis Long, a respected national security prosecutor in the Miami office who, by the account of people familiar with her work, came to it with an open mind. She concluded that the evidence did not support indicting Brennan. In April 2026 she put that conclusion in writing. She was promptly removed from the case.

When diGenova arrived that same April, one of his first moves was to ask Medetis Long to come back. She declined.

Readers of Part XXVI will recall the test this series set out after John Bolton’s guilty plea for distinguishing a real prosecution from a manufactured one. A real case predates the grievance, is built by career prosecutors across administrations, rests on the defendant’s own words, and tends to end in a plea. A manufactured case postdates the grievance, relies on handpicked prosecutors and a chosen jurisdiction, and comes apart on contact with a grand jury or a judge.

The Brennan matter now fails that test at every joint. It postdates the grievance by eight years. It was moved from one jurisdiction because career prosecutors balked, and to another because the judge was congenial. And the one experienced prosecutor who examined it on its merits, and wrote down what she found, was removed for her trouble. That is not a case collapsing on contact with a judge. It is a case collapsing on contact with a competent lawyer, before a judge ever had the chance.

The roster of the willing

If the investigation could not find evidence, it struggled almost as badly to find staff. Few career agents or attorneys wanted any part of it. Most of the prosecutors now on the team, the Times reports, are military lawyers or people dispatched from other offices. Reding Quiñones, an Army reservist, had to bring in military lawyers to fill the ranks.

The Times roster of those who did sign on reads like a casting call for a political farce. An FBI agent who had once investigated the theory that Italian satellites hacked the 2020 election. A little-known prosecutor from Fargo, North Dakota. A Stanford law professor best known for memos justifying torture during the war on terror. A young department official who had clerked for Judge Cannon. Olsen, the election denier without criminal experience. And diGenova’s own wife, Victoria Toensing.

DiGenova, for his part, complained to Washington that he never had enough lawyers, and that those he had were so junior he had to set aside time to teach them basic tasks such as presenting to a grand jury. It did not help, according to three current and former officials, that he and Toensing left in the middle of all this for a long-planned holiday in France.

He arrived on day one, the Times reports, with a 190-page memo listing more than 50 “overt acts” he wanted to investigate, beginning with a December 2016 White House meeting at which Obama and his aides authorized the intelligence assessment on Russian interference. To diGenova, that meeting was the original sin. The participants deny anything illegal occurred, and no court has suggested otherwise. A 190-page memo is a remarkable document to bring to a first day of work. It is also, in its way, a confession: the conclusion was written before the investigation began, and the investigation’s job was to catch up to it.

The collateral damage

The cost of all this is not borne only by the targets.

The Southern District of Florida, once among the most important federal prosecutors’ offices in the country, known for major drug and white-collar cases, had more than 200 lawyers. The Times reports it is now slightly over half that size after firings and resignations. The head of its criminal division has been sidelined. Its health care fraud unit, in a state where Medicare fraud is a growth industry, has been gutted. Prosecutors describe the office as leaderless and fear it has pivoted from crime to politics. Even its furniture has been reassigned to equip the inquiry’s two work spaces, one on the fifth floor of the Miami headquarters and one in Fort Pierce.

In late summer 2026, Reding Quiñones proposed spending $1 million of his office’s budget to build a secure facility in Fort Pierce for reviewing classified material. Blanche’s staff refused, noting that the FBI already ran one nearby. Officials in Washington, by the Times account, have increasingly lost confidence in Reding Quiñones, regarding him as an inexperienced manager who spent too much time in Washington. Blanche’s deputy Aakash Singh recently removed him from the attorney general’s advisory committee so he could concentrate on Miami.

Every hour spent assembling a conspiracy that the statute of limitations has already foreclosed is an hour not spent on the fraud, trafficking and violent crime that the Southern District exists to prosecute. That trade-off is the quiet scandal underneath the loud one. The enemies list does not just harm the enemies. It diverts the machinery that protects everyone else.

What Blanche actually wants

The most revealing passage in the Times account concerns not what the two camps did, but what each believed the other was really after.

DiGenova told an associate he thought Blanche cared less about proving the conspiracy than about producing quick, box-ticking indictments of lesser figures to keep the president content, and that Blanche was too anxious about his own job to tell Trump the truth: a serious investigation needs time. Blanche’s team, for its part, regarded diGenova as scattered, combative and slow.

Both may be right. And Blanche himself, earlier this year, came close to saying the quiet part on Sean Hannity’s programme while discussing the investigation’s difficulties. “I’m not suggesting that we don’t have that evidence,” he told Hannity. Attorneys general who have evidence generally do not phrase it as a double negative.

Run this through the TACO framework that anchors much of this series’ political analysis and the pattern is familiar. The Threaten phase was loud and long: Davis’s broadsides, the subpoenas hailed as bombshells by friendly outlets, the president’s fake video of Obama being arrested in the Oval Office. The Accommodate phase came when the theory met the statute of limitations and the Mar-a-Lago ruling, and the targets shrank from Obama and Clinton to a roster of former officials. We now appear to be entering Capitulate dressed as progress: the dozen remaining prosecutors, per the Times, are fast-tracking narrower cases against some chief targets that can be brought quickly, with leadership pulled back to Washington under Singh. And the Obfuscate phase is already running, in a department statement expressing “full confidence” in the prosecutors and a promise to act should evidence of misconduct present itself. Should. Eighteen months in.

Meanwhile, at the Fed

On the same Friday the Times published its account of the Fort Pierce collapse, Colby Smith and Tony Romm reported in the same paper that the White House had formed a “committee of inquiry” to examine Lisa Cook, the Federal Reserve governor Trump has been trying to fire since August 2025. The committee will consider whether she made false statements on mortgage documents, and it has scheduled a hearing for November 5, 2026, two days after the midterm elections.

Readers will remember from Part XXVII that the Supreme Court ruled 5–4 on June 29, 2026, that Cook had not been given adequate due process, while leaving open what “cause” for removal actually means. The White House has now decided to manufacture the due process itself. Its committee consists of Kevin Hassett, the president’s top economic adviser; Keith Sonderling, acting director of the Office of Government Ethics; and Andrea Lucas, chair of the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. All three serve at the president’s pleasure. Justice Department personnel may do some of the questioning. Bill Pulte, the housing director who first made the accusation and referred it to the Justice Department, is not on the panel, which in this administration counts as restraint.

The allegation itself has not grown since this series last looked at it. In 2021, Cook bought a condominium in Atlanta that was described in at least one document as a primary residence, while her Ann Arbor home was treated the same way. Her lawyers call it an inadvertent oversight, note that other records correctly list the condo as a vacation home, and point out that Trump and several of his advisers have made similar mortgage errors. She bought the condo the year before she took her seat on the Fed board, so a removal on these grounds would rest on conduct predating the office she is said to be unfit for. More than a year after the referral, the Justice Department has charged her with nothing.

Here the two stories meet. Cook’s lawyers, Abbe Lowell’s team, said on October 9 that they had grave doubts the hearing would be lawful, and that they were trying to judge whether it would be genuine rather than a box-checking exercise. That is almost exactly the phrase diGenova used, by the Times account, about what Blanche wanted from the grand conspiracy case: quick, box-checking indictments to keep the president content. One Trump loyalist about his own side, one target’s counsel about the process built for her, in the same week. When the people inside the machine and the people being fed into it reach for the same words, the words are probably accurate.

The shape is the same in both places. The criminal track runs into trouble with evidence and with the institutions meant to test it. The fight then moves to a forum the president controls: a Washington-run grand jury team in Florida, a committee of aides at the White House. The Fed chapter has a second exhibit. Jerome Powell, whose term as chair ended in May and who stayed on as a governor through January 2028, saw the Justice Department’s criminal probe of the Fed renovation dropped after legal setbacks and Republican resistance. In late September 2026 the Fed’s inspector general cleared him of wrongdoing, while faulting the roughly $2.5 billion project’s management. Trump’s response was that Powell should now be forced to resign.

Cook’s lawyers have said she welcomes the chance to clear her name, which denies the White House the picture of a target who will not show up. If the committee is a sham, they intend it to be a documented one, and the record will go back to the court that sent the case here in the first place.

The punishment is the process

None of this means the targets are safe, and it would be a mistake to read the Times account as good news for them.

A narrower, faster indictment is still an indictment. A case that cannot prove a grand conspiracy can still charge someone with a process crime, a false statement, a technical violation, and a Fort Pierce grand jury before a friendly bench is not a neutral venue. Brennan’s scheduled testimony, originally set for October 15, 2026, has been pushed back to at least early December, which means the cloud over him will hang through the November midterms.

And as the Times observes, even if no charges are ever filed, Trump has already achieved part of his purpose. Comey and Brennan in particular have been forced to bear fear and heavy legal costs, which in the president’s own view gives them a taste of what he endured. That is the logic of the enemies list in its purest form. The goal is not conviction. The goal is the experience of being pursued.

This series has argued from the start that the danger of politicized prosecution lies less in the verdicts than in the damage inflicted on the way there. The grand conspiracy case is that argument made flesh: a prosecution that may never reach a courtroom, that has hollowed out one of the country’s great prosecutorial offices, driven out the one prosecutor willing to call the evidence by its name, and burned through its own leadership in a single season, while its targets pay lawyers by the hour.

Somewhere in Fort Pierce, a secure room that was never built sits beside the one the FBI already had. That is roughly where the grand conspiracy stands: a great deal of construction proposed, and nothing yet to put inside it.
