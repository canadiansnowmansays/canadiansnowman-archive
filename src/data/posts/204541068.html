Postmark: Brampton, Ontario. A clean room. One billion dollars. One arm, learning to stand. No station to hold on to.

On March 24 of this year, NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman announced that the United States was pausing the Lunar Gateway — the small space station in lunar orbit that was to be humanity’s first permanent outpost beyond low Earth orbit — “in its current form,” which is the aerospace industry’s way of pronouncing last rites while leaving the door open for a miracle. Roughly twenty billion dollars would pivot instead toward a permanent base on the lunar surface, to be built between 2029 and 2036. A decade of planning, an international coalition spanning Europe, Japan, the United Arab Emirates and Canada, and hardware already welded — the HALO habitation module had been fabricated in Turin and shipped to Arizona for final outfitting — all of it re-filed overnight under deferred.

Eight days later, on the first of April, a rocket left Launch Pad 39B in Florida carrying four astronauts around the Moon. One of them was Jeremy Hansen of London, Ontario — the first Canadian, and the first human being of any non-American nationality, ever to fly a lunar mission.

Hold those two dates in your mind together, because no committee would have dared schedule them so close. Canada bought Hansen’s seat with a robot arm. The arm was built for the station that had just been shelved. Our astronaut flew to the Moon, in other words, on a ticket paid for with a billion-dollar limb that no longer has a shoulder to attach to.

This postcard is the sequel to Part II of this series, The Arm That Canada Built — the story of how a robotic arm assembled in a Toronto-area clean room became the most durable machine in space, flew ninety shuttle missions, built the International Space Station piece by piece, and established robotics as the one field where a country of forty million people is not merely competitive with the superpowers but, on a good day, indispensable to them. That postcard ended with Canadarm3, the third generation, bound for lunar orbit.

This one is about what happens when the orbit is cancelled and the arm is not.

The deal

The arrangement, formalized in 2020, was elegant in the way Canadian space policy has been elegant since the 1970s: we cannot afford rockets, so we pay in excellence. Canada would contribute Canadarm3 — a next-generation robotic system for the Gateway, a large arm and a small dexterous one, capable of crawling end-over-end across the station’s exterior, plugging its anchoring hand into standardized fixtures, maintaining the outpost autonomously for the long stretches when no crew was aboard. The small arm could even repair the large one. An arm that services itself, a quarter of the way to forever, while the humans are at home.

In return, Canada received lunar science opportunities and two astronaut seats on Artemis missions. The first of those seats was Hansen’s. In June 2024, as SpaceNews reported, the Canadian Space Agency awarded MDA Space of Brampton, Ontario — the same corporate lineage that built the original arm and its successor — a contract worth one billion Canadian dollars to carry Canadarm3 through final design and assembly. Hundreds of Canadian companies feed into the program. It is, by a wide margin, the flagship of Canadian space.

Then the customer for the flagship’s destination changed his mind.

The pivot

It would be satisfying, from the balcony where this publication sits, to report that the Gateway cancellation was a betrayal — another American unilateralism to shelve alongside the tariffs. The truthful version is less satisfying and more interesting: the Gateway had real problems. A 2024 Government Accountability Office report flagged that its modules were trending heavier than designed. Costs had escalated; schedules had slipped; and a reasonable person could ask why humanity’s return to the Moon required a waystation orbiting the Moon rather than boots and habitats on it. Isaacman’s answer — skip the lobby, build the house — is at minimum a defensible piece of architecture criticism.

But architecture criticism lands differently depending on whether you own the building or merely furnished it. Europe had staked its Artemis role, and its astronauts’ lunar seats, on Gateway modules; the European Space Agency spent June deciding what to do with hardware built for a station that may never exist. The UAE’s airlock is similarly orphaned. And Canada — Canada had the arm.

Here the story takes the turn that makes it a Tomorrow postcard rather than an obituary. As The Logic reported in its long examination of the moment, MDA’s chief executive, Mike Greenley, was notably unbereaved. His contract, he pointed out, is with the Canadian Space Agency, not NASA. The preliminary design was already complete. And the design could pivot: rather than finalizing the robotics for an orbiting station, MDA would finalize them for the lunar surface — for the base NASA now intends to build.

Pause on what that means technically, because it is a lovelier problem than it first appears. Every arm in the Canadarm lineage — the original, Canadarm2, the Dextre manipulator — was built for weightlessness. The original Canadarm could move payloads the mass of a school bus in orbit but could not have lifted its own weight in a Toronto parking lot; it was never asked to. An arm for the lunar surface must, for the first time in fifty years of Canadian space robotics, hold itself up. One-sixth gravity is still gravity. The engineers in Brampton are now designing the first arm in the family that has to stand — which is either a metaphor so obvious I should apologize for it, or exactly the right note, and I have decided it is the right note.

As SpaceNews reported from MDA’s May earnings call, the program is proceeding “full steam ahead” while Ottawa and Washington negotiate what the robotic contribution to the new lunar architecture will be. Greenley spoke of positive intent on both sides, and of urgency — the conversations need to conclude soon enough that the full-steam-ahead posture is steaming toward the right destination. Less than nine hundred million of the billion-dollar contract remains to be worked through, and the Canadarm3 work sits inside a company backlog of some 4.8 billion dollars — because in the meantime, MDA has been assembling a market for the technology beyond any single government program: commercial space stations, satellite servicing, debris removal, private rovers, a whole commercial robotics line the company brands Skymaker. The same earnings call that addressed the Gateway pause also announced delivery of the first batch of seventeen replacement satellites for Globalstar — a constellation Amazon has since agreed to acquire for eleven billion dollars, with MDA’s contracts riding along intact. The firm that once depended on a single arm and a single customer has spent two decades making sure it never would again. The arm, it turns out, was never really for the Gateway. The Gateway was merely its first job offer.

The flight

And what of the ticket already punched?

Artemis II launched on April 1 and splashed down in the Pacific on April 10 — ten days, a figure-eight around the Moon, the first crewed lunar flight since 1972. The crew named their Orion capsule Integrity. On April 6 they broke the record, held since Apollo 13 in 1970, for the farthest any human beings have ever travelled from Earth — 406,771 kilometres at the peak, with Hansen aboard, a distance at which our planet is a marble and Canada is not visible at all, which some of our provincial premiers might take as a philosophical instruction.

The four of them became the first humans to see the entire far side of the Moon with their own eyes. Christina Koch was the first woman on a lunar mission; Victor Glover the first person of colour; Hansen the first non-American. When the record fell, it was the Canadian who spoke on the downlink, honouring the predecessors and challenging the next generation to break the record quickly. During the flyby the crew proposed names for two craters on the far side — one for their ship, Integrity, and one named Carroll, for the commander’s late wife — proposals that will go to the International Astronomical Union, which governs such things, meaning a mission that lasted ten days may end up written on the Moon permanently. And the program behind it is accelerating: Artemis III, the next flight, is targeted for mid-2027, with the first crewed landing to follow. The window in which the new lunar architecture gets decided — and Canada’s robotic place within it — is not a leisurely one.

At the welcome-home ceremony in Houston, Hansen gathered his crewmates around him and told the crowd that they were not really looking at four astronauts: “We are a mirror reflecting you,” he said — look deeper, and you will see yourselves.

Readers of this publication’s companion series will note that Part VIII of these postcards was titled The Mirror Problem, and was about machines built to resemble us. Trust a Canadian to stand in Houston, wrapped in the largest engineering triumph of the decade, and quietly relocate the mirror. The machines reflect our engineering. The crews reflect our better selves. Both reflections are worth funding.

There is a detail from Hansen’s summer that belongs in this postcard. As the CBC reported in June, he spent a Wednesday in Nova Scotia — first at Canso, on the eastern shore, watching a suborbital launch demonstration from a small Canadian spaceport, and then at the Halifax central library, telling three hundred people that the mission had reinforced rather than changed his worldview, and that what most surprised him was how many Canadians had been paying attention. A man who had just been farther from Earth than anyone in history spent his victory lap at a rural launch pad and a public library. If you wanted a single sentence of Canadian space policy, that itinerary is the sentence.

The rest of the portfolio

The arm and the astronaut are the headlines, but the Canadian lunar file is a portfolio, and the portfolio absorbed the same shock unevenly — which is instructive, because it shows what survives an architecture change and what does not.

The casualty: a small Canadian science rover, slated to fly on a commercial lander in 2029, was cancelled this spring when the CSA trimmed its sails. The survivor: funding was maintained for the Lunar Utility Vehicle — a larger Canadian rover intended to support Artemis surface missions, targeted to fly in 2033, with preparatory contracts already out to MDA Space, Canadensys Aerospace and Mission Control ahead of a prime-contractor selection. Note the pattern, because it is the same pattern as the arm: the standalone science project dies; the infrastructure project — the machine other missions will depend on — lives. Canada keeps choosing to be the country that builds the equipment everyone else’s ambitions require. It is less glamorous than planting flags and considerably harder to cancel.

The human side of the portfolio carries its own asterisk. Jeremy Hansen’s backup on Artemis II was Jenni Gibbons, who trained for the Moon and, in the way of backups, watched it on a screen; her turn is banked. And CSA astronaut Josh Kutryk was to fly to the International Space Station this year on Boeing’s Starliner — a mission converted, after that vehicle’s long troubles, into yet another uncrewed test, with Kutryk’s flight postponed to a date best described as eventually. One Canadian flew around the Moon this spring; another had his ride to low Earth orbit taken away by a spacecraft that cannot yet be trusted with passengers. Anyone who thinks the space business proceeds in orderly lines should hold those two facts in the same month.

The middle power’s wager

Now the larger question, because every postcard in this series pairs its subject with one.

What is the lesson when a middle power pays for its seat at the table with excellence, and the table gets moved?

The cynical reading is available and I will state it fairly: Canada committed a billion dollars to an American-led architecture, the architecture changed by American fiat, and Canada’s response — continue building, stay positive, negotiate quietly — looks uncomfortably like the accommodation reflex this publication has documented in other files. One analyst put it bluntly: without the Gateway, Canada must negotiate its role in Artemis from a weaker position. The industry association’s own leadership, as The Logic reported, now speaks of ensuring the American relationship serves Canadian interests while diversifying partnerships globally — language indistinguishable from a Carney trade speech about canola, which tells you how thoroughly the lessons of the trade war have soaked into every Canadian file, including the ones in orbit.

But the cynical reading misses what the hardware knows. The deeper truth of fifty years of Canadian space robotics is that the arms have consistently outlived the programs they were designed for. The original Canadarm was built for a shuttle program that ended; the arm’s descendants kept flying. Canadarm2 was designed to assemble a station; it then spent two decades doing things nobody had imagined at design time — catching free-flying cargo ships out of the void, a manoeuvre it was never meant to perform, now so routine it has a nickname, the cosmic catch. A senior CSA official made exactly this point this spring: the robotic operations of 2026 were unimaginable when the station was first sketched. Excellence, it turns out, is the one contribution that survives a change of architecture. Modules are bolted to a plan. Capability is bolted to nothing.

That is the middle power’s actual wager, and it is shrewder than it looks from the cheap seats. Canada did not really bet a billion dollars on the Gateway. It bet a billion dollars on the proposition that whatever humans build at the Moon — a station, a base, a mining camp, a research colony — will need robotic arms, and that the world’s best robotic arms will continue to speak with a Canadian accent. The Gateway pause tests the wager’s resilience, not its logic. The Moon base will need arms more than the Gateway would have; a surface base is a construction site, and construction sites are where arms live.

So the arm without a home is, on inspection, an arm between homes — and learning, for the first time in its family’s history, to bear weight. There are worse metaphors for the country that built it. We have spent the better part of two years learning the same skill.

Somewhere in Brampton, an engineer is redesigning a wrist joint for one-sixth gravity. Somewhere above the far side of the Moon, there is a crater the Artemis II crew proposed to name Integrity, after their ship. And somewhere in between is the future this series exists to report on: not the future of the announcements and the pauses, but the one being machined, joint by joint, regardless.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one has a return address in Ontario.
