Part 83 of this series covered Google’s default-on Gemini rollout across K-12 classrooms. Part 100 covered the federal government’s parallel rush into medical AI. This entry covers the same dynamic one level up, where the people setting the rules are not federal regulators but university presidents, and where the research base for concern is considerably more developed than in either prior essay — which makes the sector’s response harder to excuse as simple unfamiliarity with the risks.

What the research actually shows

As The New York Times’ Mark Arsenault, Dana Goldstein, Alan Blinder, and Sarah Mervosh reported on September 15, 2026, a committee at MIT studying AI’s effect on campus released an August report warning of “major shifts in campus culture” — students skipping office hours and communal studying, reaching for a chatbot “at the first hint of struggle” in a pattern the committee termed the “illusion of learning.” A separate 2025 MIT study, led by researcher Nataliya Kos’myna, found that people who used chatbots as writing assistants struggled to recall what they had just written only minutes after finishing, because, in her words, “you basically didn’t engage any of the memory networks that would actually help you remember.” A study of 26,000 Chinese secondary-school students found AI adoption raised homework scores 18 percent while secure-exam scores fell 20 percent — a fairly precise demonstration that chatbot-assisted homework can substitute for the deeper learning exams are actually designed to measure, rather than reinforcing it. Other research cited in the piece links greater trust in AI to measurably lower cognitive engagement generally. None of this is speculative or industry-adjacent; it’s coming from the same universities now deciding how enthusiastically to deploy the technology their own researchers are flagging as a problem.

The presidents leaning in anyway

Set against that research base, the enthusiasm from campus leadership reads differently than it would in isolation. Sian Leah Beilock, Dartmouth’s president, has argued — in language this series covered in Part 83’s own account of her position — that failing to graduate AI-fluent students risks institutional irrelevance. Harvard’s president, Alan Garber, has said AI-backed research is approaching a boom that will outpace any prior period in the history of human knowledge production, and that researchers in many fields will soon be unable to stay at the leading edge of their disciplines without it. Ohio State is integrating AI training into every single major — business, engineering, English, veterinary medicine alike — under a mandate from president Ravi Bellamkonda that every graduate arrive fluent in their field’s AI applications. None of these are fringe positions; they’re the leaders of some of the most consequential institutions in American higher education, moving in the opposite direction from what their own peer institutions’ research is finding.

The boundaries some schools are drawing instead

A smaller number of institutions have gone the other way, and the specific mechanisms are worth naming because they differ meaningfully from each other. The University of Chicago has banned AI in certain required social-science courses and barred phones, tablets, and laptops entirely from first-year law classrooms. Berkeley’s law school prohibits using AI “to conceptualize, outline, draft, revise, and edit” any work submitted for credit — a restriction broad enough to cover nearly the entire writing process, not merely the final draft. Cornell has built a lighter-touch disciplinary track for minor AI misuse, letting students avoid a permanent record by completing a workshop and reflective assignment rather than facing a formal integrity charge; Steve Jackson, the vice provost overseeing it, described the goal as ensuring violations get “sanctioned” and “not let go unnoted,” rather than either ignored or treated as automatically career-ending. Harvard, despite Garber’s own enthusiasm at the research level, has no unified undergraduate policy at all: Dean David Deming has said he favours more AI where it “can deepen learning,” while separately signaling a shift toward in-class testing and fewer take-home assignments specifically to reduce the temptation to cheat — an implicit admission that the university doesn’t trust its own students to self-regulate under the current policy vacuum. New York City’s school system, the nation’s largest, sharply limited AI for K-8 students in September; Los Angeles’s district, the second-largest, essentially banned chatbot use at every grade level — both decisions arriving from the K-12 side of exactly the same institutional confusion running through higher ed.

Why the confusion is the actual policy

Most universities have made no institutional decision at all, leaving individual instructors — many with no particular AI expertise — to improvise their own rules course by course. Annie Chechitelli, chief product officer at the plagiarism-detection company TurnItIn, put the resulting confusion in blunt terms: many educators “don’t even know where to start,” uncertain what counts as legitimate revision assistance versus outright chatbot-generated submission, a line the Times piece itself describes as “thin” even for instructors actively trying to enforce it. The gap between using AI to revise one’s own writing and submitting AI-generated text wholesale is real in principle and, in practice, nearly impossible for most instructors to police reliably. The University of Sydney’s “two-lane” system — some assessments completed in person under conditions that exclude AI entirely, others requiring AI use specifically so students learn the tool responsibly — is being held up internationally as a rare example of an institution actually resolving the ambiguity rather than delegating it downward. Anita Sarma, an Oregon State computer science professor who studies AI’s effect on student cognition, offered the analogy that captures the sector’s overall posture better than any policy document: “It’s almost like sex ed. They will do it anyway. How do we teach them responsible use?”

That analogy is more revealing than it might first appear. Sex education exists because avoidance was tried first and failed; the shift toward harm-reduction framing came only after decades of evidence that prohibition alone doesn’t work. Higher education is skipping directly to the harm-reduction framing for a technology that has existed, in its current form, for under four years — with a research base, per MIT’s own committee, already showing measurable cognitive costs, and without the decades of accumulated evidence that made sex education’s own pivot a considered judgment rather than an assumption. Readers of this series’ Parts 83 and 100 will recognise the pattern by now: the sector best positioned to know better — universities, in this case, rather than a tech company or a federal health agency — is moving fastest anyway, on the theory that falling behind the technology is a bigger institutional risk than falling behind the evidence about what it actually does to the people using it.

The provost who wrote about cheating

The syllabus chaos was about students. On September 29, 2026, Mark Arsenault and Alan Blinder reported in The New York Times that the problem has climbed the org chart. The people enforcing the rules are breaking them.

The essay about cheating

The case at the centre of the story is so neat it reads like a Leacock sketch. In August, the news site Semafor investigated A.I.-generated writing turning up on the opinion pages of major publications, and found that an August 9 commentary in The Washington Post by Santiago Schnell, the provost of Dartmouth College and a mathematical biologist, had been written by A.I. The subject of the commentary was students cheating with A.I.

In September, the student newspaper, The Dartmouth, followed up and found extensive A.I. use across Schnell’s published work this year. He told the paper he used A.I. “for language refinement, copyediting, improving clarity and organisation and making writing processes more efficient.” The students ran that statement through an A.I. detector. It flagged the statement itself as apparently machine-written.

Dartmouth opened an investigation. In a letter published in The Dartmouth on September 25, Schnell apologised for not always disclosing his A.I. use, and wrote that he was “more aware than ever of the need for robust intellectual discussions about the appropriate use of A.I. in the higher education landscape.” Dartmouth’s president, Sian Leah Beilock — who has lately called for more A.I. use at the college, and who wrote in The Atlantic in September about campus A.I. policy — told the campus that being an early leader on A.I. was “messy.” “There will be missteps,” she wrote, “and that’s part of how an institution learns.”

The detail is worse than the summary. The student reporters Alex Klee and Isabela Pierry, writing in The Dartmouth on September 21, 2026, ran nine of Schnell’s 2026 publications through the detector Pangram and found a median of 96 per cent of the text flagged as machine-written; the Post essay scored 100 per cent. Inside Higher Ed reported his earlier defence of the Post piece: “I wrote this essay based on my own thinking. Then, I used ChatGPT to refine a few arguments, check for grammatical errors, and submit it.” On September 25, Beilock announced that a five-member committee chaired by David Kotz, a computer scientist and former Dartmouth provost, would conduct what she called a “rigorous, objective review” of the provost’s A.I. use. The Boston Globe reported that faculty and students were calling for his removal; the religion professor Christopher MacEvitt called it “a textbook case of exactly what we tell our students not to do.” By October 1, The Dartmouth reported, a petition circulated by the college’s chapter of the American Association of University Professors calling for his termination had passed 220 signatures.

The committee reported four weeks later. On October 28, Stephanie Saul reported for The New York Times that it had found Schnell’s use of A.I. improper: he had used the tool to “meaningfully alter his writing” and had “failed to disclose his use of A.I.,” violating at least one publication’s disclosure requirement. Semafor said it had also found A.I. writing in opinion articles he published in The New York Times. Beilock announced that Schnell had resigned and would leave the college, and that the decision was his. “I expect our senior leaders to hold themselves to the highest standards,” she said. Matthew Delmont, a historian of the civil rights movement, became interim provost. The college went ahead that same week with a previously scheduled conference on A.I.’s potential.

A senior, Eli Moyse, was less philosophical. He called the affair a “completely cartoonish” display of hypocrisy. “He should resign immediately or get fired,” he said. “Passing something off as your work when it isn’t your work is just plagiarism, even if it’s written by a robot.”

Not just Dartmouth

The Times finds the same pattern elsewhere. At Berkeley, The Daily Californian ran campus communications through detectors and found some messages from top administrators apparently written or altered by A.I.; a spokesman, Dan Mogulof, said leaders use it as a tool within guidelines, so long as the result “accurately and authentically captures and expresses the author’s thoughts, perspectives, tone and objectives,” and added that this proves impossible “without extensive revision, editing and/or embellishment.” Student journalists also flagged an essay by the Berkeley mathematician Zvezdelina Stankova in The San Francisco Standard — about the shaky maths skills of new Berkeley students — as substantially machine-written; she said she used A.I. for editing and that the essay was the product of “intensive human work and deliberation.” At Stanford, which has a detailed A.I. policy, the university used A.I. to alter a promotional photograph, removing a Hispanic man and replacing him with a Black woman and changing the appearance of two other students; it later acknowledged a policy violation.

Students have noticed. A George Washington University working group reported in August that many students “doubt that faculty themselves are using A.I. ethically and transparently.” At Brown, nearly two-thirds of surveyed undergraduates had some worry about faculty relying on A.I. to build lectures and course content.

The numbers behind the hypocrisy

The survey data suggest the asymmetry is real but narrower than the headlines. A national survey in autumn 2025 by Elon University and the American Association of Colleges and Universities found that about a quarter of 1,057 faculty respondents were not using generative A.I. at all. Nearly nine in ten said they did not use it for grading, and only 6 per cent used it “a lot” in their own research papers — though close to half used it at least somewhat to create or revise assignments. Students, by contrast, lean on it heavily: more than half use it weekly, and one in five every day. The picture changes higher up the org chart. In its September 29 report on the Dartmouth affair, Inside Higher Ed noted survey figures showing that 65 per cent of college chief academic officers, the provost’s own peer group, use A.I. to draft communications, and 71 per cent use it weekly for some purpose.

So this is not a story of a professoriate secretly outsourcing everything. It is a story of a minority of prominent faculty and administrators using a tool without saying so, while presiding over institutions that punish students for doing the same. That is a narrower failing, and a more corrosive one, because it concerns the rule rather than the tool.

A caution about the detectors

Fairness requires one note that the Dartmouth story’s funniest moment tends to obscure. A.I. detectors are unreliable. They produce false positives, particularly on formal, clean or heavily edited prose — which is to say, on exactly the kind of writing a provost’s office produces. The detector flagging Schnell’s apology is a delicious detail; it is not proof. The stronger evidence in his case is the Semafor investigation and his own admission. Institutions that respond to this scandal by running every faculty email through a detector will simply move the injustice they have been inflicting on students up a floor.

What the shortcut costs

The same day, Juliana Castro Varón and Dylan Freedman reviewed the new research for the Times’s “Machine Learnings” column, and it explains why the rule matters beyond fairness. The question was whether A.I. is making us, especially the young, worse at thinking.

A team from the University of California, Irvine, and McGraw Hill examined a decade of data from a McGraw Hill online maths tool: millions of sessions by students from fifth grade to university. They split the questions into two kinds: text problems that could easily be pasted into a chatbot, and visual, interactive or multistep problems that could not. After ChatGPT arrived in 2022, the time students spent on the easily pasted questions fell sharply, and their scores rose. On proctored tests away from the computer, their results were worse than before A.I. A second, multi-institution study found that adults who get used to having the struggle automated become more likely to give up on hard problems they could have solved. “Part of how A.I. is making us dumber,” said Adam Green, a cognitive neuroscientist at Georgetown, is “undercutting the development of learning how to think in younger people that now have an alternative.” The brain, he said, conserves effort the way the body conserves calories.

The encouraging finding came from Middlebury College. Researchers had more than 200 undergraduates write an essay and take a test, some with A.I. and some without, then brought them back a week later to work unassisted. Students who had used A.I. to draft whole essays floundered the second time. Students who had used it as a tutor, to explain difficult ideas and clarify their thinking, kept their high scores and wrote better essays than both the essay-drafters and the students who never used A.I. at all. Whether we become dumber, the column concludes, “might depend on how — not if — we use A.I.”

That is the real case against the provost’s shortcut, and against the students’ too. The damage is not only the dishonesty. It is the skipped struggle, where the learning happens.

The standard that should apply

The most useful line in the whole piece comes from the University of Southern California’s faculty guidelines: “There is a difference between what you can do with generative A.I. tools and what you should do.” And the practical rule that follows: “faculty who require A.I. disclosure from students should generally hold themselves to the same standard.”

That is the standard, and it is the one this series tries to hold itself to. These essays are written in open collaboration with Anthropic’s Claude, and the masthead says so. Readers should also know that Dartmouth signed an agreement last year with Anthropic and Amazon Web Services to give its faculty and students access to the technology, so the model on this masthead is very likely among the tools on that campus. The disclosure is the difference. A provost who used A.I. to tidy an op-ed and said so would have been, at worst, an early adopter. A provost who used it to write an op-ed about students’ dishonest use of A.I., and did not say so, has turned an ordinary tool into a case study in the thing he was warning against.

Marc Watkins, who founded the Mississippi A.I. Institute for Teachers, diagnosed the underlying problem: “The big thing is there’s no social contract right now. We don’t have these sorts of priors to say what’s appropriate and what’s not.” Universities spent centuries building norms about plagiarism and authorship. The machine arrived faster than the norms, and the people who were supposed to write the new ones turned out to be using it too.

The ledger

Part 100’s Rachael Bedard worried that A.I. was making her a better doctor and her students worse — the difference between a little de-skilling and never-skilling. Dartmouth shows the institutional version: a university whose leaders embrace the tool, whose rules punish students for using it covertly, and whose provost used it covertly to write about students using it covertly. The fix is not a detector and not a ban. It is the dullest and most demanding virtue there is — saying plainly what you did and how. Students have been told that for years. It turns out the lesson needed to travel in both directions.
