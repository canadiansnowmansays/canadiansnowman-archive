This ledger has spent twenty-two instalments tracing the money: the circular financing of Part III, the off-balance-sheet leases of Part II, the bond issuance driving up mortgage rates in Part X, the orbital moonshots of Parts XIII and XXI. On September 26, 2026, Bethany McLean — the financial journalist who co-wrote The Smartest Guys in the Room — published a guest essay in The New York Times that ties all of it into a single, uncomfortable question, and does it through the one historical rhyme this ledger keeps returning to: 2008.

Her question is not whether the A.I. bubble will burst. It is what we will be asked to do if it does. And the answer the industry is quietly pre-loading should alarm anyone who remembers the last time a sector declared itself indispensable.

The ask, said out loud

McLean opens with two quotations that deserve to be read slowly. In November 2025, Sarah Friar, OpenAI’s chief financial officer, floated the idea that the company might sustain its financial high-wire act with a “backstop” or “guarantee” — a government guarantee — that “allows the financing to happen.” Around the same time, Sam Altman said that given the magnitude of A.I.’s expected economic impact, the government should serve as “insurer of last resort.” Both qualified the remarks. The Register reported on November 6, 2025 that Friar had made hers on stage at The Wall Street Journal’s Tech Live event in Napa, California, agreeing “Exactly” when the idea was put to her as “some federal backstop for chip investors,” and then posted on LinkedIn that evening: “OpenAI is not seeking a government backstop for our infrastructure commitments. I used the word ‘backstop’ and it muddied the point.” Altman followed with a principle, “taxpayers should not bail out companies that make bad business decisions,” and an exception: “The one area where we have discussed loan guarantees is as part of supporting the buildout of semiconductor fabs in the US.” But the gist, as McLean puts it, is that the industry The Wall Street Journal calls “the biggest economic bet in U.S. history” is so important to the nation that if it needs more funding, the taxpayer should be on the hook.

Another way to put it, she writes, is that the government should regard A.I. the way it regarded the banks in 2008: too big to fail.

And here she lands the contradiction that this entire series, governance and ledger both, has circled for months. It is hard to reconcile the too-big-to-fail plea with the other message blaring from the same industry: that the technology, in the words of the former Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon, “could kill us all by the end of the decade” and must be restrained. A.I. is so important we cannot let it fail, but so dangerous we should not let it proceed. The companies are asking, simultaneously, to be insured against collapse and slowed to prevent catastrophe. Both requests route the risk to the public and the reward to the shareholder.

Too big to stop

McLean’s real contribution is a phrase, and it is sharper than too-big-to-fail. She traces how the arguments reinforce one another into something worse. The more the race to dominate A.I. becomes existential — whoever wins A.I. wins, as the president has it — the more must be spent on it; the more that is spent, the more economically and geopolitically consequential the industry becomes; the more consequential, the greater the imperative to speed ahead, safety be damned; and the more heedless the speed, the greater the risk. The loop does not stabilise. It accelerates. Which raises, she writes, an even scarier possibility than too big to fail: what if A.I. is too big to stop?

It is the financial twin of the argument Ezra Klein has been making about the labs: that they are fleeing a prophecy they are racing to fulfil. McLean has found the balance-sheet version of the same trap. The money cannot stop for the same reason the code cannot: everyone is certain that stopping means losing to someone worse.

The 2008 machinery, reassembled

The history is where McLean’s expertise earns its keep, and it maps onto this ledger’s spine almost line for line. “Too big to fail” as a phrase crystallised in 1984, after Continental Illinois nearly sank on bad energy loans; Representative Stewart McKinney coined it. The 2008 bailouts made it doctrine. And those too-big-to-fail banks, she notes, were themselves the legacy of Reagan-era fear that American banks had to bulk up fast or lose to Europe and Asia — the exact structure of today’s “beat China or perish” argument the Asimov Gap dismantled in Part 86.

The most damning parallel is the psychology. In the run-up to 2008, even as it became clear that subprime borrowers could not repay, the banks refused to change course. Citibank’s Chuck Prince supplied the epitaph: “As long as the music is playing, you’ve got to get up and dance.” McLean’s point is that Amazon, Alphabet, Oracle, OpenAI and Anthropic are dancing now, and cannot stop, lest they forfeit the hundreds of billions already sunk into beating the competition. Call it, as she does, rational recklessness. Part XIV met the same ghost from the other side, in Edward Yardeni’s own warning about Irving Fisher’s “permanently high plateau” on the eve of 1929.

Bigger than subprime, and the one reason it might not detonate

The scale is the part that should stop a reader cold, and McLean states it plainly: A.I.-related spending accounts for roughly half the growth in U.S. GDP by some estimates, and the vast majority of the $33 trillion in market value the S&P 500 has added since late 2022 has come from A.I.-linked spending. The subprime boom that took down the world economy was, by this measure, smaller.

But she is too careful a reporter to cry fire without checking the exits, and this ledger will honour the caveat as prominently as the alarm. The 2008 collapse was catastrophic not because of size but because subprime debt had been woven invisibly into the machinery by which banks financed themselves. A.I. investment, though larger, has not infiltrated the banking system the same way. Jim Chanos — a perpetual and vindicated sceptic — gives the essential qualifier: “The related debt levels are not systemic — yet.” That “yet” is doing heroic work, and it is the number this ledger will be watching in every subsequent instalment. The day A.I. leverage becomes woven into bank balance sheets the way subprime was, the too-big-to-stop loop acquires a detonator.

The watchers are already looking at it. On September 30, 2026, Phoebe Seers and David Milliken reported for Insurance Journal that the Bank of England’s Financial Policy Committee saw a growing risk that the dangers from A.I. and debt would materialise. The committee noted the “rapid increase” in A.I.-related debt issuance, which had raised capital markets’ exposure to the technology, and cited Morgan Stanley data putting global A.I. debt at about 450 billion dollars in early September, double the 2025 level. It warned that “while valuations remain high, a more significant shock could trigger a sharper repricing.” Four hundred and fifty billion dollars is not yet subprime. It has doubled in a year.

McLean adds the two data points that capture the perversity the Asimov Gap has been documenting. Dario Amodei, Anthropic’s chief executive, warns repeatedly of A.I.’s dangers while, reportedly, accelerating the company’s expansion ahead of its I.P.O., prompting the economist Owen Lamont’s instant classic, that it is “like Robert Oppenheimer doing an I.P.O. for the Manhattan Project in 1945.” Anthropic filed publicly for that listing on October 26, 2026, for an offering that could raise more than $100 billion at a valuation near $2 trillion, The New York Times reported. By November 6, Reuters was reporting that potential investors doubted the company could hold its roadshow the following week and still list in November, with some treating $1.5 trillion as the high end. And the former Treasury secretaries Robert Rubin and Hank Paulson, the latter the man who ran the 2008 bailout, warned in an essay in The Washington Post on September 3, 2026, that “perverse financial incentives are making it difficult to protect humanity from the dangers of A.I.,” and that the world “has never seen so much capital chasing one opportunity.” When the man who ran the last rescue starts talking about perverse incentives, that is not a man crying wolf. That is the shepherd who has seen a wolf before.

The one thing that could break the loop

McLean finds a single crack in the inevitability, and it is the one this ledger has documented from the ground up. Americans really do not like A.I. companies. The property-tax revolts of Part IX, the youth activism of Part XII, the rent evictions of Part XV, the premium hikes of Part XXII: all of it converges on the poll number that keeps recurring, a public turned decisively against the build-out. It is not beyond imagining, she wrote, that safety fears, a sufficiently shocking disaster, or a regulatory crackdown could end the lavish funding suddenly, threatening the companies and the wider economy at once. The backlash this ledger has treated as a cost-transmission story is also, potentially, the pin.

The markets have now had a first look at that pin. On November 5, 2026, two days after the midterms, the S&P 500 fell 4.4 percent, its worst day since April 2025, while the Nasdaq lost 5.5 percent and Nvidia 9.3 percent. Stan Choe of The Associated Press reported that the sell-off was led by stocks tied to the data-centre build-out, already shaken by the cost of borrowing, and that the Democratic victories “raised the possibility that governments could force pauses” in construction. Reuters, weighing what a delayed Anthropic listing would signal, wrote that the success of ballot efforts in many states had “crystallized political opposition to data centres and other aspects of AI.” One bad day is not a burst bubble. It is a price being put, for the first time, on the possibility that the public gets a vote.

The ledger

Her prescription is the right one to close on, because it inverts the industry’s ask. In 2008 the policymakers had a saying, plan beats no plan, and a plan made before a crisis beats one improvised inside it. So McLean says: plan now. But plan around the correct question. When the bubble deflates, the first question is not how to keep these particular companies afloat; it is what, exactly, we would be trying to save. The technology may be genuinely critical to the American future. Preserving it does not require bailing out the private entities leading the field this quarter, nor protecting the fortunes of their executives and investors. A nation already carrying a debt of this size has no obligation to accept a rerun of the deal that, in 2008, rescued the balance sheets of the investor class at everyone else’s expense.

The whole ever-expanding A.I. terror, McLean writes, starts to shrink the moment you remember the one lesson of 2008 that the industry most wants forgotten: no company should be too big to fail. This ledger has spent twenty-three parts adding up what the build-out costs the household. McLean has supplied the sentence that should govern the reckoning when the bill finally comes due — which is that the people who threw the party do not get to hand the public the tab, however loudly they insist the music can never stop.
