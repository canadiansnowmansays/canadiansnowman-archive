I have spent eight essays cataloguing erasures — a memorial hollowed, a museum censored, signs pulled from the trails, datasets deleted, a portrait taken down, an archivist fired, a name changed back, a library emptied. Read in a row, they make a bleak accounting, and a reader would be forgiven for closing the series with the sense that the past is simply being rubbed out and there is nothing to be done.

That is not the whole story, and I have deliberately withheld the other half until now, so that it could have an essay of its own. Because at the edge of every single erasure in this series there has been a figure — usually unpaid, usually unthanked, often anonymous — doing the opposite work. Copying the file before it fell. Photographing the sign before the crew arrived. Raising the flag again across the street. Naming the campaign after the banned book. Standing in the corridor and remembering what used to hang on the wall. This essay is about that figure. Call them the witnesses. They are the reason a Ministry of Memory, in a country that still has them, cannot fully succeed.

The Line That Names the Whole Resistance

When the Justice Department deleted its data on Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women — a subject that exists as a public crisis precisely because someone insisted on counting — Charlene Aqpik Apok, who directs an organization devoted to that data, said five words that I have quoted before in this series and want to build an entire essay around now:

“We still know what was said.”

That is the whole of it. That is the sentence a Ministry of Memory cannot defeat, because it does not live on a server the government controls or a wall the government can repaint. It lives in people — in the ones who saw the record before it was deleted, kept a copy, remember what it contained, and will say so. Orwell’s memory hole worked because it was total: once the document went down the chute, no surviving copy existed, and so, in time, no one could be sure the document had ever been real. The erasures in this series leak, every one of them, because the copy exists. Someone kept it. We still know what was said.

The Midnight Archivists

Nowhere is this clearer than in what happened when the datasets began to vanish, because the response was not a lament — it was an operation, and a remarkably organized one.

As pages went dark across the CDC, the Census Bureau, the EPA, and the rest in early 2025, librarians, data scientists, and archivists who had seen the threat coming moved to save the record before it fell. They formalized their work as the Data Rescue Project. By the middle of 2025, more than five hundred volunteers had rescued well over a thousand datasets across dozens of federal offices. Harvard’s Library Innovation Lab captured and released a backup of more than three hundred thousand datasets hosted on data.gov — a mirror of the government’s open-data catalogue, kept safe outside the government’s reach. Guerrilla archivists were trained to run backup tools that quietly copied federal pages faster than they could be pulled. Universities held weekend “data rescue” events where students and faculty nominated vulnerable datasets and mirrored them by hand.

None of this was new, exactly. The infrastructure had been built by people who understood, long before 2025, that a public record is only as durable as the copies of it held outside the building that produced it. The End of Term Web Archive has captured snapshots of the federal government’s websites at the close of every administration since 2008, on the sound librarian’s assumption that transitions are when things disappear. The 2017 Data Refuge effort had rehearsed exactly this. When the deletions came, the profession was ready, because remembering is what the profession is for.

I want to dwell on who these people are, because they are the opposite of dramatic. They are data librarians. They are the person at your state university whose job title you have never thought about, who catalogues the government statistics nobody reads until the day they are gone. They received, some of them, an internal library award for “innovation and problem-solving” — the mildest possible honour for what was, in fact, a rescue of the national memory conducted at speed and on their own initiative. There is something almost unbearably fitting in that: the people who saved the record are thanked, if at all, with the quiet institutional courtesy their profession runs on, and go back to work. They are not doing it for the credit. They are doing it because a librarian’s deepest conviction is that the record must survive its keepers.

The Witness in Every Chapter

Once you learn to see this figure, they are in every essay of this series, and I have — I confess — been placing them there on purpose, waiting for this installment to gather them up.

In Part 1, it was Sandy Weiswasser, the eighty-seven-year-old volunteer who stood in the hollowed Kennedy Center and told a reporter what it used to be, and was sent home for it. Her memory was the thing being switched off — but she had already said it out loud, and so it survived her dismissal. The tour program closed; the sentence “now it’s a black hole” is now indelible.

In Part 3, when the National Park Service pulled the rainbow flag from the monument across from the Stonewall Inn, New Yorkers gathered and raised another one, on a pole the government did not control. The state erased the marker; citizens re-marked the spot. And two federal judges — Rufe and Kelley — refused the erasure in the language of the law, one of them reaching for Orwell by name, ordering the signs and the slavery exhibit restored.

In Part 7, it was the son of Hal and Julia Moore, writing publicly against the stripping of his parents’ name from Fort Moore — insisting, in print, on the memory the renaming was designed to bury. In Part 8, it was the Naval Academy alumnus who named his campaign Operation Caged Bird, and the bookshop that filled its shelves with free copies of every banned title, and the federal judge who forced the Defense Department to disclose the six hundred books it had removed from the schools of military families. In Part 4, it was the midnight archivists. In Part 6, it was Colleen Shogan herself, fired without cause, who wrote simply: “I did my best every day.” Even the fired keeper of the record left a record of her firing.

Every erasure in this series has this shadow. The state removes; someone preserves. The state deletes; someone copies. The state repaints the wall; someone remembers the portrait and describes the blank. This is not a coincidence of my storytelling. It is the actual structure of how memory survives an assault on it — not through the institutions, which can be captured, but through the stubbornness of the individuals who will not agree to forget.

Why the Copy Defeats the Hole

Here is the mechanical reason the witnesses matter, stated as plainly as I can.

A Ministry of Memory depends, absolutely, on the absence of a comparison. The Smithsonian could roll its impeachment display back to 2008 only because most visitors would never see the earlier version and the later version side by side. The Pentagon could repaint the wall only because a photograph of the portrait hanging there was, they hoped, not being kept. The deletion of a dataset works only if no one preserved the original to hold against the silence. The entire technique rests on there being nothing left to check the edited version against. Erasure is the destruction of the control copy.

Which means the single most subversive act available to an ordinary person, in the face of all this, is not protest. It is preservation. It is keeping the copy. The archivist who mirrors the dataset, the visitor who photographs the plaque, the alumnus who remembers the shelf, the reporter who screenshots the page before it changes — each of them re-supplies the one thing the Ministry needs to be missing: the original, against which the edit can be exposed as an edit. You cannot prove a sign was removed if no one kept a picture of the sign. You can prove it easily if someone did. The witnesses are not sentimental. They are the evidentiary base of every future correction.

This is why authoritarian memory-control has always feared the archivist, the librarian, the diarist, and the person with a good memory and no fear of saying what they remember — more, sometimes, than it fears the open dissident. The dissident argues about the present. The archivist preserves the past, which is the ground the present stands on. A regime can win an argument. It cannot easily win against a copy.

What the Witnesses Cannot Do, and What They Can

I want to be honest about the limits, because false comfort is its own kind of erasure.

The witnesses do not stop the erasing. The tour program is still shut. The park signs, after the appellate stay, are coming down again. Most of the datasets are still gone from the government’s own servers, available now only in the volunteers’ mirrors, which most citizens will never think to look for. Preservation is not restoration. A dataset saved on a Harvard server is not the same as a dataset the public can find where it expects to find it, and a memory held by the people who happened to witness it is not the same as a memory carved, officially, where the whole nation can see. The witnesses buy time and they keep evidence; they do not, by themselves, win.

But what they preserve is the possibility of winning later. Every erasure this series documents is, because of them, reversible in principle — because the original still exists somewhere, held by someone, waiting for a moment when it can be restored to the public record. A Ministry of Memory that achieved its full ambition would leave a country unable even to know what it had lost. The witnesses ensure that this administration’s erasures, whatever else they accomplish, will not be total. The account has been copied. The comparison has been preserved. When the time comes to put the record back, the record will still exist to be put back.

That is not nothing. In a contest over memory, it may be everything, because it is the difference between an erasure that can someday be corrected and one that can never even be found.

The Sentence That Ends the Series’ First Half

I have closed several of these essays on a version of the same line, and now I can say why. We still know what was hung. We still know what the sign said. We still know what the Archives was for. Each was an echo of Charlene Apok’s five words, and I was laying them down like stones toward this essay.

We still know what was said.

It is not a triumphant sentence. It is spoken, always, in the past tense, about something already taken — the data already deleted, the sign already pulled, the portrait already gone. It concedes the erasure even as it defies it. But it is the sentence a Ministry of Memory cannot answer, because it does not depend on any record the Ministry controls. It depends only on there being people who saw, who kept, and who will say. As long as there are, the erasing is never finished, and the past is never quite past — it is only hidden, waiting in a mirror server or an old photograph or the memory of an eighty-seven-year-old woman who loved a building and told the truth about what had become of it.

They can pull the sign. We still know what it said. In the end, the whole of the resistance to a Ministry of Memory is contained in those two sentences, and in the ordinary, stubborn, unthanked people who keep making the second one true.

This is Part 9 of The Ministry of Memory, a series on the erasure of shared public memory — and the first installment about the people who refuse to allow it. The data-rescue archivists, the librarians, the judges, the family members, the volunteer who told the truth about a hollowed hall: at the edge of every erasure this series has documented stands a witness who kept the copy. They do not stop the erasing. They keep the original, and so they keep the erasing from ever being total. The series continues — the next essay turns to the honours a nation bestows, and who gets written out of them.
