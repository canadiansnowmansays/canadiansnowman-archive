Postmark: A living room, somewhere in America. A soft-bodied humanoid tidies the counter, fetches a glass, opens the door for a guest. Behind its eyes, some of the time, a person you have never met.

Part VII of this series went looking for Rosie. She is the robot maid from The Jetsons — that 1962 cartoon vision of the push-button home, the machine that cleaned and cooked and dispensed wisdom with a feather duster and a Bronx accent — and she has served, for sixty years, as the benchmark against which every home-robot promise is measured and found wanting. Part VII’s verdict was that Rosie remained missing: the warehouses had their robots, the factories had theirs, but the home, the hardest environment of all, was still a robot-free zone, defended by its own glorious chaos of stairs and pets and dropped socks and unpredictable toddlers.

I have to file an update. Rosie is coming — not perfected, not the seamless servant of the cartoon, but real, shippable, and taking deposits. This spring the first genuinely consumer-aimed humanoid robots began rolling toward American living rooms, and the story of their arrival is more interesting, and more unsettling, than the cartoon ever imagined. Because the Rosie that is actually arriving in 2026 solves the hardest problem in home robotics with a trick the Jetsons never contemplated: when she doesn’t know what to do, a human stranger, somewhere far away, quietly takes the controls.

The one that’s actually shipping

The company is called 1X, it is based in the Bay Area with Norwegian roots, and its robot is named NEO. As 1X announced, NEO went up for pre-order in October 2025 and began shipping toward homes in 2026 — a five-foot-six, sixty-six-pound humanoid in a soft knit suit, priced at twenty thousand dollars outright or a subscription of four hundred and ninety-nine dollars a month. As Forbes reported, the company booked ten thousand pre-orders in the first five days, opened a 58,000-square-foot factory in Hayward, California, and set a target of building up to a hundred thousand robots a year by the end of 2027.

Everything about NEO is designed to solve the problem Part VIII called the mirror problem — the deep human unease at a machine that resembles us. Where the Chinese humanoids of this series are all exposed actuators and hard shells, NEO is deliberately soft: a knit fabric body in muted tones — tan, gray, dark brown — a tendon-driven skeleton engineered to be gentle, a whisper-quiet 22-decibel operation, pinch-proof joints, a design team drawn from Nike and the fashion world. It is built not to intimidate. It unboxes itself, walks over, introduces itself, asks your name. From day one it can do the genuinely simple things: open the door for guests, fetch an item, turn the lights off at night, tidy a surface. It remembers your birthdays and your grocery list. It is, in every deliberate design choice, an attempt to make the humanoid welcome in the one place humans are most defended — the home.

And here is the thing the marketing photographs will not dwell on, the thing that makes NEO a genuine Tomorrow postcard rather than an advertisement: for anything beyond the simplest tasks, NEO cannot yet do the work alone. It needs a human — and not the human who owns it.

The stranger behind the eyes

This is the detail that reorganizes the whole story, and it deserves to be stated as plainly as the reporting states it. As eWeek reported, NEO’s first wave will be partly controlled through teleoperation — a human-in-the-loop system in which, when the robot encounters a task it has not mastered, a remote 1X employee takes over, seeing through the robot’s cameras and guiding its hands. The company calls this feature, without apparent irony, the ability to book a remote “1X Expert” to guide the robot so the job gets done while NEO learns.

Sit with what that means in a family home. You buy a humanoid robot to fold your laundry, and when it reaches the limit of its autonomy, a person you have never met — an employee in an office somewhere — sees through its eyes, into your bedroom, and moves its hands to fold your clothes. The robot is, in those moments, not an autonomous machine at all. It is a very expensive telepresence puppet, and the puppeteer is a stranger with a view of your living room.

To its considerable credit, 1X has not hidden this, and has built real safeguards around it. As The Robot Report noted, the company promises privacy features embedded in the machine: “no-go” zones the robot will never enter, and the ability to blur the faces of the people it interacts with, so the remote operator sees the task but not, entirely, the family. These are thoughtful protections, and they matter. But notice what their very existence confirms: the threat model they address is a human being looking through the robot’s eyes into your home. You do not build a face-blurring feature for a machine that is truly autonomous. You build it for a machine with a person inside.

There is a name for what NEO’s early buyers are, and 1X is honest enough to use it: they are not merely customers, they are trainers. Every time an Expert guides the robot through an unfamiliar task in a real home, that demonstration becomes training data, and the robot — and its successors — learn from it. The company’s own founder has argued that the home, not the factory, is the right place to solve the data problem, precisely because homes are where the messy, various, unrepeatable tasks live. The early adopters are paying twenty thousand dollars for the privilege of teaching the machine that will eventually replace the stranger behind its eyes. This is the reverse-centaur of The Asimov Gap wearing a knit suit and standing in your kitchen: the human labour has not vanished, it has been relocated — hidden behind the machine, piped in from a distance, and scheduled by the hour.

The race to erase the stranger

The stranger behind NEO’s eyes is not meant to be permanent, and the honest postcard must report the effort to eliminate him — because that effort is where the home-robot story connects to the deepest current in artificial intelligence.

The whole teleoperation arrangement is a bridge, explicitly temporary, across a gap the AI is racing to close. 1X unveiled in the spring what it calls a World Model — an AI system meant to let NEO learn from watching, so that a machine confronting an unfamiliar washing machine could reason its way to opening it the way a person does, looking for hinges and handles and trying another approach when the first fails, rather than waiting for a human to take the controls. And 1X is not alone. The most consequential development in home robotics this year was not any particular robot but a class of AI models — the field calls them vision-language-action models, or robotics foundation models — that aim to give a machine the general adaptability the stranger currently supplies by hand. One such model, from a company called Physical Intelligence, was reported this spring to have operated an air fryer it had encountered only twice in its entire training, by synthesizing those fragments with general knowledge into a working understanding of the appliance — exactly the kind of improvisation the Jetsons assumed and the real Rosie lacks.

Why this matters connects directly to The Asimov Gap. There is emerging evidence — a paper this year offered the first strong empirical version of it — that these robotics models follow the same data-driven scaling laws that made large language models transformational: feed them more real-world experience, and their competence improves predictably, the same compounding curve that took chatbots from novelty to ubiquity in a few years. If that holds, the stranger behind NEO’s eyes is a self-eliminating job. Every guided task the Experts perform is training data; every home NEO enters is a new environment to learn; and the whole apparatus of remote human control is a scaffold designed to be kicked away once the model has climbed high enough. The early buyers are not just paying to be trained-upon — they are, collectively, building the dataset that retires the human operator. Whether that takes three years or ten is the single most important open question in home robotics, and nobody honest claims to know the answer.

The rivals who aren’t home yet

NEO’s distinction comes into focus when you look at the rivals it is racing, because the giants everyone expected to arrive first are conspicuously not in the living room.

Tesla’s Optimus is the most-watched of all, and it is the cautionary tale. As Electrek reported, Optimus production was to begin at Fremont in the late summer, but the Gen 3 public unveiling was delayed in the spring with no firm new date, and Elon Musk himself, on the Q4 earnings call, conceded that the several hundred units then deployed were there primarily for learning, not productive tasks — still very much in the R&D phase. Every Optimus production timeline since 2022 has slipped by quarters or years. And critically, Optimus is going to Tesla’s own factories first; there is no consumer pre-order, no price, no ship date for the living room. If you want an Optimus in your home this decade, the honest analysts say, you are waiting until at least 2028.

Figure, the other American front-runner, has made the opposite and more disciplined choice: enterprise first. As industry trackers documented, Figure’s robots logged over 1,250 hours at a BMW plant, handling tens of thousands of parts across the better part of a year — a genuine, verified, commercial deployment. But that is a factory in South Carolina, not a kitchen in Ohio. Figure’s philosophy is to walk before running: prove the machine in the controlled chaos of a plant before unleashing it into the uncontrolled chaos of a home. It is the prudent path, and it means Figure, too, is not yet Rosie.

Which leaves 1X alone, for now, as the company willing to send a humanoid into the actual home in 2026 — and willing to solve the autonomy gap with a human stranger rather than wait for the AI to close it. That willingness is either brave or reckless, depending on your tolerance for the stranger behind the eyes, and reasonable people will land in different places. What is not in dispute is that it is first. The living room, defended for sixty years, has been breached — by a soft, quiet, knit-suited machine with a person, sometimes, inside it.

What Rosie actually costs

From the balcony, the arrival of the home humanoid asks a question the cartoon never had to.

Rosie the Jetsons robot was a fantasy of pure autonomy — a machine that simply knew how to run a household, with no visible strings and no hidden operator. What is arriving in 2026 is something the 1962 imagination could not conceive because it predated the network: a machine whose intelligence is partly local and partly piped in from a distant human, a robot that is autonomous when it can be and teleoperated when it can’t, with the seam between the two invisible to the family in the room. The cartoon’s Rosie raised questions about a future of leisure — what would we do with all the time the robots gave us? The real Rosie raises a question about a future of surveillance: who is watching through the robot’s eyes, when, and what do they see?

A Canadian, heir to a particular wariness about the entry of powerful institutions into private life, feels the weight of this immediately. We have spent the better part of two decades learning, slowly and painfully, what it means to let the network into our homes — the smart speaker that listens, the doorbell camera that watches, the phone that knows where we sleep. The home humanoid is that bargain raised to a new power: not a device in the corner of the room, but a mobile, dexterous, human-shaped presence that can walk into any room, open any drawer, and — in its current generation — be inhabited, at will, by a person employed by the company that made it. The privacy features are real and the company is candid, and both of those things are genuinely to 1X’s credit. But the trajectory of every networked home technology suggests that the convenience will win and the wariness will fade, and that within a few years a stranger’s occasional view into the living room will feel as normal as a doorbell camera does now.

I do not say this to condemn the machine. This series is pledged to wonder, and there is real wonder here — a soft, gentle robot that opens the door for your guests and learns your household and, within a generation, may genuinely free the elderly and the disabled from tasks that currently cost them their independence. That is not a small thing; it may be a magnificent one. The founder’s stated hope — to help people with genuine needs, elderly parents, people with disabilities — is the best possible case for the technology, and it is worth taking seriously rather than dismissing. But the honest postcard reports the whole machine, and the whole machine, in 2026, is a wonder with a stranger inside it, learning your home one guided task at a time until the day it no longer needs the guide.

Rosie is coming. She is softer than the cartoon promised, and quieter, and kinder in her design. And behind her friendly eyes, for now, is a person you have never met, folding your laundry from far away, teaching the machine to one day do it alone. The future the Jetsons drew has arrived, and it turns out it was never really about the robot. It was always about who stands behind it.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one let itself in and offered to help.
