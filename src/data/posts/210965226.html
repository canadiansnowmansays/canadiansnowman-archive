There is a particular genius to the way modern administrations handle a departure. Once, a resignation was a letter — terse, a little bitter, leaked to a friendly reporter within the hour. Now it is a Truth Social post, warm as a hallmark card, followed — one imagines, in the filing cabinets of the West Wing’s least visited office — by paperwork. Real paperwork. The kind with checkboxes. Because even a Court needs an HR department, and even an HR department needs a form for when the King’s favourite herald decides, quite suddenly and for entirely personal reasons, to leave the podium three months before the people vote.

What follows is that form, recovered — in spirit, if not in fact — from Box 402 of the Executive Office of the President’s records retention schedule.

There is something almost tender about the paperwork of power — the way an administration that treats the truth as a negotiable instrument still insists, at the exit door, on the fiction of ordinary process. A form. A checkbox. A section for benefits that do not exist. It is the bureaucratic equivalent of a marching band playing “Auld Lang Syne” for someone being walked out the side entrance: the tune is sincere even when the occasion is not.

None of which is to say Karoline Leavitt did not mean what she said about her children. People leave demanding jobs for their families every day, and there is no reason to doubt that a woman who returned to a podium four days after an assassination attempt on her boss might, eighteen months and two newborns later, want her evenings back. That part of the story is plausible, and probably true, and deserves to be taken at face value.

But administrations rarely let a true thing stand alone when a useful thing can be draped over it. A press secretary stepping down eleven weeks before the midterms, immediately reclassified as a “top outside advisor” and “an influential voice within the Republican Party,” is not simply a mother going home. It is a redeployment — the podium traded for the trail, the daily briefing traded for whatever a September and October full of rallies require. The form, if it existed, would have a box for that too. It just wouldn’t be checked.

This is not a new genre of exit. Sean Spicer resigned rather than watch Anthony Scaramucci get the title of Communications Director over him, and the White House called it his choice. Sarah Huckabee Sanders left to run for governor of Arkansas, and the White House called that a natural next step rather than a seat being kept warm for her. Every administration writes its departures as personal growth, never as strategy, and every departure happens to align, with uncanny consistency, with whatever the political calendar requires that season. What distinguishes this exit is only the tightness of the frame: a newborn, a maternity leave barely finished, a mother’s plain and sympathetic language, arriving at the exact moment a party needs its most disciplined communicator freed up for the trail. The sentiment and the strategy are not mutually exclusive. They rarely are. That is precisely what makes the genre so durable, and so worth a form of its own.
