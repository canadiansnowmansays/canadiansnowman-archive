The last time this series checked in, the maze had gone dark. A campaign-finance blackout had begun on 15 October, and the big money of 2026 was moving through channels nobody would be able to see until after the vote. Elon Musk had just sent $158 million to the main super PAC supporting Senate Republicans. The machine, as this series has argued since Part I, always finds a way through the maze.

On 3 November it found its way through. It just didn’t win.

The high scores

The figures are already familiar, and they are enormous.

Musk spent close to half a billion dollars on the 2026 midterms, more than any donor in a single election cycle in American history. Almost $200 million of it went into Texas, most of it to help Ken Paxton win a Senate seat. Trump’s own super PACs spent at least $374 million, with another $10.5 million routed through a Trump-aligned nonprofit, as Theodore Schleifer reported in The New York Times. Trump himself had predicted that he would spend more than $400 million by election day. The crypto industry’s main super PAC, Fairshake, spent about $46 million in Ohio alone to defeat Sherrod Brown, a tactic that had worked against him in 2024. In Georgia, the Republican nominee for governor, a billionaire named Rick Jackson, put more than $100 million of his own money into his campaign. Total midterm ad spending passed $11 billion.

Republicans outspent Democrats across the Senate battlegrounds. They lost nearly all of them.

Some of the money was badly spent as well as wasted. Trump had built a war chest of about $400 million that he called, according to The New York Times, “my money,” and he held off approving the spending plan until the end of the summer. By then airtime cost far more. One week in October, on a single Anchorage station, his super PAC paid $30,000 for an evening-news slot that the leading Democratic super PAC had booked for $2,700. In Texas alone, backing Paxton forced Republicans to spend close to $300 million.

The results

Paxton lost Texas by about 12 points. Brown beat the Fairshake money in Ohio and returned to the Senate two years after it had pushed him out. Jackson lost Georgia to Keisha Lance Bottoms, who becomes the first Black woman elected governor of any state. In Michigan, Dr. Abdul El-Sayed was outspent in his primary by pro-Israel super PACs, and outspent again in a general election that cost $278 million on advertising alone, the most expensive race in the state’s history. He won both.

In New Jersey, Representative Tom Kean Jr. and his Republican allies spent about $4 million more on ads than his challenger, a first-time candidate and former Navy helicopter pilot named Rebecca Bennett. She led by double digits within two hours of the polls closing, and Kean called her to concede. In Nebraska, gambling companies including DraftKings and FanDuel put more than $20 million behind two ballot measures to legalise online sports betting. Their opponents raised just over $1 million. The measures failed.

The most expensive House race in the country, Michigan’s Seventh District, cost $66.1 million, and Republicans outspent Democrats there too. William Lawrence, a progressive Democrat and climate activist who helped found the Sunrise Movement, ousted the Republican incumbent, Tom Barrett.

In Texas the same arithmetic ran down the ballot. Mayes Middleton, a Republican who spent millions of his own fortune branding himself “MAGA Mayes,” outspent the Democrat Nathan Johnson by more than six to one in the race for attorney general. Johnson won.

The national party that spent more has now lost two national elections in a row. In 2024 Kamala Harris had the money and Trump had the votes. In 2026 the positions reversed, and the result reversed with them.

Elon Land

The best illustration of the money’s limits came from Bastrop, Texas, where Musk’s Boring Company, SpaceX and Starlink sit close enough together that locals call the area “Elon Land.” On election eve, Matt Viser and Nick Miroff of The Atlantic spent several hours talking to more than a dozen people whose livelihoods depend on Musk. They could not find one who was voting for the candidate he had spent nearly $200 million to support.

One of them, a SpaceX project manager and a conservative, had paid $100 for three-quarters of a tank of gas that morning. No advertising budget could compete with that receipt.

Musk’s own verdict came in the early hours of 4 November, in a post on X that The New York Times reported: “Data centers in space looking pretty appealing right now.” He added a joke about his election-interfering space lasers not working this time.

By the following morning he appeared to have quietly deleted the post he had made on election day, urging his followers to “Vote red today!”

Charlie Warzel did the arithmetic in The Atlantic. Musk’s $474 million made him the largest non-candidate donor in any election cycle ever, and accounted for about a quarter of all the campaign money Republicans received in 2026. More than $168 million went to super PACs backing Paxton, and $216 million to his own America PAC. Wired called the operation a “shadow Republican National Committee.” It cost Musk about one-twentieth of 1 per cent of his net worth, the equivalent of someone worth $1 million giving $500. Talarico made Musk a centrepiece of his campaign, and in his victory speech he addressed “every billionaire megadonor, every trillionaire megadonor” directly: “We will not be conquered.”

The Times’ campaign-finance reporter Theodore Schleifer put the broader result in one word. The Republican advantage, he wrote, went “poof.” According to the ad-tracking firm AdImpact, Republicans spent about $250 million more than Democrats on general-election Senate advertising, outspending them in almost every competitive race. In Texas they doubled the Democrats’ spending. Rick Jackson outspent Keisha Lance Bottoms in Georgia by nearly four to one. Democrats won almost all of those races anyway. Lynn Vavreck, a political scientist who has studied the effects of advertising for years, explained why. In a year with middling conditions, she said, campaigning can make the difference. In an economy like this one, “you’re not going to advertise your way out of that.”

Why the machine lost

This series has spent a year arguing that money in American politics works less like persuasion than like plumbing. It buys access, it shapes who runs, it decides which races are contested at all. None of that stopped working in 2026. The money still chose the candidates. Trump’s super PAC had put $10 million behind Paxton, and Fairshake’s spending against Brown in 2024 had helped decide who held that seat for two years.

What money could not do was change the price of diesel. It could not explain away a war in Iran or tariffs that showed up at the grocery checkout. Michael Scherer and Ashley Parker reported in The Atlantic that Democratic strategists spent the year turning corruption into a cost-of-living argument: things are expensive, they told their ad makers, because the system is rigged. In an election where voters already believed that, half a billion dollars from the world’s richest man was not a counterargument. It was evidence.

The next level

None of this means the machine has been switched off. The maze resets every cycle, as Part XII put it, and it will reset for 2028.

But the Republicans now face a problem the machine cannot easily solve. Democrats continue to dominate in small-dollar online donations. The one Republican who has energised the party’s small donors for a decade is Trump, and he will not be on the ballot again. The party’s money will increasingly come from a handful of very large donors. One of them has just learned how much half a billion dollars buys, and is publicly musing about space.

He was not the only tech donor to lose. Matteo Wong reported in The Atlantic that crypto and AI super PACs spent some $250 million on the federal elections, and that many of the candidates they spent the most on lost. More than 30 local measures restricting data centres passed, in red and blue counties alike. The one place the money won was the measure that targeted wealth itself. Tech investors gave more than $300 million to defeat California’s Proposition 40, a one-time tax on billionaires, and it appeared headed for defeat. When the ballot question was about their own fortunes, the money still worked.

Anne Applebaum, in election-night commentary for The Atlantic, put the problem in terms this series would sign: “No other democracy spends billions of dollars on election campaigns, or gives so many opportunities for the wealthiest citizens to try to shape the result.” The Democrats, she said, now have a chance to redress that imbalance. They also have donors of their own.

Reid Hoffman, the LinkedIn co-founder whom Trump had once suggested investigating, marked election night with three emojis: a pair of eyes, an American flag and a red heart. He is a Democratic donor, and a very large one. The machine has players on both sides of the maze. In 2026, for once, the votes counted for more than the coins.

Voters in two states went further than any candidate. Montana approved a ballot measure barring corporations, nonprofits and unions that make political contributions from doing business in the state, and Michigan barred utilities and many government contractors from political giving, as The New York Times reported. Both are likely to end up as tests of Citizens United, the decision that built the maze in the first place.

Even the biggest player looked ready to make peace. Musk had spent more than $200 million trying to defeat James Talarico. Two days after the election, a clip circulated of the senator-elect calling himself “a big fan” of SpaceX and Tesla and Musk “a great business leader and entrepreneur and innovator.” Musk replied, as The New York Times reported: “Nice of him to say that.”
