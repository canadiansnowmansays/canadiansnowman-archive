British colonial administrators with Indian staff during the Raj. A small cadre of British officers governed a subcontinent of 300 million through a vast apparatus of local cooperation and control

The British Raj was not a government in the way Canadians or Americans understand the term. It was an administration — a machinery of control designed to extract wealth, maintain order, and perpetuate the fiction that foreign rule over 300 million people was not merely tolerable but beneficial. It lasted 89 years, from the Crown’s assumption of direct control in 1858 to independence in 1947. It built railways and famines with equal efficiency. It produced both the infrastructure of a modern state and the conditions for the most catastrophic partition in human history.

And it did it all while sincerely believing it was doing India a favour.

I. The Architecture of Control

The Raj governed India through a structure that was elegant in its design and devastating in its consequences. At the top sat the Viceroy, appointed by London, answerable to the Secretary of State for India in the British Cabinet. Below him, the Indian Civil Service — the “steel frame,” as it was called — administered the provinces through a cadre of British officers who were recruited, trained, and promoted entirely within a system designed to ensure that India was governed by men who understood Britain’s interests and India’s customs in roughly that order of priority.

The ICS was small. At its peak, approximately 1,000 British officers governed a subcontinent of 300 million people. They could do this because the Raj did not govern India directly so much as it governed India’s governors. Below the ICS sat a vast apparatus of Indian subordinates — clerks, translators, police officers, magistrates, tax collectors — who did the actual work of administration. The system required Indian cooperation at every level, and it got it, partly through patronage, partly through the limited opportunities it offered to educated Indians, and partly through the simple reality that resistance was punished and compliance was rewarded.

Alongside the directly governed “British India,” more than 500 princely states maintained nominal independence under British “paramountcy.” Their rulers — maharajas, nawabs, nizams — retained their palaces, their titles, and their local authority in exchange for ceding foreign policy, defense, and communications to the Crown and accepting the “advice” of a British Resident whose advice had the force of a directive. The princely states accounted for roughly 40 percent of India’s territory and 23 percent of its population. They were a patchwork of autocracies, some benevolent, some grotesque, all dependent on British approval for their survival.

This dual system — direct rule and indirect rule operating simultaneously across the same subcontinent — created the administrative and political fragmentation that would make independence and partition so catastrophically complicated. When the British left in 1947, those 500-plus princely states had to be individually persuaded, coerced, or invaded into joining one of the two new nations. Several very nearly didn’t. Kashmir still hasn’t resolved the question.

II. The Railways

Colonial-era Indian steam locomotive. Built to extract resources for British ports, the railways inadvertently became the arteries of national mobilization and the independence movement.

No instrument of British rule is more double-edged than the Indian railway system. The Raj built the largest railway network in Asia — over 40,000 miles of track by the time of independence — and it remains, in the popular imagination, the one unambiguously good thing the British did for India. This is approximately half true.

The railways were built to serve British interests. The routes connected the interior to the ports — cotton-growing regions to Bombay, jute-producing regions to Calcutta, tea plantations to export terminals. They were not designed to connect Indians to each other. The gauge was deliberately non-standard in places to prevent the network from being used against British military interests. The financing was structured through a “guaranteed return” system in which British investors were promised a five percent annual return underwritten by Indian tax revenues — meaning Indian taxpayers bore the risk and British shareholders collected the profit, regardless of whether the railways were commercially viable.

And yet the railways changed India in ways the British did not intend and could not control. They enabled the movement of people across caste and regional boundaries. They created a national labour market. They facilitated the circulation of newspapers, political pamphlets, and eventually, the organizers of the independence movement. Gandhi’s political campaigns were conducted by railway. The Indian National Congress held its annual meetings in different cities each year, and delegates arrived by train. The infrastructure of colonial extraction became the infrastructure of national mobilization.

This is a pattern the Raj repeated endlessly: build something for the purpose of control, and watch it become the instrument of resistance. The English language. The legal system. The parliamentary model. The free press. The Raj planted all of them, intending to strengthen its own foundations, and every one of them was eventually turned against it.

III. The Famines

Victims of the Bengal Famine of 1943. Between two and three million died as British policies compounded natural disaster with wartime priorities and indifference.

There is no honest account of the British Raj that does not dwell on the famines. Not because famine was unique to the colonial period — India had experienced drought and food shortage throughout its history — but because the Raj transformed natural scarcity into manufactured catastrophe through a combination of economic policy, ideological commitment, and administrative indifference.

The Great Famine of 1876–1878 killed an estimated 5.5 million people in southern and southwestern India. The monsoon failed. Crops died. But the British response, overseen by Viceroy Lord Lytton, was governed by a commitment to free-market principles that bordered on theology. Lytton refused to interfere with grain markets, believing that government intervention would distort prices and create dependency. Grain was exported from famine regions while people starved. Relief camps were established, but the rations provided were scientifically calculated to be below subsistence level — less, as historians have noted, than the caloric intake at Buchenwald. The theory was that generous relief would discourage self-reliance. The practice was mass death.

The famines continued. 1896–1897. 1899–1900. 1943.

The Bengal Famine of 1943 is the one the British would most like to forget and the one India will never allow them to. Between two and three million people died in Bengal while British India was fighting the Second World War. The causes were multiple — a cyclone, a fungal blight on rice, wartime disruption of rice imports from Japanese-occupied Burma. But the British response compounded every one of them. Winston Churchill’s War Cabinet diverted food supplies to military stockpiles and European reserves. Churchill himself, when informed of the scale of the famine, responded with comments about Indians “breeding like rabbits” and asked why Gandhi hadn’t died yet. The vulgarity was not incidental. It was diagnostic. It revealed what the Raj’s civilizing mission amounted to when the cost of maintaining it fell upon Indian bodies.

The 1943 famine occurred within living memory. There are people alive today who survived it. There are families in Bengal who count their dead from it. When Indians express anger about the Raj, they are not relitigating ancient history. They are remembering their grandparents.

IV. The Census and the Categories

British colonial illustrations categorizing Indians by caste and community. Administrative counting hardened fluid identities into rigid political divisions.

The Raj did something to India that was arguably more consequential than any famine or any railway: it counted people, and it sorted them.

The British census of India, conducted every ten years beginning in 1871, required every Indian to be classified by religion, caste, language, and occupation. This sounds administrative. It was political. Before the census, caste identity was fluid, regional, and contextual — a person’s caste position could vary by village, by occupation, by local custom. The census froze it. It turned caste into a bureaucratic category with legal and political implications. It made religion a countable, comparable quantity — so many Hindus, so many Muslims, so many Sikhs — and in doing so, it created political constituencies where none had existed in that form before.

The census made Hindus and Muslims legible to the colonial state as separate populations with separate interests. The British then governed accordingly. The principle of “divide and rule” is often attributed to the British as a cynical strategy, and it was — but it was also an administrative logic. A colonial government that could not hope to satisfy 300 million people could hope to manage them by playing their communities against each other. Separate electorates for Muslims, introduced by the Morley-Minto reforms of 1909, ensured that Muslim voters elected Muslim representatives and Hindu voters elected Hindu representatives. The system guaranteed that political identity in India would be organized along religious lines. The consequences are still with us. They are in the BJP’s voter rolls. They are in the rubble of the Babri Masjid. They are in Kashmir.

I pause on this because it connects to something I’ve observed in my own kitchen. When Rikesh and Gurneet describe themselves, they reach for language, region, and religion — Gujarati, Punjabi, Sikh, Hindu — as naturally as a Canadian might say “from Ontario” or “from the Maritimes.” These identities are real and organic. But the political weight they carry — the way they determine parliamentary representation, government jobs, university admissions, and the architecture of political mobilization — is not ancient. It is colonial. The British didn’t invent India’s diversity. They weaponized it.

V. The Racial Architecture

British colonial life in India. The racial hierarchy was embedded in everyday routines, clubs, civil service, and economic structures.

Beneath the administrative machinery of the Raj lay an assumption so pervasive that it rarely needed to be stated: the British were superior. Not merely more powerful — superior. Racially, culturally, morally, intellectually superior. And the governance of India was organized around this assumption at every level.

The clubs of colonial India — the Bombay Gymkhana, the Bengal Club, the Tollygunge in Calcutta — were restricted to Europeans. “Dogs and Indians not allowed” is the famous sign, and while historians debate whether the exact wording was posted, the policy was real. Indian officers in the colonial civil service — no matter how educated, how capable, how loyal to the Crown — could not rise above a certain rank. Indian barristers trained at the Inns of Court in London returned home to a legal system that treated their testimony as inherently less reliable than that of a British witness. Indian soldiers who fought for the Empire in two world wars — and they did, in enormous numbers, over a million in the First World War alone — returned to a country that denied them the rights they had fought to defend for others.

The racial hierarchy had economic consequences that compounded the extraction system. British firms dominated Indian industry, banking, and trade. Indian entrepreneurs operated in a regulatory environment designed to ensure that they could never compete on equal terms with British companies. The managing agency system — in which British-controlled firms managed Indian companies for a fee that amounted to a permanent levy on Indian enterprise — persisted until independence.

My great-uncle Hugh Keenleyside understood the architecture of empire from the inside. As a Canadian diplomat who helped build the postwar international order — from the United Nations Technical Assistance Board to the frameworks for decolonization — he saw clearly what the Raj represented: not merely the governance of one country by another, but the organization of an entire civilization’s resources for the benefit of the colonizer, justified by a racial ideology that would not survive scrutiny but that did not need to, because it was backed by power. The institutions Keenleyside worked to build were, in part, an attempt to create a world in which the Raj’s logic could never again operate unchallenged.

VI. The Emergence of Nationalism

The Raj, for all its sophistication, contained a contradiction it could never resolve: it governed India using the language, the legal principles, and the political philosophy of the liberal democratic tradition while denying Indians the rights that tradition promised.

The Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, began as a polite debating society of English-educated lawyers who wanted a larger role within the colonial system, not the overthrow of it. Its early petitions to the British government read like memos from a loyal branch office requesting a meeting with head office. But the logic of the movement was inexorable. If all men are created equal — if parliamentary government is superior to despotism — if the rule of law protects individual rights — then on what principle could 300 million Indians be denied the governance that 40 million Britons enjoyed?

The British answer was race, though they preferred not to say so directly. The Indian answer, increasingly, was that the British were hypocrites — and that the liberal principles they preached could be turned against the empire that preached them.

The emergence of the Muslim League in 1906 — founded one year after the Bengal partition — added a second axis to Indian nationalism. The League represented Muslim political interests within the colonial framework, and its relationship with the Congress was alternately cooperative and adversarial. The tension between a unified Indian nationalism and a separate Muslim political identity would shadow the independence movement for forty years and ultimately determine its outcome. When the Congress claimed to speak for all Indians, the League asked: all Indians, or all Hindus? The question was never satisfactorily answered. In 1947, it was answered by Partition.

The partition of Bengal in 1905 — a British administrative decision that divided the province along religious lines, creating a Muslim-majority East Bengal and a Hindu-majority West Bengal — galvanized nationalist sentiment in a way that decades of polite petitioning had not. The Swadeshi movement that followed — boycotts of British goods, promotion of Indian manufactures, mass political mobilization — was the first taste of what was coming. The economic argument merged with the political one: India could not be free while it was economically dependent on Britain, and it could not be economically independent while it was politically subjugated. The two struggles were one.

World War I accelerated everything. India contributed over a million soldiers and £100 million to the British war effort, on the implicit understanding that loyalty would be rewarded with political reform. The reward came in the form of the Montagu-Chelmsford reforms of 1919 — a set of limited legislative concessions so inadequate to Indian expectations that they produced not gratitude but fury. The Rowlatt Acts, passed the same year, extended wartime emergency powers into peacetime, allowing detention without trial and suspension of civil liberties. The message was unmistakable: India would serve the Empire but not share in it.

And then, in 1915, a lawyer returned from South Africa, where he had spent twenty years developing a technique of political resistance that would change the world. He wore Indian cloth. He spoke in terms the masses could understand. He did not ask the British for concessions. He told them they had no right to be there.

Mahatma Gandhi at his spinning wheel. The contradictions of liberal empire helped spark the nationalist resistance that would ultimately end British rule.

His name was Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi, and he is the subject of our next essay. His legacy is more complicated than the West believes, more contested than India admits, and more relevant than either side is comfortable acknowledging.
