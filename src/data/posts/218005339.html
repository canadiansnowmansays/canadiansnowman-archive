On the evening of 26 July 1956, Gamal Abdel Nasser gave a long speech to a huge crowd in Manshiyya Square in Alexandria. Partway through, he mentioned the name Ferdinand de Lesseps, the French diplomat who had built the Suez Canal. It was a code word. As he spoke it, Egyptian officials and troops moved into the offices of the Suez Canal Company in Ismailia, Port Said and Cairo and took them over. Nasser then told the crowd what he had done. The canal had been nationalised, and its revenues would pay for the Aswan High Dam, which the United States and Britain had just refused to finance.

The canal was the most important waterway in the British world. Two-thirds of Western Europe’s oil passed through it. Britain’s government had owned the largest single block of shares in the Company since Benjamin Disraeli bought them from a bankrupt Egyptian ruler in 1875, and British troops had occupied Egypt from 1882 until the last of them left the Canal Zone, under a 1954 agreement, only six weeks before Nasser’s speech.

Nasser offered to compensate the shareholders at market price and promised to keep the canal open to all shipping. The nationalisation was legal under Egyptian law, and the canal lay entirely inside Egypt. The British Prime Minister, Sir Anthony Eden, decided within days that Nasser had to go.

The last performance of the routine

Everything that follows would have been familiar to Palmerston. What makes it the right place to end the British section is that it was done, in 1956, with all the old steps in the old order, by a government that genuinely seemed not to understand that the stage had changed.

Eden, who had resigned as Foreign Secretary in 1938 over Chamberlain’s appeasement of Mussolini, saw Nasser through that lens. He compared him to Mussolini and to Hitler in public and private, and argued that a dictator who seized an international waterway today would be taking more tomorrow. The comparison did a great deal of work. It turned a dispute about ownership of a canal company into a question of standing up to aggression, and it made any negotiated settlement sound like Munich.

Britain and France, the Company’s two main owners, began military planning almost at once. Through August, September and October there were conferences in London, a mission to Cairo led by Australia’s Prime Minister Robert Menzies, a proposal for a users’ association, and a debate at the UN Security Council that produced six agreed principles for running the canal. The diplomacy was real, but it was run in parallel with preparation for a war whose purpose was to retake the canal and bring Nasser down.

The problem was the pretext. Britain and France needed a reason to land troops in Egypt that would not look like two old empires reclaiming a lost asset.

They found one in a villa at Sèvres, outside Paris, between 22 and 24 October 1956. There, British, French and Israeli officials agreed a secret plan. Israel, which had its own grievances against Egypt over cross-border raids and the closure of the Straits of Tiran, would invade the Sinai. Britain and France would then issue an ultimatum to both sides to stop fighting and withdraw ten miles from the canal, and to accept Anglo-French occupation of the canal zone to “separate the combatants.” Egypt, whose own territory was being invaded, would obviously refuse. Britain and France would then intervene as peacekeepers.

It went almost exactly to plan. Israel attacked on 29 October. The ultimatum was delivered on 30 October; Israeli forces were at that point nowhere near the canal, so the demand that they withdraw ten miles from it required them to advance. Egypt refused. On 31 October, British and French aircraft began bombing Egyptian airfields. On 5 and 6 November, British and French paratroopers and seaborne forces landed at Port Said and Port Fuad at the canal’s northern end and began fighting their way south.

What had changed

The military operation was, by most professional accounts, competently run. It did not matter. Within a week, Britain and France were stopped, not by Egypt’s army but by everyone else.

The United States. Eisenhower had not been told, was in the last week of his re-election campaign, and was furious. He saw an Anglo-French attack on a Third World country as a gift to Moscow at the exact moment the Soviet Union was being condemned for crushing the uprising in Hungary. The Americans brought a ceasefire resolution to the Security Council on 30 October, and Britain and France vetoed it. They were the first vetoes Britain had ever cast.

The United Nations. The vetoes did not end the matter. Under the “Uniting for Peace” procedure, created by the General Assembly in 1950 to get around a deadlocked Security Council, the question moved to an emergency session of the Assembly. On 2 November it voted sixty-four to five for an immediate ceasefire. Canada’s Secretary of State for External Affairs, Lester Pearson, then proposed a way for Britain and France to stop without surrendering: an international force to keep the peace while they withdrew. The Assembly adopted Pearson’s resolution in the early hours of 4 November, and the next day it established the United Nations Emergency Force, the first UN peacekeeping force of its kind, under a Canadian commander, General E. L. M. Burns. Pearson won the Nobel Peace Prize for it the following year.

The Soviet Union. On 5 November, the Soviet Premier, Nikolai Bulganin, sent letters to London and Paris asking how they would feel if they were attacked by a stronger power with modern weapons, and hinting at rockets. The threat was mostly bluster. It was also a reminder that Britain was no longer the strongest power in any room it entered.

Money. This was what actually ended it. Sterling came under heavy selling as the operation began, and Britain’s reserves fell fast. The Chancellor of the Exchequer, Harold Macmillan, who had been one of the war’s keenest supporters, discovered that the United States would not support a loan from the International Monetary Fund, or help with emergency oil supplies now that the canal was blocked, until Britain accepted a ceasefire. Nasser had sunk dozens of ships in the canal. The pipeline from Iraq through Syria had been sabotaged. Britain could not pay for its oil, could not borrow, and could not defend its currency.

At midnight on 6 November, with British troops about a quarter of the way down the canal, Britain agreed to a ceasefire. France, furious, had no choice but to follow. The last British and French troops left Port Said on 22 December 1956. UN salvage crews cleared the canal, which reopened in April 1957 under Egyptian control. It has stayed there ever since.

The cover story, and the cover-up

The final element of the old routine was the lie, and it too was performed in full. On 20 December 1956, Eden told the House of Commons that there had been no foreknowledge that Israel would attack Egypt. There had been, of course, a signed protocol. Eden resigned on 9 January 1957, officially on grounds of ill health. The truth about Sèvres came out in pieces over the following decades, and the British copy of the protocol, which Eden had ordered destroyed, is thought never to have survived. The Israeli copy did.

A Canadian postscript

Canada comes out of Suez better than out of any other chapter in the British section, and that is worth saying plainly after the Boer War chapter. Canada refused to support the invasion, and its Prime Minister, Louis St. Laurent, told the House of Commons in November 1956 that the era when, as he put it, the supermen of Europe could govern the whole world was coming to an end. The line caused an uproar among English Canadians who felt Canada had betrayed Britain, and it cost the Liberals votes in the 1957 election they lost to John Diefenbaker.

There is a smaller, stranger postscript too. When Canada offered troops to Pearson’s new force, Nasser objected to one battalion, the Queen’s Own Rifles of Canada: their name, and the Red Ensign with its Union Jack in the corner, looked too British to an Egypt that had just been invaded by Britain. Canadian logistics and signals units went instead. Pearson never forgot it, and it was one of the reasons that, as Prime Minister, he pushed Canada into adopting a flag of its own. The Maple Leaf, raised for the first time on 15 February 1965, has a little Suez in it.

Scoring Suez against the rubric

Overwhelming asymmetry. Britain and France against Egypt, alone, would have been no contest. But this is the point on which the whole episode turned. The asymmetry that mattered in 1956 was not between Britain and Egypt. It was between Britain and the United States. Satisfied locally; broken globally.

Coercion as first resort. Military planning began within days of the nationalisation, alongside diplomacy that was never given a real chance to work, and ended in a staged invasion. Satisfied.

Extraction is the point. Control of the canal and the removal of the government that had taken it, dressed as the separation of combatants. Satisfied.

Self-justifying rhetoric. Standing up to aggression, the lessons of Munich, keeping the peace between Israel and Egypt. And when that failed, a straightforward lie to Parliament. Satisfied.

Resistance gets punished. This was the plan. Nasser was to be made an example of, as Carthage and Canton and the Old Summer Palace had been. Instead he emerged the most popular leader in the Arab world, and the canal stayed in Egyptian hands. Attempted, and failed.

Five for five in intent. Zero for one in result.

What Suez tells us

The Introduction promised that the British chapters would end with the clearest demonstration in this series that the pattern can simply stop working. It is worth being precise about why it stopped, because the rest of the series depends on the answer.

It did not stop because Britain became kinder. Eden’s government ran every step of the routine as faithfully as Palmerston’s had. It stopped because three conditions that had always protected the bully had quietly disappeared. There was now a stronger power, the United States, that did not approve and could make its disapproval felt. There was now a standing international forum, the UN, where the target could appeal, where the rest of the world could vote, and which a middle power like Canada could use to build a way out. And Britain’s economy was no longer strong enough to pay for a war the markets did not believe in.

Remove those three conditions, and the playbook works again. Keep them in place, and even an old empire running every step correctly cannot make it work.

That is the question the second half of this series will keep asking. Part VII goes back to the late nineteenth century, when none of those three conditions existed, and several great powers bullied a whole continent at once. The American chapters then trace what happened when the power that stopped Britain at Suez found itself in Britain’s old position. And Part XIII will have to ask what happens when the strongest power in the world is the one running the routine, and it is the United States that stands at the UN rostrum threatening to annihilate a smaller country, as it did on 22 September 2026, rather than the one telling others to stop.
