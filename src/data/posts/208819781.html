Part IX of this series asked why the ground gave out. This one asks what it cost the people who had built their lives on top of it — a winemaker ending his own succession rather than pass down a burning inheritance, a capital city discovering it was never as insulated as it believed, and a fire that came within six miles of a plant that fuels nuclear missiles without anyone quite agreeing on whether that should have been the headline.

Physics does not care what sits on top of the ground it burns. A pine forest, a Bordeaux appellation four centuries old, a suburb of Madrid, a facility that packs rocket fuel into submarine-launched warheads — to the fire, these are all just fuel and obstacle, arranged differently. But the people living among them do not experience the fire as physics. They experience it as a specific, personal accounting of what they are about to lose, and this week produced an unusually rich set of those accountings.

The last harvest

Near Fronsac, on the banks of the Dordogne, Gabriel Barre has been making wine at Château la Grave since he took it over from his father in 2016. This year’s harvest — smoke drifting in from the fires 15 miles away, on top of a summer of record heat that is already pushing his grapes’ sugar content past what a classic Bordeaux profile can absorb — will be his last. He is not stopping because of the smoke alone. He is stopping because the smoke is simply the newest entry on a ledger that already included tariffs, falling wine sales among younger drinkers who find Bordeaux’s “luxury and snobbery” reputation off-putting, and years of debt. He does not want to hand his son, Arsène, a business built to fail. “This year, it feels like we’re on another planet,” he said. “When we talk about it on the phone with our friends, we don’t want our kids to hear because it scares them.”

A few miles away, at the four-centuries-old Château Smith Haut Lafitte, the calculus is different but the unease is the same. Managing director Ludovic Fradin isn’t especially worried about the smoke itself — Bordeaux’s flat terrain lets it disperse within a day or two, unlike Napa Valley’s mountain-ringed vineyards, where smoke can linger over the grapes for weeks. What worries him is a scenario that used to be unthinkable: the chateau has drawn up plans to evacuate its horses, goats, chickens, and llamas if the fire keeps closing the distance. “The animals are living, breathing beings, unlike the vines,” he said. “The vines are just material.” It is a strange thing to hear a winemaker say about grapes, in a region built entirely on the mythology that its grapes are much more than material — but it tells you where his actual fear has moved to.

Wine writer Jane Anson, who lives in Bordeaux, put the accumulation in terms that apply well beyond the vineyards: “There is a human thing of having one more thing to battle against. Like they needed this on top of everything else.” The village of Le Porge, east of Bordeaux, lost 152 houses to the fire — a number that belongs to no vineyard’s ledger, but sits in the same region, among the same smoke, and among the same rising insurance premiums that will outlast the fire itself by years.

The bubble in Madrid

Madrid has spent the last several years becoming something new: a booming European capital drawing wealthy expats, foreign capital, and global cultural attention, governed by a party that has staked real political capital on taking climate seriously. This week, the fires thirty miles to the west reached far enough into that story to puncture it.

Elena Sánchez, 54, sells beer from a kiosk beside an artificial lake on the capital’s western edge. On Sunday evening she pointed at the sunset — the wrong color, a hazy gray-orange smear from the fires — and said what half the city seemed to be thinking without saying: “It’s scary. I’m afraid it might reach here.” Leopoldo Salvador, 84, walking beneath the statue of Don Quixote in the Plaza España, called it plainly: “This is a wake-up call.” Cristina Herrero, a biology professor, walked the palace gardens with sheepdogs she’d evacuated from a farm outside the city, apologizing to other dogwalkers as her unsettled pack barked at their leashed dogs — “These are evacuated dogs!” Her friend Henar Hidalgo Riol, also walking an evacuated dog, named the feeling underneath all of it: “People know they’re sitting on a powder keg.” Charo Fernández, a lawyer, described smoke rolling toward the city “out of a chimney” — the same chimney-effect mechanism Part IX described in physical terms, arriving in her account as something closer to dread.

None of this is Madrid burning. It is Madrid discovering, thirty miles from a fire it can smell but not yet see burn its own streets, that the insulation it had assumed was thinner than advertised.

Six miles from a missile plant

One detail from this week belongs in this essay precisely because almost no one treated it as the headline it could have been: the Gironde fire came within six miles of ArianeGroup facilities in Saint-Médard-en-Jalles that pack chemical rocket fuel into boosters for France’s submarine-launched nuclear missiles, and a nearby site where retired versions of those same missiles are dismantled and decontaminated. Dassault Aviation, which builds fighter jets and ejector-seat pyrotechnics nearby, relocated sensitive equipment to another site as a precaution. Both companies said the situation was under control, coordinated with the French military, which built firebreaks around what one regional prefect called “a number of sensitive sites.” Nobody involved would confirm whether combustible material had actually been moved out of harm’s way. One evacuee, a social worker who fled the same town, put the plain version of the anxiety everyone else was managing more diplomatically: “It’s presumptuous to say that Saint-Médard won’t burn; it’s thinking you’re stronger than nature.”

Ordinary people, borrowed equipment

Crisis produced its own quieter category of response this week, alongside the official one. Nina Rosazza, 42, has been out with a borrowed trailer, water tank, and motor pump since a fire broke out near her home in Andernos-Les-Bains — joined, eventually, by roughly a hundred neighbors and fifty trucks, tradespeople and shop owners among them, working the smaller fires so professional crews could concentrate on the larger ones. “We’re not heroes, but we’re trying to do our part to put an end to this, because it’s a massacre,” she said. Alexandre Bidau, a construction worker from Pessac, spent Sunday helping firefighters with a borrowed 1,000-liter water tank, saving a friend’s house before it was engulfed — then spoke to a reporter from the hospital where his wife was in labor, worried aloud that his own family might be the next one forced to flee. “It was very sad; it really broke my heart to see all these burning woods,” he said. “But I also feel a sense of satisfaction from helping people save their homes.” This is the version of solidarity that doesn’t make it into a minister’s statement: unpaid, improvised, and running on adrenaline that, as one of the volunteers put it once the fire moved on, eventually wears off and leaves the reality underneath it painful.

The politics of blame

Crisis has produced real solidarity this week — volunteer firefighters with borrowed water tanks, neighbors sheltering evacuees, farmers lending equipment. It has also produced, predictably, an argument about who is responsible. In Spain, Madrid’s conservative regional president, Isabel Díaz Ayuso, requested a national emergency declaration that handed authority to the interior ministry of Spain’s left-leaning prime minister, Pedro Sánchez — a decision Sánchez’s own transportation minister used to score a point, blaming the region’s reliance on national disaster response on Ayuso’s labor disputes and budget cuts. Ayuso, for her part, said she’d leave the point-scoring to others: “The first fool who gets in the way to heat things up, that’s on their conscience.” In France, the far right’s Jordan Bardella and the far left’s Jean-Luc Mélenchon both accused Macron’s government of moving too slowly to deploy the retardant-dropping A400M aircraft — to which a government spokeswoman replied, with more bite than most official statements manage, that adapting a military cargo plane is “a bit longer and more complex than a children’s Lego set.” I will have much more to say about this instinct — the reflex to litigate blame inside an active emergency — when this series reaches the populists later on. For now it is enough to note that the fire does not pause for the argument, and the argument does not appear to be slowing anyone down.

The reroute

One small, almost embarrassing detail belongs here too, because it captures something the death tolls and vineyard ledgers cannot. Sunday’s final stage of the Tour de France — traditionally the sport’s most ceremonial day, a champagne-and-photographs procession down the Champs-Élysées — was shortened and rerouted because police officers assigned to race security were needed instead for wildfire response. The organizers called it an act of “national solidarity.” I believe them. But I also think it’s worth sitting with the image: a country’s oldest sporting ritual, quietly rearranged around a fire, in the same week its president was calling that fire the hardest his country has faced since the Second World War. That is what it looks like when a crisis stops being a discrete emergency and starts reorganizing the ordinary machinery of a country’s summer.

None of the people in this essay lost their homes to a vapor pressure deficit. They lost them, or nearly lost them, or watched their neighbors lose them, to a fire — and the deficit was simply the reason the fire could do what it did. That is the whole relationship between these two essays, and it is worth stating plainly rather than leaving it implied: the mechanism does not care about vineyards or missile plants or Tour de France stages, but it is not an abstraction either. It cashes out, always, in a specific person’s specific loss. Gabriel Barre’s last harvest is what a moisture deficit looks like from inside a wine cellar. Elena Sánchez’s wrong-colored sunset is what it looks like from a park bench. The house was built for winter. This week, across two countries, a great many people found out what their particular room of it was worth.

Degrees

152 — the number of houses destroyed in the single village of Le Porge, east of Bordeaux, a number that belongs to no ledger but the plainest one: homes that existed a week ago and do not exist now.
