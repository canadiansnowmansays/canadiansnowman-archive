There is a version of this story where the Justice Department behaves the way it behaved in Ferguson, in Chicago, in Minneapolis the first time — in 2020, when a different Minneapolis police officer killed a different person and the federal government came in fast, took the case seriously, and eventually prosecuted him in federal court alongside the state. That version does not require imagination. It requires only memory. It happened six years ago, in the same city, under a Justice Department led by people who believed a civil-rights investigation into a law-enforcement killing was a core function of the office rather than an optional one.

This is not that version. This is the version where two more Minneapolis residents die at the hands of federal agents, and the Justice Department’s Civil Rights Division spends the following six months demonstrating, methodically, everything it is capable of not doing.

Two Cases, Two Tracks, Same Silence

Start with what should have happened. Renee Good was killed by an ICE officer on January 7. Alex Pretti was killed by CBP officers seventeen days later. In the ordinary run of things, both deaths trigger a criminal civil-rights investigation by DOJ’s Civil Rights Division — the section of the department built specifically to determine whether a law-enforcement officer, federal or otherwise, violated someone’s constitutional rights in the course of a killing.

In Good’s case, that investigation never opened. The Justice Department closed off the possibility almost immediately, a decision made with a speed that stands in sharp contrast to the department’s usual posture, which tends toward caution and delay rather than swift foreclosure — except, it turns out, when swift foreclosure is the desired outcome. In Pretti’s case, DOJ said it would investigate. Six months later, there is no public evidence that investigation has produced anything, moved toward any conclusion, or generated so much as a preliminary finding that the public has been allowed to see.

Separately, DHS has run its own internal reviews — Homeland Security Investigations look at Good’s case, CBP’s Office of Professional Responsibility conducted an initial review of Pretti’s. Both told reporters these reviews are ongoing. Ongoing is doing a great deal of work in that sentence. It has been true since January. It remains true in July. An investigation that is permanently ongoing, with no interim disclosure and no evident timeline, is functionally indistinguishable from an investigation that isn’t happening at all — except that it lets the department say, when asked, that something is in fact underway.

The Man They Assigned

Reporting from CBS earlier this year surfaced a detail that explains a great deal about how seriously DOJ has actually treated the Pretti matter. The lawyers assigned to the case were not the department’s specialists in law-enforcement civil-rights investigations — the people who, in an earlier era, built the expertise that let the Civil Rights Division take on Ferguson and Chicago and the first Minneapolis case competently. Instead, the assignment went to newcomers to the division, including a lawyer named Robert Keenan.

Keenan is not an anonymous staffer. He made news early in this administration for arguing, in the prosecution of a police officer in the Breonna Taylor case, that the appropriate sentence was a single day in jail. That is not a neutral biographical detail. It tells you what kind of judgment the department wanted brought to bear on a case involving a nurse shot ten times in five seconds by federal agents. You do not staff a case with someone who has already signaled, on the public record, an extraordinarily narrow view of what accountability for a law-enforcement killing should look like — unless the outcome you are hoping for is exactly the kind of outcome that person has already shown he is inclined to produce.

This is worth connecting back to the argument I made in Part II about Todd Blanche’s own theory of the presidency. If the department’s operational leadership genuinely believes that officers carrying out the executive’s enforcement priorities are, in a meaningful sense, exercising protected official authority, then staffing decisions like this one are not an accident or an oversight. They are the theory being implemented one personnel assignment at a time. Nobody needs to issue an explicit directive telling the Civil Rights Division to go easy. You simply hire the people who were already inclined to go easy, and let institutional inertia do the rest.

When the Prosecutors Quit Instead

There is one more detail from this period that belongs in any honest account of who has been doing the investigating, and it did not come from DOJ’s press office — it came out sideways, in reporting almost nobody outside Minnesota noticed. Six federal prosecutors in the state reportedly resigned rather than carry out an instruction from the Justice Department: not to investigate the ICE officer who killed Renee Good, but to investigate her widow.

Sit with that pairing for a moment. The agency that killed an American citizen faced no criminal civil-rights investigation. The dead woman’s husband, grieving and doing nothing but existing in the aftermath of his wife’s death, drew enough departmental interest that career prosecutors in the same U.S. Attorney’s office chose to leave their jobs rather than participate. I do not know, and DOJ has not said, precisely what theory of a case against Good’s widow was being contemplated. What I do know is that when the people closest to the process — career federal prosecutors, not activists, not commentators — found the direction untenable enough to resign over, that tells you something no official statement will. It tells you where the department’s appetite for scrutiny was actually pointed, and it was not pointed at the agent who fired the gun.

A Division That Used to Matter

None of this happened in a vacuum of institutional capacity. The Civil Rights Division’s criminal section has, across administrations of both parties, built up real expertise in exactly this kind of case — officer-involved shootings, in-custody deaths, the specific and difficult work of building a federal civil-rights case against someone wearing a badge. That expertise does not maintain itself. It depends on the department choosing, year over year, to staff the section with people who have done the work before and to give them the latitude to keep doing it seriously.

What we are watching now is what happens when that choice is reversed — not through a public purge, which would draw attention and resistance, but through the quieter mechanism of assignment. You do not need to fire the specialists. You simply route the highest-profile, most politically inconvenient cases to people like Robert Keenan instead, and let the specialists watch from the sidelines while a case that should be a career-defining prosecution becomes, in Blanche’s own words, standard. Multiply that choice across the department, case after case, and in a few years you have hollowed out a division’s competence without ever having to explain why.

The Inversion

I want to return to something Quinta Jurecic, The Atlantic’s staff writer on law and democracy, said when I first started following this story: what we are watching is almost an inversion of the pattern Americans have come to expect. Historically, when state or local law enforcement is reluctant to hold one of its own accountable, the federal government is the institution that steps in. That was the shape of Ferguson. That was the shape of the first Minneapolis case, the one involving Derek Chauvin, where federal and state prosecutions ran in parallel and the federal government was, if anything, the more aggressive partner.

Here, the roles have reversed entirely. It is Minnesota — the state, and specifically Hennepin County — pushing to investigate, to charge, to hold agents accountable. It is the federal government standing in the way: withholding evidence, declining to open the investigations it would normally lead, and leaving a state prosecutor’s office to do, with a fraction of the resources and none of the subpoena power, work the Civil Rights Division was built to do and has simply declined to do.

This inversion is not a natural drift. It required active choices — the decision to close the Good investigation immediately, the decision to staff Pretti’s case with lawyers whose prior record points one direction, the decision, which the next part of this series will examine in detail, to withhold physical evidence from the state investigators trying to do the job DOJ has abandoned. Each of these choices is individually defensible in the bloodless language of prosecutorial discretion. Collectively, they describe an institution that decided, at the outset, what outcome it wanted and then declined to gather the evidence that might have complicated it.

What “Standard” Means

Recall Blanche’s own description of the Pretti investigation, delivered at a press conference: he did not want the takeaway to be that a massive civil-rights investigation was underway. This, he said, was a standard investigation by the FBI. I want to sit with that word — standard — because it is doing exactly the opposite of what it appears to be doing.

A standard FBI investigation into a civil-rights matter, historically, is not a small thing. It is a serious process with real institutional weight behind it. By describing it as merely standard, Blanche was not offering reassurance. He was pre-emptively lowering expectations, signaling to his own department and to the public that this case would not receive the elevated scrutiny that two dead American citizens, shot by their own government, would ordinarily warrant. He said this out loud, at a podium, on the record. It was not a slip. It was the clearest possible statement of intent from the man who now runs the department responsible for deciding whether anyone will ever answer for what happened to Renee Good and Alex Pretti.

Minnesota’s own investigators, for their part, have not waited for Washington’s permission. Hennepin County Attorney Mary Moriarty has told me directly that the state’s Bureau of Criminal Apprehension is conducting its own investigation into both deaths, using the evidence available to the state — principally the video evidence captured by bystanders, since the federal government has been unwilling to share much else. Moriarty has been careful to say her office has not yet reached a decision on whether it can prove a crime was committed. That caution is itself notable: a state prosecutor being more scrupulous about evidentiary standards than the federal government has been about opening an investigation in the first place.

What Blanche Says Now

Everything above was written before Blanche’s confirmation hearing. He was asked about Good and Pretti directly, twice, and it is worth including his answers rather than letting this piece stand as though he never had to respond.

Sen. Amy Klobuchar of Minnesota raised the case by name, noting that federal investigators had for months blocked her state’s investigators from the scene of Pretti’s death despite a judicial warrant, and had failed to process Good’s car for evidence. She credited the department with finally sharing evidence with state investigators earlier that week, then asked whether that cooperation would continue — including on the newer shootings in Texas and Maine. Blanche’s answer was that these investigations would be run like any other, that the definition of full cooperation could be disputed, and that a lot of the reporting Klobuchar had referenced was not accurate about what had actually been happening since January.

Sen. Alex Padilla of California pressed harder. He noted that months after the shootings, the officers involved had not been fired or charged, and asked what role DOJ should play in ensuring independent, transparent review of federal officer-involved shootings generally. Blanche disagreed with the premise that the passage of time meant no justice was occurring, said these investigations simply take time, and pointed to the department’s work with Minnesota as evidence cooperation was real.

I want to hold both of those answers up against what this piece has actually documented, rather than simply accept or dismiss them. Blanche is right that some evidence changed hands this month — Part V covers exactly how and why. He did not mention, and was not asked to explain, that the handover came only after Minnesota agreed to trade its own evidence in a separate case, or that the identical stonewalling he calls inaccurately reported is, as of this writing, still happening in Houston. “Not accurate” is a claim now on the record, under oath. Readers can weigh it against the specifics in this piece and the next one and decide for themselves how it holds up.
