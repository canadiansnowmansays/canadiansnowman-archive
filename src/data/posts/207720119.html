Randolph Frederick Edward Spencer-Churchill was born on May 28, 1911, at his parents’ house on Eccleston Square in London, the only son of Winston and Clementine Churchill, and his parents nicknamed him “the Chumbolly” before he was even born — a piece of private baby talk that survived, oddly, into public retellings of the family’s history, a small early sign of just how much attention this particular child would receive before he had done anything at all to earn it. Winston himself, whose own relationship with Randolph the elder had been distant, disapproving, and cut short by death when Winston was twenty, appears to have resolved, consciously or not, to be a different kind of father to his own son — warmer, more openly affectionate, considerably more indulgent. The most-repeated image of Randolph’s childhood, recorded by more than one family memoirist, is of the young boy holding forth at the dinner table among his father’s most eminent guests, confident enough in his own opinions to interrupt adult conversation, while Winston, rather than silencing him, would raise his cigar for quiet and let the boy finish. It is a charming image of paternal indulgence. It is also, in retrospect, a fairly precise diagnosis of what went wrong.

Randolph was educated at Eton and then Christ Church, Oxford, following his father’s own path through elite British education rather than his father’s own struggles within it — where Winston had been the backward boy failing Sandhurst’s entrance exam twice, Randolph arrived already fluent in the register of privilege his father had needed a lifetime to earn, and the difference in starting position seems, if anything, to have left him less equipped for adversity rather than more. He left Oxford without a degree and moved directly into journalism and the lecture circuit, trading on his father’s name and his own genuine gift for provocative, well-turned political commentary, and by his twenties he was earning substantial fees as a journalist and public speaker — a faster, easier version of the writing-as-income strategy his father had pioneered as a young cavalry officer three decades earlier, minus the wars and the personal risk that had originally made his father’s journalism worth reading.

The political career Randolph was groomed for from childhood proved considerably harder to acquire than the journalism had, and it is worth walking through the failures in some detail, because his eventual single term in Parliament tends to get treated as a minor footnote rather than the culmination of a genuinely difficult, repeatedly humiliating decade of trying. Randolph stood for Parliament and lost three times across the 1930s, embarrassing his father publicly on each occasion — a inversion, worth noting, of the family’s usual pattern, in which it had always been Winston’s own setbacks that got absorbed into a larger recovery narrative. Here it was the father watching the son fail, repeatedly, at the one thing the family had assumed would come naturally to him. He finally won a seat, for Preston, in a wartime by-election in September 1940 — entering Parliament, in other words, at almost exactly the moment his father became Prime Minister, which meant Randolph’s own parliamentary career began under the shadow of an achievement so large it made his own election feel almost incidental by comparison. He served in uniform during the war as well, with the 4th Queen’s Own Hussars — the same regiment his father had once served in — and was for a time attached to the newly formed Special Air Service under David Stirling, joining raids behind enemy lines in the Libyan desert before being assigned, in 1944, to the British military mission to Tito’s partisans in Yugoslavia alongside his friend Evelyn Waugh. The mission nearly killed him before it properly began: the Dakota transport carrying Randolph and Waugh into Croatia crashed on its first attempt in July 1944, and Randolph was among only ten survivors, suffering spinal and knee injuries serious enough to require weeks of hospital treatment in Bari before he insisted on returning to finish the assignment. It is worth noting the echo here, because it is almost too neat: a Churchill man surviving a catastrophic crash and returning immediately to the danger that had nearly killed him is precisely the recovery template this series has traced since Leonard Jerome’s Wall Street ruins. The difference, in Randolph’s case, is that nobody outside the family was paying particularly close attention. His father was, at that exact moment, managing the Normandy campaign and the future shape of postwar Europe. A son’s near-fatal plane crash in the Balkans, however genuinely harrowing, simply could not compete for public or even private attention with what Winston Churchill was doing at the same time on a considerably larger stage — the same asymmetry that would define nearly every attempt Randolph made, for the rest of his life, to establish an identity distinct from his father’s.

An earlier episode, from his Oxford years, is worth including as well, because it shows the same instinct for public controversy that would define his later journalism already fully formed by his early twenties. In February 1933, the Oxford Union held its famous “King and Country” debate, resolving that the House would in no circumstances fight for its King and country — a pacifist motion that scandalized much of the British establishment and was later cited, not entirely fairly, as evidence of a generation’s dangerous unpreparedness for the war that followed six years later. Randolph, already a controversial undergraduate figure, involved himself prominently in the surrounding uproar, and the episode cemented an early reputation — one that would follow him for the rest of his life — as a young man who could always be relied upon to place himself at the center of whatever argument was loudest, regardless of whether the position he was defending was the popular one or even, always, the coherent one. He lost his Preston seat in the 1945 general election, the same election that ended his father’s premiership, and never won another, standing unsuccessfully again in 1950 and 1951 before accepting, by the mid-1950s, that his political career was, in the assessment of his own contemporaries, effectively finished.

There is a specific act of paternal sacrifice buried in this failure that deserves to be named plainly, because it is one of the more genuinely touching details in the entire family record. When Winston Churchill’s own premiership ended for the final time in 1955, he was offered a peerage — the Dukedom of London, no less, a title of the highest rank — and declined it, as he had declined a similar honor at the end of the war a decade earlier. The reason, by most accounts and confirmed by family biographers, was Randolph: under the conventions of the period, a hereditary peerage passed to Winston would eventually have forced Randolph, as the heir, out of any future House of Commons seat and into the House of Lords instead, foreclosing the one political path Randolph might still, however implausibly by 1955, have hoped to pursue. Winston Churchill turned down one of the highest honors the British state could offer a private citizen specifically to preserve a possibility his son’s own repeated failures had already made almost entirely theoretical. It is difficult to imagine his own father, Randolph the elder, making an equivalent sacrifice for him — which is, perhaps, the clearest single measure of how much more generously this generation’s father tried to manage the inheritance than the previous one had.

The drinking, which eventually did more than any electoral defeat to define Randolph’s reputation, appears to have deepened alongside the political failures rather than following them — contemporaries recalled him developing serious habits in the company of his father’s own hard-drinking political and literary circle as early as his twenties, at the side of figures like F.E. Smith, Lord Birkenhead, men who had made heavy drinking part of the same performative political culture Randolph had grown up admiring at his father’s table. His personality, by the near-unanimous account of friends and biographers alike, could be genuinely warm, generous, and courageous when sober, and cruel, arrogant, and quarrelsome when drunk — which, by most accounts, was frequently. His marriage to Pamela Digby in 1939 produced his only son, another Winston, who would go on to serve as a Conservative MP himself and eventually write his own father’s biography — a third consecutive generation of Churchill men writing a father’s or grandfather’s life story, as though narrating the previous generation’s record were itself a hereditary occupation the family kept passing down alongside the name. Pamela herself divorced Randolph in 1946 and went on to a remarkable career of her own entirely independent of the Churchill name — marrying the American businessman and diplomat Averell Harriman decades later and eventually serving as United States Ambassador to France in the 1990s, which makes her, in her own right, very likely the single most consequential American-side figure this entire series has touched without yet spending a part on directly.

The most famous anecdote of Randolph’s later years, repeated in nearly every account of his life, involves a 1964 operation to remove a lung tumor that turned out, on examination, to be benign — prompting his friend Evelyn Waugh to remark, with the particular cruelty only a close friend can manage, that it was typical of doctors to locate the one part of Randolph that was not malignant and to cut it out. It is a very good line, and it has done Randolph’s posthumous reputation no favors whatsoever, reducing an entire complicated life, in the popular memory, to a punchline about his own personality’s toxicity. What the anecdote leaves out, and what deserves equal billing here, is what came after: told by his doctor that continued heavy drinking would kill him within six months, Randolph stopped almost entirely, and spent his final years — genuinely ill, his body by then compromised well beyond what abstinence alone could repair — working with a team of researchers on the official biography of his father, publishing two substantial volumes covering Winston’s early life before his own death in 1968 at fifty-seven. Winston S. Churchill’s authorized biography, the multi-volume project later completed by Martin Gilbert after Randolph’s death, remains the standard scholarly reference for Churchill’s life to this day. Whatever else Randolph failed at, across three decades of failed elections and a public reputation built largely around his own worst behavior, he did not fail at this. He died having given his father’s life the kind of sustained, serious, well-researched attention that his father had never quite managed to give him in return.

There is a version of Randolph’s story, told by his harshest contemporaries and repeated uncritically in a great deal of shorter popular writing since, that reduces him entirely to the drunk, spoiled black sheep of a family that otherwise produced greatness in every generation. That version is not false, exactly — the drinking was real, the personal cruelty when drunk was real, the repeated public humiliations of failed elections were real. But it is incomplete in a way that matters for a series built around the word inheritance rather than around simple moral accounting. Randolph inherited his grandfather’s appetite for political spectacle and his father’s gift for vivid, quotable prose, and he inherited neither man’s capacity for recovery — not because he lacked the temperament for it, but because the family’s own recovery template, built by a Wall Street speculator and refined by a war hero prime minister, required a stage large enough to make catastrophic setbacks recoverable, and there was, by the time Randolph came of age, no stage left in the family sized for him. His grandfather Randolph the elder had lost the Chancellorship of the Exchequer and had no war to hide the loss inside. His son Randolph the younger lost three parliamentary elections in a row and had, by then, an already-legendary father whose own shadow made every one of his failures both more visible and less individually consequential than they might otherwise have been. He was not ruined by drink alone. He was, in a specific and rather sad sense, ruined by arriving third in a family sequence that had already used up its available supply of dramatic, stage-sized redemption.
