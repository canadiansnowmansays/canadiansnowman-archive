I. A Proud Tower, Viewed From Outside

Four essays into this series I have written a great deal about choreography — honour guards, ballroom renovations, the careful placement of an AI dialogue inside a meeting whose real business was tariffs. What I have not written about, until now, is the possibility that the choreography itself is the danger. That was the argument Robert D. Kaplan made on 21 September 2026, three days before Xi Jinping lands in Washington, in an essay for the New York Times carrying the plain, unhedged title “America Is Blundering Toward War With China.”

Kaplan is not a excitable man by trade. He has spent decades writing about the Balkans, the Indian Ocean, and the slow mechanics of imperial overreach, usually from a position of studied detachment. So it is worth taking seriously when a writer of that temperament opens with a claim this blunt: that no country in the American diplomatic record has been misjudged as badly, or as repeatedly, as China — not Iraq, not Afghanistan, not even Vietnam, which was itself, in his telling, a downstream casualty of an earlier China miscalculation. His argument is not that Washington and Beijing are approaching a random or unforeseeable danger. It is that both are walking, with unusual clarity of step, into a mistake that has a shape historians already know.

II. Three Times, By His Count

Kaplan’s essay is built as a history of American misreadings of China, and it is worth summarising his three before getting to what he thinks may be a fourth, because the pattern he draws matters more than any single fact in it.

The first misjudgment was the “Who Lost China” debate of the late 1940s and early 1950s, when Washington assumed Chiang Kai-shek’s Nationalists would hold power after the Second World War and was blindsided when Mao’s Communists won the 1946–1949 civil war outright. The State Department officials who had correctly predicted a Communist victory were purged rather than vindicated, accused of insufficient loyalty to a regime they had watched collapse from the inside. Kaplan’s sharper point is that the American diplomats who got the outcome right still misread its character — reporting from Chiang’s authoritarian wartime capital, they mistook the discipline of Mao’s stage-managed base camp for benevolence, and missed the totalitarian instinct that would go on to kill tens of millions of people. The purge of the officials who’d called it correctly then gutted the State Department of exactly the expertise that might have prevented Washington’s next China-adjacent catastrophe — Vietnam, fought in the 1960s partly because the specialists who understood Asia had already been driven out in the 1950s.

The second misjudgment, on Kaplan’s account, was almost the reverse of a mistake: the 1972 opening brokered by Richard Nixon and Henry Kissinger, which treated China as a chess piece against the Soviet Union and held as the organising logic of the relationship until the 1989 Tiananmen crackdown complicated it. What emerged from that opening — the deliberately unresolved formula on Taiwan known as the Shanghai Communiqué — is the hinge this whole essay turns on, and I’ll come back to it in a moment.

The third was the bipartisan bet, dominant through the 1990s and into the 2000s, that admitting China to the World Trade Organization would pull the country toward liberalisation, the assumption Bill Clinton held and one Kaplan says the entire Washington establishment shared in the afterglow of the Cold War’s end. China, in his blunt summary, was not Europe. Admission to the WTO fuelled the economic and technological rise that has produced the country now sending its leader to Washington this week — not the liberal convergence Washington’s elite expected to buy with market access.

Kaplan adds a detail to the first misjudgment that is worth dwelling on, because it undercuts the comfortable idea that the 1949 outcome was simply unforeseeable. He credits the historian Frank Dikötter, of the Hoover Institution, with establishing just how much material aid Stalin was quietly funnelling to Mao’s forces during the civil war — support that made a Nationalist victory considerably less likely than Washington’s planners assumed at the time. The point isn’t that Dikötter’s archival work should have been available to 1940s policymakers; it wasn’t. The point is that even China specialists operating in good faith, closer to the ground than anyone in Washington, were working from an incomplete picture, and that incompleteness is a permanent condition of this relationship rather than a solvable intelligence failure. China, Kaplan writes, is hard to fathom even for an expert class insulated from the partisan distortions that warp so much Middle East analysis. If that was true of officials with access to Chongqing and Yan’an in 1948, it is worth asking what confidence anyone should have in a White House currently organised around improvisation rather than institutional expertise.

III. The Beam Nobody Wants to Touch

What holds those three misjudgments together, and what makes Kaplan’s essay relevant to a series that has spent three parts tracking a frozen Taiwan arms package, is the Shanghai Communiqué itself — the formula, dating to Nixon’s 1972 visit, under which Washington acknowledges there is only one China with its capital in Beijing while declining to say what it would do if that status quo were violently disturbed. It is not a treaty. It is closer to a load-bearing beam nobody has fully inspected in fifty years, holding up a relationship that both governments have found more useful to leave ambiguous than to resolve.

Kaplan’s warning is that the beam is under more strain now than at any point since 1972. He notes the slip-ups — Joe Biden’s repeated departures from strategic ambiguity, each one walked back by his own staff — but reserves his sharpest language for what he calls Trump’s volatility on Taiwan and the wider Asia-Pacific, a volatility this series has already spent an entire essay documenting in granular form. Readers of Part IV will recognise the $14 billion arms package the moment Kaplan gets to it, because he cites the same negotiating-chip framing this series flagged in May: Trump treating Taiwan’s defence as leverage to be spent rather than a commitment to be honoured. Kaplan’s version of that argument runs the same direction mine did, independently arrived at, which is either reassuring or alarming depending on how much stock you put in two writers reaching the same conclusion from different source material.

IV. The 1914 Problem

The part of Kaplan’s essay doing the most actual work is a citation to the Yale historian Odd Arne Westad, who has argued that the First World War was set in motion, in part, by a German assumption that Britain might sit out a continental war rather than honour its commitments to France and Belgium. Berlin miscalculated not because it wanted a world war, but because it misread how firm an ambiguous-sounding commitment actually was. Kaplan’s application of that logic to the present is not subtle: if Beijing comes to believe Washington might not actually defend Taiwan — a belief that a frozen arms sale, an avoided word in Pentagon speeches, and a president who calls the island’s defence “a very good negotiating chip” would all, cumulatively, tend to encourage — the risk of war does not fall. It rises. Ambiguity is only stabilising for as long as both sides read the same firmness underneath it. The moment one side starts reading weakness instead, the ambiguity that was supposed to prevent a crisis becomes the thing that causes one.

This is, I think, the single most useful reframe Kaplan offers a series that has spent four parts treating Obfuscate as a fourth, milder alternative to Threaten, Accommodate, and Capitulate — the quiet option, the one that costs nothing because nobody has to commit to anything. Kaplan’s history suggests that reading understates the risk built into the category. Obfuscation is not a costless holding pattern. It is a bet that the other side will keep reading your silence the way you intend it to be read, indefinitely, with no mechanism for correcting the interpretation if it starts to drift. Part IV closed on the observation that Washington and Beijing are each withholding a different thing — a weapons sale, a redline — for reasons that only resemble each other from a distance. Kaplan’s contribution is the reminder that the resemblance doesn’t have to be exact for the danger to be real. Both governments are running a version of the same 1914 experiment, whether or not either intends to.

Kaplan adds one further destabiliser to the mix that readers of Part III will recognise immediately: artificial intelligence. He notes, almost in passing, that because neither Washington nor Beijing seems genuinely interested in jointly regulating the technology, competition over AI is poised to further destabilise a relationship already under strain — a line that reads very differently once you know, as this series has documented, that the two governments’ actual AI dialogue got folded into a single Sunday afternoon at a Manhattan bank and produced, as its headline deliverable, an agreement to notify each other after something has already gone wrong. Bessent’s own framing of that dialogue — that Washington can afford “wholesome” cooperation because it believes it holds the technological lead — is not so far from the negotiating-chip logic Trump applies to Taiwan. Both rest on the assumption that the advantage is durable enough to be generous with. Kaplan’s essay doesn’t say that assumption is wrong. It says the entire history of the US-China relationship is a catalogue of Washington’s confident assumptions about China turning out to be wrong, which is a considerably more unsettling thing to have said about you three days before your president sits down across the table.

V. What a Softer Approach Would Not Fix

Kaplan is careful, and this deserves stating plainly because it cuts against how his essay could easily be misread, to reject the opposite error too. He is explicit that a softer, less resolute American posture toward Taiwan is not the safe alternative to Trump’s volatility — it is, in his phrase, its own kind of misunderstanding, one that could embolden exactly the attack it hopes to avoid. Washington’s Taiwan policy has functioned, since 1979, as a peculiar kind of tightrope: firm enough to deter, vague enough to avoid provoking, predictable enough that neither side has to guess what happens if the line gets crossed. Kaplan’s argument is not that America should abandon ambiguity for clarity, or commitment for retreat. It is that the specific quality holding the tightrope up — steady, predictable signalling, the kind that requires discipline rather than drama — is exactly what an administration organised around one man’s improvisational instincts is least equipped to supply.

He reaches, near the end of his essay, for Barbara Tuchman’s history of the years before the First World War, and for an 1845 Edgar Allan Poe line she used as her title’s epigraph: a proud tower, and death looking down on it from a great height. It is a grim note to end on, and Kaplan does not soften it. His closing argument is that no other geopolitical fault line currently open — not Ukraine, not Gaza, not even Iran — carries the same capacity for miscalculated catastrophe as the one this series has spent five essays now documenting from Toronto, at a safe and slightly bemused remove.

VI. The View From the Proud Tower

I have called this series an opera because summits are theatre, and theatre is the honest word for events where everyone involved already knows roughly how the evening is supposed to end. Kaplan’s essay is a useful corrective to that framing, because it argues the ending is not actually known — that the same performed warmth this series has spent four parts documenting sits on top of a structural fault line neither government has fully reckoned with since 1972, and that the specific behaviour producing this week’s warmth (silence on Taiwan, a frozen arms package, an administration that treats commitments as chips) is the behaviour historians will point to, if this goes badly, as the moment the ambiguity stopped working.

I don’t know whether Thursday’s summit will supply the correction or the confirmation. Neither, I suspect, does Kaplan, and neither does anyone currently rehearsing an honour guard on the South Lawn. What I can say, four essays and one outside historian into this project, is that the TACO scorecard this series opened in Part I needs one more footnote before Xi’s plane lands: Obfuscate was never the safe option on the board. It was always just the option whose cost hadn’t come due yet.
