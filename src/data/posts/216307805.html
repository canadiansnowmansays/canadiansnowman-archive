Stephen Gordon Sept 18, 2026

On Thursday, Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. did two things that, on their own, would each have been worth a column. Taken together, on the same day, they tell you almost everything this series has been arguing for a year.

In the morning, his department announced eight new appointments to the United States Preventive Services Task Force, a panel that has spent more than a year unable to function because Kennedy kept blocking it, and whose recommendations quietly determine whether your insurer has to pay for a mammogram, a colonoscopy or a depression screening. In the afternoon, Kennedy walked onto a stage in Washington, in front of the anti-vaccine organization he founded, and told the crowd, “You have a strong and steadfast friend at the White House.” Two audiences, one day, one man deciding who gets to be trusted with medical evidence and who gets to be trusted with him.

The Man Who Came Back

As the Atlantic’s Tom Bartlett and, separately, the New York Times’ Christina Jewett and Teddy Rosenbluth reported, Kennedy addressed the Children’s Health Defense conference in Washington on Thursday and delivered the speech of a man with nothing left to lose politically — or perhaps a man who has simply stopped pretending he needs to. He floated the autism-vaccine link that decades of research have closed the book on. He told the crowd “we have no idea what the risk profile is” for vaccines the CDC he oversees recommends. He read aloud from a hepatitis B vaccine’s list of possible adverse events, lingering on anaphylaxis — a reaction that occurs in roughly one dose per million — while saying nothing about the millions of deaths the vaccine has prevented. He closed with a promise to “die with my boots on” chasing answers about vaccine risk, and told the room that Vaccine Safety Datalink research was underway behind the scenes at HHS, a database anti-vaccine activists have long treated as a hidden vault of evidence rather than the routine surveillance tool it is.

He was not alone up there. Mark Gorton, president of the pro-Kennedy MAHA Institute, told the same crowd that the success of the polio vaccine was “a massive medical fraud” and received a standing ovation for it. Andrew Wakefield — the British doctor whose 1998 paper linking the MMR vaccine to autism was retracted and whose medical license was revoked — spoke immediately after Kennedy. Mary Holland, the group’s CEO, introduced Kennedy by praising him for taking on “the corrupt medical pharmaceutical complex.” Kennedy also told the crowd he would be examining the health effects of electromagnetic radiation and “chemtrails” — condensation trails the EPA has repeatedly confirmed are, in fact, condensation.

This is not new material for Kennedy. It is the material he built a career on before he had a cabinet seat. What’s new is that he is saying it as the country’s top health official, to the organization he ran until 2023, during the worst measles outbreak the United States has seen in thirty-five years — and that, according to the Times, he did so a single day after President Trump’s own nominee for surgeon general, Dr. Nicole Saphier, gave the Senate a full-throated defense of vaccine safety and efficacy at her confirmation hearing. The administration is not, on this subject, speaking with one voice. It has simply stopped being embarrassed by the fact.

The Deaths Nobody Will Count

The backdrop to Thursday’s speech is Pennsylvania. As the Atlantic reported, the state had recorded 731 measles cases since April, 117 of them in the past week alone, concentrated heavily in the state’s Amish community, where vaccination rates in Lancaster County run around 25 percent — a community Kennedy praised in 2021 for declining vaccines, telling an audience at an Amish country fair that “it’s so important for us to think more like the Amish think.” Four people have died in the outbreak, two of them infants. By the time the Times filed its report the same evening, national case counts had reached roughly 3,300 for the year, and Pennsylvania had confirmed a fourth death — an eighteen-year-old, joining two infants too young to be vaccinated and a forty-year-old unvaccinated woman.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, which Kennedy runs, is currently reporting zero measles deaths for 2026, nationally. After the first Pennsylvania deaths were reported, Kennedy publicly questioned whether measles was actually the cause and suggested — without evidence — that the deaths might have been “fabricated.” Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro has spent recent days pressing HHS to formally acknowledge the deaths; while Kennedy was on stage at the CHD conference Thursday, HHS’s official X account described Shapiro’s demand as “political blackmail.” In his remarks that afternoon, the only reference Kennedy made to measles at all was a complaint that deaths from the disease “get headlines” while alleged vaccine dangers do not.

Shapiro’s response, posted afterward, is worth repeating in full because it says in one sentence what this series has spent a year saying in several thousand words: “In the midst of the largest and deadliest measles outbreak in America in 35 years, RFK Jr. chose to spend his time today addressing the premier vaccine-denial conference in America. He needed to say one sentence: I urge all Americans to get the measles vaccine now to protect themselves and their families during this deadly outbreak.” He did not say that sentence. He said the other one, about friends in the White House.

It’s worth noting, too, that Trump’s own team is capable of saying the sentence Kennedy won’t. When Trump described the measles vaccine as “lethal” at last month’s executive order signing — the order this series covered on August 13 — Dr. Mehmet Oz corrected him on CBS’s Face the Nation days later: “The MMR vaccine is not a lethal vaccine.” That correction came from inside the administration, on television, and changed nothing about what Kennedy said to the CHD crowd three weeks later.

The Panel Nobody Was Watching

While Kennedy was preparing for Washington, his department was quietly finishing the other half of Thursday’s business. As the New York Times’ Maggie Astor, Dani Blum and Nina Agrawal reported, HHS announced eight new members of the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force — a body that, since the Supreme Court ruled last year Kennedy could unilaterally remove and override its members, he has simply prevented from meeting. Four postponed meetings, the most recent in July. Two remaining panel leaders fired in May. Seats that should have been filled before their predecessors’ terms expired sat empty for months, until Thursday.

The new members are not, by background, a preventive-medicine panel in the traditional sense. The new chairman, Dr. Seth Corey, is a senior fellow at Do No Harm, an organization that calls gender-affirming care “dangerous, life-altering procedures that lack scientific justification.” Dr. Patrick Hunter, another appointee, has sought to link gender-affirming care to violence and sits on the Florida medical board that approved the state’s ban on such care for minors. Venkatesh Murthy has criticized both gender-affirming care and Covid vaccines. Dr. Dennis Wulfeck and Dr. Ronald Karlsberg have likewise raised doubts about Covid shots. Stephen Parente, a former Trump health-economics adviser, isn’t a physician at all. Against this, two appointees — a family-medicine doctor and a gastroenterologist — read as almost incidental. The task force has historically drawn on primary-care generalists; this new panel leans hard toward specialists and advocates, a composition Dr. Alex Krist, its former chair, called a departure from “evidence, data and outcomes” as the basis for recommendations that affect what preventive care hundreds of millions of insured Americans can access without paying out of pocket.

The selection process matched the substance. Nomination and application materials that federal rules require to be public were withheld; a Times reporter who went in person to review them at the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality was turned away over unredacted personal information that had supposedly not yet been scrubbed, months after the review period began. The Times sued for the records under the Freedom of Information Act in August and is still waiting. And in the press release announcing the new members, HHS described the task force using language nearly identical to its own longstanding description — minus two words. “Scientifically independent” didn’t make the cut. Kennedy separately insisted, in a Fox News op-ed, that the panel remains independent. Dr. Aaron Carroll of AcademyHealth put the gap between the two claims plainly: “You can appoint people, but you can’t rebuild the evidence machinery underneath them by press release.”

Reading Thursday Through TACO

Thursday is the clearest single-day illustration this series has had of the four-move pattern it keeps returning to, because for once both halves happened in daylight, hours apart, with no month of silence in between to obscure the connection.

The threat was never subtle: fire the officials who won’t comply (Monarez a year ago, the task force’s own leadership in May), block the bodies that might push back (four postponed meetings), and when a governor asks you to count the dead, call it blackmail. The accommodation came from elsewhere in the administration — Saphier’s confirmation-hearing defense of vaccines, Oz’s on-air correction of the president — real, on-record positions that let wavering senators and skeptical viewers believe the guardrails are holding, even as the guardrails’ composition is being replaced by press release. The obfuscation is now practically a signature: two words quietly dropped from a public description, a redaction excuse that outlasts the redaction, a Cabinet secretary suggesting without evidence that reported deaths were invented rather than simply disputing the count. And the capitulation, when it lands, doesn’t apologize for any of it — it walks back onto a stage in front of the people who cheered when a colleague called the polio vaccine a fraud, and calls them friends.

None of this required Kennedy to hide anything on Thursday. That may be the most notable shift the day represents. A year ago, this series covered a health secretary who worked to reassure the Senate he held mainstream-enough views to be confirmable. A year later, with an election two months out and his own movement’s base reportedly growing impatient with the pace of change, he no longer seems to think reassurance is the job. Pennsylvania has counted four bodies this outbreak has produced so far. The CDC he runs has counted zero.
