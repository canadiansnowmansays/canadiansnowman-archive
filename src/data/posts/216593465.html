by Stephen Gordon

The patient readers have earned essay 1,752. One hundred and seventy essays ago — which in this series’ peculiar geography is also one hundred and seventy years ago — I told you about the autumn of 1582, when Pope Gregory XIII deleted ten days from October to bring the drifting calendar back to the sun, and I told you that England refused the correction, not because the arithmetic was wrong but because a pope had signed it, and I told you the refusal would run for a hundred and seventy years, and I lit a fuse and said the Numbers Game plays long. This is the essay at the end of that fuse. In September 1752, the British Empire finally paid the invoice it had been disputing since Elizabeth’s reign, and Wednesday the 2nd was followed directly by Thursday the 14th, and eleven days — the pope’s ten, plus one more accrued in 1700 by the very act of refusing, which is my favourite detail in the whole affair and we will get to it — were written off the books of every subject from London to Boston to the Hudson’s Bay posts of what would become my country.

Begin with the accrual, because it is the purest interest charge in this series. The whole point of the Gregorian reform, essay 1,582, was Lilius’s rule: century years are not leap years unless divisible by 400. The year 1700 was such a year — a leap year under the old Julian reckoning, a common year under the new. So when 1700 arrived, Gregorian Europe skipped the leap day and Julian Britain took it, and the gap between the two calendars, ten days for the previous century, widened to eleven. Attend to what that is. Britain’s debt to the sun grew by a full day as a direct consequence of continuing to dispute it. The refusal was not a frozen error; it was an error at compound interest, accruing on schedule, every century, forever, until paid. This series has audited many institutions that mistook stubbornness for a store of value. The calendar is the only creditor in the files that charged them for it by rule, in advance, publicly, and was still ignored.

What finally moved the empire was not shame but friction. By mid-century, Britain was a global commercial and military power whose every foreign transaction required a conversion. Merchants’ contracts, diplomats’ treaties, sailors’ rendezvous, newspapers reporting continental events — everything crossed the eleven-day gap and had to be double-dated, Old Style and New Style, O.S. and N.S., a whole civilization annotating itself like a badly kept spreadsheet. The reform, when it came, was steered through Parliament in 1751 by the Earl of Chesterfield, with the mathematical case supplied by the astronomer Earl of Macclesfield, and Chesterfield’s own account of the passage — from his letters, with the tongs — is a gift to every communicator who has ever briefed a board: he confessed that he did not trouble the Lords with the celestial mathematics, which would have been so much Celtic to them, but carried the day with agreeable words and well-turned periods. The most consequential technical correction in British history was passed on charm, the members nodding along to arithmetic they did not follow, and I have sat in enough boardrooms to certify that this is not a story about the eighteenth century.

The Act did three things, and the second is the one nobody remembers and everybody lives with. First, the deletion: September 2, 1752, to be followed by September 14. Second — and here I need my English readers to sit down — it moved New Year’s Day. Until 1752, the legal year in England did not begin on January 1. It began on March 25, Lady Day, which meant that the date-line of English history is a permanent trap: what a Londoner of the time called February 1731 is what we would call February 1732, and generations of researchers have fallen into the seam. To align with the reformed system, the year 1751 was simply cut short — it ran from March 25 to December 31, a year of 282 days, the shortest year in English history, deleted not by fire or plague but by statute. And third, the Act grandfathered the money: contracts, rents, and terms were to run their natural length in real days, not calendar dates, so that no creditor could collect and no debtor could skip eleven days of obligation.

The seam claimed its most famous casualty in the colonies, and the case is worth the paragraph because it walks on two hundred million calendars to this day. A Virginia planter’s son had been born, by the reckoning in force at the time, on February 11, 1731. Run him through both provisions of the Act — eleven days forward for the deletion, one year forward for the relocated New Year, his birthday falling in the trap-zone between January 1 and March 25 — and the same morning becomes February 22, 1732. The infant was George Washington, and the United States accordingly celebrates its founding father’s birthday on a date on which, by the calendar under which he was actually born, nothing whatever occurred. The first fact in the American national record — the birth of the first president — carries a conversion footnote from a British statute correcting a papal correction of a Roman general’s rounding error. No republic escapes the old ledgers. They are grandfathered into everything, including the birthdays of the men who declare independence from them.

Which brings us to the riots, and to the finest lesson in evidence this series has yet had the pleasure of filing. The legend — you have heard it; it is in a hundred books — is that mobs filled the streets crying Give us our eleven days!, convinced the government had shortened their lives by statute. And the legend’s paper trail, when the historians went looking, runs not to any contemporary account of an actual riot, but to a painting: William Hogarth’s An Election Entertainment of 1755, a satirical canvas of a chaotic campaign banquet, in which a stolen protest placard bearing the slogan lies underfoot. Hogarth was lampooning an election in which the calendar had been demagogued as an issue — the Tories jeering the reformers — and posterity took the satirist’s prop for a news photograph. The riot that defines 1752 in popular memory is, on the best evidence, a depiction of a slogan, mistaken for the event itself. This series has spent the year on disputed doors, disputed arms, disputed last words; the calendar riots are the complete specimen: not an exaggerated event but an invented one, minted by art and circulated as history for two and a half centuries because it was too good to audit. The words are painted. The riot is not. File it beside Eppur si muove, in the drawer marked things too perfect to have happened.

Which is not to say nobody was aggrieved — the grievances were real and they were, as always, denominated in money: wages, rents, interest, and above all taxes. And it is in the tax ledger that this essay finds its buried treasure, the fossil I promised, the detail that made me laugh aloud in the research and that you will verify within the week if you have British affairs of any kind. The Treasury, bound to collect a full year’s revenue, declined to let eleven days of taxation vanish into the statutory hole. So the tax year, which had ended on Lady Day, March 25, was extended by eleven days — to April 5. And when 1800 arrived and the Julian ghost accrued its next phantom leap day, the Treasury shifted once more, to April 6. And there — to this day — it sits: the tax year of the United Kingdom begins on April 6 for no reason on earth except that the sixteenth century’s calendar error, compounded by a hundred and seventy years of theological stubbornness, was amortized by the eighteenth century’s revenue service and never subsequently cleaned up. Every April, one of the world’s great financial systems opens its fiscal year on a date that is, in the strictest sense, the shadow of the Council of Trent falling across a spreadsheet. Institutions, this series has found, act only when the error threatens the crown jewel. Here is the corollary, and it deserves its bold: institutions repair the error and keep the scar — and the scar outlives the institution’s memory of the wound. Nobody at His Majesty’s Revenue is defending the Julian calendar. They are merely still, after two hundred and seventy-four years, invoicing around it.

Benjamin Franklin, then a Philadelphia printer whose colonies went through the same deletion on the same night, offered the sanest contemporary verdict — paraphrased here from his almanac’s good humour: what a pleasure, he suggested, for the man who loves his pillow, to lie down on the second of the month and not be obliged to rise until the fourteenth. It is the correct response to a calendar correction and very nearly the only recorded one: the days were not stolen; the labels moved; the mornings arrived as usual. Franklin could see it because Franklin, a printer, understood in his hands the difference between the text and the thing — a distinction this series has been prosecuting since Gutenberg, and which the calendar affair states more cleanly than any entry yet: the map lost eleven days and the territory lost none, and half an empire mourned the map.

Dior’s relationship to this material is, as the veterans will anticipate, one of complete sovereignty. I informed her on the lakeshore that in 1752 the second of September was followed by the fourteenth, and she received it as she receives all calendar intelligence, which is as noise from a system she does not recognize. Dior runs on the solar original: light, warmth, the angle of the morning — the very signals the Julian calendar drifted away from and the Gregorian was built to track. She was never eleven days out of true, because she never left the sun for the paperwork. It has taken me until essay 1,752 to state the household’s standing irony plainly: I keep the count, and she keeps the time.

The count moves on, as it does, and now it comes home. The next landmark is essay 1,755, in which a large, half-blind, short-tempered man in a Fleet Street garret attempts to count the English language one word at a time — and discovers, as this series' counters so often do, that the frame will not hold still. It is the shortest number on the entire map, and the heaviest one this side of the protected entries, and I will write it the way this series writes the ones that matter.

The eleven days fell due and were paid, and the scar is still on the invoice. Check your tax calendar. The sixteenth century says hello.
