In 1992, James Carville famously distilled Bill Clinton’s presidential campaign to three points, the most memorable being: “It’s the economy, stupid.”

Thirty-three years later, America’s healthcare crisis requires similar clarity: It’s the prices, stupid.

Not access. Not quality. Not innovation. Not lawsuits. Not “lazy” patients or “greedy” doctors or “government bureaucracy.”

It’s the prices.

Americans pay more — vastly, incomprehensibly, bankruptingly more — for the same healthcare services that cost a fraction of the price everywhere else in the developed world. An MRI that averaged $1,119 in the United States in 2015 averaged $215 in Australia. A hip replacement that averaged $29,067 in the US cost $6,757 in Spain. Insulin that averaged $98.70 a vial in America in 2018 cost about one-sixth as much in Canada.

This is Part 3 of Code Blue, our 10-part investigation into America’s 80-year failure to solve healthcare. In Part 1, we praised Senator Josh Hawley’s tax relief proposal while noting it doesn’t address the underlying cost disease. In Part 2, we documented nine years of Republican promises without delivery. Now we answer the fundamental question: Where does the money actually go?

The answer will make you angry. It should.

The $2.4 Trillion Question

Start with the big number: Americans spent $4.9 trillion on healthcare in 2023, or $14,570 per person, according to the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services. Using the OECD’s internationally comparable measure, U.S. spending reached $14,885 per person in 2024 — almost double the $7,860 average of comparable high-income peer countries that year (OECD, Health at a Glance 2025; KFF, “How does health spending in the U.S. compare to other countries?”, March 11, 2026).

If American healthcare costs matched that peer average, Americans would spend roughly $7,000 less per person — on the order of $2.4 trillion a year. That’s about $28,000 per family of four. Every year. Not as a one-time rebate, but as permanent savings.

Where does that $2.4 trillion go? Let’s follow the money.

Administrative Waste: The $1,900 Per Person Tax

The single largest difference between American healthcare and every other system isn’t medical — it’s clerical.

One widely cited estimate put U.S. healthcare administrative costs at $2,497 per person in 2017, against $551 in Canada — about 34% of American health spending versus 17% of Canada’s (Himmelstein, Campbell and Woolhandler, “Health Care Administrative Costs in the United States and Canada, 2017,” Annals of Internal Medicine, January 2020; reported by TCTMD, “US Administrative Healthcare Costs Are $600 Billion More Than Canada’s”, January 8, 2020).

That’s a gap of roughly $1,900 per person — more than $600 billion a year, by the authors’ estimate, in administrative overhead.

What does that buy? Armies of billing specialists. Insurance claims adjusters. Prior authorization reviewers. Medical coders. Collections agencies. Hospital billing departments.

American physicians and their staff spend a large share of their working time on billing and insurance paperwork that barely exists in single-payer systems.

Meanwhile, traditional Medicare — America’s single-payer system for seniors — runs on administrative costs commonly estimated at a low single-digit percentage of spending. Private insurers’ overhead and profit run several times higher.

Why? Because traditional Medicare doesn’t spend money on:

Marketing departments

Profit margins for shareholders

Executive compensation packages (UnitedHealth’s CEO made $23.5 million in 2023)

Claims denial operations

Networks designed to confuse consumers and limit choice

This isn’t a bug in the American system. It’s a feature. Administrative complexity isn’t waste — it’s revenue protection. Every denied claim is money saved. Every prior authorization delay is a chance the patient will give up or pay out of pocket. Every confusing bill is an opportunity for surprise charges.

Drug Prices: The Insulin Scandal

Few things illustrate American healthcare pricing better than insulin.

Insulin was discovered in 1921–22 by Frederick Banting, Charles Best and their colleagues at the University of Toronto. Banting, Best and James Collip sold the patent to the university for $1 each, so that no one could profit from a monopoly on it.

By 2018, Americans were paying an average of $98.70 per vial across all insulin types — 6.3 times the price in Canada (RAND, “Insulin Prices Are Dramatically Higher in the United States Than in Other Countries,” October 6, 2020). The medicine is the same; often so is the manufacturer. Only after years of public pressure did Eli Lilly announce, on March 1, 2023, a 70% cut to Humalog’s list price and a $35 monthly out-of-pocket cap.

Why the sixfold price difference? Because they can.

For nearly two decades, Medicare Part D was explicitly forbidden by law from negotiating drug prices, until the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 changed that — and even now, negotiations cover 10 drugs whose new prices take effect January 1, 2026, then 15 more for 2027 and 15 for 2028, rising to up to 20 a year after that (CMS, Medicare Drug Price Negotiation).

Every other developed country negotiates. The result:

Across all drugs, U.S. prices in 2022 were 278% of prices in 33 other high-income countries; for brand-name drugs, 422% (RAND, “International Prescription Drug Price Comparisons: Estimates Using 2022 Data”, February 1, 2024). Generics are the exception: Americans pay less for them than most countries do.

Humira (arthritis medication): in six peer countries, the average price was 37% of the U.S. price.

Harvoni (Hepatitis C cure): the peer average was 60% of the U.S. price.

(Both from the International Federation of Health Plans’ 2015 price data, as charted by the Economic Policy Institute, “Prices of health care almost uniformly higher in the U.S., sometimes staggeringly so.”)

Americans spent $1,443 per person on pharmaceuticals in the mid-2010s. The Swiss spent $939, the British $779, the French $697, Germans $667, and Canadians $613 (Papanicolas, Woskie and Jha, “Health Care Spending in the United States and Other High-Income Countries,” JAMA, March 13, 2018).

The pharmaceutical industry’s defense? “Research and development costs!” But the same companies sell the same drugs at a fraction of the price elsewhere and remain highly profitable doing it. Americans aren’t paying for research the rest of the world skips. They’re paying more for the same pills.

Hospital Pricing: The Chargemaster Con

In 2013, journalist Steven Brill published a Time magazine investigation that should have sparked revolution: “Bitter Pill: Why Medical Bills Are Killing Us.” His discovery? American hospitals use secret price lists called “chargemasters” that bear no relationship to actual costs, market rates, or rational pricing.

Examples from Brill’s investigation:

A chest X-ray that Medicare reimburses at $20.44 was billed to an uninsured patient at $283

A CBC blood test for which Medicare pays $11.02 was billed at $157.61

A single generic Tylenol pill was billed at $1.50

The pattern held in later international surveys. The International Federation of Health Plans’ 2015 data found:

Average U.S. MRI price: $1,119

United Kingdom: $788

Switzerland: $503

Australia: $215

(Health Exec, “MRI prices higher in the US,” May 14, 2018, citing Peterson-KFF)

Hip replacement:

United States average: $29,067

Spain: $6,757

United Kingdom: $16,335

Switzerland: $17,112

Australia: $19,484

(Becker’s Spine Review, “Total hip replacement cost in 7 different countries,” July 26, 2016)

Appendectomy:

Average across six peer countries: 29% of the U.S. average price

Lowest peer country: 11% of the U.S. price

Giving birth (uncomplicated vaginal delivery):

Average across six peer countries: 38% of the U.S. average price

Lowest peer country: 12% of the U.S. price

(Economic Policy Institute, from IFHP 2015 data)

The reason? In every other country, prices are regulated, negotiated, or set by government agencies. In America, hospitals can charge whatever they want — particularly to uninsured patients and out-of-network providers.

Even insured Americans aren’t protected. “Surprise billing” — where an in-network hospital uses out-of-network anesthesiologists or radiologists — trapped many patients in five- and six-figure bills until the No Surprises Act, passed in December 2020, took effect in 2022.

Physician Compensation: The Doctor Paradox

Here’s where the conversation gets uncomfortable: American doctors are paid significantly more than doctors anywhere else in the world.

Primary care physicians (general practitioners), mid-2010s (JAMA, March 13, 2018):

United States: $218,173 average

Germany: $154,126

Canada: $146,286

United Kingdom: $134,671

France: $111,769

Specialists: The same JAMA study found that American specialists also earn significantly more than their counterparts elsewhere.

Before physician readers close this essay in fury, let’s be absolutely clear about what this means — and what it doesn’t.

American doctors aren’t overpaid for the system they work in. They’re compensating for:

Medical school debt commonly running to $200,000 or more: German public universities charge no tuition. Canadian medical tuition, though rising, is well below American levels. French medical school costs even less. Many American doctors start their careers deep in debt before they see their first patient.

Much higher malpractice insurance premiums: In the highest-cost American markets, premiums for obstetricians and other high-risk specialists can run to six figures a year. These costs are baked into American physician compensation.

Administrative burden requiring extensive support staff: American physicians spend a substantial share of their working time on paperwork, billing codes, and insurance authorization forms, far more than doctors in simpler systems. That administrative burden means American doctors either work longer hours or see fewer patients — both reduce effective income.

Fewer, longer visits — at higher prices: Contrary to the stereotype, American primary-care visits are not short by international standards. A review of 67 countries found U.S. consultations averaging about 21 minutes by 2012, against about 8 minutes in Germany and 16 in France in 2002, the latest figures it had for those countries (Irving et al., “International variations in primary care physician consultation time”, BMJ Open, November 2017). German doctors see far more patients; American doctors see fewer, and each visit costs more.

Here’s what German doctors get that American doctors don’t:

Graduated medical school without tuition debt

Handle far less insurance paperwork

Face lower malpractice exposure

German primary care physicians earned $154,126 annually. But they’re not “underpaid” — they’re appropriately compensated within a system that doesn’t saddle them with debt, paperwork, and malpractice insurance costs.

The uncomfortable systemic truth:

You cannot reduce American healthcare spending from $14,885 per capita to the $7,860 peer average without addressing physician compensation. But the solution isn’t “pay doctors less” — it’s “fix the system that requires them to be paid more.”

If you:

Made medical school free or low-cost (like Germany)

Reduced malpractice insurance costs through tort reform

Eliminated most insurance authorization paperwork

...then American physician compensation could align with international norms without anyone taking a pay cut relative to their actual working conditions.

A surgeon performing a $29,000 hip replacement isn’t pocketing $29,000. The surgeon’s fee is a small fraction of the bill; most goes to the hospital, administrators, insurance processing, and various middlemen.

The real scandal isn’t that American doctors earn $218,000. The scandal is that a system this expensive delivers such mediocre outcomes while burning out the physicians trapped inside it.

Many American doctors report burnout. Not because they’re overpaid, but because they’re drowning in paperwork, fighting insurance companies, and experiencing moral injury from turning away patients who can’t pay.

Any honest healthcare reform must address physician compensation — not by cutting salaries in isolation, but by fixing the broken system that inflates costs at every level.

That’s the conversation neither party wants to have. Because it requires admitting that “just pay doctors less” is political fantasy, while “restructure medical education, malpractice law, and insurance billing” is hard legislative work.

The Middleman Economy

Between you and your doctor sits an entire economy of middlemen, each extracting revenue:

Pharmacy Benefit Managers (PBMs): The three largest (CVS Caremark, Express Scripts, OptumRx) processed nearly 80% of U.S. prescriptions in 2023 (FTC, “FTC Releases Interim Staff Report on Prescription Drug Middlemen,” July 9, 2024). They negotiate “rebates” from drug manufacturers, set formularies, and determine which pharmacies are “in network.”

Medical billing companies: Entire firms exist solely to help doctors navigate insurance billing codes.

Prior authorization: Insurers make doctors seek approval before treatment. When federal inspectors reviewed Medicare Advantage denials, 13% of denied prior authorization requests and 18% of denied payment requests actually met Medicare’s coverage rules (HHS Office of Inspector General, “Some Medicare Advantage Organization Denials of Prior Authorization Requests Raise Concerns About Beneficiary Access to Medically Necessary Care”, April 27, 2022). Doctors spend hours fighting for medically necessary care.

Hospital consolidation profits: Waves of hospital mergers have created regional near-monopolies in many markets. The result? Employers and private insurers paid hospitals an average of 224% of what Medicare would have paid for the same services in 2020 — more than double (RAND, “Prices Paid to Hospitals by Private Health Plans: Findings from Round 4 of an Employer-Led Transparency Initiative”, May 17, 2022).

Revenue cycle management companies: These firms help hospitals maximize billing to “recapture revenue.” Translation: finding ways to upcode, unbundle, and maximize charges.

Little of this apparatus exists on anything like this scale in other developed countries. Not because they’re anti-business or anti-innovation, but because their systems are designed around care delivery, not revenue extraction.

The Quality Myth

The standard American defense: “We pay more, but we get better care.”

The data says otherwise (World Bank, 2023):

Life expectancy:

United States: 78.4 years

Canada: 81.6 years

Switzerland: 84.2 years

Japan: 84.0 years

Infant mortality (per 1,000 live births):

United States: 5.5

Canada: 4.7

Germany: 3.1

Japan: 1.8

Maternal mortality (per 100,000 live births, modelled estimates):

United States: 17

Canada: 12

Germany: 4

United Kingdom: 8

Avoidable deaths: The Commonwealth Fund’s 2024 Mirror, Mirror report ranked the US last among 10 high-income countries, including last on health outcomes such as deaths that could have been prevented with timely, effective medical care.

Americans aren’t getting better outcomes. They’re getting bankrupted for mediocre results.

The Path Forward

The good news: We know exactly how to fix this. Other countries have solved these problems using different approaches — some with single-payer systems, some with regulated private insurance, some with hybrid models.

In Part 4, we’ll examine what works: How Switzerland achieved universal coverage with competing private insurers. How Germany combines public and private insurance with strict price controls. How Singapore uses mandatory health savings accounts. How Canada delivers care through provincial single-payer systems.

The solutions exist. The question isn’t “Can it be done?” The question is: “Why haven’t we done it?”

For 80 years, Americans have been told healthcare reform is impossible, too complex, or un-American. Meanwhile, Americans pay about double what comparable countries pay, for worse outcomes, while going bankrupt from medical bills that would cost a fraction of the price across any border.

It’s not impossible. It’s not too complex. And it’s not un-American to demand that a medical system serve patients instead of extracting maximum revenue.

In Part 4, we’ll show you exactly how other countries did it — and why their solutions could work in America.
