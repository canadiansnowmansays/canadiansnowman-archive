Every carnival has that one game the operator lets you win just often enough to keep the line moving. You whack the mole, it goes down, another one pops up somewhere else, and the whole apparatus is rigged so that the house — not you — decides how long the game runs. I have spent five instalments of this series documenting Donald Trump’s version of that game: lose a tariff authority in court, reach for the next statute on the shelf, repeat. Section 122 after the International Emergency Economic Powers Act died in February. Section 301 after Section 122’s ninety-day clock ran out. A threat to withhold trade from deficit countries in September unless the Federal Reserve cuts rates, dressed up in the same IEEPA ruling that supposedly ended this whole exercise.

What I had not anticipated, six instalments in, is that Congress itself would build him a new mallet. Not lend him one. Build it, wrap it, and hand it over with a bow, while telling itself — and Volodymyr Zelensky, and thirty-odd national-security experts who ought to know better — that this is what toughness on Russia looks like.

The bill, and the bait

The Lindsey Graham Sanctioning Russia Act is heading for a House vote this week, and on its face it reads like exactly what its Ukrainian champions want it to read like.

Most of the sanctions currently squeezing Russia exist by executive order, meaning a president can lift them with a signature whenever he feels like it. The Graham bill would convert a large share of those sanctions into statute, requiring congressional approval before they could be unwound. That is a real constraint, and it is why the late Senator Graham — no one’s idea of a Russia dove — championed it, and why Zelensky himself has pushed for its passage.

But buried inside that constraint is an escape hatch wide enough to drive the whole trade war through: a national-interest waiver that lets Trump suspend any provision in the bill simply by asserting it serves the national interest. Thomas Wright, writing for The Atlantic on September 15, 2026, laid out the mechanism plainly — a law built to look like handcuffs turns out to be a key. I won’t reproduce his phrasing here, but the gist is worth sitting with: this is not a bill that binds a president who has spent his second term demonstrating, repeatedly and in court, exactly how much he dislikes being bound.

And the sanctions provisions are, in a sense, the least of it. The part of the bill that should actually be setting off alarms in this series has almost nothing to do with sanctions at all. It’s about tariffs.

The real mechanism

Tucked into the legislation is authority to impose levies of up to one hundred percent on the five largest importers of Russian crude oil and the five largest importers of Russian natural gas — a list that, as things currently stand, includes China, India, France, Japan, Hungary, and Slovakia. There is a further provision letting the president tariff whichever five countries he judges to be the biggest facilitators of Russian sanctions evasion, a category vague enough to mean essentially whatever the White House wants it to mean on a given afternoon.

Peter Harrell, a trade lawyer who served in the Biden administration, told Wright this construction hands Trump more room to manoeuvre than the traditional tariff statutes ever did — no guardrails, no real restrictions. Readers of this series will recognise that sentence. It is, almost word for word, the same complaint that has trailed Section 122 and Section 301 through every instalment of Whack-a-Mole so far. The difference this time is that Congress isn’t the victim of Trump’s statute-shopping. Congress is the supplier.

Harrell raised a possibility that deserves particular attention from a Canadian vantage point, even though Canada is not on any of the bill’s top-five lists: Trump’s track record suggests he might simply choose to treat the European Union as a single country for tariff purposes, letting him hit the entire bloc at once rather than parsing out individual importers. If that reading holds, the “five countries” ceiling that makes the bill sound narrow and targeted evaporates the moment it meets an administration that has already shown, across two terms, exactly how elastic it considers statutory language to be.

TACO, refreshed

This is where the TACO framework — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — earns its keep again, though with a twist worth flagging. In the earlier instalments of this series, Trump supplied all four verbs himself: he threatened a tariff regime, allies and courts pushed back, he accommodated by finding a new statutory home for the same basic threat, and the administration obfuscated about which authority it was actually relying on this week. What the Graham bill introduces is a fifth actor performing the Accommodate step on Trump’s behalf, before he has even had to ask. Congress, in its eagerness to look tough on Russia, is doing the statute-shopping for him — pre-loading a tariff authority so broad that Trump barely needs to threaten anything. The mole doesn’t need to relocate. Congress is digging him a new hole.

There’s a precedent for how loosely this administration reads “national security” as a tariff pretext, and it isn’t hypothetical. Trump’s twenty-five percent tariff on India, since struck down by the Supreme Court, was billed as punishment for India’s purchases of Russian oil. Reporting at the time suggested the real trigger had less to do with oil and more to do with Trump’s irritation that Prime Minister Narendra Modi failed to credit him publicly for the India-Pakistan cease-fire brokered the previous year. If that is the standard of proof required to justify a tariff under the existing statutes, the Graham bill’s oil-and-gas top-five lists will not hold him for long. He does not need China, India, France, Japan, Hungary, and Slovakia to actually be the five worst offenders. He needs a plausible enough story that a court will let him keep collecting revenue while the case works its way through appeals — and as this series has documented since February, the courts have consistently allowed exactly that, tariff revenue flowing throughout litigation regardless of how the underlying case eventually resolves.

Why the guardrails failed twice

Democrats in the House tried, and failed, to amend the bill before it reaches the floor — proposals to stop Trump treating the EU as a single country, to tighten the national-interest waiver, and to strip out the broader tariff authority over countries with no real connection to Russian sanctions evasion. All three amendments died. Which means the version heading to a vote this week is the maximalist version: broad country-aggregation language undefined, a waiver wide enough to make the sanctions half of the bill largely decorative, and tariff authority over a “evasion facilitator” category that a White House press office could stretch to cover nearly anyone.

Eric Ciaramella of the Carnegie Endowment, who worked Ukraine policy on the National Security Council during Trump’s first term, offered the most honest defence of the bill I’ve seen: if Trump wants to hurt Europe, he already has plenty of tools to do it, so this is only a marginal addition to that risk, and it applies some political pressure on him to get tougher on Moscow. That is a coherent argument. It is also, on the evidence of this series, a bet that has not paid off across five prior instalments of watching Trump find new statutory homes for tariff authority the moment the old one gets struck down. Political pressure has not previously been the variable that moves this president off a tariff position that serves either his oil-price anxieties or his grievances. It is worth noting, too, that Trump waived sanctions on Russian oil at the start of the Iran war specifically to manage prices at the pump — and with the midterms roughly a month away and oil prices still elevated, there is little in his recent conduct to suggest Moscow is about to feel real pressure from this bill, sanctions provisions included.

The litigation clock, again

One pattern this series has returned to again and again is the gap between when a tariff authority gets challenged in court and when a court actually does anything about it. The February ruling against the IEEPA tariffs, which every trade lawyer following the case regarded as unusually fast by the standards of these things, still took the better part of a year to arrive — and for the entire run-up to that ruling, the administration kept collecting tariff revenue without interruption. Section 122 lasted its full ninety-day window before Section 301 was waiting in the wings to replace it. There is no reason to expect the Graham bill’s tariff provisions would move through the courts any faster, particularly given that this authority arrives with the added legitimacy of having been passed by Congress rather than asserted by executive order. A challenge to an EU-as-single-country reading, or to a stretched definition of “evasion facilitator,” would be litigating against a statute with a legislative record behind it — a materially harder case to win quickly than challenging an emergency-powers order that a president simply declared into existence.

That timeline matters enormously for anyone outside the top-five lists who assumes this bill doesn’t touch them. Canada, notably, appears nowhere in the oil or gas importer rankings Wright cites, and readers of [[great-canadian-trade-war]] might reasonably conclude this fight belongs to Europe and Asia. But the “facilitator of evasion” category was engineered — whether by design or by careless drafting, it hardly matters which — to be elastic enough that no G7 economy with any exposure to Russian energy markets, financial clearing, or shipping insurance can consider itself categorically excluded. If the pattern documented across five prior instalments of this series holds, the operative question is never really whether a country meets the statutory definition. It’s whether the White House finds it politically useful to claim that it does, and whether a court is willing to intervene before the tariff revenue has already been banked for the better part of a year.

What this means for the Tracker

Readers who have followed this series since October know the shape of the argument by now: a court closes one door, the administration finds another already ajar, and the country whose economy absorbs the resulting tariff usually has no seat at the table where the statutory reinterpretation happens. What’s new in the Graham bill is the source of the ajar door. It isn’t a decades-old statute Trump’s lawyers dusted off and repurposed, the way Section 122 and Section 301 were. It’s brand new, purpose-built, passed with bipartisan Ukrainian-solidarity cover, by a Congress that appears not to have read its own waiver language closely enough to notice what it was building.

If the House passes this bill in anything close to its current form, add it to the list alongside Section 122 and Section 301 — not as a mole Trump had to chase down, but as one Congress raised for him from an egg. The next instalment of this series will depend on how the Senate handles it, and on whether Trump moves quickly to test the EU-as-single-country reading Harrell flagged. Either way, the mallet keeps changing hands. It just changed hands faster this time, and from a direction nobody watching this series expected.
