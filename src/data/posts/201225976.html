In 1955, a Harvard historian named Louis Hartz published a book that would shape how a generation of Americans understood themselves.

The book was called The Liberal Tradition in America, and its argument was, by the standards of academic history, almost startlingly simple: Americans had always shared a common set of beliefs — a commitment to democracy, individual liberty, and property rights — and this shared liberal tradition explained why the United States had never produced a serious socialist movement, never experienced a genuine feudal order, and never succumbed to the authoritarian temptations that had destroyed so many European democracies in the first half of the twentieth century. America was exceptional. Its exceptionalism was explained by its consensus. And its consensus was so deep, so baked into the national character from the very beginning, that most Americans didn’t even recognize it as a set of beliefs. It was simply the water they swam in.

Hartz was wrong about a great many things, and the scholars who came after him spent several decades demonstrating exactly how wrong. The liberal consensus he described excluded, by design and by practice, the majority of the people living in America. The harmony he celebrated was purchased at a price paid almost entirely by people whose exclusion from the consensus was structural rather than accidental. His America was, in many ways, a white America — and his framework had almost nothing useful to say about race, which is to say it had almost nothing useful to say about the most consequential fact of American history.

But here is what is easy to forget, looking back from 2026: Hartz’s story held. It was incomplete, partial, sometimes dishonest in its omissions. And it held. It gave Americans a common language for understanding themselves, a shared framework for arguing about their country, a narrative infrastructure sturdy enough to support democratic self-governance across a continent for several generations. When it was smashed — as it deserved to be, on the merits — nothing was built to replace it. And the cost of that absence is what this series is about.

I. The Consensus Historians and Their World

Hartz was not alone. He was the most theoretically ambitious member of a school of mid-twentieth century American historians who shared, broadly, his conviction that America was defined by its agreements rather than its conflicts.

Richard Hofstadter, perhaps the most gifted writer among them, argued in The American Political Tradition that American politicians of left and right shared far more than they acknowledged — a commitment to capitalism, individualism, and the competitive marketplace of ideas that cut across party lines and made American political conflict a family quarrel rather than a civilizational struggle. Daniel Boorstin, in The Genius of American Politics, argued that Americans had always been practical rather than ideological — that the secret of American democracy was its distrust of abstract theory, its preference for muddling through over systematic transformation.

These were brilliant men writing brilliant books, and they were not propagandists, exactly. They acknowledged complexity. Hofstadter’s work was suffused with irony and psychological depth. But they were, almost all of them, writing in a specific moment — the Cold War — and for a specific purpose that shaped their conclusions in ways they did not always acknowledge.

The Cold War made the American story urgent in a way it had not been before. The United States was engaged in a global competition with the Soviet Union — a competition not merely of arms and economics but of ideas. The Soviets were offering the world a story: that capitalism was exploitation dressed in democratic clothes, that America’s vaunted freedoms were available only to the privileged, that the real America was the America of lynchings and Pinkertons and company towns. The consensus historians were, in part, offering a counter-story: that America’s liberal tradition was real, that its democracy was genuine, that its freedoms — however imperfectly distributed — were worth defending and, crucially, worth emulating.

Many of them were the children or grandchildren of Jewish immigrants who had found in America an improbable refuge — from pogroms in Russia, from poverty in Eastern Europe, from the gathering catastrophe that would become the Holocaust. They had personal reasons to believe in the American story, and personal reasons to articulate it clearly. They wrote best-sellers. They contributed to popular magazines. They advised presidents. They shaped the self-understanding of a generation of Americans who came of age believing, broadly, in what their country claimed to stand for.

It was an incomplete story. It was, in important ways, a flattering mirror held at a deliberate angle. And it held a nation together.

II. What the Consensus Got Wrong

The consensus historians’ central failure was not dishonesty, exactly. It was selective attention elevated to a governing principle.

Consider the question of democracy — one of the consensus school’s central claims. Hartz and his colleagues were right that American democracy was, by nineteenth-century standards, extraordinarily broad. As Appelbaum notes, more than half of adult white men could vote in Revolutionary America, while in England only about one in seven could do the same. Within a generation of independence, eighty or ninety percent of adult white men in many states held the franchise. This was genuinely unprecedented. The consensus historians were not wrong to call attention to it.

What they consistently underemphasized was what that percentage was a percentage of. White men. Not women, who under coverture had their legal personhood subsumed into their husbands’. Not Black Americans, most of whom were enslaved. Not Native Americans, denied citizenship on their own continent. When you count the people who were excluded from the extraordinary democratic experiment, the extraordinary democracy starts to look rather less extraordinary — more like a well-organized minority government of propertied white men who had developed unusually sophisticated mechanisms for managing disagreements among themselves while maintaining, with considerable violence, the exclusion of everyone else.

The consensus historians also had almost nothing useful to say about race — which, as W.E.B. Du Bois had argued decades earlier, was the central fact of American history. Hofstadter’s The American Political Tradition discussed the Civil War and Reconstruction with the kind of distanced, ironic detachment appropriate to a political misunderstanding rather than the apocalyptic moral struggle it actually was. Slavery was, in the consensus framework, an aberration from the liberal tradition rather than a foundation of it — an embarrassing deviation from the American creed rather than one of the primary mechanisms by which American wealth was accumulated and American power consolidated.

This was not merely an analytical error. It was a moral failure that had political consequences. A national narrative that described the Black American experience as peripheral to the main story of liberty and democracy left Black Americans no legitimate place in the story of their own country — and left white Americans free to imagine that the story of their country was essentially complete, that the imperfections had been corrected or were being corrected, that the work was more or less done.

It was not done. It has never been done. And when a new generation of scholars arrived to say so, the consensus story — which had already been doing most of its work through selective omission rather than outright falsehood — did not survive the confrontation.

III. The Moment of Rupture

The sixties broke things that needed breaking, and some things that didn’t.

What needed breaking was the consensus school’s comfortable elision of race, gender, and class. What the rupture produced, as a byproduct of the necessary correction, was the destruction of the only widely shared national narrative Americans had — without any agreement about what should replace it.

The historians who entered the academy in the 1960s and 1970s brought with them questions the consensus school had never asked. What did the American story look like from the bottom up rather than the top down? What did liberty mean to the people for whom it was legally unavailable? What did democracy mean to the women whose legal personhood had been dissolved into their husbands’? What did the frontier mean to the peoples whose land it was before it became a frontier?

These were the right questions. They produced scholarship of real importance — histories of slavery and its economics, of women’s political organization, of Indigenous resistance and survival, of labor movements and their suppression, of the Black freedom struggle in all its complexity and courage. The picture of America that emerged from this scholarship was far more complete, far more honest, and far more useful than anything the consensus school had produced.

But — and this is the crucial structural problem — the new historians were not, in the main, trying to build a new national narrative. They were trying to dismantle the old one. They were suspicious of national narrative as a category. As Jill Lepore told Appelbaum, “People were suspicious of any national story as the handmaiden of ethnic nationalism, or white nationalism.” Patriotic histories had been used for too long to buttress the systems they were now working to expose. So they decentred the nation. They wrote the histories of groups rather than the history of the country. They challenged the master narrative without constructing a replacement.

Scholars largely stopped writing single-volume histories of the United States. The last major attempt before Lepore’s These Truths in 2018 had been decades earlier. The field fractured into subfields — women’s history, African American history, labour history, environmental history — each producing important work, none of them organized around the question of what America was and what it meant to be part of it.

The flattering mirror was smashed. The room where it had stood was left dark.

IV. Into the Vacuum

Nature abhors a vacuum. So does politics.

The scholars who dismantled the consensus story did not intend to hand the American narrative to the culture warriors. But intention and consequence are different things, and the consequence of a generation of academic history that treated the national story as suspect was that the national story was left to be told by people with far less interest in truth than in mobilization.

On the right, popular historians like Bill O’Reilly and Newt Gingrich filled the gap with a simplified, triumphalist account of American greatness that looked, in its broad outlines, like the consensus school with the complexity removed and the celebration amplified. Their books sold millions of copies. Their version of the American story reached audiences that academic historians had largely stopped trying to reach.

On the left, the critique of the national narrative became, in some quarters, the narrative itself — a story about a story, a meta-history whose primary content was the exposure of the old history’s lies. This was intellectually necessary work. It was not, by itself, a story that could hold a country together.

The political system, meanwhile, proved entirely unable to manage the dispute. In 1994, as Appelbaum documents, when the federal government attempted to develop common history standards for American schools — standards that reflected the new, more honest scholarship — the result was a political firestorm. Lynne Cheney, who had commissioned the work, denounced the standards she had requested. Rush Limbaugh called them an attempt to teach students that America was “a rotten place.” The Senate voted to reject the standards, 99-1 — one of the most lopsided votes in that body’s history, on a subject that had nothing to do with national security or economic catastrophe but everything to do with who got to tell the American story.

That vote was, I would argue, the moment the abandonment became official. When the United States Senate voted 99-1 that there was no acceptable version of American history education, it voted, in effect, that there was no acceptable American story. The argument about what the story should say had become so heated that the only politically safe position was to have no story at all.

That is the position America has occupied, more or less, ever since.

V. What Was Lost

I want to be careful here, because the argument I am making is easy to misread.

I am not arguing that the consensus historians were right. They were not — not fully, not honestly, not in ways adequate to the country’s actual history or the needs of its excluded citizens. The story they told deserved to be challenged. The scholarship that challenged it was necessary and valuable and has made American historical understanding immeasurably richer.

What I am arguing is that the form of what they produced — a coherent national narrative, accessible to non-specialists, organized around the question of what America was and what it meant — was itself valuable, independent of its content. And that the destruction of that form, without any serious effort to build a better one, left a void that has proven far more dangerous than the distorted story it replaced.

Jill Lepore, who in 2018 published These Truths — the first serious attempt by a major historian to write a single-volume national history in decades — understood this. She understood that the academy’s retreat from national narrative had not produced a more honest public discourse about American history. It had produced a less honest one, because the people filling the vacuum were not constrained by the standards of evidence and argument that even the most partial academic historian had to observe.

After These Truths was published, Lepore was astonished by the reader response. Letters came from across the country, from people who had been waiting — without knowing they were waiting — for someone to try to tell the whole story. One woman wrote to say she had bought the book to study for her citizenship exam and had kept reading, enthralled, page by page through the whole journey. “I really feel like I belong here now,” she wrote.

That woman’s letter contains, I think, the answer to the question of why the story matters. She did not feel she belonged because the story was flattering. She felt she belonged because the story was complete — because it included the darkness alongside the light, the betrayals alongside the ideals, the long unfinished work alongside the genuine achievements. She could see herself in it. Not despite its honesty, but because of it.

The consensus story, partial and flattering as it was, gave a generation of Americans enough of a shared framework to argue about their country together. The destruction of that story, without any replacement, gave the next generation a vacuum. And what has grown in the vacuum is not honest complexity. It is competing mythologies, each more interested in winning the argument than in telling the truth.

That is the inheritance of 1994. That is what the 99-1 vote produced.

And that is why, at 250, a room full of distinguished Americans could not agree on the word for loving their country.
