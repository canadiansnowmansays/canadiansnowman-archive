The introduction to this series promised not to leave Poe’s prisoner without mentioning that he survives. This is that part.

Of the 96 reversals in the Reversal Ledger, ten were stopped. They were blocked by courts, stalled in Congress or allowed to expire before they could take permanent hold. A further five were partly blocked, and twelve more are still being fought in court. It would be easy to tell the story of the last twenty months as one of uninterrupted collapse. The ledger does not support that story. The guardrails bent in many places, and in some they held.

The question this part asks is why those, and not others.

The ten

Tariffs. On 20 February 2026, in Learning Resources v. Trump, the Supreme Court ruled six to three that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act of 1977 does not authorise the president to impose tariffs. The case was brought by an educational-toy company from Illinois. The administration had used the Act to justify sweeping tariffs on goods from nearly every country.

Birthright citizenship. On 30 June 2026, in Trump v. Barbara, the Court struck down by five votes to four the executive order that would have denied citizenship to children born in the United States to parents without permanent legal status. The Fourteenth Amendment’s guarantee, ratified in 1868, stands.

The Federal Reserve. On 29 June 2026, in Trump v. Cook, the Court refused to let the president fire Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook. Fed governors keep the protection, written into the Federal Reserve Act of 1913, that they may be removed only for cause.

The Kennedy Center. The centre’s board voted twice to add Trump’s name to it. On 15 September 2026, a federal judge blocked the change for the second time, ruling that the 1964 statute naming the building for President Kennedy can be changed only by Congress.

Offshore wind. In December 2025 the Interior Department halted construction on five offshore wind projects. In January 2026 courts let two of them, Revolution Wind and Empire Wind, resume. Revolution Wind finished installing its turbines in September.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. The bureau’s acting leadership stopped requesting the Federal Reserve funding that the Dodd-Frank Act provides. On 25 September 2026 a federal judge ruled that halt unlawful.

Public Service Loan Forgiveness. A rule that would have excluded employers with a “substantial illegal purpose” from the programme, a category critics said was designed to cover organisations the administration disliked, was vacated nationwide on 30 June 2026, a day before it took effect.

DACA. The first administration’s attempt to end protection for people brought to the country as children was blocked by the Supreme Court in 2020. DACA’s deportation protections survive, though a 2025 appeals court ruling against the programme may end its work permits in Texas.

Public land sales. A proposal from Senator Mike Lee of Utah to sell between 2.2 and 3.3 million acres of federal land was dropped from the 2025 budget bill after protests from hunters, anglers and western Republicans. No new vehicle for it has appeared.

Planned Parenthood. The 2025 budget law barred Planned Parenthood from Medicaid for one year. The ban expired in July 2026.

What they have in common

Look at those ten together and a pattern appears. It is not a perfect pattern, but it is strong enough to be useful.

The text was clear. The most decisive defeats came where the law said something plain and the administration asked the courts to read it otherwise. The Fourteenth Amendment says “all persons born or naturalized in the United States, and subject to the jurisdiction thereof, are citizens.” The Kennedy Center statute names the building for Kennedy. The Federal Reserve Act says governors may be removed “for cause.” The emergency powers law of 1977 says nothing about tariffs, and the Constitution gives Congress the power “to lay and collect taxes, duties, imposts and excises.” Even a Court with a clear majority sympathetic to presidential power, the same Court that decided Slaughter the same week, would not read those words out of existence.

Compare the reversals that locked in. Slaughter turned on a question the Constitution does not directly answer: how far the president’s executive power extends over agencies Congress created. The rulemaking reversals of Part II turned on statutes written broadly enough to allow more than one reading. Where the text left room, the reversal usually prevailed. Where it did not, the reversal usually failed.

The reversal was improvised. The introduction noted that half of the ten stopped items were the president’s own initiatives rather than proposals drafted in advance by think tanks, industries or legal scholars: the tariffs, the Kennedy Center, the attempt to fire Cook, the halt to offshore wind and DACA. These were decisions made quickly, often announced before the legal case for them had been built. The reversals that held, by contrast, such as the civil service rule, the removal of the environmental review regulations and Slaughter itself, had been planned for years by people who knew which statute to cite and which court to file in. The birthright citizenship order is the exception that proves the rule. It rested on a legal theory that a few scholars had argued for decades, and it still lost, because the text was against it.

Someone with resources sued. The guardrails do not operate on their own. Each of the ten was stopped because someone with standing, money and lawyers went to court or to Congress. A toy company challenged the tariffs. States and immigrant families challenged the birthright order. Lisa Cook challenged her own dismissal. The owners of Revolution Wind, with billions of dollars invested in the water, challenged the stop-work order. Hunters and anglers, a constituency Republicans in the West cannot ignore, stopped the land sale. The pattern is uncomfortable for anyone hoping the system protects the powerless. It protected people and institutions with the means to defend themselves, and it protected others only when their interests happened to coincide.

The partial victories

Between the ten clear victories and the reversals that locked in lies a wide grey band, and much of what will happen next will be decided there.

A federal judge has blocked the policy ending gender care for about 2,000 transgender federal prisoners, and an appeals court refused to lift the block; the administration asked the Supreme Court for emergency relief at the end of September. A judge stayed the changes to the childhood vaccine schedule in March 2026, and an appeals court heard argument on 6 October. An appeals court held the transgender military ban unconstitutional in June 2026, although the Supreme Court has let it operate in the meantime. The cap on the overhead costs universities can charge to National Institutes of Health grants was blocked and then abandoned when the government declined to appeal. A judge voided the mass layoffs at Voice of America, though the journalists have not been allowed back. More than 1,400 congregations won protection from immigration raids at their places of worship, but only for themselves.

These cases show the second great feature of the period: the Supreme Court’s emergency docket. Again and again, the Court has allowed policies to take effect while the lawsuits against them go on: the military ban, the passport rule, the ending of humanitarian parole, the termination of research grants. Its interim orders are not final rulings. But a policy that operates for two years while the courts deliberate does much of its work regardless of the eventual verdict. Soldiers are discharged, passports are reissued and grants are not renewed, whatever the final judgment says.

The price of winning

Even the clean victories came at a cost the ledger records.

The Kennedy Center keeps its name, but after the court ruled against it, the board voted to close the main building until 2028. The national cultural centre will spend two years dark. The tariffs were struck down, but the duties collected before the ruling were collected, and businesses that paid them, or passed them on to customers, will spend years trying to recover the money. Revolution Wind finished its turbines, but the episode taught every investor in American offshore wind that a project with all its permits can be stopped by an order from Washington, and the industry’s cost of capital reflects that. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau won its funding back, but the staff and cases lost while it was idle are lost. The Planned Parenthood ban expired after a year, but clinics that closed during that year have not all reopened, and a 2025 Supreme Court decision now lets states exclude the organisation from their own Medicaid programmes.

A guardrail that stops a car after it has gone halfway through has still done its job. But the car is damaged, and so is the guardrail.

What the walls teach

There is a lesson here for whoever tries to restore what was lost, and it mirrors the lesson for those who did the losing.

The reversals that failed were those that ran against clear text, were improvised and met determined opponents with resources. The reversals that held were those that exploited ambiguity, were prepared in advance and faced no one able to stop them. A restoration will succeed or fail by the same rules. It will last if it is written into clear law, planned before the election rather than after, and supported by constituencies with the standing and means to defend it in court. It will fail if it relies on executive orders drafted in the first week and on the hope that judges will read ambiguous statutes generously.

In Poe’s story, the prisoner frees himself from his straps with the help of the rats, but the walls still close in. Then General Lasalle’s army arrives and pulls him back from the edge. The guardrails of the American system are something like the rats: real, and essential, and not enough on their own. The rescue, if it comes, will come from outside the courtroom.

The next part of this series turns to the people who designed the machine in the first place: who wrote the reversals, how long they spent preparing, and what their patience can teach the other side.
