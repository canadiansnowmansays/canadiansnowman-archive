Iranian missiles and drones over Dubai skyline, March 2026 — the moment that tested the UAE’s influencer “Advertiser Permit” system.

In the early days of March 2026, as Iranian missiles and drones lit up the skies above Dubai, the city’s enormous population of social-media influencers faced a choice. Some of them posted what they saw — explosions near their hotels, plumes of smoke above the Marina, people packing bags at the Burj Khalifa. The videos gathered millions of views. Then, within days, the United Arab Emirates Public Prosecution office posted a warning: circulating rumors through social media platforms would be “subject to legal accountability.” A BBC analysis of 129 influencer posts from Dubai in the days that followed found they contained nearly identical language — “stability,” “safety,” “strong leadership” — sometimes uploaded within minutes of each other.

The influencers had understood the terms of their arrangement. They had not needed individual phone calls. They had needed only to understand who issued the permit that allowed them to operate — and what would happen to the permit if they said the wrong things.

Dubai’s system is explicit: an Advertiser Permit, media content standards, a public prosecution warning. It is a permission structure whose terms are written down, whose enforcement is formal, whose compliance is documented. It is, in that sense, more honest than the American version.

The American version has never been written down. It has operated through the signals that institutions send about which expressions carry consequences and which do not — through the presence or absence of sanctions, through the language of the rebuke or the non-rebuke, through the calculation that every targeted community has to make about whether the cost of speaking exceeds the cost of silence.

This part of the series examines how the American permission structure for anti-Muslim expression was systematically rebuilt — how the penalties that had previously constrained explicit religious bigotry in mainstream political discourse were removed, one by one, over the decade between 2010 and 2020, and what the completed structure looks like in 2026.

The Penalty Structure and Its Removal

Every political culture has a penalty structure — a set of social, institutional, and political costs associated with certain kinds of expression. The penalty structure does not eliminate the expression; it constrains it, pushes it to the margins, makes it expensive enough that most mainstream political actors decline to use it.

American political culture maintained a penalty structure around explicit religious and ethnic bigotry that, while never comprehensive or consistently applied, had real effects. When Kevin McCarthy stripped Steve King of his committee assignments in 2019 for defending “white nationalist” and “white supremacist” in a newspaper interview, he was activating the penalty structure. When Republican leaders distanced themselves from David Duke’s 1991 Louisiana gubernatorial campaign, they were activating the penalty structure. The penalty was not criminal. It was institutional — the withdrawal of standing, committee assignments, endorsements, party support. It was enough to make explicit bigotry politically costly for most mainstream politicians most of the time.

The penalty structure for anti-Muslim bigotry was never as robust as for other forms of racial and religious targeting. The Ground Zero Mosque episode of 2010 demonstrated that explicitly anti-Muslim political arguments could circulate in the mainstream without triggering the penalties that would have attached to comparable arguments about other communities. But there were still constraints. Calling for a ban on Muslim immigration was a fringe position in 2010. Using a mosque shooting as political material required some degree of rhetorical management. The space for explicit anti-Muslim expression in mainstream political discourse was larger than for other communities, but it was not unlimited.

Between 2010 and 2016, the constraints were progressively loosened. The mechanism was not a single decision or a single figure. It was cumulative — each loosening making the next one easier, each mainstream acceptance of previously marginal language normalizing the next step.

The Trump Candidacy and the Muslim Ban

Donald Trump, December 2015, proposing a “total and complete shutdown of Muslims entering the United States”

Donald Trump’s announcement, in December 2015, of a proposed “total and complete shutdown of Muslims entering the United States” was, in the context of the history this series has traced, the most explicit invocation of the exception clause by a major American political figure since the Know-Nothing era. It was not framed in theological terms — Trump described it as a security measure, necessary until “our country’s representatives can figure out what is going on” — but its explicit targeting of a religious group, by name, as a category to be excluded from the country, was unambiguous.

The Republican primary field’s response was the first major test of whether the penalty structure had survived. Several candidates criticized the proposal. Jeb Bush called it “unhinged.” Chris Christie said it was the wrong approach. Marco Rubio suggested it was unconstitutional. But none of the criticism stuck, and none of the candidates who made it was willing to sustain it through the months that followed. Trump’s polling increased after the announcement. The primary electorate responded positively. The other candidates recalculated.

By the time Trump won the Republican nomination in July 2016, the Muslim ban had become not merely an acceptable position within the Republican primary electorate but a popular one. The penalty structure had not merely failed to activate — it had revealed itself as hollow. The institutional mechanisms that should have imposed costs on the proposal had encountered a political environment in which the costs had already been reduced to near zero, and had declined to impose them anyway.

The travel ban that Trump signed on January 27, 2017 — seven days after taking office — was the executive order version of the December 2015 proposal. It targeted seven Muslim-majority countries. It was challenged in courts across the country. Multiple versions were struck down. The third version survived to the Supreme Court, which upheld it in Trump v. Hawaii in 2018 in the same opinion that acknowledged Korematsu had been “gravely wrong the day it was decided.”

The court’s simultaneous repudiation of Korematsu and validation of the travel ban was the most precise illustration the series has encountered of the gap between constitutional principle and constitutional practice. The principle — that religious targeting is constitutionally prohibited — was reaffirmed. The practice — a policy that the president had publicly described as a Muslim ban, implemented against majority-Muslim countries — was upheld. The gap between them is the exception clause, still operating, in a Supreme Court opinion.

Laura Looms Large

Laura Loomer in 2025, with direct access to the White House and influence on MAGA personnel decisions.

Laura Loomer is a useful figure for understanding how the exception clause’s most extreme expressions moved from margin to mainstream in the Trump era — not because she is important in herself, but because her trajectory illustrates the mechanism.

Loomer first gained notoriety as a political provocateur in 2017, when she stormed the stage during a Central Park performance of Julius Caesar to protest what she characterized as anti-Trump content. By 2018, she had been banned from Twitter, Facebook, PayPal, and Uber for anti-Muslim content that violated those platforms’ hate speech policies. She had described Islam as a “cancer,” called for Muslim immigrants to be banned from holding public office, and described Ilhan Omar — then a newly elected congresswoman — as someone who “supports female genital mutilation.”

She ran for Congress in Florida in 2020, winning the Republican primary in a safely Democratic district and losing the general election. She ran again in 2022, losing in the primary. She remained in proximity to Trump throughout — attending Mar-a-Lago events, maintaining communication with Trump’s inner circle.

By 2025, she had a direct line to the president of the United States. She was, by multiple accounts, influential on personnel decisions — specifically on decisions to remove national security officials she deemed insufficiently loyal to the MAGA agenda. A figure whose content had been removed from every major social media platform for anti-Muslim hate speech was influencing the staffing of the United States National Security Council.

This is the fifth element of the template at its most complete: not merely the absence of a penalty for explicit religious bigotry, but the active rewarding of it. The person at the top had not merely declined to withdraw the license. He had promoted the license-holder.

Ogles and Johnson, Revisited

Rep. Andy Ogles (R-TN) — mainstream Republican openly stating “Muslims don’t belong in American society

Against this background, Andy Ogles’s March 9 post — “Muslims don’t belong in American society. Pluralism is a lie.” — is not an aberration. It is a data point in a progression that has been running for at least a decade.

Ogles is not Loomer. He is a more conventional Republican politician who has found that explicit anti-Muslim rhetoric is electorally useful in his district without carrying the penalties it would have carried in 2005 or 2010. He has pushed for the denaturalization and deportation of New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani. He has called for immigration bans from Muslim-majority countries. His March 9 post was an escalation of positions he had held publicly for years without consequence.

The consequence question is answered by Mike Johnson’s response.

Johnson said Ogles had used “different language than I would use.” He then validated Sharia law anxiety. He did not say the statement was wrong. He did not say it violated American constitutional values. He did not say that Muslim Americans are entitled to the same rights as other Americans without condition. He said: different language.

The Speaker of the House, the third in line to the presidency of a country whose founding document prohibits religious discrimination, offered the most explicit political permission structure available short of endorsement: I would have said it differently. The implication: the sentiment is available.

That is Dubai’s Advertiser Permit in the American idiom. You don’t need a formal document. You need the Speaker to tell you that the only problem with saying Muslims don’t belong in America is the specific phrasing.

The Loyalty Test, Final Form

Counter-protesters defending religious liberty — the recurring American counter-tradition against the exception clause.

The series has traced the loyalty test through each of its iterations: the Puritan demand for theological conformity, the Know-Nothing demand that Catholics renounce papal authority, the federal government’s demand that the Latter-day Saints renounce plural marriage, the 1960 demand that Kennedy prove his church would not govern him, the post-9/11 demand that Muslim Americans publicly condemn terrorism on a schedule no other community was asked to meet, the 2010 demand that Muslim Americans prove their sensitivity to non-Muslim grief by voluntarily limiting their constitutional rights.

In 2026, the loyalty test has reached its logical endpoint: there is no test that can be passed, because the demand is not for a demonstration of loyalty but for an acknowledgment of conditional membership. “Muslims don’t belong in American society” cannot be satisfied by any performance of loyalty, because it is not a loyalty demand. It is a belonging claim — the assertion that membership in this religious community is, in itself, disqualifying.

The Houston speech cannot answer this argument. Wilford Woodruff’s Manifesto cannot answer this argument. The argument is not that Muslim Americans have failed to prove their loyalty. It is that their membership in the community — their religion itself — places them outside the scope of the American promise.

That is the exception clause at maximum extension: not the asterisk that qualifies the promise, but the argument that the promise never applied.

It is the argument that Roger Williams rejected in 1636, that the Quakers died on Boston Common refusing, that every targeted community in this series has had to confront in some form. It has never been finally answered. It keeps returning, in each generation, wearing the face of the current target.

In March 2026, it is wearing the face of forty thousand Muslim Americans in Nashville, and a congregation in Columbia, Tennessee, whose mosque sits less than a mile from the office of the man making the argument.
