There are rules in Washington that are written down, and rules that everyone simply knows. The second kind are often the more durable, because they survive by habit rather than enforcement. Nobody has to tell a federal prosecutor to keep quiet about a candidate in the last weeks before an election. Nobody has to explain why agents do not turn up on a politician’s doorstep in October. It is understood, the way it is understood that one does not bring a brass band to a funeral.

On Monday, October 12, 2026, homeland security agents brought the brass band.

According to Devlin Barrett, Hamed Aleaziz, Ernesto Londoño and Alan Feuer of the New York Times, writing on October 12, federal agents executed a search warrant that morning at the Washington-area home of Representative Ilhan Omar of Minnesota. Election Day is November 3. The search took place twenty-two days before it, deep inside the 60-day window in which the Justice Department has, for decades, avoided exactly this kind of visible, overt investigative step against a political figure.

Omar has been on this series’ roster since its early parts, listed among the targets whose treatment by the administration seemed to follow from their opinions of the president rather than from anything in a case file. Until Monday, her entry was mostly a matter of threats: things said on podcasts, at rallies and on Fox News. On Monday, the threats acquired a warrant.

What the agents were looking for

The Times reports that the warrant, executed by Homeland Security Investigations (the criminal investigative arm of ICE), lists a series of potential immigration crimes, including false statements and fraud in immigration paperwork, plus a general conspiracy charge. A Justice Department spokeswoman confirmed only that agents had conducted a judicially authorized search of a home in the Washington area as part of an ongoing federal criminal investigation, and declined to say more.

The theory of the case, so far as anyone outside the investigation can see it, is remarkable less for its ambition than for its distance in time. Omar was born in Mogadishu in October 1982. Her family fled the Somali civil war when she was about eight, spent years in a Kenyan refugee camp, and was admitted to the United States as refugees in the mid-1990s. She became a citizen in 2000, at the age of 17.

Investigators, according to the Times, have been examining whether her late father, Nur Omar Mohamed, lied or left things out when he sought legal status, and whether his daughter derived her citizenship from a man who may not himself have been eligible to naturalize. Nur Omar Mohamed died in 2020. The paperwork in question is roughly three decades old.

If prosecutors could establish that the citizenship was improperly obtained, the Times notes, the Justice Department could seek to denaturalize her, civilly or criminally, and a denaturalized person can then be placed in deportation proceedings. That is the endpoint the administration’s senior figures have been describing out loud for most of a year. The search is the first step that looks like a means to it.

There is a curious problem buried in the timeline. Late in 2025, the Times recalls, White House border czar Tom Homan told Newsmax that earlier efforts to examine Omar’s immigration history had run into the statute of limitations. Criminal immigration fraud offences carry limitation periods; most of what a refugee family filled in during the 1990s would be well beyond them. Civil denaturalization has no such clock, which is presumably why Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin keeps telling interviewers that lying on an application has “no expiration.” Yet the warrant reportedly lists crimes, including a conspiracy count. Which conduct falls within a limitation period, and who the conspirators are supposed to be, are questions the public record does not answer. A conspiracy charge has a way of appearing when the underlying acts are too old to charge on their own.

Saying the verdict first

The Part XXVI test, set out in June when John Bolton pleaded guilty, asks a handful of plain questions. Does the investigation predate the grievance? Was it built by career prosecutors across administrations? Does it rest on the defendant’s own words? Does it survive contact with a judge or grand jury?

There is one more signal the test did not need to spell out, because it seemed too obvious: real prosecutions do not announce their conclusions in advance. The officials running this one have done little else.

In March 2026, the Times reports, Vice President JD Vance told a conservative podcaster that the open question was what remedies existed “now that we know that she has committed immigration fraud,” and how to “build a case.” The sequence deserves a second look: conviction first, case afterward. Vance said he had discussed the matter with the White House senior aide Stephen Miller.

In September, Mullin went further. Speaking with the podcaster Benny Johnson at the Republican Party’s convention in Dallas, he repeated, as established fact, the old internet claim that Omar had married her own brother to bring him into the country, and promised deportation if wrongdoing could be proven. As Virginia Grace McKinnon reported for the Daily Signal on September 14, 2026, Mullin also offered the convention a forecast: “Something’s going to happen.” Homan followed on Fox News on September 13, confirming that HSI was investigating, declining to discuss specifics, and insisting there was nothing political about it.

That brother-marriage claim has been around since 2016. Earlier federal inquiries into it, the Times notes, closed without charges. The Minneapolis Star Tribune, after its own reporting years ago, could neither confirm nor refute it. Omar has called it absurd and offensive. None of that slowed the Secretary of Homeland Security, who treated it from a convention stage as something “we know.”

Then, on Saturday, October 10, two days before the agents arrived, President Trump told a rally in Tennessee that Omar “comes from Somalia,” a country he described as having no government and no police, only “crime and squalor.” Of Somalis in Minnesota, he added that what they had done “should never be allowed and let’s see what happens.”

Let’s see what happens. Forty-eight hours later, something happened.

What the test shows

Run honestly, the Part XXVI test does not produce a clean verdict here, and it is worth saying where it cuts the other way.

There is some prior history. The Biden Justice Department opened its own inquiry into Omar in 2024, examining her finances, campaign spending and interactions with a foreign citizen, as the Times has previously reported. That inquiry predates this administration. And the search warrant was not rubber-stamped. Prosecutors first applied more than a week before Monday, and the judge approved it only after an extended back-and-forth about constitutional issues, including the Speech or Debate Clause that protects members of Congress. A judge found probable cause. That is a real fact, and this series does not get to wave it away because it is inconvenient.

But probable cause to search is a low bar, set deliberately low, and it is not a finding that anyone committed a crime. And the rest of the test reads poorly for the government.

Does it predate the grievance? The immigration track, the one that produced Monday’s warrant, does not. Trump’s hostility to Omar dates to his first term, when crowds at his rallies chanted “send her back.” The current investigation, the Times reports, was opened “in recent months,” after Homan, Vance and Mullin had already declared her guilty in public.

Who built it? Not career prosecutors working steadily across administrations, but Homeland Security, under a secretary who had promised a result on a podcast.

Does it rest on the defendant’s own words? No. It rests on what her father may have written on forms when she was a child, and he is not available to explain them.

Has it produced a plea, or survived a grand jury? Not yet. No charge has been filed.

Compare Bolton, the one case on this list that passed. His investigation began under one administration and continued under another. It turned on classified material in his own hands and his own writing. It ended with his own admission. Omar’s file turns on a dead man’s 1990s paperwork, revived by officials who announced the outcome before the evidence.

The rule, and who used to invoke it

The 60-day rule is not a statute. It is, as the Times explains, an interpretation of department policy, urging prosecutors to avoid overt and unnecessary investigative steps that could affect an election, particularly when those steps could wait until afterward without harming the case. There are exceptions: evidence about to be destroyed, crimes still underway, people in danger. Nothing reported so far suggests that a search of a congresswoman’s home over thirty-year-old paperwork could not have waited until November 4.

What makes the rule worth defending is that, for most of its life, the department has treated a publicly visible search of a politician’s home or business as the very thing it was meant to prevent. It has a history, and the history runs straight through this series.

The most famous breach in living memory belongs to James Comey, who on October 28, 2016, eleven days before that election, told Congress that the FBI had found new emails relevant to the Clinton investigation. The Justice Department’s inspector general later judged that a serious error. Comey has spent much of 2026 on the receiving end of the department’s attention, as Parts XXIX and XXXI documented. The man most associated with breaking the norm is now a target of the administration that most loudly claims to honour it.

And loudly is the word. In 2024, Trump and his lawyers, some of whom now run the Justice Department, accused special counsel Jack Smith of violating the 60-day rule when he filed papers in September of that year describing the evidence in the election-obstruction case. As the Times points out, that filing was made in response to a court order, which the policy does not cover. The objection was made anyway, at length, as a matter of principle.

Principles that apply only to the people who invoke them have a simpler name. In the vocabulary of this series’ TACO framework, the 60-day rule has been through the full cycle. It was threatened as a weapon against Smith; accommodated for as long as it protected Trump; and, now that it stands in the way, obfuscated with the language of an “ongoing federal criminal investigation” that, after decades of rumour, apparently could not wait three more weeks.

Why her, why now

Omar’s own seat is not in play. She represents one of the safest Democratic districts in the country, covering Minneapolis, and faced no serious competitor in her primary. A search of her home will not change the result in Minnesota’s 5th District.

That is precisely why it is worth asking what it is for. The search does not need to defeat Omar to do its work. It needs to put an image in circulation: federal agents at the door of a Somali-born, Muslim congresswoman, three weeks before a national election, while the president tells rally crowds that Somalis should never have been allowed to do what they did to Minnesota. The image is the message, and it reaches well beyond one Minneapolis district. Fox News had the story before the Times did.

It also fits a pattern Omar has been describing all year. In December 2025, as Axios reported on December 15, 2025, she said ICE agents had pulled over her U.S.-born son after a stop at Target and released him only when he produced his passport; ICE later said it had no record of the stop. On January 27, 2026, a man sprayed her with apple cider vinegar from a syringe at a Minneapolis town hall, and Trump suggested she had probably arranged it herself. On August 6, as the Minnesota Reformer reported on August 10, a Richfield police officer found an undercover HSI agent sitting outside her town hall in an SUV whose plates did not match the vehicle. The agent first said she was event security, then said she was there for an unrelated drug operation, then left. Omar wrote to DHS that, at worst, it confirmed fears that ICE was being used to surveil political opponents.

Taken one at a time, each of these can be explained away. Taken together, they describe an agency that has spent ten months circling one member of Congress, and on Monday it landed.

The Speech or Debate problem

There is one more reason the Justice Department historically approached searches of members of Congress with something close to dread. In May 2006, FBI agents searched the Capitol Hill office of Representative William Jefferson of Louisiana, a Democrat later convicted of bribery. It was the first search of a sitting member’s congressional office, and it provoked bipartisan fury. The D.C. Circuit later ruled that the search had violated the Speech or Debate Clause, because agents had reviewed legislative material without first letting Jefferson identify what was privileged.

Jefferson was caught with $90,000 in his freezer. The case against him was real. Even so, the courts insisted the process for searching a legislator be careful, because the power to rummage through a lawmaker’s papers is the power to read the legislature’s mail.

Monday’s search was of a home, not an office, and the judge reportedly weighed the Speech or Debate question before signing. But members of Congress work from home, as everyone does now. Whatever the agents carried out of Omar’s house will be sorted by somebody. Whether that somebody is a careful filter team or an interested party with a podcast audience is a question worth asking before, rather than after, the first selective leak.

What to watch

Omar responded on Monday that the search was an attempt to target political enemies and weaponize federal law enforcement, and said she “will not be silenced.” The House Democratic leadership, Hakeem Jeffries, Katherine Clark and Pete Aguilar, called it a “rogue abuse of power.” The Justice Department said nothing beyond its one sentence.

Three things will tell us what kind of case this is.

First, whether anything follows before November 3. If charges or a denaturalization filing land in the last three weeks of the campaign, the department will have abandoned the 60-day rule entirely. If nothing comes until well after, the search will look like what its critics already say it is: a photograph with a warrant attached.

Second, whether the conspiracy count names anyone. Conspiracy requires conspirators. If the theory depends on a dead man and the child he brought to Minnesota, it is less a prosecution than a séance.

Third, whether the warrant affidavit is ever unsealed. The most useful sentence in any search warrant is the one explaining why a judge believed there was probable cause. In June, this series argued that the real cases are the ones that rest on the defendant’s own words. Here, the most important words belong to people who are not defendants at all: Vance, Mullin, Homan and the president, each of whom told the country what the investigation would find before it found anything.

A rule that applies only to one side was never a rule. It was a courtesy, and it has now been withdrawn.
