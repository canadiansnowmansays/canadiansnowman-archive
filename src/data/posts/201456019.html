Donald Trump at a 2024 campaign event in front of a table of grocery staples, promising to immediately lower prices on Day One.

There is a particular species of political audacity that requires not merely a short memory, but the confident assumption that everyone else’s memory is shorter. Donald Trump has always possessed this quality in abundance, deploying it with the cheerful shamelessness of a man who has never once been inconvenienced by his own prior statements. This morning, as the Bureau of Labor Statistics confirmed that U.S. consumer prices rose 4.2 percent in May — the fastest pace since April 2023, the third straight month of acceleration — it is worth sitting with that number for a moment and asking: how did we get here? And what, exactly, did the man who got us here promise?

He promised everything. He delivered the opposite.

The Pitch: A Master Class in Expectation-Setting

Standing at a rally in Montana in August 2024, Trump made his central economic pledge explicit: “Starting on Day 1, we will end inflation and make America affordable again.” A week later, he staged a theatrical press conference outside his New Jersey golf course, surrounded by cartons of eggs, bacon, milk, and grocery staples. “When I win, I will immediately bring prices down,” he said. ABC News

Immediately. Not over time. Not through the complex machinery of monetary policy and supply chain reform. Immediately. Day One. As if the presidency came with a grocery rebate.

He repeated it on the campaign trail, often followed by the phrase “drill, baby, drill.” It was a pledge he made to voters who had spent years watching prices climb — years of sharply rising costs that had taken a toll on their hard-earned pay and their livelihoods. yahoo

And the voters believed him. After the election, Trump himself explained why he won: “I won on the border, and I won on groceries. When you buy apples, when you buy bacon, when you buy eggs, they would double and triple the price over a short period of time, and I won an election based on that. We’re going to bring those prices way down.” NPR

The mandate, as Trump himself defined it, was affordability. He didn’t ask for patience. He asked for power, and Americans gave it to him.

It is worth pausing here to acknowledge what made this pitch so effective. Inflation was a genuine problem under Joe Biden, and the voters who prioritized it at the ballot box were not wrong to be angry. Inflation peaked at roughly 9 percent in the summer of 2022 under President Biden — the highest rate in about 40 years. People felt it in their bones: at the pump, at the checkout, at the dinner table. The price of ground beef. The price of a dozen eggs. The price of filling a tank. These were real, painful, daily experiences, and no amount of macroeconomic reassurance from the Biden White House made them hurt less. PolitiFact

Trump was not wrong that inflation was a crisis. What he was wrong about was almost everything else.

The Context He Conveniently Omits

The version of economic history that Trump tells begins in January 2021 and ends approximately the moment he was inaugurated for the second time. In his telling, Biden created an inflation catastrophe, and Trump rode in to fix it. This narrative has the advantage of being roughly half true, which in Trumpworld qualifies as rigorous analysis.

The part he leaves out: Biden’s decision to respect central bank independence, tighten monetary policy, cut the budget deficit, and pursue more restrained fiscal spending helped bring inflation down from its peak. By Biden’s last month in office, year-over-year inflation was about 2.9 percent — not victory-lap territory, but a trajectory that was unmistakably downward. Trump inherited a cooling economy. The fever had broken. He inherited the convalescent patient and immediately began arguing he’d performed the surgery. TimePolitiFact

He also, almost immediately, began walking back the promise. In a Time magazine interview in late November 2024 — before he had even taken office — Trump said he would “like to” bring down the price of groceries but added: “It’s hard to bring things down once they’re up. You know, it’s very hard.” The man who had promised to end inflation on Day One was already negotiating his exit before Day One arrived. MS NOW

And then there were the tariffs. This is the part of the story that Trump finds most inconvenient, because it represents an inflation risk that was not visited upon him by fate or geopolitics or the ghost of Joe Biden — it was a deliberate policy choice. A new study by economists at the Federal Reserve found that Trump’s tariffs raised core goods prices by 3.1 percent, and that as of February 2026, the tariffs “can explain the entirety of the excess inflation in the core goods category since January 2025.” Not some of it. Not a contributing factor. The entirety. Reason Magazine

Analysis from the Harvard Business School Pricing Lab found that the price of imported goods rose about 4.0 percent between March and September 2025, while domestic goods rose 2.0 percent — accounting for an added 0.7 percentage-point increase in CPI inflation as of August 2025. The tariffs that Trump promised would be paid by foreign countries were, as economists had unanimously predicted, paid by American consumers. Every last cent. Center for American Progress

By early 2026, before the war in Iran had changed anything, the tariff-driven price pressure was already baked into the system. The Iran war did not create the problem. It detonated it.

The Report Card: What the Numbers Actually Say

This morning’s data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics is not a political argument. It is an accounting. The numbers do not care about press releases from the White House or Truth Social posts at 6 a.m. They simply describe what things cost.

Energy prices drove the bulk of May’s increase, rising 3.9 percent over the month and 23.5 percent since last year. To understand why that number matters far beyond the gas station, you need to understand something basic about how a modern economy works — something that Trump himself once articulated with unusual clarity and then proceeded to ignore entirely.

Everything you buy was moved. The fruit in your supermarket traveled from a farm on a refrigerated truck. The ground beef came from a processing facility on a diesel flatbed. The soap under your bathroom sink came off a container ship and then onto a distribution centre lorry. Nearly all goods — including food — that are bought and sold must travel from where they’re produced. Those costs climb with higher gasoline, diesel and jet fuel prices. This is not economics theory. It is logistics arithmetic. aol

The price of diesel — which powers 18-wheeler trucks — has climbed to $4.83 a gallon in the U.S., a 28 percent jump since the war started. “Can’t underscore what a massive jolt this is to the logistics, trucking, and agriculture sectors,” said Patrick De Haan, a petroleum analyst at GasBuddy. When it costs more to fill a commercial truck’s tank, that cost does not evaporate at the loading dock. It travels with the cargo, all the way to the shelf, all the way to the receipt in your hand. PBS

“Food gets to the grocery store on diesel, whether it’s on a truck or on a boat,” as one economist put it. That observation, blunt and obvious as it sounds, is the key to understanding why a war in the Persian Gulf shows up in the price of a package of frankfurters in Toledo. The Strait of Hormuz is not an abstraction for American consumers. It is a surcharge, invisibly attached to nearly everything in their shopping cart. aol

Goldman Sachs estimated that higher fertilizer prices — driven by limits on Gulf region exports — could alone boost food prices by about 1.5 percent this year, and that as a rule of thumb, a 10 percent increase in oil prices raises headline inflation by 0.2 percentage points, with much of the rise coming from transportation costs. Oil has risen far more than 10 percent. The math compounds quickly. aol

The average price of regular gasoline stood at $4.15 a gallon on Wednesday — roughly a dollar higher than a year ago. Electricity has risen 5.9 percent since last year, driven by both the data center boom and the war’s broader impact on global energy markets.

National average U.S. gas prices 2023–2026, showing the sharp recent rise toward $4/gallon.

At the grocery store — the very arena where Trump staked his electoral claim — the picture is exactly what the supply chain logic predicts. Ground beef is up 12.1 percent compared to last year. Frankfurters up 7.7 percent. Lettuce and tomatoes more expensive. As the New York Times reported, Campbell’s Soup announced it would need to charge five to six percent more in its coming fiscal year because of the higher cost of energy and materials that come through the Strait of Hormuz. “Oil obviously gets into products, whether it’s packaging, whether it’s logistics,” said the company’s chief financial officer. “And then we’re seeing some aluminum which comes out of that region is very, very elevated.” When Campbell’s — a company that sells soup, not jet fuel — is passing oil costs on to consumers, the transmission mechanism is working exactly as described. It is just not working in the direction Trump promised. U.S. News & World Report

Airline fares jumped 2.7 percent in May alone and are up 26.7 percent since this time last year — the direct arithmetic of jet fuel prices as the Strait of Hormuz remains contested.

And wages? The number that matters most — the one that determines whether people are actually getting ahead — tells a stark story. Real average hourly earnings, adjusted for inflation, have fallen 0.7 percent over the past year, wiping out all of the gains made in the early months of Trump’s term. In inflation-adjusted terms, workers’ pay is exactly where it was when Trump returned to office in January 2025. Not better. Not the promised golden age. Square one.

The OECD now projects that the United States will have the highest inflation among G7 nations by the end of 2026, at 4.2 percent — up from 2.6 percent in 2025. Canada, by comparison, is projected at 2.4 percent. France at 1.8 percent. The country that was going to lead the world back to affordability is instead leading the developed world in price increases. There is a certain irony in that, if irony is something you can still afford. aol

The Excuses: Iran, Biden, and the Eternal External Shock

The White House, naturally, has a response. It always does. Spokesman Kush Desai was quick to highlight some of the better numbers in today’s data — falling prices for prescription drugs, dairy products, cars, health and auto insurance. These are real. Cherry-picking is not exclusively a Democratic sport, and one should acknowledge the cherries that exist.

But the central excuse — the one Trump has deployed from the golf course to the Oval Office and repeated just last Sunday on NBC’s Meet the Press — is that the war with Iran is to blame. A temporary disruption. An external shock. Force majeure. Not his fault.

Let us grant him the Iran war for a moment. Let us set aside entirely the question of whether launching a war in a region that controls a major global oil chokepoint constitutes, in any meaningful sense, an energy policy. Let us simply stipulate: geopolitical disruptions happen. They are not always of a president’s making.

Even granting all of that, the record does not absolve him. Before the war began on February 28, inflation was already at 2.4 percent, above the Fed’s 2 percent target, and trending upward. The tariff-driven price pressure was already in motion. The war did not create the inflationary environment — it dramatically worsened one that already existed, one that Trump’s own policy choices had constructed. Yahoo!

And then there is the energy promise, which deserves special attention because Trump made it so specifically and so repeatedly. “Slashing energy costs is among the most important actions we can take to bring down prices for American consumers,” he said in a February speech in Texas, just before the U.S. and Israel attacked Iran. “Because when you cut the cost of energy, you really cut — you just cut the cost of everything.” PBS

He was right about the second part. Energy is embedded in the price of nearly everything — food, manufacturing, logistics, airline tickets. When energy costs rise, everything rises with it. He explained this mechanism himself, clearly and correctly, and then proceeded to launch a war in the Persian Gulf that sent gas prices past four dollars a gallon and airline fares up more than a quarter in a single year.

There is a word for promising to lower the cost of energy and then presiding over a war that raises it by twenty percent. Several words, actually. We will be polite and call it irony.

The Audacity: Doing What He Accused Biden Of Doing

What is perhaps most remarkable about the Trump economic performance is not the failure — presidents fail; economies are complex; wars happen; exogenous shocks occur. The remarkable thing is the complete, unblinking refusal to acknowledge any distance whatsoever between the promise and the reality.

Much as the Biden-Harris administration did in the months leading up to the 2024 election, Trump has argued that the economy is better than Americans tell pollsters they believe it to be, pointing to positive indicators such as rising wages. But while taking credit for conditions he inherited from Biden, he has tried to blame his predecessor for less positive conditions that remain unchanged. aol

He is, in other words, doing precisely what he spent four years hammering Biden for doing. Telling people the economy is great when they cannot afford steak. Calling their lived experience a misperception. Pointing to indicators while ignoring receipts. Insisting that the problem is messaging, not policy.

He has called affordability concerns a Democratic “con job,” insisting he has “the greatest economy we’ve ever had.” He said this as gas approached $4.15 a gallon and real wages were declining. He said this as America was on track to have the worst inflation in the G7. He said this the same week the Federal Reserve was openly debating whether to raise rates rather than cut them — the exact opposite of the environment he promised. The Hill

The political toll is becoming visible. On inflation and rising prices, Trump’s approval has moved to minus-45 — with 68 percent disapproving. Perhaps most striking, the Republican shift: while 28 percent of Republicans disapproved of Trump on inflation and rising prices in mid-February, that number has jumped to 40 percent. When four in ten of your own supporters have turned against you on the issue that got you elected, “con job” is probably not the right rhetorical move. aol

The TACO Reckoning

For readers of this series, the TACO framework — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — needs no introduction. It describes the recurring pattern of Trump’s political behavior, and on the economy it finds perhaps its purest expression.

He threatened — relentlessly, brilliantly, effectively — using Biden’s inflation record as a cudgel for four years of campaigning, turning grocery receipts into a political indictment and making the promise of affordability central to his return to power.

He accommodated — his donors, his ideological allies, his long-standing belief that tariffs are a magic wand — deploying a trade policy that economists across the spectrum predicted would raise consumer prices, because that is what tariffs do. They are taxes. They have always been taxes. The only novelty was calling them something else.

He capitulated — to the logic of military escalation, helping launch a war in a region whose geography is defined by the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly 20 percent of the world’s oil supply passes, and then expressing something very close to surprise when energy prices rose.

And now he obfuscates — calling the inflation a temporary disruption, a Biden legacy, a con job perpetrated by anyone with a calculator. The White House issues press releases. Kush Desai highlights the egg prices. The story, as always, is that everything is fine and anyone who disagrees is part of the problem.

The eggs, as it happens, are indeed cheaper — down more than 35 percent compared with last year, a genuine bright spot driven by the recovery of the poultry industry from bird flu. Trump will mention this. Frequently. Loudly. On Truth Social, probably with capital letters.

He will not mention the beef, up 12.1 percent. Or the gas, up a dollar a gallon. Or the airline tickets, up 26.7 percent. Or the wages that, in real terms, have gone nowhere. Or the Fed, which is debating rate increases rather than the cuts Trump demanded. Or the OECD, which projects America will lead the developed world in inflation by year end.

The Reckoning Ahead

The midterms are coming. The voters who handed Trump power on the explicit promise of cheaper groceries are now paying $4.15 for gas and watching their real wages slide backward. As Moody’s chief credit officer Atsi Sheth told the New York Times this morning: “Weakening of pricing power tells you something about how the seller was thinking about their final consumer, and that tells you a little bit about growth. Despite the still relatively low unemployment, the household’s capacity to consume is eroding.”

Eroding. Not booming. Not golden. Eroding.

The particular cruelty of the current moment is that the voters who trusted this promise most — working-class Americans in the places Trump carried most decisively — are the ones most exposed to energy price shocks, most likely to drive long distances, most likely to be buying ground beef rather than sirloin, most likely to feel the full weight of a 4.2 percent inflation rate on a paycheck that has not kept pace.

Trump won an election on the price of eggs and the cost of living. He is now presiding over the fastest inflation in three years, the highest projected inflation in the G7, and a Federal Reserve that is considering raising rates rather than cutting them.

That, too, is a kind of record. The kind that only holds up until people start checking receipts.
