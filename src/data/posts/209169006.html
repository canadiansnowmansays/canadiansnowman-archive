A diary, by its basic nature, is not written for anyone but its author, which is precisely what makes reading someone else’s so uncomfortable and so revealing at once. Anthony Fauci’s ran, by most accounts of the roughly 1,100 to 1,500 pages Senator Rand Paul released this past weekend, from the closing weeks of 2019 through his retirement from government at the end of 2022 — a private, typewritten record, apparently kept on a government-linked computer, that Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s staff discovered and handed to Paul, the chairman of the Senate Homeland Security Committee and Fauci’s longest-running antagonist in Congress. Paul has spent years arguing that Fauci lied to lawmakers about the origins of Covid-19 and about his institute’s funding of gain-of-function research in Wuhan, China. The diary was released, and read by nearly every major American news outlet within days, in search of the entries that would finally prove it.

What those outlets found instead — and this is worth stating plainly, because it is the single most important fact about the diary that the surrounding spectacle has mostly obscured — was not a smoking gun. It was, in the words of one contemporaneous New York Times account, an echo of past disclosures showing Fauci wrestling with a fast-moving crisis as scientists’ own views evolved in real time, not a document contradicting his public testimony in any legally significant way. On January 26, 2020, Fauci wrote that the virus had likely jumped from an animal to a human somewhere connected to a Wuhan market, and mused, in the same entry, that the market itself was probably an amplifier rather than the original source — a distinction that turned out to track closely with where the broader scientific consensus eventually landed, and one he had already stated publicly, in almost identical language, in a U.S.A. Today interview three weeks later. When a respected virologist raised concerns in early 2020 that the pathogen might show signs of deliberate engineering, Fauci convened a conference call with eleven leading scientists to debate the possibility; his diary entry on the call notes candidly that there was “not total agreement,” with only two of the eleven convinced the virus had emerged naturally and the rest genuinely uncertain. Paul has cited that entry as proof the scientific establishment was more divided than Fauci let on publicly — a fair point about the state of scientific opinion in early 2020, but not, on its own, evidence that Fauci lied about anything, since Fauci never claimed unanimous certainty existed among virologists, only that the natural-origin hypothesis remained, in his own professional judgment, the most likely explanation.

The diary’s treatment of the lab-leak question deserves a slightly closer look than the surrounding coverage generally gave it, since it is the specific accusation Paul built the entire hearing around. Fauci has, for years, maintained in interviews — including a notably tense 2023 exchange with the Times‘s David Wallace-Wells, in which he snapped, “David, have you ever testified before a congressional committee?” when pressed on the subject — that he remained genuinely open to the lab-leak hypothesis throughout, even as he personally found the natural-origin explanation more scientifically persuasive. The diary entries released this week are broadly consistent with that account rather than contradicting it: Fauci’s January 2020 notes describe “conspiracy theories” about deliberate genetic modification and release, using language that reflects the dismissive framing common across public health circles at the time, but the entries also show him convening exactly the kind of expert deliberation — the eleven-scientist call, the internal debate about whether findings should be referred to the F.B.I. if suspicions of engineering were substantiated — that a person actively concealing a known truth would have had little reason to document for his own private record. A cover-up rarely leaves behind a paper trail of the cover-up’s own internal debate.

The diary’s genuinely damaging material, and there is some, sits in a different register entirely — not the scientific record but the personal one, and it is here that the document does real damage to Fauci’s public standing, whatever it fails to prove about the pandemic’s origins. The entries chronicling his fame are, without much exaggeration, cringeworthy: exclamation points multiplying alongside case counts and death tolls, a level of excitement about celebrity phone calls that reads, even to a reader sympathetic to the underlying strangeness of sudden fame, as genuinely tone-deaf given what shares the same page. “Global — 3,300,000/230,000; USA — 1,130,000/66,000,” reads one May 2020 entry, tallying infections and deaths worldwide and domestically, before pivoting immediately to note that “Fauci-maddness continues with multiple articles and every sort of paraphernalia and restaurant and foods named after me.” He recorded, apparently without irony, a claim from the Irish ambassador that he was “beloved in Ireland.” He wrote about an accidental “butt call” to Barbra Streisand that turned into a conversation about vaccine boosters, about Julia Roberts confessing that “every time she sees me her throat gets dry,” about a testimonial dinner where Robert De Niro, Al Pacino, and Francis Ford Coppola were among those praising him. None of this is illegal. Very little of it is even unusual for a person suddenly and involuntarily thrust into global celebrity. But it reads badly, and it was always going to read badly, regardless of who released it or why.

There is one area where the diary does catch a genuine, specific inconsistency worth naming plainly, because a series trying to be fair to its subject cannot simply wave away the parts that complicate the sympathetic account. In a 2022 interview with ABC News reflecting on his career, Fauci said he’d had “nothing to do” with school closures during the pandemic. His own diary, from March 2020, records him describing having “convinced” New York City Mayor Bill de Blasio to close the city’s schools, and separately notes a conversation with a top aide to California Governor Gavin Newsom, who had “decided to close the schools in California as well as the bars and restaurants” based on Fauci’s own suggestion. That is a real discrepancy, not a matter of scientific uncertainty evolving in real time, and it is the kind of contradiction that gives Fauci’s critics their most legitimate point in the entire diary controversy: a man who advised, by his own contemporaneous account, exactly the kind of school-closure decisions he later minimized his role in, when asked about it years afterward on national television. Whether that later statement reflects a deliberate downplaying of an unpopular policy legacy or simply the ordinary blurring of memory that afflicts anyone asked to summarize a chaotic multi-year crisis in a single television soundbite is a judgment call this series cannot make on his behalf. But the underlying fact — diary and interview do not match — is real, and it belongs in any honest accounting alongside everything the diary fails to prove.

That last point matters more than most of this week’s coverage has acknowledged. Diaries are not written for publication, and judging the private, unguarded thoughts of an eighty-five-year-old man against the standard we apply to public statements is, on some level, an inherently unfair exercise — one that would embarrass nearly anyone whose private notebook was seized by a political enemy and published for the specific purpose of maximizing embarrassment. Gal Beckerman’s essay on the diary for The Atlantic made this case as directly as anyone has: that what Paul released is less a confession than a fairly ordinary record of a person “astonished at what was happening in his life,” in the words of Peter Staley, Fauci’s longtime friend from the AIDS-era fights, who has said Fauci kept the notes originally to help draft the memoir he eventually published in 2024. A diary that catalogs both the death toll and a beer named in your honor on the same page is not evidence of misplaced priorities so much as evidence of an ordinary, flawed human being trying to process an entirely extraordinary set of circumstances in the only private space he had left to do it.

The question of whether Fauci should have expected this material to remain private is a separate one, and here the honest answer cuts somewhat against him. The diary, by every indication, was kept on a government-linked computer system rather than a genuinely private device — a decision that, whatever Fauci’s intentions, meant the notes were never fully shielded from disclosure under federal records law, and Kennedy’s characterization of them as “government records created on a government computer by a federal employee,” rather than a personal journal, is not an unreasonable reading of how federal recordkeeping actually works. A man with Fauci’s decades of experience inside the federal bureaucracy, who understood better than almost anyone how thoroughly government systems can be subject to disclosure, might reasonably have been expected to know that a diary kept on that infrastructure was never as private as a locked notebook in a sock drawer. That does not make Kennedy’s decision to hand the material to Fauci’s most committed political enemy, specifically for the purpose of embarrassing him, an act of disinterested transparency rather than a targeted act of political retribution — both things can be true. Fauci made himself vulnerable to exactly this kind of disclosure by where he chose to keep his private notes. Kennedy and Paul chose, deliberately, to use that vulnerability as a weapon rather than as a historical record.

What the diary ultimately proves, then, is considerably narrower than either side wanted it to be. It does not establish that Fauci lied to Congress about gain-of-function research, or that he knew and concealed a lab origin for the virus, or that his public statements about masking, school closures, or lockdowns diverged in any legally meaningful way from his private judgment at the time. What it does establish, in embarrassing and occasionally unflattering detail, is that a man who spent five decades building a reputation for scrupulous nonpartisan restraint was, in his own unguarded private thoughts, considerably more consumed by his own sudden fame than any of that carefully cultivated public reputation would have suggested. That is a real, fair criticism of Fauci’s character. It is not the criminal cover-up Rand Paul spent years promising the diary would reveal, and the gap between what was promised and what was actually delivered is itself one of the more telling facts of this entire episode — a gap that did not stop the chairman from calling Fauci before his committee anyway, nor from building an entire hearing around a document that, on its own terms, mostly failed to prove what it was released to prove.

That hearing — three hours, more than a hundred invocations of the Fifth Amendment, and the specific question of whether silence in that room was cowardice or the only dignity still available to him — is where this series turns next.
