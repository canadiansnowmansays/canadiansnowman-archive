The cracks are real. They are growing. And some of them are imported.

For most of this series, I have told a story of Canadian resilience — the country that survived conquest through negotiation, that defined itself against American absorption, that built institutions and embraced multiculturalism and produced, in its quiet, unglamorous way, one of the most successful democracies on earth.

This essay tells a different story. Because any honest account of a country must include the parts that make its citizens uncomfortable, and Canada in the twenty-first century has accumulated an uncomfortable number of cracks in the foundation that this series has been describing.

The cracks are real. They are growing. And some of them are imported.

In January 2022, a convoy of trucks descended on Ottawa and occupied the downtown core for three weeks.

The stated grievance was the federal government’s COVID-19 vaccine mandates for cross-border truckers. The actual phenomenon was something broader, angrier, and considerably more alarming than a dispute about public health policy.

The convoy was not a Canadian invention. It was an American import — a transplantation of Trumpist political culture into a Canadian context, arriving with the same rhetoric, the same media ecosystem, and in many cases the same funding streams. The language was American: freedom as an absolute rather than a negotiated value, government as tyranny, mainstream media as the enemy. The social media amplification came from American platforms and American influencers. A significant portion of the funding came from American donors through GiveSendGo, a crowdfunding platform favoured by the American right. The flags included not just Canadian flags but Trump flags, Confederate flags, “Don’t Tread on Me” flags, and at least one swastika — though the organizers disavowed the latter, the fact that it appeared at all was itself a statement about what currents were running beneath the surface.

Ottawa — quiet, bureaucratic, boring Ottawa, a city whose principal forms of excitement are canal skating and the annual tulip festival — was occupied by a movement whose ideological roots were not in the Canadian political tradition but in the American one. Residents of the downtown core endured weeks of air horns, diesel fumes, and harassment. Businesses closed. Workers could not reach their offices. The parliamentary precinct — the symbolic heart of Canadian democracy — was blockaded.

The convoy was eventually cleared using the Emergencies Act — the first time it had been invoked since its creation in 1988 as a replacement for the War Measures Act. The invocation was controversial. A public inquiry, led by Justice Paul Rouleau, found that the decision to invoke the Act was justified given the circumstances. Federal Court Justice Richard Mosley subsequently ruled that it was not. The legal and political debate continues, and will continue for years.

Air horns, diesel fumes, harassment—and symbols that included Trump flags and ‘Don’t Tread on Me’ banners.

But the debate that matters — the one that the convoy forced into the open — is not about the Emergencies Act. It is about whether the Canadian political culture that this series has described — the culture of compromise, institutional deference, negotiation over confrontation — is strong enough to resist the gravitational pull of American-style populism.

The cracks are not confined to the convoy. They are structural, and they have been accumulating for years.

Canadian politics has become measurably more polarized. The Conservative Party, under Stephen Harper, had already moved in a more ideological direction during its decade in power from 2006 to 2015. Under Pierre Poilievre — who won the Conservative leadership in 2022 with the largest mandate in the party’s history — the shift accelerated. Poilievre adopted the rhetoric and tactics of American right-wing politics with considerable skill: the populist framing of elites versus ordinary people, the distrust of institutions, the contempt for mainstream media, the social media savvy, the provocative simplicity.

For a time, it worked. Poilievre built a twenty-point lead over the Liberals. He dominated question period. He connected with voters who felt left behind by the housing crisis, by inflation, by a cost of living that had spiralled beyond the reach of young Canadians in ways their parents would not have recognized. His critique had genuine force, because the problems he identified — unaffordable housing, immigration levels that had outpaced infrastructure, a sense that government was serving insiders rather than citizens — were real.

But the style was American, and when a genuinely American threat materialized — when Trump threatened tariffs, annexation, and the absorption of Canada into the United States — the American style became a liability. Poilievre’s rhetoric of domestic grievance sounded tinny against the backdrop of an actual foreign threat. His instinct for confrontation, which had been effective against Justin Trudeau’s Liberals, was ill-suited to a moment that demanded national unity. And his caucus, sensing the shift, began to fracture. Four Conservative MPs crossed the floor to the Liberals. Others went quiet. The twenty-point lead evaporated.

The discontent has a geography. In Alberta — a province whose relationship with Confederation has been strained since Pierre Trudeau’s National Energy Program transferred billions in provincial wealth to federal priorities in 1980 — separation has moved from a recurring grievance to an active constitutional confrontation.

The roots are old, but the crisis is new. “Wexit” emerged as a genuine political movement after the 2019 federal election returned a Liberal government that Alberta had voted almost unanimously against. Premier Danielle Smith — who has spent her tenure threading the needle between enabling separatist sentiment and disavowing it — quietly lowered the threshold for a referendum petition, altered provincial petition laws, and passed a Sovereignty Act whose original text contained no “within a united Canada” qualifier. The addition of that phrase resolved nothing: you cannot be sovereign and united simultaneously, and Smith’s attempt to occupy both positions told its own story about where her government’s centre of political gravity actually lies.

In February 2026, Smith announced a ten-question referendum to be held on October 19, 2026. Nine questions concern immigration and constitutional adjustments. The tenth asks Albertans whether the provincial government should commence the legal process required for a binding independence referendum — a vote, in other words, on whether to hold a vote. The indirection is deliberate. Smith says she will campaign for the pro-Canada side while honouring whatever result Albertans produce. Whether that constitutes statesmanship or abdication of responsibility is a question Albertans are actively arguing.

What makes the Alberta situation qualitatively different from the convoy — and considerably more alarming — is the American dimension. It begins with the Premier herself. In January 2025, Danielle Smith visited Trump at Mar-a-Lago and met with him twice in a single weekend, becoming the first premier to do so as the tariff crisis loomed — a unilateral diplomatic sortie that broke with every other province’s Team Canada approach and raised the obvious question of what, exactly, was being negotiated and on whose behalf. Her separatist allies went considerably further. The Alberta Prosperity Project, the far-right group driving the independence movement, held three separate meetings with U.S. State Department officials, discussing the logistics of Alberta breaking from Canada, including switching to U.S. currency and border security arrangements. They sought a fourth meeting and reportedly requested a $500 billion U.S. Treasury credit line to bankroll an independence referendum. Their legal counsel claimed the entire U.S. administration was “extremely enthusiastic” about a free and independent Alberta, and said he had a “much stronger relationship” with the Trump administration than with the Canadian Prime Minister. NBC NewsThe Jerusalem Post

Here is where the 51st state logic reveals itself in its full absurdity. Trump’s stated ambition was to absorb Canada whole. His administration’s tactical interest, it appears, was to assist in breaking Canada apart first — softening the target, province by province, beginning with the most resource-rich one. Alberta separatists who believed they were pursuing independence were, in this reading, doing Washington’s preliminary work for it. The province that calls itself the Texas of Canada was being invited to audition for the role.

British Columbia Premier David Eby used an old word for it: treason. A former senior American diplomat called the Trump administration’s engagement with the separatists “irresponsible as hell.” Both reactions tell you something about what was actually happening beneath the surface of a movement that framed itself as a defence of Alberta’s interests.

It should be noted that the numbers, for now, are on the federalist side. A late May 2026 Ipsos poll found that 72 per cent of Albertans would vote to remain in Canada if a binding referendum were held, against 18 per cent who would vote to leave. Angus Reid found a similar picture: 60 per cent would vote No to the October question, against 35 per cent who would vote Yes — and when asked a simpler hypothetical, the federalist position strengthened further, to 67 per cent. Separatism has, as it always has in Alberta, a consistent ceiling well below majority support. Global NewsAngus Reid Institute

But the polling obscures as much as it reveals. Seven in ten Albertans believe that if the No side wins in October, the separatists will never accept the result. That number — 69 per cent — is more alarming than the separatist vote intention itself. It suggests that a democratic outcome in favour of Confederation may not resolve the underlying crisis. Political movements built on grievance rarely accept defeat gracefully, and this one has already demonstrated a willingness to court foreign powers and rewrite the rules of the game to suit its purposes. Angus Reid Institute

Alberta separatism is not a convoy. It does not have a beginning, a middle, and a clearing. It is a chronic condition with acute episodes, and the October 2026 referendum represents the most acute episode in its history. The immune system is engaged. Whether it is sufficient is, as of this writing, genuinely unknown.

The crack that Poilievre exploited — the crack of domestic discontent, of institutional distrust, of populist anger — is real and it persists. The housing crisis is not resolved. The cost of living has not normalized. The immigration debate has not been settled. These are genuine problems, and they will outlast the current crisis with the United States. The question is whether they will be addressed through the Canadian tradition of institutional reform or through the American tradition of populist disruption.

Social media has done to Canadian public discourse what it has done to every democracy it has touched: fragmented the information space, rewarded extremism, and eroded the shared factual foundation on which compromise depends.

The CBC — whatever its considerable and well-documented failures — represented a shared national information space. It was the one institution that most Canadians, regardless of political orientation, could identify as a common reference point. It was imperfect, underfunded, accused of bias from both sides (which is, in a perverse way, evidence of rough balance), and increasingly irrelevant to younger Canadians who get their news from platforms that the CBC cannot compete with.

That shared space is shrinking. Canadians increasingly consume information from the same algorithmically optimized platforms that have fragmented American discourse. Canadian political influencers operate in the same engagement economy as their American counterparts, rewarded for the same extremism, subject to the same incentives, producing the same tribal polarization. The border that exists on the map does not exist on the internet, and the internet is where an increasing proportion of political culture is formed.

The 2020 Nova Scotia massacre—the worst in Canadian history—exposed gaps in firearms regulation and RCMP response.

Gun violence, while still dramatically lower than in the United States, has been rising. The 2020 Nova Scotia massacre — in which a gunman disguised as a police officer killed twenty-two people over thirteen hours in a rampage across rural communities — was the worst mass shooting in Canadian history. The subsequent inquiry revealed catastrophic failures by the RCMP and systemic gaps in firearms regulation. The event demonstrated that Canada is not immune to the kind of mass violence that Americans have learned to treat as background noise.

And the broader crisis of democratic confidence that affects the entire Western world has not exempted Canada. Trust in government has declined. Trust in media has declined. Trust in institutions — the courts, the public service, the electoral system — has declined. The Canadian instinct for institutional deference is not a permanent feature of the national character. It is a cultural practice, and cultural practices can erode.

But here is what the convoy also revealed, and what the subsequent elections have confirmed: the cracks are real, but the foundation is holding.

The convoy occupied Ottawa for three weeks and then it was cleared. The Emergencies Act was invoked and then it was reviewed. The courts examined the government’s actions and rendered judgments. The institutions — imperfect, slow, frustrating, boring — functioned.

Pierre Poilievre adopted American-style populism and built a commanding lead. And then Donald Trump threatened to annex Canada, and the country rallied not around populism but around the institutional tradition that this series has traced from the Plains of Abraham to the present day. Poilievre’s lead evaporated. Four of his MPs crossed the floor. Five, counting the NDP defector. Mark Carney — a banker, an institutionalist, a man whose entire career has been built on the quiet management of complex systems — won a majority government that the Toronto Star described as an “anti-Trump coalition.”

The Canadian immune system activated. Not perfectly. Not without cost. Not without the kind of ugly, divisive politics that Canadians prefer to believe is an American phenomenon. But it activated. The country, confronted with a genuine external threat, reached for the tradition of institutional competence rather than the tradition of populist anger.

Whether it has enough strength for what comes next is the question this series cannot definitively answer. The cracks are real. The populist infection is real. The American influence on Canadian political culture is real and growing. But the foundation — the culture of compromise, the institutional deference, the instinct for negotiation over confrontation — proved, in the spring of 2026, that it still has strength.

Whether it has enough strength is a question for the next generation of Canadians to answer. What this generation has demonstrated is that the answer is not yet no.
