On a date that should have made the front page of every newspaper in the country, Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche — Donald Trump’s former personal lawyer — issued an order ending “any matters currently pending or that could be pending” against the president, his family members, their businesses, and their “affiliates” with the Internal Revenue Service. All tax returns already filed. All audits in progress. All examinations underway. Closed.

The beneficiary was the man who appointed the acting attorney general.

That is not a coincidence. That is the template.

The Setup: A Decade of Hostilities

Donald Trump’s relationship with the IRS is, by his own description, personal.

“Every year they audit me, audit me, audit me,” he told a Republican primary debate in February 2016. “Nobody gets audited — I have friends that are very wealthy people. They never get audited. I get audited every year.”

He was not wrong about the frequency. Under IRS procedures, the president’s annual tax return is audited every year — not because of any animus, but because the presidency creates inherent conflicts of interest that require scrutiny. The IRS opened 10 audits of Trump and his holding company between 2019 and 2022 alone, questioning a wide range of his tax positions.

What Trump described as persecution, tax historians describe as standard practice for a man with 400-plus business entities and a documented history of aggressive tax avoidance stretching back decades. The IRS struggled to get its arms around the various ways Trump appeared to be minimizing his taxes, according to a 2022 House Ways and Means Committee investigation — noting that “animosity” between Trump’s tax counsel and the agency was making the process more difficult.

That animosity had a financial basis. One audit alone — examining how Trump claimed losses on his Chicago tower — could have resulted in a bill exceeding $100 million, as the New York Times has reported. Other audits questioned enormous business losses he routinely recorded, a claimed tax break on his Seven Springs estate in New York, and a range of other positions across an empire of more than 400 entities.

Then there was the criminal record. In 2022, the Trump Organization was convicted on 17 counts of tax fraud for helping executives dodge taxes on Manhattan apartments, luxury cars, and other perks. Fined $1.6 million — the statutory maximum.

And there was the civil record. New York Attorney General Letitia James won a finding that Trump and his companies committed a decade of business fraud, inflating his net worth by billions to secure favorable loans and insurance terms. The financial penalty was voided by an appeals court on Eighth Amendment grounds, but the liability finding stood. Every appellate judge who addressed the merits agreed: the fraud happened.

This was the landscape Todd Blanche surveyed when he sat down to write his memo.

The Settlement That Wasn’t

Todd Blanche, the acting attorney general, told Congress this week that the audit protection for Mr. Trump and his family was a typical part of how the I.R.S. settles litigation.Credit...Demetrius Freeman for The New York Times

Here is the official version of events, the one Blanche offered to Congress this week: the audit immunity was a typical component of how the IRS settles litigation. Trump sued the government for failing to prevent Charles Littlejohn from leaking his tax returns. The government settled. Dropping pending IRS examinations is a normal part of such resolutions.

Tax experts disagree. The Littlejohn lawsuit focused on the unauthorized disclosure of Trump’s returns — a privacy violation, a data breach, a failure of government security. It had nothing to do with the underlying tax positions those returns contained. Audit amnesty in an actual tax controversy would typically come from the IRS itself, not the acting attorney general, and would apply only to specific issues under review.

What Blanche issued was something else entirely: a sweeping order, originating from the Justice Department rather than the IRS, covering not just Trump but family members and businesses with no connection to the original lawsuit, protecting not just the disputed positions but all tax returns already filed, extending potentially to the far reaches of an empire that has expanded dramatically in recent years — into cryptocurrency, media ventures, and licensing deals that this series has been documenting since 2025.

The judge originally assigned to the Littlejohn case, Kathleen M. Williams of the Southern District of Florida, is now investigating whether Trump’s lawyers deceived her when they brought the original suit. She may lack the power to unwind the audit protection. Because the deal is between the government and Trump, third-party challenges may face standing problems in court.

The house, again, found a way to win.

The Math

What is this worth?

Start with the Chicago tower audit: over $100 million in potential liability, now closed.

Add the Seven Springs estate tax break. Add the audit of his holding company. Add whatever the IRS may have initiated since 2022 — including during his second term, when the president is again subject to the annual audit practice.

Now consider what the series has been documenting since May 2025: a business empire that has added cryptocurrency ventures valued at hundreds of millions, media settlements totaling $90.5 million, overseas licensing deals, and streams of revenue that arrive precisely because their recipient is president of the United States. The IRS can still examine future returns. But Trump may now be able to use positions locked in by the immunity — including vast claimed losses — to offset future taxable income from those new ventures.

“He’s been audited many, many times, and many of those audits have continued for years and years and years,” tax historian Joseph J. Thorndike told the Times. “I have no reason to believe that any other president has had anything like this kind of relationship with the I.R.S., as a private citizen or president.”

The Republican Silence

When Blanche also announced a $1.8 billion fund to compensate people who claimed to be victims of federal overreach, Republican senators erupted. The furor held up the Republican legislative agenda for weeks. Several senators voted to end the fund. Blanche eventually abandoned it entirely.

The audit immunity attracted almost no comparable attention. A Democratic effort to cancel the audit protection failed on a voice vote. Republicans, who spent years demanding scrutiny of Democratic tax returns and denouncing the weaponization of federal agencies, looked the other way while the acting attorney general granted the sitting president an apparently unprecedented and enormously valuable personal financial benefit.

The silence has a pattern. Earlier in this series, we documented how the emoluments violations attracted legal challenges that were eventually mooted when Trump left office in 2021. We documented how the $400 million Qatar aircraft circumvented Congressional scrutiny through executive framing. We documented how the cryptocurrency ventures that enriched Trump’s family operated in regulatory gaps that Trump’s own administration widened.

In each case, the mechanism is different. The structure is the same. The president extracts value. The oversight institutions fail to respond. The extraction becomes precedent.

The Prosecutor Who Won — and Lost

New York Attorney General Letitia James speaks during a press conference at the office of the Attorney General on December 15, 2025 in New York City. (Michael M. Santiago/Getty)

There is a coda to this story that belongs in this series.

Letitia James spent years documenting what this series has spent a year documenting: the systematic inflation of assets, the manipulation of financial statements, the extraction of favorable terms from banks and insurers who were given fraudulent information. She won. Every appellate judge who examined the merits agreed the fraud occurred. The financial penalty was voided, but the liability stood.

In August 2025, the Justice Department opened a grand jury investigation into James, seeking records related to her civil fraud case against Trump. In October 2025, a federal grand jury in Virginia indicted her on bank fraud charges.

The woman who proved Trump committed fraud was indicted by Trump’s Justice Department.

Trump has long said, publicly and without apology, that a decade ago he approached his taxes in the way he did because “it makes me smart.” The audit immunity memo suggests he has not changed his view. What has changed is that he now controls the agency that was supposed to be watching.

The Template, Continued

Part 1 of this series opened with Fred Trump’s 1940s FHA fraud — the overstatement of construction costs, the extraction of federal money, the transfer of risk to government guarantors while profit flowed to the family. We described it as a template.

The template has been refined across three generations. The mechanisms have grown more sophisticated: from overbilling on construction contracts to overvaluing real estate assets, from casino bankruptcies that transferred losses to bondholders to a Justice Department memo that transfers audit liability to the American public.

The Chicago tower losses — the ones that drove a $100 million audit — were built on the same logic: extract maximum value, transfer maximum cost. Trump claimed losses he may not have been entitled to. The IRS spent years trying to determine the truth. Now it cannot.

“The house always wins,” we wrote in Part 1, “unless the marks stop playing.”

The marks are the American taxpayers who will never collect what the audit might have found. The marks are the IRS agents who spent years building examinations that Blanche erased with a memo. The marks are anyone who believed that wealth and power do not purchase exemption from the obligations that everyone else bears.

The house found its most elegant play yet: it didn’t beat the IRS. It simply made the IRS irrelevant.
