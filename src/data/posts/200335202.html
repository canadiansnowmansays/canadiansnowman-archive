Friedrich Hayek did not know Bill Pulte. He could not have — Pulte wasn’t born until 1984, forty years after Hayek published The Road to Serfdom. But in Chapter 10 of that book, titled “Why the Worst Get on Top”, Hayek described him with uncomfortable precision.

“The totalitarian leader,” Hayek wrote, “must collect around him a group which is prepared voluntarily to submit to that discipline they are to impose by force upon the rest of the people.” Advancement within such a system “depends largely on a willingness to do immoral things.” To be useful, “a man must be prepared to break every moral rule he has ever known if this seems necessary to achieve the end set for him.”

On Tuesday, June 2, 2026, President Trump named Bill Pulte — heir to a homebuilding fortune, current director of the Federal Housing Finance Agency, a man with no known day of national security experience — to serve as acting Director of National Intelligence. He will now oversee the CIA, the NSA, and all seventeen American intelligence agencies. He will do this while continuing to run the FHFA and serve as self-appointed chair of both Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. His own family's charitable foundation has issued a public statement clarifying that "Bill Pulte does not represent, nor is he a spokesperson for, all members of the Pulte family, in any capacity." Trump, apparently, did not get the memo.

Hayek had his number before either of them was born.

The Record

Bill Pulte, the director of the Federal Housing Finance Agency, at a hearing in Washington last year.Credit...Eric Lee/The New York Times

Let us be precise about what Pulte has actually accomplished, because the record is the argument.

From his housing perch, Pulte used social media to publicly accuse Trump’s perceived enemies of mortgage fraud — referring Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook and New York Attorney General Letitia James for criminal prosecution over irregularities on mortgage documents so routine that Trump’s own Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent committed the same ones. He pushed so aggressively for the James prosecution that a U.S. attorney was fired, an inexperienced White House lawyer was illegally installed as his replacement, and the case collapsed entirely when courts ruled the appointment invalid. He publicly brawled with Bessent at a party. He drafted a termination letter for Jerome Powell. He allied himself with Ed Martin, the far-right operative who ran the Justice Department’s “weaponization” task force.

None of these efforts produced a conviction. None produced a durable prosecution. What they produced was something Trump values far more: visible, performative fealty. The willingness to name enemies loudly, to sharpen the blade publicly, to be the most aggressive enforcer in the room regardless of evidence or outcome. In Hayek’s terms, Pulte demonstrated that he was “prepared to break every moral rule” in service of the leader’s ends. Trump took his phone calls. Now Trump is giving him the keys to the intelligence vault.

As David Frum noted in the Atlantic on Tuesday, Pulte “does have a disgraceful record of putting the prosecutorial powers of government at President Trump’s vindictive service.” That record, Frum correctly observes, is not incidental to the appointment. It is the reason for it.

The Legal Wreckage

The law creating the Office of the Director of National Intelligence — enacted after the catastrophic intelligence failures of September 11 — explicitly requires that the director “have extensive national security expertise.” Pulte does not appear to meet this requirement. As the New York Times reported on Tuesday, national security experts said so plainly, Senator Angus King called himself “puzzled,” and Senator Susan Collins said, with quiet devastation: “I truly know nothing about him.”

Senator Mark Warner was less measured. Trump had chosen someone with a demonstrated “eagerness to use the authorities of government to pursue political retribution,” he said, and the appointment made clear that the president was “not looking for an intelligence leader who will follow the facts or speak truth to power, but rather someone who will be willing to shape intelligence around the president’s wishes.”

Senator Elissa Slotkin, who helped create the DNI office after September 11, was visibly seething. Representative Jim Himes called it “potentially catastrophic for national security.” Even Senator James Lankford, a conservative Republican on the Intelligence Committee, managed only: “That should be a neutral role.” Which, under the circumstances, reads less like reassurance than epitaph.

Pulte will serve in an acting capacity — no confirmation hearing, no senators asking him under oath to define signals intelligence or explain the organizational relationship between the DNI and the CIA director, no committee probing whether he even holds a security clearance. This too is by design. Acting appointments are the administration’s preferred instrument for installing loyalists in roles that might not survive institutional scrutiny. The rule of law has not been abolished; it has simply been routed around.

The Structural Horror

Here is the part that should frighten us most — and it is not hypothetical.

The DNI role was created because the pre-9/11 intelligence community was siloed, competitive, and catastrophically bad at synthesizing information across agencies. The job demands the ability to evaluate intelligence, challenge analysts, distinguish signal from noise, and resist the seductive pull of information that confirms what the president already believes.

Pulte has demonstrated, consistently and in public, the precise opposite skill set. He pursues conclusions first and evidence second. He pressures institutions toward predetermined verdicts. He treats allegations as convictions. His specialty is the performance of accountability — not accountability itself.

Brendan Smialowski / AFP / Getty

And critically, the abuse of sensitive data is not a theoretical future concern. It is already his track record. The Government Accountability Office opened an investigation last year into whether Pulte improperly used mortgage data in his retribution campaigns. Top Fannie Mae officials were fired after they complained that a Pulte aide had allegedly shared confidential federal housing data with a competitor. As David Graham noted in the Atlantic, these abuses — troubling enough at the FHFA — pale against what becomes possible at the DNI. Pulte would have access to the full apparatus of American intelligence: intercepted communications, foreign asset networks, classified threat assessments, and the entire documented record of investigations into Donald Trump’s ties to Russia.

That last point is almost certainly the point. Graham puts it plainly: it is hard not to suspect that the opportunity to pursue Trump’s revenge against anyone involved in investigating his Russia connections is precisely why someone otherwise so unqualified has been chosen for the job.

Gabbard, before her departure, was already down this road: pursuing Trump’s voter fraud claims through the intelligence apparatus, showing up unannounced at an FBI search of an elections office in Georgia. Pulte will not change the direction. He will accelerate it — and now he will have the tools to make it stick.

The Leacock Footnote

There is an almost irresistible satirical dimension to this appointment that deserves a moment’s acknowledgment — not because it diminishes the danger, but because Leacock would have recognized it instantly.

Bill Pulte made his name — to the extent he made it at all — as the heir to a homebuilding dynasty who was pushed off his own family company’s board in 2020. His primary connection to the residential real estate business is a group of five aging mobile home parks he acquired in Florida for roughly $3 million in the two years before his nomination — properties where recent visits by the New York Times revealed broken fences, overflowing trash bins, windows covered with cardboard and faded American flags, and duct tape patching torn screens. One tenant had gone months without a working stove. Rents at some parks jumped as much as 73 percent after Pulte’s companies took over. He had promised to make them into “nice communities.” He was then appointed to oversee the most consequential mortgage agencies in the world, governing a portfolio representing roughly 70 percent of American mortgage-backed securities. Home prices remained stubbornly high throughout his tenure. Since the Iran war, 30-year mortgage rates have climbed back to where they were last summer. First-time buyers remain locked out.

Now this same man will house the nation’s secrets.

In Mariposa, Ontario, as Leacock knew, Reeve John Henry Bagshaw’s second cousin gets the post office contract not because he can sort mail but because he brought the right potato salad to the right picnic. The scale here is different. The principle is identical. The homebuilder who couldn’t keep homes affordable has been handed the keys to the intelligence community’s vault.

Hayek’s Conclusion, and Ours

America under Donald Trump is not a totalitarian state — Frum is right about that. Free institutions remain too strong, Trump’s methods too chaotic and lazy, his goals more fundamentally larcenous than ideological. But Trump does aspire, consistently and systematically, to cripple the rule of law and the proper functioning of institutions at every opportunity. In Bill Pulte, he has found what Hayek described: an appointee plainly willing to do immoral things, a man who has proven he will subordinate every professional norm to the leader’s wishes.

This is not an aberration. It is the pattern. Across this series — in education, in housing, in justice, in health, in defense — we have documented the same substitution: loyalty for competence, personal devotion for institutional knowledge, political utility for public interest.

The intelligence community is the last place this pattern should arrive. Its failures are not measured in embarrassing headlines. They are measured in what we don’t see coming.

Bill Pulte cannot identify an intelligence failure he’s never studied. He cannot challenge an analytical conclusion he lacks the training to evaluate. He cannot resist pressure to shape intelligence around the president’s wishes — because that, precisely, is the job he has been hired to do.

The blind leading the blind. Except some of what we cannot see is classified.
