Part of the series: Israel/Palestine: The Original Sin and Its Compound Interest

By Stephen Gordon | CanadianSnowmanSays

The devastated site of the Nova music festival in the Negev desert on the morning after the October 7, 2023 Hamas attack — the scene of the worst massacre of Jews since the Holocaust.

At 6:29 a.m. on Saturday, October 7, 2023 — Shabbat morning, the Jewish Sabbath, the last day of the Sukkot holiday — Hamas launched the largest military operation in its history against Israel.

Approximately 3,000 Hamas fighters breached the Gaza security fence at multiple points simultaneously, using bulldozers, paragliders, motorcycles, and explosives. They overwhelmed Israeli military posts, attacked kibbutzim and towns near the Gaza border, and descended on the Nova music festival in the Negev desert, where young Israelis were dancing in an open field at dawn.

What followed was the worst single-day killing of Jewish people since the Holocaust.

By the end of the day, approximately 1,200 people were dead — the majority of them civilians. Families were killed in their homes, some sheltering in safe rooms while Hamas fighters worked through their houses methodically. Festival attendees were hunted through the desert. Children were killed. Elderly residents of kibbutzim were murdered. Sexual violence was committed against women, documented subsequently by a UN Special Representative on Sexual Violence in Conflict. 251 people — soldiers and civilians — were taken hostage into Gaza, some of whom remain there as this series is written.

The atrocity of October 7th requires no qualification, no contextualizing asterisk, no “but” appended to its description. It was a war crime. The deliberate targeting of civilians, the sexual violence, the taking of hostages, the killing of children — these are violations of international humanitarian law by any legal definition. The people who planned and executed October 7th bear full moral and legal responsibility for those acts.

This series has spent ten instalments establishing the historical context that produced October 7th. Context is not justification. Understanding is not excuse. The historians and analysts who have spent decades warning that the occupation, the blockade, the settlements, and the dispossession would produce catastrophic violence were correct in their prediction and horrified by its confirmation. Being right about the conditions that produce violence does not make the violence acceptable.

October 7th was an atrocity. What Israel did in response — and what Western governments did in response to what Israel did — is also part of this accounting.

A burned-out home in Kibbutz Be’eri, one of the communities hardest hit during the October 7 attacks.

The Intelligence Failure

Before examining the Israeli response, the intelligence failure that allowed October 7th to occur demands acknowledgment.

Israel operates what is widely considered the most sophisticated intelligence apparatus in the Middle East. The Mossad, Shin Bet, and military intelligence together employ thousands of analysts, run networks of informants, use signals intelligence of extraordinary sophistication, and have penetrated Hamas’s organizational structure at multiple levels over decades. The Gaza security fence — the system of barriers, sensors, cameras, and remote-controlled weapons that surrounded Gaza — was described by Israeli officials as impenetrable.

It was not impenetrable. And the intelligence failure was not a simple operational gap. Subsequent reporting — including in the New York Times, the Washington Post, and the Israeli newspaper Haaretz — established that Israeli intelligence had obtained documents describing a Hamas operational plan remarkably similar to what occurred on October 7th. The plan had been in Hamas’s possession for over a year. Israeli analysts had reviewed it and concluded it was too ambitious to be operationally credible. It was filed, and the warning was not acted upon.

The failure was compounded by a systemic bias in Israeli intelligence assessment: the assumption that Hamas was deterred, that its leadership understood the consequences of a major attack, and that the economic incentives Israel had provided — allowing Qatari cash payments to flow into Gaza to maintain a minimal level of economic stability — had bought a kind of rough stability. The intelligence community had been watching the deterrence and missing the preparation.

Accountability for the intelligence failure has been politically contested in Israel. Calls for an independent state commission of inquiry — equivalent to the Kahan Commission that investigated the Sabra and Shatila massacre — have been resisted by Netanyahu, whose political survival is partially contingent on the inquiry not beginning until after the election he faces. The military intelligence chief resigned in April 2024, acknowledging responsibility. Netanyahu has not acknowledged any personal responsibility and has sought to shift blame onto his predecessors and subordinates.

Hamas fighters breaching the Gaza security fence on the morning of October 7, 2023 — an intelligence and deterrence failure that Israeli officials had previously described as “impenetrable.”

The intelligence failure is important not only as an accountability question but as an analytical one. A government that was confident enough in its deterrence to allow 251 hostages to be taken — because it had not prepared for the possibility that Hamas would attempt something of this scale — was a government that had fundamentally misread the political and psychological state of the population it had blockaded and occupied for seventeen years.

The Israeli Response: Scale, Intent, and International Law

Israel’s military response to October 7th began within hours and has continued for over eighteen months as this series is written. Its scale has been unprecedented in the history of the conflict and in the modern history of urban warfare.

By any metric available — tonnage of bombs dropped, percentage of buildings destroyed, civilian casualty rates, displacement of population, destruction of civilian infrastructure — the Israeli military campaign in Gaza has been one of the most destructive conducted by a Western-aligned military since the Second World War.

The numbers, drawn from UN agencies, the Gaza Health Ministry, and independent monitoring organizations:

More than 50,000 Palestinians killed as of early 2025, by the most conservative counts — a figure the Lancet medical journal has suggested may significantly undercount the true death toll when indirect deaths from disease, malnutrition, and lack of medical care are included. The majority of the confirmed dead are women and children.

Over 1.9 million people — approximately 85 percent of Gaza’s population — displaced from their homes, many multiple times, into a territory where the infrastructure of survival has been systematically destroyed.

Over 60 percent of Gaza’s buildings damaged or destroyed, including the majority of its hospitals, universities, schools, mosques, and cultural heritage sites.

The entire civilian infrastructure of northern Gaza — water treatment, sanitation, electricity, telecommunications — deliberately destroyed in the early phases of the campaign.

Widespread destruction across Gaza following the Israeli military campaign, one of the most intense urban bombardments of the 21st century.

A famine declared by international humanitarian organizations across northern Gaza, with the UN World Food Programme reporting that virtually the entire population of Gaza is facing acute food insecurity.

The legal characterization of these facts has been the subject of proceedings before the International Court of Justice, initiated by South Africa in December 2023. The ICJ issued provisional measures in January 2024, ordering Israel to take all measures within its power to prevent acts in Gaza that could fall within the scope of the Genocide Convention. It did not declare genocide — the legal threshold for that determination requires prolonged proceedings — but its provisional measures finding that there was a “plausible risk” of genocide represented the most significant international legal judgment on the conflict in its history.

The legal debate about whether Israel’s conduct constitutes genocide under the Genocide Convention’s specific legal definition — which requires proof of specific intent to destroy a group in whole or in part — is ongoing and genuinely contested among legal scholars. What is not genuinely contested, except by Israel’s government and its most committed defenders, is that the scale of civilian casualties and infrastructure destruction in Gaza far exceeds any reasonable definition of proportionality under international humanitarian law. The laws of war permit military operations against legitimate military targets even when civilian casualties result. They do not permit the destruction of civilian infrastructure on the scale visible in Gaza, the use of famine as a weapon of war, or military operations whose effects are indistinguishable from collective punishment.

The Hostages

The 251 people taken into Gaza on October 7th deserve specific acknowledgment in this accounting, because they are among the most direct human consequences of the catastrophe and because the hostage situation was used, misused, and politically exploited in ways that compounded the original crime.

Of the 251 taken, 168 were recovered alive over the course of the war. 87 were retrieved already deceased. They included elderly Holocaust survivors, young people taken from the music festival, soldiers, and civilians of multiple nationalities. Their families lived for more than two years in a condition of uncertainty that is among the most acute forms of suffering the conflict produced on the Israeli side. statista

The hostage situation was a central factor in the political dynamics of the Israeli response. Netanyahu’s government faced domestic pressure from hostage families and their supporters — who argued repeatedly, publicly, and with increasing fury that the military campaign was not bringing the hostages home and may have been killing them — and from the far-right members of his coalition, who argued that any ceasefire agreement would require releasing Palestinian prisoners and halting military operations in ways that compromised Israel’s security objectives. The gap between those two pressures was never honestly addressed. It was managed, deferred, and exploited.

A U.S.-brokered deal under President Trump’s 20-point peace plan ultimately led to the release of all living hostages, a partial Israeli withdrawal, and the exchange of nearly 2,000 Palestinian prisoners. All 20 living Israeli hostages remaining in Gaza were released within three days of the October 2025 ceasefire. The last body — that of Master Sergeant Ran Gvili — was recovered by the IDF on January 26, 2026. His recovery marked the first time since 2014 that Hamas held no Israeli captives in Gaza.

The hostage families — who marched, protested, and demanded accountability from their own government with a moral authority that no political actor could dismiss — represented the human cost of October 7th most immediately. That they ultimately got their people back does not retroactively justify the years of delay, the political exploitation of their suffering, or the cost in Palestinian lives accumulated in the interval. The accounting runs in multiple directions simultaneously. That is what makes it an accounting and not a verdict.

Lebanon: The Second Front

Beginning on October 8, 2023 — the day after October 7th — Hezbollah opened what it described as a “support front” from southern Lebanon, launching rockets, anti-tank missiles, and drones against northern Israel in daily exchanges that continued for nearly a year.

The exchanges displaced approximately 60,000 Israelis from communities in northern Israel and approximately 90,000 Lebanese from communities in southern Lebanon. They caused significant damage on both sides of the border without producing a decisive military outcome — Hezbollah was calibrating its involvement to maintain pressure without triggering a full-scale war, and Israel was managing the northern front while focusing its military effort on Gaza.

In September 2024, Israel dramatically escalated. The pager and walkie-talkie attacks — in which communications devices used by Hezbollah operatives were booby-trapped and detonated simultaneously, killing and maiming thousands of Hezbollah members — were followed by the assassination of Nasrallah and a ground invasion of southern Lebanon. Israel’s military campaign degraded Hezbollah’s capabilities significantly and demonstrated intelligence penetration of the organization at a depth that shocked security analysts globally.

A ceasefire agreement brokered by the United States and France was announced in November 2024. Under its terms, Hezbollah was to withdraw north of the Litani River and the Lebanese army was to deploy to the south, with Israeli forces withdrawing over a 60-day period. The implementation has been contested and incomplete, with Israel conducting strikes on Lebanese territory after the ceasefire and the full withdrawal of Israeli forces from Lebanese soil remaining unresolved as this series is written.

Destruction in southern Lebanon after Israeli operations against Hezbollah in 2024.

The Lebanon campaign illustrates the same fundamental problem as the Gaza campaign: military operations can degrade an organization’s immediate capability without addressing the political conditions that sustain it. Hezbollah remains embedded in the Shia community of southern Lebanon. Iran’s support, though disrupted by the losses, has not been severed. The political vacuum that Hezbollah fills in Lebanon’s dysfunctional political system has not been addressed. The conditions that produced Hezbollah in 1982 — the absence of a Lebanese state capable of governing its own territory, the Israeli military presence that periodically invades it, the Iranian strategic interest in maintaining a proxy force on Israel’s northern border — remain substantially unchanged.

The Western Response and Its Moral Cost

The Western response to October 7th and its aftermath represents one of the most consequential failures of moral consistency in the recent history of liberal democracy, and it has extracted a price in international credibility that will take decades to recover.

In the immediate aftermath of October 7th, Western governments — led by the United States, followed by Britain, Canada, Germany, France, and Australia — expressed unconditional solidarity with Israel, invoked Israel’s right to self-defence, and committed to continued military support. This solidarity was morally appropriate given the scale of the October 7th atrocity.

What followed was not morally appropriate.

As the scale of civilian casualties in Gaza mounted — as hospitals were bombed, as famine was declared, as the ICJ issued its provisional measures finding, as journalists and aid workers were killed in airstrikes that Israel initially claimed were targeted at militants and subsequently acknowledged were errors — the same Western governments maintained their military support, their diplomatic cover at the UN Security Council, and their public characterizations of Israel’s conduct as consistent with international humanitarian law.

The United States used its Security Council veto to block ceasefire resolutions in October 2023, December 2023, and February 2024 — each time preventing the international community from demanding a halt to operations whose humanitarian consequences were visible to the entire world in real time. It eventually abstained on a ceasefire resolution in March 2024, allowing it to pass, but continued military aid transfers throughout.

Canada — my Canada, this series’ Canada — initially aligned fully with the US position, approved military export permits for components that ended up in Israeli weapons systems, and was late and inadequate in its calls for humanitarian pauses. The Carney government has been more forthcoming than its predecessor in acknowledging Palestinian civilian suffering, but Canadian military exports to Israel and Canadian diplomatic cover at key moments place Canada firmly in the category of Western governments whose stated values and actual conduct diverged catastrophically during this period.

The cost of this divergence has been measured in the collapse of Western moral authority across the Global South — the countries of Africa, Asia, Latin America, and the Middle East that watched Western governments invoke international law to condemn Russia’s invasion of Ukraine and then invoke Israel’s right to self-defence to cover operations in Gaza that by the same legal standards were in violation of international humanitarian law. The hypocrisy was not subtle. It was categorical. And it has been registered in the diplomatic shifts, the UN voting patterns, and the public opinion of billions of people who will now evaluate every future Western invocation of international law in the light of what they observed between October 2023 and the present day.

Where It Stands

As this series is written — April 2026 — the situation in Gaza is this: a fragile ceasefire, negotiated in phases, has produced the release of some hostages and some Palestinian prisoners. Israel has announced intentions to resume military operations. The humanitarian situation in Gaza remains catastrophic. The reconstruction of Gaza’s civilian infrastructure has not meaningfully begun. The political question of who governs Gaza after the military campaign — a question the Israeli government has deliberately avoided answering — remains entirely unresolved.

What is no longer ambiguous is Israel’s territorial intention. IDF Chief of Staff Eyal Zamir stated in December 2025: “We will not allow Hamas to reestablish itself. We have operational control over extensive parts of the Gaza Strip, and we will remain on those defense lines. The yellow line is a new border line, serving as a forward defensive line for our communities and a line of operational activity.” Israel now controls more than half of Gaza. The other half — less than half the territory of one of the most overcrowded places on earth — is where more than two million Gazans are confined. Netanyahu has explicitly taken credit for this: Israel has leveled the buffer zone, is keeping it, and is shooting anyone who approaches it from inside Gaza. The ceasefire has not ended the war. It has consolidated its territorial gains while the world’s attention moved elsewhere. For those Gazans still alive and still displaced, the prospect of return to their homes — many of which no longer exist — is vanishing in real time.

The situation in Lebanon is this: a contested ceasefire, Hezbollah degraded but not destroyed, southern Lebanon partially evacuated and partially reoccupied, the Lebanese state as dysfunctional as it was before the war, and the conditions that produce Hezbollah unchanged.

The situation in the West Bank is this: settler violence at record levels, with the Israeli human rights organization Yesh Din documenting unprecedented numbers of attacks on Palestinian communities, some conducted with IDF soldiers present and complicit. The Palestinian Authority’s legitimacy is lower than at any previous point. The prospect of a Palestinian state is more remote than at any point since the Oslo Accords.

The situation in Israel is this: a society traumatized by October 7th, divided between those demanding the return of the hostages at any cost and those demanding the continuation of military operations at any cost, governed by the most right-wing coalition in the country’s history, facing an International Criminal Court arrest warrant for its Prime Minister for war crimes and crimes against humanity, and moving steadily further from the political centre that might have sustained a peace process.

This is the aftermath of the original sin and its compound interest, in the year 2026.
