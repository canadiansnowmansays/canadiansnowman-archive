SECTION I: INTRODUCTION

America faces threats.

These threats are serious. They are numerous. They are, in the judgment of this office, best understood by reading this document, which will explain them clearly, concisely, and with a minimum of typos.²

The fact pattern under the Biden Administration was clear: individuals at the highest levels of government used their powers to target political enemies. This is relevant to counterterrorism because everything is relevant to counterterrorism. This office has a broad mandate.

Previous administrations — both Republican and Democrat — failed to address the threats outlined in this document. They tried hard. They were wrong. They ran programs with names like “Countering Violent Extremism,” which, in retrospect, sounds like something they made up. This strategy takes a different approach: identifying threats clearly, naming them boldly, and promising to destroy them with the full force of American power, which is to say: mapping them.

We will do a lot of mapping.

SECTION II: THE THREE THREATS

This strategy identifies three categories of terrorist threat facing the United States. They are presented here in order of seriousness, which is also the order in which they appear, which may or may not reflect their relative priority, depending on which section you read first.

THREAT CATEGORY ONE: ISLAMIST TERRORISM

Islamist terrorism is a serious and longstanding threat to the United States and its allies. Iran, which this strategy identifies as “the greatest threat to the United States emanating from the Middle East,” sponsors terrorism, funds proxy forces, and has demonstrated sustained hostility to American interests.

It should be noted that the 2025 National Security Strategy, produced by this administration one year ago, described the Iranian threat in considerably more measured terms. The difference between the 2025 assessment and the 2026 assessment is approximately one war, the details of which are available in other documents.

This office has identified Iran as the greatest threat.

This office also spent the past year describing its strategy as imminent.

We present these facts without further comment.³

³ The CIA has assessed that Iran “continues to maintain substantial capabilities” and “is nowhere near close to collapse.” This assessment was delivered during the active conflict. It was not incorporated into this strategy, which was finalized before anyone asked the CIA.

THREAT CATEGORY TWO: NARCOTERRORISTS AND TRANSNATIONAL GANGS

Drug cartels and transnational criminal organizations pose a significant threat to American communities, borders, and the legal frameworks governing the use of military force in international waters.

This strategy designates these organizations as “narcoterrorists,” which is a useful term because it allows military operations that might otherwise require legal justification to be reframed as counterterrorism operations, which require different legal justification, some of which this office is still working on.

The strategy promises to address narcoterrorism through a combination of law enforcement, intelligence, and “necessary and specific actions in self-defense,” which is a phrase that means several things depending on which body of water you are in when you read it.

THREAT CATEGORY THREE: VIOLENT LEFT-WING EXTREMISTS, INCLUDING ANARCHISTS AND ANTI-FASCISTS

This category represents the most significant analytical contribution of the 2026 Counterterrorism Strategy and the one most likely to be discussed in international security literature for years to come.

The strategy identifies “Violent Left-Wing Extremists” as a top-tier terrorist threat equivalent in priority to Islamist terrorism and narcoterrorism. These extremists are characterized as “anti-American, radically pro-transgender, and anarchist.”⁴

They are supported by foreign nations.

The strategy does not name these nations.

The strategy promises to name them eventually.

This office has promised things before. See: foreword.

The strategy commits to “mapping” these extremists — identifying their membership, their funding streams, their ties to “international organizations like Antifa,” and their connections to foreign state sponsors. It further promises to “use law enforcement tools to cripple them operationally before they can maim or kill the innocent.”

For context: Antifa has no membership rolls, no organizational structure, no treasury, no leadership, no headquarters, and no known fish-oil supplement sales. It is described by most terrorism scholars as a loose affiliation of people who dramatically think of themselves as anti-fascist fighters and whose primary activities consist of showing up at demonstrations in black clothing and occasionally breaking a window.

This strategy has elevated them to the status of a foreign-backed transnational terrorist network with state sponsors.

The state sponsors will be mapped.

The mapping will be imminent.⁵

SECTION III: STRATEGIC RECOMMENDATIONS

This strategy recommends the following:

  1. Identify terror actors and plots before they happen.

  2. Cut off their arms, funding, and recruiting streams.

  3. Destroy them by “taking necessary and specific actions in self-defense to neutralize imminent threats.”

  4. Map the transgender anarchists.

  5. Find the foreign nations sponsoring the transgender anarchists.

  6. Map those too.

These recommendations are comprehensive. They are also, this office acknowledges, somewhat general. A strategy typically includes specific discussions of priorities, how the instruments of national power will be deployed, and the risks and rewards of various options.

This strategy includes the word “imminent.”

We believe this is a reasonable starting point.⁶

The strategy does not include a timeline, a budget, a threat assessment methodology, an interagency coordination framework, or a definition of success. These are known in the field as “the rest of the strategy.” They will be forthcoming.⁶ᵃ
