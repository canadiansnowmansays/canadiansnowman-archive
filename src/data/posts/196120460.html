Ruins of the Old Summer Palace (Yuan Ming Yuan) in Beijing, deliberately burned by British and French forces in 1860 during the Second Opium War. The site remains unrestored — a deliberate monument to the Century of Humiliation

The opium arrived legally.

That is the detail most Western accounts elide, because it complicates the clean moral narrative of British imperialism as straightforward theft. The East India Company did not smuggle opium into China in the dead of night. It lobbied for the trade, conducted it at scale, and defended it before Parliament as a legitimate commercial activity. The British government agreed. The trade was conducted in the open, and it was conducted because the British had run out of other options.

For a century, European merchants had faced an intractable problem. China produced things the world wanted — silk, porcelain, tea — and wanted almost nothing in return. The trade deficit was settled in silver, which flowed out of Europe and into China in quantities that alarmed treasuries. The Qianlong Emperor’s letter to George III, dismissing British manufactured goods as curiosities, was not diplomatic posturing. It was an accurate description of the commercial reality. China was self-sufficient in everything that mattered, and the West had nothing to offer that Chinese consumers wanted.

Opium solved the problem. Indian opium, grown under East India Company supervision, could be sold to Chinese consumers who wanted it very much once they had tried it. The trade balanced the books. Between 1800 and 1839, opium imports to China grew from roughly 4,000 chests per year to more than 40,000. An estimated two million Chinese were addicted by the time the Daoguang Emperor decided the situation had become a national crisis.

Commissioner Lin Zexu, appointed to suppress the trade, confiscated 1,100 tonnes of opium from British merchants in Canton in 1839 and destroyed it publicly, dissolving it in saltwater trenches dug into the beach at Humen and flushing the solution into the sea. He also wrote a letter to Queen Victoria — a letter she almost certainly never read — appealing to her conscience: if opium was too harmful to sell to British subjects, why was it acceptable to sell to Chinese ones?

The British response was not a reply. It was a fleet.

Commissioner Lin Zexu oversees the public destruction of over 1,100 tonnes of confiscated British opium at Humen in 1839 — an act that provoked the First Opium War

The First Opium War, 1839 to 1842, was not a war in any sense that tested the two sides equally. British steamships moved against the wind. British cannon outranged anything the Chinese coastal batteries could answer. The Nemesis, an iron-hulled East India Company warship, became famous for its ability to navigate shallow rivers and attack shore positions that had never needed to defend against a vessel that did not need the tide. Chinese soldiers fought with genuine courage against an enemy whose weapons operated on principles they had not encountered. It did not matter.

The Treaty of Nanking, signed aboard a British warship on the Yangtze River in August 1842, established the template for everything that followed.

China ceded Hong Kong island to Britain in perpetuity. Five ports — Canton, Amoy, Fuzhou, Ningbo, and Shanghai — were opened to British trade and residence. China paid an indemnity of 21 million silver dollars, covering both the cost of the destroyed opium and the cost of the war that followed its destruction. British citizens in China would be subject to British law administered by British courts, not Chinese law — a provision called extraterritoriality that effectively meant Britain governed its own subjects on Chinese soil.

The signing of the Treaty of Nanking aboard a British warship, August 1842. China ceded Hong Kong, opened five treaty ports, paid a massive indemnity, and accepted extraterritoriality — the template for a century of unequal treaties.

The Qing court signed because it had no choice. The emperor’s armies had been unable to stop the warships. But the court did not fully understand what it had signed. The assumption was that the treaty was a temporary accommodation — foreign barbarians had always eventually been absorbed or pacified. History suggested they were right: the Mongols who conquered China in the 13th century had, within two generations, become Chinese emperors performing Chinese rituals. The Manchus who founded the Qing dynasty in 1644 had done the same. The assumption that the British would follow the same pattern was not unreasonable. It was simply wrong.

The Europeans were not interested in ruling China. They were interested in trading with it on terms they controlled. That distinction — between conquest and penetration — was one the Qing court had no framework to process. Tributary relationships were comprehensible. Commercial dominance enforced by military superiority without colonial administration was a new kind of relationship for which Chinese statecraft had no category.

Before the ink on the Treaty of Nanking was dry, the supplementary Treaty of the Bogue extended its provisions, granting Britain most-favoured-nation status — meaning any concession subsequently granted to any other foreign power would automatically apply to Britain as well. It was a ratchet with no reverse position. The United States and France demanded and received their own treaties within two years, with similar most-favoured-nation clauses. The legal architecture of foreign privilege in China was being constructed, clause by clause, and the Qing court understood neither the speed at which it was being built nor the difficulty of dismantling it once constructed.

The opium trade, meanwhile, continued. Commissioner Lin’s dramatic destruction of the British stocks had ended his career — he was exiled to Xinjiang — without ending the trade. The Treaty of Nanking had not explicitly legalised opium, a deliberate ambiguity that allowed the trade to continue under legal cover while the British government maintained diplomatic deniability. The second Opium War would resolve the ambiguity.

The Second Opium War, 1856 to 1860, confirmed the lesson of the first.

The pretext was the Arrow incident — British and Chinese officials disputed whether a Chinese-owned, Hong Kong-registered vessel boarded by Qing authorities had been flying a British flag at the time. The dispute escalated to a Franco-British military expedition that culminated in the sacking of the Yuan Ming Yuan — the Old Summer Palace — outside Beijing.

The Yuan Ming Yuan was not simply a palace. It was an 860-acre complex of gardens, pavilions, libraries, and imperial collections built over more than a century, combining Chinese architectural tradition with European baroque elements commissioned by Qing emperors who had employed Jesuit architects. It contained irreplaceable imperial treasures: bronzes, jade, silk, paintings, clocks, porcelain, manuscripts. The British and French forces looted it for three days. Then they burned it.

The decision to burn the palace — made by Lord Elgin, son of the man who had removed the Elgin Marbles from the Parthenon — was deliberate and intended as a message to the emperor, who had ordered the torture and killing of British envoys sent under flag of truce. Elgin wanted to punish the emperor personally without inflicting further suffering on the Chinese population. He chose the emperor’s personal treasure.

The ruins of the Yuan Ming Yuan still stand in Beijing. They have not been restored. They are maintained as ruins, surrounded by a park, visited by school groups and tourists. The decision to leave them as they are is a political decision, renewed by every successive Chinese government. The broken marble columns and collapsed stone foundations are not archaeology. They are a reminder. The Century of Humiliation needs its monuments, and China has kept this one.

The sacking and burning of the Old Summer Palace (Yuan Ming Yuan) by Anglo-French forces in 1860. One of the greatest cultural losses of the Century of Humiliation, its ruins still stand as a national reminder.

What followed the two Opium Wars was not peace. It was a prolonged dismemberment conducted by multiple powers simultaneously.

France fought a series of wars with China through the 1880s, ultimately absorbing Vietnam — which had been a Chinese tributary state for centuries — into French Indochina. Britain extended its control over Burma, another tributary state, in 1885. Russia, expanding across Central Asia, extracted territorial concessions along China’s northern and northwestern frontiers — an estimated 1.5 million square kilometres of territory ceded through a series of “supplementary” treaties in the 1850s through 1880s that the Chinese court signed under military duress.

The treaty port system that these concessions created deserves particular attention, because it shaped the physical landscape of Chinese cities in ways still visible today. A treaty port was a coastal or riverine city in which foreign nationals lived under their own laws, in their own neighbourhoods, served by their own courts, police, and post offices. Shanghai’s International Settlement and French Concession — the districts where foreign companies built their headquarters, foreign merchants built their homes, and Chinese citizens could enter but not reside — were the most famous examples. By the end of the 19th century, dozens of Chinese cities had concession districts where the ordinary law of the country did not apply to the people who had imposed extraordinary terms on it.

The Shanghai Bund today — the waterfront of neoclassical and art deco buildings constructed during the treaty port era, when foreign powers operated under their own laws on Chinese soil

The geography of Shanghai’s Bund — the famous waterfront boulevard of European neoclassical and art deco buildings facing the Huangpu River — is a physical record of the treaty port era. The buildings that line it were constructed by British, American, French, German, and Japanese commercial interests as headquarters for banks, trading companies, and shipping firms. They were built to impress, and they do. Walking the Bund today, the scale of the commercial penetration is legible in stone and steel. The Chinese government has not demolished these buildings. It has kept them, as it has kept the ruins of the Yuan Ming Yuan — as monuments to what was done and to the motivation for undoing it.

Japan’s emergence as the decisive shock.

China and Japan had a relationship of civilisational hierarchy — Japan had spent centuries adopting Chinese writing, Buddhism, administrative systems, and artistic forms. In the Chinese view, Japan was a tributary state that had borrowed heavily from the centre. When Japan defeated China in the First Sino-Japanese War of 1894 to 1895, winning Taiwan and the Liaodong Peninsula and extracting an indemnity equivalent to three times the annual revenue of the Chinese state, the psychological impact was unlike any of the European defeats. Being beaten by European powers was humiliating. Being beaten by a country that had modelled itself on China was something different — a category collapse that forced a fundamental reassessment.

The reassessment, when it came, pointed in directions the Qing court found threatening. Intellectuals who had studied Japan’s Meiji Restoration — the rapid modernisation programme that had transformed Japan from feudal isolation to military power in a single generation — argued that China needed to do the same: adopt Western institutions, reform the examination system, restructure the military, separate the functions of the state from the ritual authority of the emperor. The Hundred Days’ Reform of 1898 attempted exactly this. The Empress Dowager Cixi, who had managed the court from behind the throne for decades, crushed it in a coup after 103 days, executed the leading reformers, and had the Guangxu Emperor confined to an island in the imperial gardens for the rest of his life.

The Empress Dowager preferred the Boxers.

The Boxer Uprising of 1899 to 1901 deserves its own extended treatment — which it will receive in the next part of this series — but its place in the Century of Humiliation is worth marking here. The Boxers were members of a Chinese nationalist movement, “The Society of Righteous and Harmonious Fists,” who believed, among other things, that martial arts training could confer invulnerability to bullets. They attacked foreign missions, Chinese Christian converts, and the infrastructure of foreign penetration — railways, telegraph lines, mines — with considerable violence and remarkable courage given what they were fighting.

The Empress Dowager, calculating that the Boxers might succeed in expelling the foreigners, declared war on all foreign powers simultaneously in June 1900. It was the worst miscalculation of her long career.

Eight nations — Britain, France, Germany, Russia, Japan, the United States, Austria-Hungary, and Italy — assembled an expeditionary force that marched on Beijing, relieved the besieged foreign legations, and occupied the Forbidden City. The Empress Dowager fled disguised as a peasant. The peace settlement that followed, the Boxer Protocol of 1901, required China to pay an indemnity of 450 million taels of silver — more than the entire annual revenue of the Chinese government — spread over 39 years at four percent interest. Foreign powers were granted the right to station troops in Beijing. China was required to demolish forts between Beijing and the sea.

The Forbidden City — the literal centre of the known world, where the Son of Heaven received the tribute of all nations — was occupied by eight foreign armies. Some soldiers posed for photographs in the imperial throne room.

Soldiers of the Eight-Nation Alliance in Beijing after relieving the legations during the Boxer Uprising, 1900–1901. The subsequent Boxer Protocol imposed a crushing indemnity — one tael of silver for every Chinese citizen.

There is a number that Chinese students are taught, and that functions in Chinese political discourse the way that certain dates function in other national memories: 450 million taels.

The indemnity imposed by the Boxer Protocol was structured at one tael per Chinese citizen — a deliberate humiliation encoded in the mathematics of the settlement. The foreign powers were not merely demanding reparations. They were putting a price on each Chinese life, and the price was calculated to consume an entire generation’s surplus. The interest payments on the indemnity were not fully settled until 1940, during the Japanese occupation.

The United States, to its partial credit, eventually returned its share of the indemnity — approximately $30 million — for Chinese use in educating students in the United States. The programme funded the Tsinghua University predecessor institution and sent hundreds of Chinese students to American universities. Several of those students returned with doctorates in physics and engineering. Some of them built the Chinese nuclear programme. The irony is, again, not lost on Chinese historians.

I am writing this series from Toronto, at a distance of both geography and culture from the events it describes. That distance is part of the Canadian outsider perspective I bring to all of my work on China. I find China genuinely extraordinary — its history, its scale, its demonstrated capacity for civilisational achievement. I also understand, through the silence of a friend in Shenzhen who stopped responding to my political essays, that the system governing contemporary China is not separable from the history producing it.

The Century of Humiliation is not a talking point deployed by Xi Jinping for domestic political convenience. It is the lived memory of a civilisation that spent a century being told, through gunboats and indemnities and occupied throne rooms, that it was not the centre of anything. The restoration narrative — the Long Return of this series’ title — is not propaganda overlaid on a contented population. It is the response of a nation that knows exactly what was taken from it and has spent 75 years deciding how to respond.

Understanding that response requires understanding what was taken. This part has tried to begin that account.
