The Three Amigos — Putin, Xi, and Kim Jong-un, Tiananmen Square, 2025. Not a photograph of a supplicant and his patrons. A photograph of three peers. Rao Aimin/Xinhua, via Associated Press

There is a photograph from Beijing, taken in 2025, that tells you everything you need to know about how the world has changed.

Three men stand on a ceremonial balcony above Tiananmen Square, clapping. To the left, Vladimir Putin, architect of a war that has now consumed four years and hundreds of thousands of lives. In the centre, Xi Jinping, the man who has staked his legacy on restoring China to what he calls its rightful place at the centre of history. And to the right, neat in a dark suit, with the composed expression of a man who has outlasted every prediction of his imminent collapse, Kim Jong-un of North Korea.

This is not a photograph of a supplicant and his patrons. This is a photograph of three peers.

How did that happen? The short answer involves a pandemic, a war in Ukraine, a nuclear weapons programme, and a ruthlessness that made the other two look almost moderate by comparison. The long answer — the true answer — begins somewhere else entirely. It begins with geography. It begins with a peninsula that has been, for the entirety of recorded history, the most dangerous piece of real estate in Northeast Asia.

It begins, in other words, with the dagger.

The Land of the Morning Calm

The Korean Peninsula juts southward from the Asian continent for roughly a thousand kilometres, flanked by the Yellow Sea to the west and the Sea of Japan to the east. To the northwest, the Yalu River forms a border with China. To the northeast, the Tumen River separates Korea from both China and Russia. Across a narrow strait to the southeast lies Japan.

Koreans have a saying about this geography. They describe their country as a small shrimp surrounded by whales. The peninsula is roughly 44 times smaller than China and 102 times smaller than Russia. Even Japan’s total land area is larger. And every one of those whales has, at one point or another in Korean history, tried to swallow it.

The geopolitical logic is remorseless. China has always viewed the Korean Peninsula as a natural buffer protecting its northeastern hinterland — as close and essential as, in the Chinese phrase, lips and teeth. A friendly Korea is a shield. A hostile one is a hammer hanging over the Chinese dragon’s head. For Russia, the peninsula offers potential access to warm-water ports and a northeastern anchor in any contest for Pacific influence. For Japan, Korea is the dagger pointed at its side — not because Korea itself was ever the threat, but because any great continental power that controlled the peninsula could use it as a staging ground to reach the Japanese home islands.

The peninsula has no choice about any of this. Its strategic significance was inscribed by geology long before any human being arrived to suffer the consequences. Every Korean ruler, across four thousand years of recorded history, has had to decide the same unanswerable question: how do you survive between the whales?

The answers have varied. The methods have ranged from tribute and accommodation to fierce resistance to deliberate isolation to, in the twentieth century, the most radical solution of all — the acquisition of weapons capable of annihilating the whales entirely.

That last solution is Kim Jong-un’s. But its logic runs all the way back to the beginning.

The Bear and the God

Korean history officially begins with a myth. According to the thirteenth-century Samguk yusa — the “Memorabilia of the Three Kingdoms” — the god Hwanung descended from heaven to a sandalwood tree on a mountain, accompanied by ministers of wind, rain, and cloud. A bear and a tiger living nearby begged to become human. Hwanung gave them sacred food and instructions: remain in a cave for one hundred days, and humanity would be theirs. The tiger gave up and ran away. The bear persisted, endured, and was transformed into a woman. She and Hwanung conceived a son, Dangun Wanggeom, who founded the kingdom of Gojoseon in 2333 BCE and ruled for fifteen centuries.

The bear endured. Hwanung, the bear-woman, and Dangun — the founding myth that still binds both Koreas. North Korea even claimed to have found his tomb near Pyongyang.

South Korea still celebrates October 3rd as National Foundation Day in Dangun’s honour. North Korea, for its part, claimed in 1993 to have discovered Dangun’s tomb — conveniently located near Pyongyang — and reburied him with full state honours. Even across the ideological chasm that divides the peninsula, the founding myth belongs to both.

Modern historians treat Gojoseon as a real polity — probably emerging around the seventh or eighth century BCE in northern Korea and Manchuria, with the founding myth added later for nation-building purposes. The location matters: the first Korean capital at Wanggeomseong, which later historians often identified with present-day Pyongyang, was established in the heart of what is now North Korea. The origin point of Korean civilization is the northern half of the peninsula. This is the geography that Kim Jong-un’s regime has always exploited — the claim to be the authentic Korea, the Korea of the founders, the Korea that did not collaborate with the colonizers or shelter beneath an American military umbrella.

Gojoseon lasted, in one form or another, until 108 BCE, when the Han Dynasty of China conquered it and established four commanderies in the territory. The first great Korean state ended not through internal collapse but through the arrival of a Chinese army. It was not the last time.

The Triangle and Its Tensions

What followed Gojoseon’s collapse was centuries of competition, alliance, and war among a shifting constellation of Korean kingdoms, always with the shadow of China overhead and the threat of Japan across the water. By the first century BCE, three major kingdoms had consolidated control of the peninsula: Goguryeo in the north, commanding Manchuria and the upper half of the peninsula; Baekje in the southwest; and Silla in the southeast.

The Three Kingdoms period, which ran from roughly the first century BCE to 668 CE, established patterns that would repeat, in different registers, for the next two millennia. Every Korean state had to triangulate between China and Japan. Every Korean state discovered that allying with one external power against another came with its own price. And every Korean state learned that even victory could deliver you into the hands of your supposed ally.

The unification of the peninsula under Silla is the clearest illustration. Silla allied with the Tang Dynasty of China to defeat first Baekje and then the formidable Goguryeo, which had repelled Chinese invasions for generations — most famously when General Eulji Mundeok drowned the Sui Dynasty’s million-man army in the Salsu River in 612, a defeat so catastrophic that it contributed to the collapse of the Sui and the rise of the Tang. But once Tang forces had helped Silla prevail, they tried to stay — establishing commanderies throughout the peninsula, including within Silla itself. Silla then had to fight the very ally it had invited in, defeating Tang’s navy at Gibeolpo in 676 and driving every Chinese soldier from Korean soil.

United Korea, born 676 CE. The lesson already inscribed in blood: great power partners are great power predators who haven’t yet found the right moment.

This lesson never left Korean strategic consciousness. It reappears in every century, under every dynasty. And it is absolutely present in the mind of Kim Jong-un, who has read his country’s history with attention, and who has drawn from it the only conclusion it seems to support: the peninsula survives only when it cannot be absorbed.

A Thousand Years, One Idea

Goguryeo — the fierce northern kingdom whose warriors repelled Chinese armies and whose spirit North Korea still invokes today. The historical root of the ‘impossible to break’ mythology.

Before leaving the Three Kingdoms period entirely, it is worth pausing on Goguryeo — the northern kingdom, the one whose territory most closely maps onto what is now North Korea. Goguryeo was the great military power of the peninsula for seven centuries, a kingdom whose armies repelled Chinese invasion after Chinese invasion with a ferocity that still resonates in North Korean state mythology today. When the Sui Dynasty sent a million men southward in 612, General Eulji Mundeok didn’t merely defeat them — he annihilated them, drawing the army deep into Korean territory before cutting off their supply lines and drowning the retreat in the Salsu River. Of the 305,000 soldiers in one Chinese column, fewer than 3,000 made it home. The Sui never recovered from the humiliation. The Tang, which replaced the Sui, spent decades trying to subdue Goguryeo before finally succeeding — and only then through the treacherous alliance with Silla that ultimately cost China the peninsula entirely.

Kim Jong-un’s regime invokes Goguryeo constantly. The warrior spirit, the defiance of Chinese military power, the willingness to absorb punishment and keep fighting — these are the historical materials from which North Korean identity is constructed. When state media describes North Korea as a country that cannot be broken by outside pressure, the ghost of Goguryeo is present in every sentence. The reference is not accidental. It is a regime that has read its own history and decided which parts to weaponize.

The Goryeo Dynasty (918–1392), from which the Western name “Korea” derives, consolidated the peninsula but could not insulate it. The Mongol invasions of the thirteenth century were catastrophic — not a single campaign but six, spanning decades, each more brutal than the last. Cities were burned to rubble. Populations were massacred or enslaved. The great Buddhist woodblock collection — the Tripitaka Koreana, 80,000 carved blocks representing centuries of accumulated scholarship — was destroyed in the first invasion, then painstakingly reconstructed over sixteen years in a second set that survives to this day, a testament to the Korean determination to preserve culture under conditions of existential assault. Tens of thousands of Koreans were abducted and sold into slavery across the Mongol empire. Goryeo survived as a vassal state, its kings marrying Mongol princesses, compelled to provide troops and ships for the Mongol invasions of Japan, its identity preserved only at the cost of sovereignty.

The experience left a scar that never fully healed. A people who had survived the Mongols, the Japanese invasions, the Manchu humiliations — who had seen their civilization dismembered and rebuilt, their scholars enslaved and their sacred texts burned — do not approach the world with easy optimism about the intentions of great powers. They approach it with a hard, specific knowledge of what great powers do when given the opportunity.

Then came the Joseon Dynasty in 1392, which would endure for five hundred and eighteen years — one of the longest continuous dynasties in world history. Joseon’s political philosophy was Confucian, its social structure rigid, its foreign policy premised on a careful tributary relationship with China that acknowledged hierarchy in exchange for stability. For long stretches, it worked. The peninsula enjoyed periods of genuine cultural flowering — the invention of the Hangul alphabet under Sejong the Great in the fifteenth century, advances in astronomy, medicine, and cartography, an extraordinarily rich literary and historical tradition.

But Joseon also carried within it a particular theory of survival that would have fateful consequences. Having been battered by the Japanese invasions of the 1590s — Toyotomi Hideyoshi’s campaigns that devastated the peninsula over seven years, killing hundreds of thousands — and then by the Manchu invasions of the 1630s, Joseon made a considered choice. It would limit contact with the outside world. It would control its borders. It would reduce its exposure to the forces that had brought it so close to destruction.

Western observers in the nineteenth century coined the phrase that would stick: the Hermit Kingdom. They meant it dismissively — a backward, fearful people hiding from progress. They were wrong about the motivation. Joseon’s isolation was not timidity. It was strategy, born from a specific, accurate assessment of what outside contact had historically delivered: invasion, occupation, and humiliation. The Joseon court looked at the world and concluded, not irrationally, that the world was dangerous and that the peninsula’s best protection was to minimize its surface area.

They were right about the danger. They were wrong about the remedy. By the time the nineteenth century arrived with its Western gunboats, unequal treaties, and a modernized imperial Japan, the Hermit Kingdom had not industrialized, had not built a modern military, and was governed by a court consumed with factional politics. The whales were circling again, and this time there was no Eulji Mundeok to drown them in a river.

Joseon court life — the Hermit Kingdom that chose isolation as strategy after centuries of invasion. A beautiful, rigid world that ultimately left it unprepared for the modern era.

The Name That Became a Curse

Here is what makes the title of this series something more than a historical echo: the phrase “Hermit Kingdom” did not die with the Joseon Dynasty in 1910. It transferred, almost seamlessly, to the state that now occupies the northern half of the peninsula.

The first use of “Hermit Kingdom” in its modern sense — referring specifically to Joseon under the Chosŏn dynasty — appeared in William Elliot Griffis’s 1882 book Korea: The Hermit Nation. By 1905, when Japan made Korea a protectorate, the phrase had already become standard shorthand in Western discourse for Korean isolation, Korean backwardness, Korean incomprehensibility. It was always, at bottom, a term of contempt.

Kim Il-sung, Kim Jong-il, and Kim Jong-un did not invent the isolation of the North. They inherited it, weaponized it, and ultimately transformed it into the central feature of their rule. The shoot-to-kill orders at the Chinese border, the jamming of foreign broadcasts, the public executions of those caught watching South Korean dramas — these are not aberrations from Korean history. They are a particular, extreme, ideologically supercharged version of a tendency that has been present in Korean statecraft for four hundred years.

The difference — and it is a crucial difference — is that Joseon’s isolation was chosen to avoid the attention of great powers. North Korea’s isolation is performed for the attention of great powers. Where Joseon tried to disappear, Kim Jong-un tries to be impossible to ignore.

That inversion tells you almost everything about the distance between the Joseon court and the man on the Beijing balcony.

The Dagger Reconsidered

There is one more thing the geography tells us, and it runs directly into the present moment.

The description of Korea as a dagger pointed at Japan’s side was always framed from the Japanese perspective — a threat to be neutralized, a peninsula to be controlled before some continental power could use it as a launching pad. But look at it from the peninsula itself, and the metaphor transforms entirely.

A dagger is a weapon. A weapon requires a hand to hold it.

For most of Korean history, that hand belonged to someone else — China, the Mongols, Japan, and then, after 1945, the competing hands of the United States and the Soviet Union, each gripping one half of the blade. The history of Korea is in large part the history of a people trying to reclaim their own weapon from the hands of others who found it useful.

Kim Jong-un’s nuclear programme, seen in this light, is not an irrational provocation. It is the most radical possible answer to the oldest question in Korean geopolitics: how do you prevent the whales from swallowing you? You make yourself indigestible. You acquire the capacity to make the act of swallowing fatal. You turn the dagger, at last, around.

Whether that answer is wise — whether a nuclear North Korea makes the peninsula more secure or simply more dangerous — is a question this series will return to. But understanding why that answer was reached requires understanding the full weight of what came before it: the Japanese invasions, the Mongol subjugation, the Chinese commanderies, the colonial humiliation, the partition, the war, the famine, and the long, grinding education in what it means to be a small country on a peninsula that the world’s great powers have never been able to leave alone.

We begin at the beginning. The bear chose to endure. The tiger ran.

Korea has always been the bear.
