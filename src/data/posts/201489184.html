This morning, for the first time in the twenty-four-year history of Space Exploration Technologies Corp., anyone with a brokerage account and $135 can own a piece of it.

The bell rings at 9:30 Eastern. SPCX opens on the Nasdaq. The largest initial public offering in the history of capital markets becomes a stock — a number on a screen, moving in real time, subject to the collective judgment of every retail investor who attended a Fidelity webinar this week, every pension fund manager who took a meeting at Goldman’s Manhattan office, every day trader who has been watching crypto derivatives on SpaceX futures since May, every index fund that will be forced to buy approximately $7 billion worth of SPCX within fifteen trading days whether it wants to or not.

Twenty-four years of private empire. One morning on the Nasdaq.

Ralph Kramden, we need to talk.

What the Market Is Actually Buying

Before the first trade prints, it is worth being precise about what $135 a share represents — because the number is the same for everyone but the asset is not.

For the 4,400 current and former SpaceX employees who are becoming millionaires today, SPCX is the payoff on a bet they made with their careers. Trevor Hise, a launch engineer who graduated college in 2011 against his parents’ wishes and stayed at SpaceX for twelve years, holds more than 100,000 shares worth at least $13.5 million. His father was a plumber at Kennedy Space Center. Gavin Petit, who joined in 2012 at $80,000 a year and took his bonuses in stock rather than cash when the rockets were still unproven, holds more than 50,000 shares — enough to be a millionaire several times over. He used earlier share sales to pay off his house in Denver. He kept the rest.

For roughly 400 of those employees, the number clears $100 million. Not millionaires. Centimillionaires. From stock that was part of their compensation at a company that was, for most of their tenure, a start-up that might not survive the next launch.

There is a parallel universe in which this story ends differently. Rumors have circulated for years that early SpaceX employees traded their stock for restaurant gift cards — Chili’s is the specific institution name that has passed into company legend. Those employees exist. They are, according to multiple SpaceX workers, consumed by regret. The gap between a Chili’s gift card and $13.5 million is the gap between not believing and believing, measured in years of vesting schedules and one Tuesday in September 2008 when a rocket worked on its fourth attempt and everything changed.

For the retail investor who attended a Fidelity webinar and bought 37 shares, SPCX is something different: a lottery ticket dressed as a blue chip, a way to own a piece of the future at a price that requires believing the future arrives on schedule.

For the index fund manager who had no choice — whose QQQ ETF will absorb SpaceX into the Nasdaq-100 within fifteen trading days under a rule Nasdaq implemented in March 2026 specifically to accommodate mega-IPOs — SPCX is an involuntary position in a company losing $4.9 billion a year that happens to be valued at $1.77 trillion.

Same ticker. Same price. Entirely different assets, depending on who is holding them and why.

The Cascade Nobody Voted For

The Nasdaq-100 fast-entry rule deserves more attention than it has received in the IPO coverage.

Under the rule change implemented in March 2026, mega-IPOs whose market capitalisation places them in the top 40 of the Nasdaq-100 can be fast-tracked into the index within fifteen trading days of their debut — rather than waiting months for the traditional inclusion criteria to be satisfied. SpaceX, at $1.77 trillion, would debut as roughly the seventh-largest company on the exchange. It qualifies on day one.

The practical consequence: every passive fund tracking the Nasdaq-100 — including the QQQ ETF, one of the most widely held investment vehicles in America — will be required to purchase approximately $7 billion worth of SPCX within three weeks of today. Not because the fund managers chose to own a money-losing rocket and AI conglomerate controlled by one man with 82% of the votes. Because the index rule requires it.

This is the moment the retirement accounts of ordinary Americans become involuntary passengers on the rocket. The 401(k) holder who attended no webinar, made no decision, and has no opinion about orbital AI compute or SpaceX’s Artemis contract will own SPCX before the end of June — because their index fund owns the Nasdaq-100, and the Nasdaq-100 now owns SpaceX.

Ralph Kramden threatened to send Alice to the moon against her will. The index inclusion rule does the same thing, at scale, to millions of American retirement savers. The destination is exciting. The consent was not requested.

The Moon That May Not Be Ready

This series has tracked the SpaceX story from Ralph Kramden’s apartment through the Apollo anniversary through Artemis II’s April launch through Flight 12’s deliberately missing heat shield tile. The narrative arc has been building toward a crewed lunar landing — the moment the “To The Moon” phrase becomes simultaneously a financial reality and a literal destination.

This week, the experts threw cold water on the timeline.

NASA administrator Jared Isaacman said he is “extremely confident” in a crewed moon landing before the end of 2028. “We’re going to return to the moon before the end of 2028,” he said at a briefing this week. “Just watch along.”

The independent experts are watching. They are not as confident.

“I think me and most people would say it’s not a realistic date,” said Casey Dreier, chief of space policy at the Planetary Society. Phil McAlister, the former director of NASA’s commercial space division, called it “unrealistic” while declining to call it impossible. Clayton Swope of the Center for Strategic and International Studies said: “It’s looking increasingly likely that the answer is no. But there is still a chance for a Hail Mary play.”

The obstacles are concrete. Neither SpaceX nor Blue Origin — the two private companies NASA has contracted to provide lunar landers — has completed development of a vehicle. Starship has suffered repeated failures during test flights. Blue Origin’s New Glenn exploded in May and damaged the company’s only launchpad. The 2028 timeline requires Starship to achieve orbital flight, demonstrate in-orbit propellant transfer across more than ten tanker missions, dock with an Orion capsule, and land humans on the lunar surface — all within roughly 30 months.

Dreier identified the structural problem with characteristic precision: “That’s a lot of power and hope that you place in just two people to provide a capability that is actually essential to a national goal. NASA is a passive witness to its fate.”

NASA is a passive witness to its fate. Read that sentence again, and then consider that the company whose lunar lander capability NASA is passively witnessing just raised $75 billion from the public markets on a valuation that treats the 2028 moon landing as a near-certainty rather than a Hail Mary.

The market is pricing the moon. The experts are pricing a delay. One of them is right. We will find out which on a timeline that the first day of SPCX trading cannot accelerate.

The Google Revelation

Buried in the week’s coverage, almost lost in the IPO noise, was a disclosure that reframes the entire SpaceX compute thesis.

Google will pay SpaceX $920 million a month for computing power.

This is separate from the Anthropic deal — $1.25 billion a month — disclosed in the original S-1. Two of the most consequential AI companies in the world are paying Musk’s rocket company a combined $2.17 billion every month to rent server capacity. The Colossus data centre infrastructure, built ostensibly for xAI’s Grok, is becoming an AI compute utility before it is even fully constructed. SpaceX is not just a rocket company with a sideline in AI. It is, increasingly, a compute landlord whose tenants include the two most important AI companies not named OpenAI.

The Anthropic detail carries a specific irony worth noting. Anthropic — the company that makes Claude, a competitor to Grok — is paying $1.25 billion a month to the man who built Grok, for the compute infrastructure that Grok does not fully utilise because Grok’s usage has disappointed. The competitor is subsidising the competition’s infrastructure. And both companies are now funding the IPO valuation of the entity that houses it all.

At $2.17 billion in monthly compute revenue, annualised to roughly $26 billion, the AI segment’s losses look different. The segment lost $6.35 billion from operations last year on $12.7 billion in capital expenditure. But the compute lease revenue — accelerating fast, with two anchor tenants now confirmed — suggests the orbital compute thesis is not science fiction. It is a business that is already operating, at scale, with blue-chip counterparties, before a single data centre satellite has been launched.

What Happens When the Bell Rings

The private secondary markets have been trading SpaceX between $129 and $137 in early June — bracketing the $135 IPO price closely. That narrow range suggests sophisticated buyers who had access to the secondary market believe $135 is approximately fair. It does not tell us where the stock opens when retail participation, index fund cascades, and first-day momentum trading enter the picture simultaneously.

The roadshow structure was designed for volatility. SpaceX fixed the price rather than setting a range, which means the $135 IPO price is not a negotiated midpoint — it is a floor declared by one man who does not negotiate. All price discovery happens in the secondary market, starting this morning. With retail investors expected to represent 30% of buyers — triple the typical proportion for large IPOs — and roughly 1,500 retail investors given a dedicated roadshow event on June 11, the opening session is where the real auction takes place.

Credible analysts disagree on valuation by a factor of three. The bull case prices a Starlink-as-global-mobile-carrier, orbital-AI-compute, Cybercab-fleet-in-every-city future. The bear case prices a money-losing conglomerate with governance so concentrated that minority shareholders have no meaningful recourse and a moon landing timeline that independent experts call unrealistic. Both cases are represented in the shareholder base as of this morning.

What is certain: when the first trade prints, twenty-four years of private empire become a public number. The man who sold the dream does not stop controlling the company. The woman who built the company does not receive more credit. The engineers who took their bonuses in stock instead of Chili’s gift cards become millionaires. The index fund holders become passengers. And the moon — the actual moon, the destination, the reason Ralph Kramden’s phrase works as a series title at all three levels simultaneously — remains approximately 384,000 kilometres away, on a schedule that the market is pricing more optimistically than the people who study it for a living.

We are going to the moon.

The market opened this morning and said so.

The experts said: probably not by 2028.

Somewhere in between those two sentences is the actual future — and SPCX, for better or worse, is now the instrument through which the world will be forced to price the difference.

A Note for the Series

Eight parts later, the moon is a stock. The punchline is that the stock may be pricing a timeline the rocket isn’t ready to meet. And the most powerful man in the history of private enterprise is about to find out, in real time, on a public exchange, whether the market agrees with the engineers or with him.

The series will continue as the story develops. Flight 13 is scheduled for later this month. The Artemis timeline will either hold or slip. The Tesla-SpaceX merger chatter will either materialise or dissipate. Anthropic will file publicly and OpenAI will follow.

Ralph Kramden never found out what was actually on the moon. He just knew it was far away and that threatening to send Alice there was the only power he had.

We are about to find out if the distance was the point, or the destination.
