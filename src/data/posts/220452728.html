For five essays, this series has mostly been about what other people want from Ukraine. Washington wants its refinery strikes to stop. Moscow wants its ports, its power and, in the end, its existence. Investors want Russian oil assets. Politicians want lower diesel prices before an election.

This essay steps away from all of that. It gives the floor to two Ukrainians who spoke this month, through very different doors, about what their country is. One is a historian. The other is a father. Neither asked to be a symbol, and both are trying, in their own ways, to stop their country from being treated as a thing to be traded.

The reporter from Donetsk

Illia Ponomarenko was born in 1992 in a small town in the Donetsk region of eastern Ukraine and studied at Mariupol State University. When Russia moved into his home region in 2014, he began working as a reporter. He has not stopped since. Illness kept him out of the army, so he turned to what David Frum, interviewing him for The Atlantic on October 14, 2026, called “a deeper fight: the fight over whether Ukraine has a history of its own.”

His first book, I Will Show You How It Was: The Story of Wartime Kyiv, came out in 2024. His second, The Land of Tears and Hope, written in English, is published by Bloomsbury in November. It tells the Ukrainian story from the Slavic tribes and the Vikings through the Cossacks, Soviet terror and the Maidan to the present war.

One caution. Frum recorded the conversation in early October, before the diesel deal and the days described in Parts IV and V, and some of what Ponomarenko says about the air war was overtaken within days. That does not make it less valuable. It shows what Ukrainians were thinking just before the latest blow.

Which city is older

Frum asked a deceptively simple question: how many years separate the founding of Kyiv from the founding of Moscow?

Ponomarenko laughed, and then explained why the question matters. Kyiv’s beginnings as a continuous city go back roughly to the time of the fall of Rome. Moscow came much later, as a distant fort in the forest. The cities that became the historic core of Russia, Vladimir and Suzdal among them, were settled by people moving out from the old Kyivan lands in search of new territory. “The simple truth,” he said, “is that what ended up being Ukraine gave birth to what ended up being Russia, not vice versa.”

He was careful not to turn that into chauvinism: Russians, Ukrainians and Belarusians share common roots, he said, but Ukraine holds “the biggest share” of them. Frum drew out why it matters now. Americans who want Washington to tilt toward Moscow, he said, insist the war is about NATO expansion. “But when you actually listen to actual Russians, they say, No, no, no; this is all about Yaroslav the Wise.” From Moscow’s point of view, Frum argued, the war is about whether Ukraine exists as a culture and a society at all, “except when they’re talking in English to gullible Americans.”

That is the deepest reason a deal over Ukraine’s head can never be just a deal. If the other side believes your country is a misunderstanding, every concession is a down payment.

The vicious circle

Ponomarenko’s central idea is a circle. Ukraine, he says, is rich, fertile and in the wrong place. For centuries its people have won some measure of independence, lost it again, and become “a powerhouse for new empires and a donor of resources for new wars.” The Russian Empire was built in large part on Ukraine’s wealth. So was Stalin’s Soviet Union. Hitler wanted it for his Lebensraum. “You have a really rich empire if you have Ukraine,” he said.

Frum added a dark footnote: by rough scholarly estimates, about two million people were taken over five centuries from what are now Ukraine, Poland and western Russia into the Ottoman slave trade, one of the least studied of the world’s great slave traffics. Ukraine has been traded before, in the most literal sense.

So “tears,” in his title, is not a figure of speech. The “hope” is that this time, for the first time in many centuries, Ukraine might break the circle and hand its children a free country.

A history that names its own shames

What struck me most, and struck Frum too, is that Ponomarenko does not write a flattering history.

Frum grew up in Canada, home to one of the world’s largest Ukrainian diasporas; more than a million Canadians claim Ukrainian ancestry. The diaspora writing of his youth, he recalled, often leaned defensive, with crimes against Jews denied or excused. What has changed since 1991, he said, is the ability to tell a story of national pride while honestly including national shame.

Ponomarenko’s example is Bohdan Khmelnytsky, the seventeenth-century Cossack leader on Ukraine’s banknotes and on the great equestrian statue in Kyiv’s Sofiiska Square. He was a founding father of Ukrainian nationhood, and his uprising against the Polish crown also unleashed terrible massacres of Jews. Ponomarenko explains the social system that set poor, oppressed peasants against Jewish tax collectors and leaseholders, but he refuses to justify it. “I explain how it happened, what happened,” he said. “But I do not idealize.”

Then he turned to the present. Ukraine’s chief rabbi is a fierce advocate for Ukrainian victory; his adopted son died fighting as a volunteer. A former chief mufti of Ukraine’s Muslims resigned his post to serve as a battlefield medic. In central Kyiv there is a museum to Sholem Aleichem and a plaque on the building where Golda Meir was born. A country fighting for its existence, he says, has found a purpose that “unites confessions, religions, many ethnicities.”

Not yet beaten

On the war itself, Ponomarenko offered something the Western headlines rarely do: evidence that Ukraine adapts.

When Russia began sending jet-powered drones at Kyiv, flying at 600 to 700 kilometres an hour, too fast for the machine-gun crews on pickup trucks, it was a shock. Within weeks, he said, Ukrainian companies had five or six prototype interceptors in testing. “The Russian campaign against Kyiv was a shock,” he said. “But at the same time, the Ukrainian response to this is very fast and very sharp.”

Reporting from Kyiv since bears him out, and shows what is at stake. Russia launched more than 3,000 jet-powered Shaheds at Ukraine in September alone, up from 350 in June, as Maria Varenikova and Paul Mozur reported for the New York Times on October 15, 2026. Ukraine often brings them down with F-16 missiles that cost nearly $1 million each. Nearly two dozen Ukrainian companies are racing to build cheap, AI-guided interceptors; one, the F-Jet 80, costs about $7,000. “The faster I make it work,” its designer told the Times, “the more peacefully I will sleep in Kyiv.”

He also described real gains near Kramatorsk and Sloviansk, where careful operations encircled Russian units and cut off what Ukrainians call the “northern claw.”

This is worth holding against what came next. The interceptors Ponomarenko described are exactly what the Starshield restriction now threatens, because the fastest drones need the connection most. A country that solves a problem in weeks has just had its solution throttled by an ally.

What Ukrainians think of America

Frum asked how Ukrainians feel about the United States now. Ponomarenko answered honestly. Trump, he said, is one of the least popular figures in Ukraine, and that has hurt how many Ukrainians see America itself. People had thought American support was “as static as the Earth itself.”

But he pushed back on his own countrymen. He tries, he said, to separate what this administration says and does from the American people, between 60 and 70 per cent of whom, across both parties, still support Ukraine. That so many people across an ocean should care about a country many of them had barely heard of before the war, he said, “is something that is to be deeply appreciated.”

Frum’s reply is one I will let stand: “For those of us who, like you, believe in the role of the United States as the guarantor of the free world, and who believe that the phrase free world means something, this has been a shameful hour.”

The father from Sumy

The second voice belongs to a man who never wanted to be heard.

Yevhen Ovcharov is a career police officer from Sumy, in Ukraine’s northeast, who was serving in the infantry on the eastern front, 300 miles from home. His story was told this week by Siobhán O’Grady and Liubov Sholudko in the New York Times on October 13, 2026.

At 9:45 p.m. on July 3, 2026, he watched a red speck on his live military map, a 500-pound Russian glide bomb, sliding toward his hometown. He called his wife, Nataliia. She told him not to worry: she and the girls were walking home from the park. Their elder daughter, Veronika, 13, was trailing behind with a friend. Their younger, Milana, 5, was beside her mother.

At 9:46, the call cut off.

For six minutes he heard nothing. At 9:52 his phone rang. It was Veronika, screaming that she was covered in blood and that Milana was lying completely still. “No one is helping,” she sobbed. “Dad, please come save us.” It was a four-hour drive. When he reached the hospital after 2 a.m., an ambulance driver told him his wife and daughter had died.

The only real defence against glide bombs is to shoot down the aircraft carrying them, and Ukraine still lacks the planes to do it. In July, Russia launched a record 8,300 of them. From June to August, by a United Nations estimate, Russian attacks killed more than 1,100 Ukrainian civilians and wounded 7,000.

Veronika survived with shrapnel scars and a burst eardrum. She wears a necklace her mother gave her on her 13th birthday, engraved: “Dream, Act, Love. Daughter, you can do everything. I’m proud. I love you — Mom.” Her father carries Milana’s blue Stitch toy in his bag everywhere he goes.

He also went looking for the police officer who had tried to save Milana. Dmytro Zuienko had given her CPR for 15 minutes, staying on even after others moved away for fear of a second strike.

At the site of the strike, by chance, Ovcharov met Mykhailo Fedorov, then Ukraine’s defence minister, who suggested a mission: tell the world why Ukraine needs more air defence and more planes. On September 21, Ovcharov and Veronika travelled to Stockholm to tell their story to Sweden’s defence minister, because Sweden has promised the jets that can stop glide bombers before they drop.

“Let the world see what Russia did to my family,” he said at his wife’s and daughter’s graves. “I want to tell Europe and the United States that our kids also want to live.” Then he looked back at the graves. “They also wanted to live.”

Two voices, one argument

Put the historian and the father side by side and they make the same argument from opposite ends.

Ponomarenko says Ukraine is old, real and its own, and has been passed from empire to empire for centuries. Ovcharov says what that passing costs, in a single family, on a single evening, on a walk home from the park. One explains why Ukraine refuses to be traded. The other shows what it pays for refusing.

Neither is asking for pity. The historian asks to be read. The father asks for jets. Both are asking the world to treat Ukraine as a country rather than a bargaining chip.

An ordinary Wednesday

The capital has become a battlefield of its own. Kim Barker, Maria Varenikova and Oleksandra Mykolyshyn of the New York Times spent a day there, Wednesday, October 7, and called it an average one: “Not too quiet, not too loud.” The air-raid alarm went on and off seven times, for about ten and a half hours. Parts of the city lost power and water. Six people died.

It began at 3:11 a.m. Within three minutes the missiles were landing. Nikita Horskyi and Alona Horska, whose 16-storey building has no shelter, made beds in the hallway, where two walls give more protection than one. Their son Mark, 10, hugged his stuffed goose. He thinks “God, help us,” he said, after every explosion. His sister Arina is 5.

At about 5:12 a.m., a second wave hit. Anatoliy Kavunenko, 66, had gone to his early shift as a security guard. His wife, Antonina, called and called. Later, still in her pyjama trousers, she stood before two burned bodies. “My brain can’t accept that this black piece of coal is my Tolik,” she said.

Mark’s school shelters in a basement rented to a gym, the Shogun Fight Club: punching bags, wrestling mats, no desks. That morning his fifth-grade class was practising the English word “bedroom” when the alert sounded. In the afternoon Oksana Stelmashchuk, 40, checked her phone to see what was flying, found a single drone on the city’s edge, and told her baby, Sophia, born on August 26 as drones struck beside the maternity hospital: “It is still far away, my girl.”

That night the Horskyi children ate burgers, brushed their teeth and climbed into their own beds. It was a good day, all in all, they said.

President Trump, the reporters noted, has said nothing about what Russia is doing to Kyiv. Moscow is killing more Ukrainian civilians than at any time since the full-scale invasion began.

Kyiv answers in its own way. On the night of Sunday, October 18, after a salvo of Russian missiles struck the city centre, Constant Méheut of the New York Times heard a song rise from an apartment window into the hush. It was “How Can I Not Love You, Kyiv of Mine,” a 1962 ode to the capital that has become the anthem of its defiance.

Christmas in Kyiv

Frum asked Ponomarenko, at the end, what one more chapter of his book would say if he could write it from the future.

He did not promise victory. His book ends, he said, on the word “hope.” But he knew what the afterword would be. Christmas Eve in Kyiv, on a frosty night, in the first Christmas after the war. Ukraine now officially keeps two Christmases, December 25 and January 7, one for each tradition, and every year of the war, he said, more Ukrainians choose the December date, turning west.

That would be his afterword: Christmas in postwar Kyiv, in an independent Ukraine. “It would mean that our hope came true,” he said, “of saving this country and giving it, handing it over to our children.”

Somewhere in Sumy there is an apartment with a dollhouse on the floor and a bed with “Princess” on the footboard, pushed up against her parents’ bed. That is the Christmas Ponomarenko is hoping for: one that Veronika gets to have, that Mark and Arina can sleep through in their own beds, and that no other child in Ukraine has to spend the way she will spend this one.
