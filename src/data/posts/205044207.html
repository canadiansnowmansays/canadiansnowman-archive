A dispatch from the eve of the two hundred and fiftieth birthday, at which the guest of honour did not speak and the weather did.

President Trump at Mount Rushmore in 2020.Credit...Anna Moneymaker for The New York Times

There is an old and disreputable literary device called the pathetic fallacy, in which the natural world is described as though it shares the emotions of the humans blundering about within it — the storm that rages as the king rages, the sun that breaks through as the lovers reunite. Serious writers are warned against it. It is considered cheap, sentimental, a crutch for those who cannot manage genuine feeling and so outsource it to the shrubbery.

I raise this only because on Friday evening, on the eve of the United States’ two hundred and fiftieth birthday, the President of the United States delivered a speech at Mount Rushmore, and the sky — I want to be precise and factual here, because the facts require no embellishment whatsoever — the sky opened up, hurled down hail the size of ping-pong balls, loosed lightning across the Black Hills, drove the assembled patriots twice into a gift shop and a cafeteria, and rained upon the four stone faces of the presidents until, as the New York Times correspondent on the scene observed, they appeared to be weeping.

I am not saying the mountain was weeping. I am a rational man, a retired businessman, a keeper of a sardonic disposition and a skeptical husky. Mountains do not weep. What I am saying is that if a novelist had submitted this scene — a man beneath four carved presidents, calling half his countrymen “godless” and “evil” on the eve of the nation’s founding-day, while the heavens physically interrupted him not once but twice and the granite faces streamed with rainwater — any competent editor would have sent it back with a note reading too heavy-handed; nobody will believe the weather would be this on-the-nose. Reality, as ever, has no editor.

Let us walk through the evening, because it deserves to be preserved.

The Production. One does not want to undersell the staging, which achieved a kind of accidental perfection. As the light failed over South Dakota, military helicopters crossed and re-crossed the face of the mountain. A B-52 bomber — the aircraft designed to deliver nuclear weapons, here repurposed as a party favour — thundered overhead. The sound system offered, in sequence, AC/DC and Lynyrd Skynyrd’s “Free Bird,” a song about a man explaining to a woman why he cannot possibly commit to her, which is either a bold thematic choice or no thematic choice at all. Great spotlights raked across what the Times, with a straight face I can only envy, called “the presidential noggins.” The evening closed, as all solemn commemorations of national founding must, with the Village People. Before the President arrived, a Fox News anchor warmed up the crowd by interviewing a man dressed as Abraham Lincoln. A second man, dressed as George Washington, descended the amphitheatre stairs. One begins to suspect that if the actual founders had wished to attend, they would have been turned away at the gate for insufficiently resembling their impersonators.

The History. Into this setting the President introduced his theme, which was communism. He declared it “the enemy of July 4, 1776.” Now, I am the last man to interrupt a good rally with a fact, but I feel a duty to the calendar. Karl Marx published The Communist Manifesto in 1848. This is seventy-two years after 1776. To call communism the enemy of the 1776 Declaration is to accuse a man of a crime committed three-quarters of a century after the victim signed his name — a feat of retroactive villainy that would impress even the most creative prosecutor. The President went on to rank communism as a greater threat to America than World War I, World War II, Pearl Harbor, and 9/11, which is a great deal of weight to place on an ideology that, at the birthday in question, had not yet been invented. He name-checked Karl Marx personally, at a birthday party, which is roughly the energy of arriving at a child’s christening to warn the assembled godparents about a man who will not be born for two more generations.

The Founders in Attendance. But here is the part that detained me longest, and it is the reason a climate writer finds himself, improbably, writing about a rally. The speech was delivered beneath the faces of four specific men, and one of them was Thomas Jefferson. I have spent a good part of this year, in another series, on Jefferson the scientist — the man who kept a weather diary for fifty years, some eighteen thousand observations of temperature and wind and rain, logged faithfully from July 1776 until the very week of his death, which fell, as it happens, on the Fourth of July. Jefferson believed that a self-governing people had a duty to observe the natural world and to believe what the observations told them. He would place a thermometer in every county if he could. He trusted data the way other men trust scripture.

HE KEPT THE WEATHER RECORDS FOR 50 YEARS

The least his heirs could do is read them.

And there he was on Friday night, carved into the granite, rained upon, while below him a speech unfolded whose relationship to observable reality was, let us say, aspirational. I find I cannot improve on the image, so I will simply leave it: the greatest weather-observer of the American founding, weeping stone tears in a hailstorm, above a podium from which the science of weather is routinely dismissed as a hoax. If Jefferson’s shade was present, it was taking notes on the storm. It was the only thing in the amphitheatre that would have interested him.

The Mountain Itself. There is one more thing that must be said, quietly and without the joke, because it is the true bottom of this. The Black Hills — the Paha Sapa — are sacred to the Lakota, taken from them in violation of a treaty the United States signed and then ignored when gold was found. The mountain onto which four presidents’ faces were dynamited is, to the people who were there first and are there still, a wound. Friday’s celebration, staged upon it, included among its decorations a Native man in a headdress, positioned near the pyrotechnics like bunting. I will let the reader sit with the geometry of that: a “Freedom” festival, on sacred land taken by broken treaty, at which the descendants of its rightful keepers appear as set-dressing. The hail fell on all of it equally. The sky, at least, does not distinguish between the invited and the dispossessed.

The President has, it is reported, mused about adding his own face to the mountain — a fifth head, beside the four. The effort has “stalled,” in the delicate word of the wire services. I would gently suggest that Friday night constituted the mountain’s formal reply. It summoned lightning. It threw down ice. It wept, or did the thing that a rational man is not permitted to call weeping, upon every face already present and, one suspects, upon the very idea of a sixth. Mother Earth, if I may briefly abandon my rationalism, is not accepting new applications at this time.

The fireworks went off eventually, over the streaming stone faces, to the sound of the Village People. The crowd emerged from the gift shop. The republic turned two hundred and fifty. And somewhere in the granite, if you believe the Times, or if you simply believe your own eyes, four old men who had actually built the thing being celebrated looked down through the rain and did not appear to be enjoying the party thrown in their name.

They kept the records, those men. Especially the one from Virginia. The least their heirs could do is read them — and, when the sky delivers a verdict in hail and lightning on the eve of the birthday, at least have the grace to come in out of the rain.

The Morning After

The Fourth dawned clear over the Black Hills, as mornings after storms often do. The hail had melted. The rubble — the metaphorical rubble; the mountain, I should reassure the literal-minded, remains structurally sound and un-augmented — had been swept from the amphitheatre. The gift shop reopened. The lemonade resumed at nine dollars a cup.

The speech, by mid-morning, had already been folded into the great composting heap of things that were said and then immediately succeeded by other things that were said. The word “communism,” deployed some prodigious number of times against a fourteen-hundred-mile-long enemy that did not exist in 1776 and does not meaningfully exist in the Democratic Party of 2026, had done its overnight work and could be retired until the next rally, when it would be summoned again like a loyal old dog. The B-52 had returned to its hangar. “Free Bird” had faded. The Village People, one trusts, went home.

And the four presidents remained where they have been since the dynamite finished in 1941 — looking out over a country turning two hundred and fifty, dried now by the July sun, saying nothing, as the carved are obliged to do. Whatever they witnessed Friday night they will witness again, because that is the fate of a face fixed in stone above a stage: you cannot look away, you cannot leave, and you cannot register a complaint except through the weather, which is not, strictly speaking, yours to command.

Though on Friday, for about an hour, one could almost believe it was.

“Beautiful storm last night. Very powerful. People are saying the most powerful storm in the history of Mount Rushmore, maybe ever. The lightning came for ME — that's how you know I'm doing something right."

Happy birthday, America. Your founders are watching. Try to make it a morning they’d want to wake up to.
