Part XIX closed with Hawaii’s crown stolen by resident foreign businessmen and American Marines despite a monarch’s genuine attempts at reform. Tonga, the only Pacific island kingdom to retain its monarchy into the present day without interruption by foreign annexation, offers this series a genuinely instructive contrast, because its ruling dynasty appears to have drawn precisely the right lessons from watching neighbouring Pacific monarchies fall, one after another, to exactly the combination of foreign commercial pressure and colonial ambition that ended Hawaii’s independence.Tonga’s monarchy traces its documented lineage back over a thousand years to the Tuʻi Tonga dynasty, whose paramount chiefs held both political and sacred religious authority across a maritime empire that, at its height around the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, extended Tongan influence across a substantial portion of the western Pacific through a combination of naval power, tribute relationships, and strategic marriage alliance with neighbouring island chiefdoms, a genuinely sophisticated regional hegemony this series has not previously examined in quite this Pacific-specific form. The scale of this early maritime empire is worth dwelling on briefly, since it is easy to underestimate how sophisticated Pacific island political organization had already become centuries before any European contact. Archaeological and oral historical evidence indicates the Tuʻi Tonga’s authority and tribute networks extended not merely across Tonga’s own scattered islands but as far as Samoa, Fiji, and Niue, maintained through a combination of long-distance double-hulled voyaging canoes capable of substantial open-ocean travel, strategic marriages binding distant island chiefly families to the Tongan royal line, and a tribute system that brought goods, labour, and political deference back to the Tongan capital from a genuinely vast maritime hinterland by the standards of any pre-industrial state examined anywhere in this series. This thalassocracy, a state whose power rests on control of the sea rather than of contiguous land, gradually declined by the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries as distant tributary chiefdoms increasingly asserted their own independence, but the underlying cultural memory of Tongan regional preeminence remained a durable element of the dynasty’s own self-conception well into the era of European contact centuries later.

Over subsequent centuries, the Tuʻi Tonga’s accumulated spiritual authority gradually separated from practical political and military power in a division this series recognizes immediately from Japan’s own shogun-emperor arrangement examined in Part XVI, with a secondary line, the Tuʻi Kanokupolu, eventually assuming most substantive governing authority while the original Tuʻi Tonga line retained primarily ceremonial and religious prominence.

This fragmented arrangement was decisively reunified in the nineteenth century by Tāufaʻāhau, a chief from the Haʻapai island group who, after a prolonged campaign of military consolidation and, crucially, conversion to Wesleyan Christianity under the influence of European missionaries, was crowned in 1845 as King George Tupou I, the first monarch to unite all of Tonga’s traditional chiefly lines under a single formally recognized crown modelled deliberately, and rather self-consciously, on the constitutional form of contemporary European monarchies. This deliberate modeling was not merely cosmetic. Tupou I, watching the escalating pattern of European and American commercial and colonial encroachment across the wider Pacific with evident and well-founded alarm, promulgated Tonga’s first written constitution in 1875, formally abolishing the traditional system of hereditary chiefly land control and redistributing land rights to ordinary Tongan subjects, establishing a Legislative Assembly with genuine, if limited, popular representation, and explicitly asserting Tonga’s status as a sovereign, internationally recognized kingdom rather than a mere chiefdom or tribal confederation vulnerable to being dismissed by European powers as lacking the formal trappings of a real state.

Tāufaʻāhau’s own conversion to Christianity deserves a moment’s attention, because it illustrates a pattern this series has already documented in Akbar’s Mughal court and will note again elsewhere: a ruler adopting a foreign religious framework not out of simple personal conviction alone, but as a genuinely useful tool for consolidating authority. Wesleyan missionaries, active in Tonga from the 1820s onward, offered Tāufaʻāhau not merely spiritual instruction but literacy, printed texts, and a moral framework that helped him justify overriding the claims of rival chiefly lines whose own authority rested on the older, more fragmented traditional religious system centred on the Tuʻi Tonga’s sacred status. His conversion and subsequent promotion of Christianity across Tonga, formalized well before his own 1845 coronation, gave him a unifying ideological banner distinct from and in some respects superior to the older chiefly hierarchy he was in the process of subordinating to his own expanding authority, a genuinely effective legitimating strategy that this series has now seen deployed, in different form, by rulers as far apart in time and geography as Constantine adopting Christianity for the Roman Empire and Wu Zetian reaching for Buddhist scripture in Tang China, examined back in Part III.

This proactive constitutional strategy paid off directly in Tonga’s diplomatic relationship with the era’s dominant regional and global powers. Britain formally recognized Tongan independence and sovereignty through a Treaty of Friendship signed in 1900, negotiated directly by Tupou I’s own successor at a moment when Germany, already an active colonial power elsewhere in the Pacific through its territories in Samoa and New Guinea, had begun showing its own interest in extending influence over Tonga specifically, giving Tongan negotiators real diplomatic leverage to secure British protection on considerably more favourable terms than a weaker or less internationally legible kingdom might have achieved. While this treaty did establish a British protectorate arrangement granting Britain control over Tonga’s external affairs and defence, considerably more limited than the outright annexation Hawaii, this series’ subject in Part XIX, ultimately suffered, Tonga’s own internal governance, land ownership system, and ruling dynasty remained entirely intact and undisturbed throughout the protectorate period, a genuinely different outcome from the fate this series has now documented befalling nearly every other monarchy that fell under any form of nineteenth or twentieth century Western colonial administration. Tonga’s protectorate status was formally and peacefully dissolved in 1970, restoring full sovereign independence to a kingdom that, unlike Hawaii, had never actually lost its own monarchy or ruling institutions at any point in the intervening century, an outcome this series regards as directly traceable to Tupou I’s own deliberate, preemptive constitutional strategy rather than any accident of geography or foreign disinterest, since Tonga’s own agricultural and strategic resources, while more modest than Hawaii’s, were by no means so unattractive to nineteenth-century commercial interests as to guarantee it would simply be left alone.

Tonga’s more recent political history shows this same underlying pattern of proactive, negotiated constitutional adaptation continuing directly into the twenty-first century, in a manner this series recognizes immediately as directly comparable to the strategy Britain itself pursued across Part XVIII. King George Tupou V, who reigned from 2006 until his death in 2012, presided over a period of genuine and sometimes violent domestic pressure for expanded democratic representation, a movement led substantially by a pro-democracy campaigner named ʻAkilisi Pōhiva, who had spent decades in Tongan politics arguing that a Legislative Assembly in which the great majority of seats were reserved by law for the king’s own appointees and Tonga’s hereditary noble class, with only a small handful of seats open to election by ordinary commoners, no longer reflected the aspirations of an increasingly educated and internationally connected Tongan population. This pressure reached a genuine crisis point in November of 2006, when serious rioting broke out in the capital, Nuku’alofa, following an announcement by the government that fell well short of the fuller democratic reform many Tongans had been promised, destroying a substantial portion of the city’s central business district and resulting in several deaths, an event that shocked a kingdom with no comparable history of serious civil unrest and forced the monarchy to recognize, in the most direct possible terms, that the slow pace of promised political reform had genuinely exhausted public patience. Rather than resist this pressure through force, as this series documented Louis XVI and Nicholas II ultimately choosing to their own destruction in Parts XIV and XV, Tupou V responded by voluntarily and substantially surrendering his own remaining executive authority, announcing in 2008 that he would cede most of his personal governing powers to a Prime Minister chosen by and answerable to a reformed, more genuinely representative Legislative Assembly, and formally implementing these changes through a new political settlement that took effect with Tonga’s first fully contested parliamentary elections in 2010, transforming Tonga’s monarchy in a single deliberate decade from a genuinely powerful executive throne into something considerably closer to Britain’s own largely ceremonial constitutional arrangement.

It is worth adding one further comparison here, because it sharpens exactly how unusual Tonga’s outcome actually was among its immediate Pacific neighbors. Samoa’s own traditional chiefly system, the fa’amatai, never achieved comparable centralization into a single recognized crown before German and later New Zealand colonial administration effectively absorbed its governance structures into a colonial framework; Fiji’s own indigenous chiefly confederation was ceded to Britain outright in 1874 by its paramount chief, Cakobau, specifically to escape mounting debt and internal instability, becoming a full British colony rather than a protected but internally sovereign kingdom; and the Kingdom of Rapa Nui, Easter Island, saw its own traditional ariki mau kingship effectively dissolved entirely following Chilean annexation in 1888. Measured against this broader regional pattern, Tonga’s preservation of full internal sovereignty under only a limited external protectorate stands out as a genuinely exceptional outcome, one this series attributes directly to the specific timing and shrewdness of Tupou I’s own constitutional strategy rather than to any accident of more favourable geography, since Tonga’s neighbors faced broadly comparable colonial pressures and, in most cases, considerably less deliberate and less proactive royal leadership in response to them.

What makes Tupou V’s decision particularly instructive for this series’ broader argument is the evident deliberateness and self-awareness behind it. Tongan officials and later historians examining the reform have generally described it as a conscious strategy to preserve the monarchy’s long-term survival by voluntarily shedding exactly the kind of concentrated, unaccountable executive power that this series has now repeatedly documented producing violent confrontation once a population’s patience for it finally runs out, whether in Charles I’s England, Louis XVI’s France, or Nicholas II’s Russia. Tupou V reportedly studied Britain’s own constitutional evolution directly as a model for Tonga’s reform, having been educated in part in the United Kingdom, and his decision reads, in this series’ broader comparative context, as a rare case of a reigning monarch explicitly and self-consciously choosing to compress into a single decade the kind of gradual, multi-century accommodation this series traced across Britain’s own much longer constitutional evolution in Part XVIII, rather than waiting for that same pressure to build toward the kind of crisis point that has, in nearly every other case this series has examined, forced monarchs into far more sudden and far less controlled concessions, or into no concessions at all followed by outright collapse.

Tonga today retains a reigning monarch, currently King Tupou VI, who succeeded his brother in 2012, presiding over a small but genuinely functioning constitutional monarchy with an elected Prime Minister and Legislative Assembly holding the great majority of substantive governing authority, while the crown retains meaningful ceremonial, cultural, and residual reserve powers, along with continued and evidently sincere public affection and respect across Tongan society. The reformed system’s own first real test of durability came remarkably quickly and remarkably peacefully: ʻAkilisi Pōhiva, the same pro-democracy campaigner whose decades of advocacy had helped drive the 2010 reforms, was himself elected Prime Minister in 2014, becoming the first commoner rather than a hereditary noble to hold the office under the new constitutional arrangement, a transition of power from the very movement that had once rioted against the crown’s own slow pace of reform to the office now created specifically to answer that movement’s demands, achieved entirely through the ballot box rather than through any further unrest, which stands as about as clean a demonstration as this series has anywhere encountered of a monarchy’s voluntary self-limitation actually working exactly as its architects intended it to.

This durable outcome is a combination this series has now seen prove reliable, in one form or another, across every monarchy examined in this part of the series that chose accommodation over confrontation.

What Tonga’s case adds to this series’ broader argument, set alongside Britain in Part XVIII and against Hawaii’s stolen crown in Part XIX, is a genuinely important refinement: proactive constitutional accommodation appears to buy a monarchy durable survival not only against internal domestic pressure for reform, which is the lesson Britain’s own centuries-long evolution most directly illustrates, but potentially against external colonial and commercial pressure as well, since Tonga’s own deliberate, self-conscious modernization under Tupou I gave nineteenth-century Western powers considerably less pretext to dismiss Tongan sovereignty as illegitimate or premodern than they found available, whether accurately or not, in Hawaii’s own case just one generation later.

It is worth adding one final layer to this comparison before leaving the Pacific behind, because Tonga’s own royal family has faced its own version of the ceremonial-versus-substantive tension this series traced through Japan’s imperial line in Part XVI and Britain’s own crown in Part XVIII. Tupou VI, since assuming both the throne and, briefly and controversially, the office of chair of the country’s Privy Council and certain reserved constitutional powers in the years immediately following his brother’s reforms, has periodically drawn domestic criticism from Tongan pro-democracy advocates who argue that the 2010 constitutional settlement left the crown with more residual reserve authority, particularly over judicial appointments and certain cabinet matters, than a fully consistent constitutional monarchy on the British model would retain. This ongoing, generally peaceful domestic debate over exactly how much reserve power a reformed Tongan crown should retain is itself, this series would note, a healthy sign rather than a warning one: it demonstrates that Tonga’s 2010 settlement created genuine, contestable political terrain, precisely the kind of ordinary constitutional argument a mature democracy is supposed to be capable of holding without the crisis or violence this series has documented accompanying nearly every other monarchy’s own transition toward reduced authority.

Part XXI crosses from the Pacific to the Himalayas, for the single most extraordinary act of voluntary self-limitation examined anywhere in this entire series: a king who held genuinely absolute power, entirely secure and unthreatened by any of the domestic or foreign pressures this series has traced destroying or reforming every other monarchy so far, and who nonetheless decided, entirely on his own initiative, to give almost all of it away.
