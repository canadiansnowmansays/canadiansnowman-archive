In 1842 Edgar Allan Poe published a short story about a man condemned by the Spanish Inquisition in Toledo. He wakes strapped to a wooden frame in a stone cell. High above him a pendulum swings, its lower edge a crescent of polished steel. Each swing is like the last, and each one is a fraction lower. “Down—steadily down it crept,” Poe wrote, and the horror of the story is not the blade itself but the arithmetic. No single swing kills. The prisoner has time to notice, to reason, even to hope. He simply cannot make the blade go back up.

Americans like to describe their politics as a pendulum. The metaphor is meant to be reassuring. What swings one way will swing the other; the voters correct excess; the country always finds its centre. Every liberal who has watched a hard-won policy torn up since January 2017 has been told some version of this, and many have told it to themselves. It can all be put back. Progress resumes.

Poe understood something the comforting version leaves out. A pendulum can swing back and forth and still come down. What matters is whether the pivot stays where it is, or whether something beneath the motion keeps ratcheting it lower.

This series is about the ratchet.

One Monday afternoon

On Monday, 28 September 2026, The New York Times published four stories within a few hours of each other. None led the front page for long. Read together, they are a fair sample of the whole.

The first, by Michael C. Bender, reported that the Education Department had formally rescinded the Biden administration’s Title IX rule. That rule had extended sex-discrimination protection in schools to gay and transgender students. It had also widened the harassment complaints schools must investigate, reaching off-campus school events, and let schools decide cases on the ordinary civil standard of whether misconduct was more likely than not. Its replacement restores the narrower 2020 rule written under Betsy DeVos. Education Secretary Linda McMahon’s department noted that nothing of substance had changed. That was accurate. A federal judge in Kentucky had vacated the Biden rule in January 2025, weeks before the inauguration, and the incoming administration simply chose not to appeal.

The second, by Brad Plumer and Maxine Joselow, reported that the Transportation Department had finalised new fuel economy standards: an average of 34.9 miles per gallon for new cars and light trucks in model year 2031, against the 50.4 the Biden administration had set. Congress had already removed the fines for automakers who miss the target, which leaves the standard largely decorative.

The third, by Plumer and Claire Brown, published a letter obtained through a freedom-of-information request. On 11 March 2025, the chief executive of a coal trade group called America’s Power wrote to Energy Secretary Chris Wright. She proposed that the department use a rarely invoked emergency power in the Federal Power Act to stop aging coal plants from retiring. Two months later the department began doing exactly that. It has since kept nine units at six plants running past their retirement dates, sometimes over the objections of the utilities that own them, at a cost to ratepayers running into the hundreds of millions. At a meeting with coal executives in July, the head of the Environmental Protection Agency, Lee Zeldin, put the relationship plainly: “Many of the items that were on your wish list are now done.”

The fourth, by Ann E. Marimow, reported that the Solicitor General had asked the Supreme Court for emergency permission to stop providing hormone therapy to roughly 2,000 transgender federal prisoners. Two lower courts had blocked the policy.

Four stories, four different mechanisms. One regulation erased by a court and then formally buried. One lowered by rulemaking after Congress had already pulled its teeth. One industry request granted almost verbatim. One order that the courts have so far refused to let stand. Those four mechanisms are the spine of this series.

The pendulum is real

Start with the reassuring part, because some of it is true.

Title IX has now been rewritten five times in fifteen years. The Obama administration issued guidance on sexual harassment in 2011. The DeVos Education Department withdrew it in 2017 and replaced it with a formal rule in 2020. The Biden administration replaced that rule in April 2024. A court vacated the Biden rule in January 2025, and on 28 September 2026 the vacated rule was taken off the books. Each administration arrives, and the rule changes position.

Fuel economy has the same rhythm. Congress created the standards in 1975, under Gerald Ford, in response to the Arab oil embargo. Obama raised them sharply in 2012. Trump’s first administration cut them in 2020. Biden raised them again in 2024. On Monday they were cut again.

The purest pendulum of all is the Mexico City Policy, which bars foreign aid to organisations that provide or promote abortion. Ronald Reagan created it in 1984. It has been rescinded by every Democratic president since and restored by every Republican one. It was restored again in January 2025, for the seventh swing in forty-one years.

If the whole story looked like this, the comforting version would hold. A future administration could reverse these on its first afternoon, or at least begin to. Of the 88 reversals catalogued for this series, 31 fall into that category: executive orders and memos that a new president could undo with a pen.

That leaves 57 that cannot be undone that way. The ten in the ledger’s “Stopped” column were blocked outright and never took effect. The other 47 are where the ratchet engages.

The ratchet

A ratchet lets a wheel turn one way and stops it turning back. American government has several, and in the last twenty months they have been engaged one after another.

The first is time. The Biden Title IX rule took three years to write and drew about 240,000 public comments. Undoing it took one district judge in Kentucky and an administration’s decision not to appeal. Rules are expensive to build and cheap to destroy, so each swing of the pendulum costs the party of construction most of a presidential term.

The second is the Congressional Review Act. When Congress uses it to strike down a regulation, the agency may not issue another rule “in substantially the same form” unless Congress passes a new law authorising it. In 2025 and 2026 the Act was used against the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s overdraft-fee rule, California’s authority to set its own vehicle emission standards, and the twenty-year ban on copper and nickel mining near Minnesota’s Boundary Waters. None of those comes back on the next swing. Each would need a new act of Congress.

The third is the Supreme Court. In June 2024, in Loper Bright, the Court ended the long-standing practice of deferring to agencies’ reasonable readings of the statutes they administer. A future Democratic administration trying to rebuild an ambitious environmental or labour rule will face judges with far more room to strike it down than Biden’s lawyers did. On 29 June 2026, in Trump v. Slaughter, the Court overruled Humphrey’s Executor, the 1935 decision that had let Congress protect the members of independent regulatory commissions from dismissal at the president’s pleasure. Ninety years of independent regulators ended in a single opinion. No future president can restore that protection. Only a future Court can.

The fourth is plain physics and contract. Oil leases issued in the Arctic are binding agreements, and cancelling them invites damages claims. Mining claims staked while a national monument is shrunk can survive its later restoration. The carbon emitted by a coal plant kept open by emergency order stays in the atmosphere. Automakers plan their fleets five to seven years ahead, so a rule finalised in September 2026 is shaping cars that will be on the road into the 2040s, whoever wins in 2028.

The last ratchet is the one no rule touches. A pardon cannot be revoked. A refugee resettlement office that has closed does not reopen by proclamation. A research cohort abandoned when its grant was cancelled cannot be recruited again, years older. A harassment complaint that went uninvestigated in 2025 does not get investigated in 2029.

This is why the question at the heart of this series matters. Can what has been undone be put back? Much of it can, in the sense that a future government could write something similar. Very little of it can in the sense that matters: as though it had never been undone.

Whose progress?

When this series began, my working assumption was that it would catalogue the dismantling of Democratic initiatives. The ledger did not cooperate.

Of the 88 items, 43 were Democratic creations. But 30 were bipartisan: laws passed with support from both parties, or policies that presidents of both parties kept. Ten more are older than the modern party lines altogether, such as the independence of federal statistics or the citizenship clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. Five were Republican.

Consider what sits in those last two groups. The National Environmental Policy Act, whose implementing regulations were removed in January 2026, was signed by Richard Nixon. The fuel economy standards cut on Monday were signed by Gerald Ford. Executive Order 11246, which barred discrimination by federal contractors, was signed by Lyndon Johnson in 1965 and kept by every president of either party for sixty years until it was revoked on 21 January 2025. The Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty was Ronald Reagan’s. The National Climate Assessment, now abandoned, was mandated by a law George H.W. Bush signed. The inspectors general, seventeen of whom were fired in one night in January 2025, were created by a 1978 law that passed with broad support from both parties.

This is not the reversal of a Democratic programme. It is the dismantling of a postwar bipartisan settlement about what the federal government is for, how it is staffed, and what it owes to people who are not its friends.

Honesty requires one caveat, and this series will keep to it. Not every reversal in the ledger ran against majority opinion. Polling has consistently shown majority public support for restricting transgender athletes in girls’ and women’s sports, and the Supreme Court upheld two such state laws in June 2026. An argument that everything undone was popular and everything imposed was the work of a sinister few would be easy to knock down, and it would not be true. The stronger case, and the one the evidence supports, is narrower. A great deal of what was undone was popular, or bipartisan, or both. A great deal of what replaced it was written by people with a direct interest in the outcome.

Who wrote it

Which brings us back to Monday’s coal letter, and the question of who whispered in whose ear.

The ledger records, for each reversal, where the idea came from. Twenty items trace directly to Project 2025, the Heritage Foundation’s 900-page blueprint for a second Trump term. The candidate disowned it during the 2024 campaign, and then his administration hired its authors. A tracker kept by the Center for Progressive Reform found that 53 per cent of its domestic recommendations had been started or completed within a year of the inauguration. The Heritage Foundation, reported by Bloomberg Law, told its donors the same figure in June 2026 and called it a big deal.

Eighteen items trace to industry requests: the coal plants, the auto rules, the mining leases, the consumer-finance rules that banks and credit card companies had sued to stop. Nine came from a conservative legal movement that spent forty years building the bench that now decides these cases. Six were driven by Republicans in Congress through the budget and rescission bills.

Thirty-five were the president’s own, or his appointees’: the tariffs, the renamings, the firings, the treaties abandoned. A pattern is worth watching here. Half of the ten items the courts stopped outright fall in that group, among them the emergency tariffs, the Kennedy Center renaming and the attempt to fire a Federal Reserve governor. The reversals that have locked in hardest, such as Slaughter, the civil service rule and the removal of the environmental review regulations, were drafted, litigated and staffed years in advance by people who knew exactly which ratchet they were engaging.

Where the walls stopped

It would be dishonest to leave the prisoner without mentioning that he survives.

Ten items in the ledger were stopped outright. On 20 February 2026 the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorise tariffs. On 29 June 2026 it refused to let the president fire Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook. The next day, in Trump v. Barbara, a 5–4 majority struck down the executive order ending birthright citizenship. District courts have blocked the Kennedy Center renaming, the prison hormone policy, the vaccine schedule changes and the halt to offshore wind construction. Revolution Wind installed its last turbine on 18 September 2026. The guardrails have not all failed. A later essay in this series will ask why some held and others did not.

But look closely at how Poe’s story ends. The prisoner does not save himself. He cleverly smears his straps with food so the rats gnaw him loose, and escapes the blade. Then the walls of the cell grow hot and begin to close, pressing him towards the pit. He is pulled back at the very edge by General Lasalle, whose French army has taken Toledo. The rescue comes from outside the cell.

In American terms, that outside force has a date. It is 3 November 2026, the midterm elections, and after that 7 November 2028. Voters can stop the blade. What they cannot do, and what no election can do, is return the country to the moment before the first swing.

What this series will do

Over the coming weeks, The Pendulum and the Ratchet will work through the ledger one tier at a time. It will cover what a new president could reverse on day one and what it would cost to try; the reversals that would take years of rulemaking, and those that only Congress can repair; the locks that nothing short of a new Supreme Court can open; the places where the courts held; the people and institutions who wrote the blueprint; the bipartisan settlement being taken apart; the damage that cannot be put back; and finally, what a serious restoration would require, in what order, and how long it would take.

A Canadian writing about this owes his readers one admission. Canadians have watched the pendulum from the shore for most of our history, occasionally amused and usually reassured. We had come to believe, as Americans did, that whatever swung out would swing back. The events of the last twenty months suggest that belief was never a law of nature. It was a habit, kept up by people of both parties who agreed not to cut the rope. The blade is lower now than it was. The question for the rest of this series is how much lower, and whether anyone is coming through the gates of Toledo in time.
