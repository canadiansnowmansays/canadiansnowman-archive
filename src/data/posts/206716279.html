Every monarchy this series will visit eventually has to answer for what it costs the people living under it. Almost none of them answer for it in advance. The kingdom of Israel is the exception, and it’s worth spending real time with, because somewhere around the eleventh century BCE, a prophet stood in front of a nation demanding a king and told them, in specific and itemized detail, exactly what a king would take from them — and they asked for one anyway. That single scene, preserved in the biblical book of 1 Samuel, is the oldest piece of political ambivalence about monarchy in the Western canon, and it never got resolved. It just got inherited, generation after generation, by every people who ever looked at a strong central ruler and felt simultaneously safer and smaller.

The context matters before the warning does. By the time the Israelites came to the prophet and judge Samuel asking for a king, they had spent generations governed by a loose confederation of tribes, held together by shared religious identity and led, in moments of crisis, by charismatic judges — Deborah, Gideon, Samson — who rose to meet a specific threat and then, notably, did not pass the office to their children. It was closer to the Sumerian lugal pattern than to anything Egypt or Zhou China would recognize: temporary authority, earned through crisis leadership, expected to recede once the crisis passed. It worked, unevenly, for roughly two centuries. And then the Philistines got better organized, Samuel’s own sons proved corrupt and unfit to inherit his judgeship, and the elders of Israel came to him with a request that the text presents as both entirely reasonable and, in the same breath, a kind of quiet betrayal: “appoint a king to lead us, such as all the other nations have.”

That phrase — such as all the other nations have — is doing enormous work, and it’s worth sitting with. The Israelites were not asking for kingship in the abstract. They were asking to stop being unusual. Every neighboring power they dealt with, from Egypt to the Canaanite city-states to the rising Philistine confederation, had a king, and a nation without one felt, to its own elders, structurally disadvantaged — under-defended in war, unpredictable in succession, embarrassing in diplomacy. This is a pattern this series will see again and again in smaller kingdoms and chiefdoms throughout history: monarchy spreading not purely because it worked better, but because operating without it started to look like a liability in a world where the neighbors all had one. Kingship, in other words, was already becoming the default international standard for statehood, and refusing to adopt it carried its own cost.

Samuel’s response to the request is the part of this story that deserves to be read in full by anyone thinking seriously about political power, because it is not vague. He does not warn the Israelites that a king might be bad. He tells them precisely what a king will do, itemized, as though reading from an administrative memo rather than delivering a prophecy: the king will take their sons and make them run before his chariots; he will appoint some as commanders and others as plowmen and reapers and makers of weapons; he will take their daughters as perfumers and cooks and bakers; he will take the best of their fields, vineyards, and olive groves and give them to his officials; he will take a tenth of their grain and their flocks; and on the day this all becomes unbearable, Samuel warns, “you yourselves will become his slaves,” and God will not answer them when they cry out about the king they themselves demanded. It is, essentially, a tax and conscription schedule delivered as prophecy, and the extraordinary thing is not that Samuel predicted it — any observer of Egyptian or Mesopotamian kingship could have predicted it — but that the Israelites, having heard the itemized bill in advance, said give us a king anyway.

That refusal to be talked out of it tells you something true about why monarchy spread as fast and as durably as it did across the ancient world: security and international standing were worth more to the people demanding a king than the freedom being traded away for it. This is the same trade every population in this series will make, in different costume, right up through Bhutan’s people in 2008 telling their own king, in effect, the opposite — that they’d rather keep some of the security of monarchy without surrendering their freedom for it. Israel’s founding kingship story is the mirror image of Bhutan’s founding democracy story, separated by three thousand years, and this series will return to that mirror when we get there.

Saul, anointed as Israel’s first king shortly after this exchange, is instructive precisely because he fails, and fails in a way that establishes a pattern this series will trace across nearly every monarchy we examine. The account of his anointing is itself telling: Samuel finds him searching for his father’s lost donkeys, an ordinary young man from the smallest clan of the smallest tribe, and when the moment comes for Israel’s elders to present their new king publicly, Saul is found hiding among the baggage, apparently reluctant to step into the role at all. That reluctance did not last. Saul’s early reign showed real capability — a swift and decisive relief of the besieged city of Jabesh-Gilead against the Ammonites gave him the kind of battlefield legitimacy that, as this series has already seen in Mesopotamia, was often the actual foundation beneath any king’s more elaborate theological claims. But Saul’s authority ran through Samuel, not around him, and

Saul’s undoing came in two specific breaches of that arrangement. First, waiting for Samuel to arrive and perform the customary sacrifice before a battle with the Philistines, and watching his army begin to scatter as the delay stretched on, Saul performed the sacrifice himself — a priestly function that was not his to claim, king or not. Second, commanded through Samuel to destroy the Amalekites and their livestock entirely as an act of judgment, Saul instead spared the Amalekite king and kept the best of the flocks, offering the pragmatic justification that the animals were being saved for sacrifice. Samuel’s reply is the line that defines the entire relationship between prophetic authority and royal power in this system: “To obey is better than sacrifice.” The kingship, Samuel tells him, has already been torn from him and given to another — a shepherd boy named David, anointed in secret while Saul still occupies the throne. What follows is one of the more genuinely tragic stretches of the Hebrew Bible: Saul’s descent into paranoid pursuit of David across the wilderness, his consultation of the medium at Endor in violation of his own laws against such practices on the eve of his final battle, and his death by his own sword at Mount Gilboa rather than capture by the Philistines. Israel’s first king, in other words, is deposed not by rebellion or foreign conquest but by the withdrawal of the same prophetic sanction that had installed him — legitimacy running through a channel the king himself never controlled.

This is Zhou China’s Mandate of Heaven in miniature, arrived at independently: legitimacy that flows through the king but is never fully possessed by him, subject to a standard set by something — someone — outside his own authority, and revocable when he fails that standard badly enough.

David’s reign, when it comes, is the moment Israelite kingship consolidates into something recognizable as a state rather than a tribal confederation with a king attached. He takes Jerusalem, a Jebusite stronghold with no prior tribal claim attached to it, specifically so that the capital belongs to no single tribe and every tribe can accept it equally — a genuinely shrewd piece of statecraft, choosing neutral ground as the seat of power precisely to defuse the tribal rivalries a homegrown capital would have inflamed. He centralizes worship around the Ark of the Covenant’s move to Jerusalem, binding religious and political authority to the same city for the first time. And he receives, through the prophet Nathan, a promise that becomes the theological bedrock for everything that follows in Jewish and later Christian political theology: that his dynasty, unlike Saul’s, will endure — “your throne shall be established forever.” This is the moment Israelite kingship shifts from something closer to the Sumerian pattern, in which any capable man might be raised up and any failing one deposed, toward something closer to Egypt’s dynastic permanence, a promise of hereditary continuity underwritten by divine covenant rather than divine embodiment.

And yet even David, promised an eternal dynasty, is not exempt from the same prophetic check that felled Saul. When David arranges the death of Uriah the Hittite in order to take Uriah’s wife Bathsheba, it is Nathan who confronts him — not with vague disapproval, but with a parable specifically constructed to trap David into condemning his own conduct before he realizes it describes him. “You are the man,” Nathan tells him, and David, king of a unified and expanding state, submits to the rebuke rather than silencing the prophet who delivered it. That submission is not a small detail. It is the entire mechanism that keeps this monarchy from sliding into Egypt’s unconditional model: even the king who receives an eternal covenant remains, in this system, answerable to a standard he does not control and cannot simply legislate away.

Solomon inherits all of this — the covenant, the capital, the consolidated state — and proceeds to demonstrate exactly what Samuel warned about, at a scale Samuel likely could not have imagined. His building program, most famously the Temple in Jerusalem, is genuinely extraordinary: cedar imported from Tyre, a workforce organized into rotating labor levies drawn from across the tribes, a construction project that took seven years for the Temple alone and thirteen more for his own palace complex. His court maintained, according to the text, a wildly extravagant provisioning schedule and a harem numbering in the hundreds, largely built through diplomatic marriages meant to secure alliances with neighboring powers — the same marriage-as-statecraft logic this series will see taken to its most extreme form centuries later in the Habsburgs. And his administration divided the kingdom into twelve districts, each responsible for provisioning the royal court for one month of the year, an arrangement that, tellingly, did not map neatly onto the traditional tribal boundaries — a deliberate administrative overlay designed to weaken tribal loyalty in favor of loyalty to the crown, the exact kind of bureaucratic centralization Uruk’s temple economy pioneered two thousand years earlier for entirely practical rather than political reasons.

The cost of all this was, precisely as Samuel had predicted, forced labor and heavy taxation, and the international side of Solomon’s reign shows the other half of what that wealth bought him. His trading fleet, launched from Ezion-Geber on the Red Sea in partnership with the Phoenician king Hiram of Tyre, sailed to a distant land called Ophir and returned laden with gold; his court is described receiving envoys and tribute from across the region, and the visit of the Queen of Sheba — arriving with camels bearing spices, an extraordinary quantity of gold, and precious stones, reportedly to test Solomon’s famed wisdom with hard questions — became, for later Ethiopian tradition especially, the seed of a genealogical claim this series will return to directly when we reach the Solomonic dynasty of Ethiopia, whose rulers traced their line to a son said to have been born of that very meeting. Solomon’s reputation for wisdom and wealth, in other words, did not stay confined to Israel’s borders; it became source material for legitimacy claims made by monarchies thousands of miles away and thousands of years later, which is as clean an example as this series will find of how a founding king’s story keeps generating new political capital for descendants and imitators long after the original reign has ended.

Solomon’s death exposed how thin the consent underneath it had become. His son Rehoboam, meeting with representatives of the northern tribes at Shechem to be confirmed as king, was asked directly to lighten the labor and tax burden his father had imposed. Rehoboam, rejecting the counsel of his father’s older advisors in favor of younger courtiers who urged him to rule even more harshly than Solomon had, delivered an answer that reads, three thousand years later, like the single worst piece of political communication in the entire Hebrew Bible: “My father made your yoke heavy; I will add to your yoke. My father scourged you with whips; I will scourge you with scorpions.” The ten northern tribes, hearing this, simply left — the kingdom split, permanently, into a northern Israel and a southern Judah, within a single generation of Solomon’s death, and the united monarchy Samuel had warned against and David had built never came back together.

What Israel’s founding kingship story leaves this series with, more than any other case examined so far, is the recognition that the itemized cost of monarchy has been known and speakable since at least the eleventh century BCE, and that populations have chosen it anyway, over and over, when the alternative felt more dangerous than the yoke.

It is also worth noting how long this particular story’s afterlife runs. The Davidic covenant — the promise of an eternal throne — outlived the united kingdom, outlived the Babylonian exile that ended the line’s actual rule, and became the theological seedbed for messianic expectation in Second Temple Judaism and, later, for Christian claims about Jesus’s genealogy running back through David. European monarchs centuries afterward, constructing their own divine-right theologies, borrowed heavily and explicitly from this precedent — a king anointed by God’s own designated authority, ruling through covenant rather than mere inheritance or conquest. Israel’s kingship story did not stay contained in Israel. It became one of the two or three most influential templates for how monarchy justified itself across the entire span of Western history, which is a striking legacy for an institution the nation’s own founding prophet had warned against, point by point, before a single Israelite king ever sat on a throne.

Samuel’s warning did not prevent monarchy. It simply became the first entry in a genre this series will keep encountering: the honest accounting of what a crown costs, delivered before the crown is worn, and disregarded because security, order, and the appearance of parity with more organized neighbors were worth more, in the moment of decision, than the freedom being traded for them. Rehoboam’s failure at Shechem is the other half of that lesson — that the same yoke, once accepted, does not stay fixed at the weight originally promised, and that kings who forget this tend to discover the limit of their subjects’ patience exactly once, and permanently.
