TO OUR PATRONS:

The John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts is closed, effective immediately, for Renovation and Reconstruction. We regret any inconvenience this may cause to ticket-holders, performers, school groups, and members of the general public who were under the impression, as recently as this morning, that the building was structurally capable of hosting the performing arts.

Please note that Renovation and Reconstruction cannot actually begin at this time. It is, in the words of the Board, a “very large and complex job,” and the Board would like it clearly understood that this job cannot commence until the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals rules on a separate and unrelated matter regarding what may be written on the front of the building. Should that ruling go the wrong way — a phrase the Board uses to mean “against the Board” — and should it not subsequently be overturned by the United States Supreme Court, the Renovation and Reconstruction will also not take place. The building will, in that case, remain closed for a second, distinct, and unrelated reason.

We appreciate your patience during what the Board has assured us is a temporary condition, in the sense that all conditions are eventually followed by other conditions.

WHY IS THE CENTER CLOSING?

Two things happened on Tuesday, in the following order:

  1. U.S. District Judge Christopher R. Cooper ruled that the President cannot place his name on the building, and cannot get around that ruling by naming the plaza in front of it instead — a decision that, per Judge Cooper, leaves the Board’s naming plans just as blocked as they were before it tried to rephrase them.

  2. The Board of Trustees, appointed by the President, voted almost unanimously to close the building.

We want to be clear that these two events are not related, in the sense that the Board has not said they are related, and unrelated, in the sense that the second one happened minutes after the first.

A NOTE ON STRUCTURAL SAFETY

The Board has cited a partial ceiling collapse in the Grand Foyer earlier this month, along with what it describes as a serious risk of bankruptcy, as grounds for closure. Patrons may wonder why a building requiring emergency structural attention was not simply repaired on an emergency basis, particularly since the Board’s own governing statute already permits emergency repairs without a building-wide closure. We wondered this too. The Board’s answer, which Judge Cooper also noted in his ruling, is that it did not attempt this, and instead closed the entire venue for the third time since February.

A NOTE ON FUNDRAISING

The Board has separately indicated that the Center’s finances are in serious trouble, and that the only viable path to solvency is a closer association with the President, including but not limited to the addition of his name to the facade. Patrons who followed Center coverage over the past year may recall that ticket sales fell sharply, several planned engagements were cancelled by performers, and fundraising slowed considerably in the period immediately following the President’s previous, court-ordered-partial, association with the Center. The Board has not addressed this sequence of events directly, other than to propose more of the same association as the cure for it.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

When will the Center reopen? This depends on the outcome of litigation currently before the D.C. Circuit, and, in the event of an unfavourable outcome for the Board, litigation currently not yet before the Supreme Court but which the Board has already discussed publicly as though it were. Patrons are advised not to purchase advance parking.

What happens to previously scheduled performances? Performers who had not already withdrawn will not be performing. Patrons holding tickets to these engagements are directed to the Center’s refund policy, which the Board has not yet closed for renovations, though this notice reserves the right to revisit that statement.

Is the ceiling actually a safety hazard? Yes. This notice does not dispute that a partial ceiling collapse occurred. It disputes only the proposition that the correct response to a partial ceiling collapse is an indefinite, building-wide closure contingent on the outcome of a naming rights dispute, rather than, for example, fixing the ceiling.

Has the President indicated what will happen if the ruling does not go his way? The Department of Justice told the court that without the naming and inscription arrangement, the Center would become “unsafe, decrepit,” and eventually “required to be taken down” — a filing that, once translated out of legal prose, functions less as a structural assessment than as a warning. This notice takes no position on whether that filing should be read as an engineering report, a negotiating position, or a mission statement, and directs patrons to form their own view.

Isn’t this the judge’s fault for blocking the renovation funding? No court has blocked a dollar of renovation funding. Judge Cooper’s rulings concern the naming and inscription questions only; the roughly $257 million Congress had already appropriated for repairs was never at issue, then or now. What did change, starting the month the President’s name went up on the facade, was the box office: performers withdrew, subscribers cancelled, and donors who had spent decades keeping a nonpartisan memorial solvent quietly stopped writing cheques to a partisan one. The building’s finances were not in crisis before he arrived. They have not recovered since he did. A patron asking which came first, the funding shortfall or the President, is not asking a hard question. The Board would simply prefer that patrons ask it about the judge instead.

Is the building actually closed right now? On paper, as of this notice, yes. In the sense that matters to anyone planning to walk up and try the doors, less clear: an earlier injunction against closing the building remains in effect, and that injunction has not yet been lifted. The Board considers the building closed. The courts, at time of posting, have not yet agreed. Patrons are advised to check both this notice and the docket before making travel arrangements.

Who currently sits on the Board of Trustees? Members appointed by the President following his removal, by executive action, of the previous Board earlier this year. The current Board’s near-unanimous vote to close the building is, per its own statement, evidence of strong institutional consensus. This notice does not have an independent basis on which to dispute that characterisation, the previous Board having been unavailable for comment since approximately February.

A WORD FROM THE PRESIDENT

Following the Board’s vote, the President posted, in relevant part, that the closure was for “Safety reasons,” that the Reconstruction “cannot begin” pending the D.C. Circuit’s ruling, and that a ruling against the Board — which he does not expect — would mean the Renovation “will not take place” at all. Patrons are encouraged to read this statement as many times as necessary to determine whether the building is closed because it is unsafe, or unsafe because it is closed, or neither, or both, on a rotating basis depending on which explanation is required that week.

IN CLOSING

The Kennedy Center opened in 1971 as a living memorial to a president who did not ask that his name be added to it twice. It remains, as of this notice, a building. Management thanks you for your understanding, and reminds you that the gift shop, coat check, and parking garage are also closed, effective immediately, for reasons that will be litigated separately.

Companion notice to The Name Game Parts IX (”Only Congress Can Change It”) and Postscript (”Not a Plaque, a Sentence”) — the same building, the same name, now closed rather than merely renamed.
