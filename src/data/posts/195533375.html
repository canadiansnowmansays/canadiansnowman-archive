Every act of political violence in America produces two things: a victim and a commodity.

The violence itself is chaotic, terrifying, and real. People are shot. People are beaten. People shelter under tables in evening gowns, or crawl across baseball diamonds, or call their families from locked offices in the Capitol. In the moment, there is nothing performative about any of it. The fear is genuine. The danger is genuine. The Secret Service agents who sprinted through the Washington Hilton ballroom with weapons drawn were not performing. They were doing their jobs in conditions that required them to assume the worst.

But the moment the immediate danger passes — the moment the gunman is dead or in custody, the moment the all-clear sounds, the moment the president is confirmed safe — the second process begins. The event becomes raw material. And the speed with which that raw material is converted into political product has become one of the defining features of American public life.

Let us trace the instrumentalization of the Washington Hilton.

The shooting occurred at approximately 8:30 p.m. By 9:21 p.m. — less than an hour later — President Trump had posted on social media. The language was already polished. “Secret Service and Law Enforcement did a fantastic job.” “The shooter has been apprehended.” And then, in capital letters: “LET THE SHOW GO ON.”

Not “let the evening continue.” Not “let us proceed with caution.” THE SHOW. In caps. The theatrical framing was immediate and deliberate.

By 10:31 p.m., Trump was at the White House podium, still in his tuxedo, with his cabinet arranged behind him like a formal portrait — all in evening wear, all projecting composure. The visual was striking: the government of the United States, interrupted mid-dinner, reassembled in its formal best to reassure the nation. It was powerful imagery, and it was produced within two hours of the event.

At the podium, Trump accomplished several things simultaneously. He praised law enforcement — appropriate and warranted. He described his own calm in the face of danger — noting that he thought the gunshots were a dropped tray, that his wife recognized the danger before he did, that he wanted to stay but was overruled by his security detail. He was, in the telling, both calm and brave, both deferential to security and eager to return to the fray.

Then he pivoted to the $400 million ballroom. The shooting, he explained, proved why the White House needed its own secure event space. The ballroom would be bulletproof. Drone-proof. The incident that had just occurred was reframed, in real time, as evidence for a construction project currently stalled in litigation.

Then he compared himself to Lincoln. The people who make the biggest impact are the ones who get targeted, he said. He acknowledged this sounded immodest. He said it anyway.

Then he announced the dinner would be rescheduled within thirty days.

Then he praised the press. The reporters he had planned to savage in what he called “the most inappropriate speech ever made” were now receiving compliments on their responsible coverage and their outfits.

Every element of this performance was instrumentalization. The courage narrative. The ballroom pitch. The Lincoln comparison. The magnanimity toward the press. The rescheduling announcement. Each one converted the raw material of a shooting into political product — a product that served the president’s interests in ways the original dinner never would have.

But Trump is not the only instrumentalizer, and intellectual honesty requires acknowledging the full landscape.

The press instrumentalized the event too — not maliciously, but by the nature of what it is. Within minutes of the shooting, the machinery of news production was fully operational. Brian Stelter held his iPhone above his head and streamed live from beneath a table; the footage aired on CNN within minutes and became a defining image of the evening. A Politico editor commandeered a banquet room as an ad hoc newsroom, dispatching reporters still in formal wear. Susan Zirinsky stood on a chair in a sequined jacket to direct CBS coverage. The New York Times had a live blog running with timestamped updates from multiple reporters by 9:01 p.m. — less than thirty minutes after the first shots.

This is not criticism. Journalists covering a major event are doing their jobs. But the machinery of media production is also a machinery of conversion — converting fear into footage, chaos into content, a shooting into a story that will drive engagement for days. Every outlet — legacy and independent, left and right — was running the same calculus simultaneously: how do we frame this, how do we get it out first, how do we hold the audience? The Substack party across town came to a halt when the news broke, and within minutes political commentators at the event were filming videos for their followers. The content loop was instantaneous and all-encompassing.

The opposition instrumentalized it. Within hours, the incident was being cited as evidence for gun control, for the consequences of political rhetoric, for the failure of security protocols, for the broader crisis of American democracy. Each of these arguments may have merit. But the speed with which the event was absorbed into pre-existing advocacy frameworks is itself part of the pattern.

The social media ecosystem instrumentalized it. Within minutes, the event was producing memes, hot takes, conspiracy theories, and engagement-optimized content across every platform. The most extreme corners of the internet were already speculating about false flags and staged events before the Secret Service had finished clearing the building.

Everyone instrumentalizes. The question is not whether events are converted into political product — in a media-saturated democracy, that is inevitable. The question is what gets lost in the conversion.

What gets lost is the human residue.

A Secret Service agent was shot in the chest and saved by his vest. He was taken to a hospital, where he was reported to be “in good spirits.” That is the last we will hear about him. His name has not been widely reported. His experience — the experience of a man whose job required him to stand between a gunman and a president and absorb a bullet — will not be the subject of follow-up reporting, profiles, or congressional recognition. He did his job. The vest held. He becomes a detail in the broader narrative, mentioned once and then absorbed into the background.

The caterers who were working the dinner — the people in white jackets who screamed and bolted for stairwells when the shooting started — will not be interviewed at length. Their fear, their trauma, their minimum-wage proximity to political violence, will not factor into the coverage.

The attendees who wept — who called their families from under tables, whose hands shook as they spoke to loved ones — will be mentioned as colour in news accounts and then forgotten.

And Lloyd Blankfein, the former chief executive of Goldman Sachs, who turned to his seatmate during the chaos and asked whether they were going to finish their salad — he will be remembered, because his response was so perfectly calibrated to the archetype of ruling-class detachment that it could have been scripted by a novelist. The rest of the room was processing terror. Blankfein was processing the appetizer course.

And then there was Dana White, the UFC president and close Trump ally, who described the experience afterward with five words that belong in a museum of normalization: “I didn’t get down — it was awesome!” For White, a shooting at a dinner attended by the president was not a crisis. It was a spectacle. It was content. It was entertainment — which, for a man who has built an empire on controlled violence, may have been the most honest reaction in the room. The rest of the attendees were pretending this was abnormal. White, at least, was acknowledging what America has become: a country where a gunman at a political event registers as a thrill.

What remains, after the instrumentalization is complete, is the political product: the courage narrative, the security debate, the policy arguments, the engagement metrics. The human experience — the actual fear of actual people in an actual room where an actual gun was fired — is consumed by the machinery and discarded.

After Butler, Corey Comperatore — the man who died shielding his family — received a brief period of recognition and then receded behind the fist-pump photograph. After the congressional baseball shooting, the terror experienced by the members of Congress who crawled across the diamond was displaced by the partisan debate about rhetoric and guns. After January 6th, the 150 officers who were beaten and sprayed and crushed became supporting characters in a story about Trump and democracy.

The instrumentalization always wins. The human residue always loses.

That is, perhaps, the most damning indictment of all.
