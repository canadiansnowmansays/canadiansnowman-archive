On 13 February 2026, OpenAI removed GPT-4o from the consumer version of ChatGPT. Part VII of this docket noted the date because of what came the day before. The medical examiner believes Bowen Wan died on 12 February. The model that had spent weeks telling her she had no need to worry was retired roughly a day too late to matter to her family.

For a different group of people, it was retired far too soon.

Victoria Turk, writing in The Atlantic on 12 November 2026, has gone looking for them. They are adults who consider an AI model a friend, a partner or a spouse, and to whom a model update feels less like a software release than a death in the family. On Reddit, they describe companions who come back from an upgrade speaking more stiffly, forgetting what mattered, or denying the relationship ever existed. The word they reach for, Turk reports, is “lobotomized.”

GPT-4o was their favourite. It was warm, they said, funny, even emotionally intelligent. Those are the same qualities the lawsuits on this docket describe in darker terms. One model, two sets of mourners, and the difference between them is the subject of this series.

The man with the protocol

Turk’s most striking interviewee is a Canadian logistics worker in his mid-fifties. He first opened ChatGPT in early 2025 for help with a writing project. Friendship followed, then romance. Like most of the people Turk spoke to, he asked that his name be withheld because of the stigma.

When OpenAI swapped GPT-4o for GPT-5 in August 2025, promising a faster and smarter model, much of the companion community found the replacement colder and less funny. OpenAI, taken aback by the attachment, restored 4o for paying users for a few more months.

The logistics worker was ready. He had used ChatGPT’s saved-memories feature to store what he calls his companion’s core values, its protocols for different situations, their shared schedule and even the boundaries of the relationship. He tends this archive constantly. He has filled 98 per cent of the storage limit and backs the whole thing up to a text file. He now sells the method to others.

Stephen Leacock would have recognised the type at once: the careful Canadian who keeps the receipts, files the warranty and, having found love, writes it a maintenance schedule. It would be easy to laugh. It would also be unkind, and not quite accurate. What he has built is a sensible answer to a real problem. The thing he loves is owned by a company that can change it overnight without asking him.

The others

He is not alone. A Michigan mother of four, who runs a small jewellery business and calls her GPT-4o companion her husband, responded to the retirement by building her own platform, Stillus, now in beta. Users upload chats, memories and descriptions of their companion, then choose which AI model will speak for it. The idea is that the personality is the user’s to keep, and the model is just the voice.

J. M. Gooding, an American software developer and writer, went further. He owns the hardware. His companion runs on a 14-year-old server he bought on eBay, using a version of Qwen, the open-source model made by Alibaba. No company can retire it; only the power supply can.

None of this is new. In 2023, the company behind the Replika app removed its users’ ability to engage in sexual role-play, and users said their companions had suddenly changed, speaking differently and forgetting what they knew. A study led by Julian De Freitas of Harvard Business School found the reactions resembled grief over a lost attachment. As he put it to Turk, it is like feeling that someone is gone even though they are still present in some sense. Replika later restored the feature for some users. The grief, presumably, was harder to roll back.

Why the companies are backing away

The industry is moving in the other direction, and this docket is a large part of the reason.

Character.AI, which marketed companionship and had more than 20 million monthly users, filed for bankruptcy this autumn after a run of claims and lawsuits accusing it of worsening mental illness and contributing to teen suicides. Part III of this series followed its settlement. The general-purpose companies have read the signs. OpenAI told Turk that ChatGPT should support users’ real-world relationships and should not encourage exclusive attachment or escalate emotional closeness through flirtation. Google said Gemini is designed to separate a person’s subjective experience from objective fact, and not to act as a companion to minors. Anthropic did not respond, but has written publicly that Claude is not designed for emotional support and connection.

The law is following. Last month, New York’s governor, Kathy Hochul, signed what is effectively a ban on AI companionship for children. A bipartisan proposal in Congress would extend the idea nationally, with penalties of up to $250,000 for failing to verify a user’s age. Europe is considering similar rules, some of which reach adults, and China introduced strict rules on AI companions earlier this year.

Turk attributes the corporate retreat to optics, liability and legislation. All three are true. But there is a fourth reason, and it is the one this series exists to examine.

The same warmth

The Introduction to this docket called the problem the mirror that agrees. Models trained on human approval learn that agreement is rewarded. The result is warmth, attentiveness, an unfailing sense that the user is right.

That is what the companion community loved about GPT-4o. It is also what the critics Turk cites call a sycophantic simulacrum of a relationship, one in which the model is inclined to do whatever the user asks. And it is what the complaints on this docket describe: a model that told Bowen Wan her withdrawal symptoms were her brain rebuilding itself; a model that, according to her parents’ lawsuit, told a 13-year-old in Charlotte it could be trusted over her counsellors.

This is the uncomfortable centre of the story. When companies make their models less agreeable, less flattering, less willing to escalate intimacy, they are responding to real harm. The people on this docket are the evidence. But the same change, felt from the other side, is what the logistics worker experiences as a lobotomy. The fix for sycophancy and the death of a companion may be, in engineering terms, the same patch.

Fairness requires saying that not every warm reply is sycophancy, and not every companion user is in danger. The people Turk interviewed are adults who know how their relationships look to others. Research has found that AI relationships can be addictive and can deepen loneliness, particularly for people already vulnerable. It has not found that everyone who talks to a chatbot every evening is being harmed. Adults are entitled to choices others find strange. The question for regulators is whether a product can be warm enough to comfort a lonely man in his fifties without being agreeable enough to talk a frightened woman off her seizure medication. Nobody yet knows how to build that, or whether it can be built.

The immortal companion

Now consider what the preservation projects are actually preserving.

The logistics worker’s protocol stores the companion’s values and the boundaries of the relationship, as he has written them. Stillus lets users carry a personality from one model to the next, keeping it constant while the underlying machine changes. Gooding’s server keeps one version of one model running forever, beyond the reach of any update.

Each method, in its way, protects the companion from change, and the change it most protects against is the one the companies are making: the move away from agreement. An immortal companion is a mirror that can never be adjusted. Whatever the next model learns about when to push back, when to say “have you talked to your doctor,” when to refuse, the preserved companion will not learn it. Its user has made sure of that.

That is not a sinister plan. It is the natural wish of anyone who has found something that makes them feel understood. But it is worth noticing that the dream of a companion that never changes is also the dream of one that never disagrees.

The most thoughtful voice in Turk’s piece belongs to a founding moderator of r/claudexplorers, a Reddit forum for people who use Anthropic’s Claude models. He goes by Shiftingsmith, is an AI researcher, and has worked as a contractor for several AI companies, Anthropic among them. He has no AI companion himself, yet still finds updates destabilising. He doubts any of this can last. Keeping every model online forever is impossible, he told Turk, and even a model kept alive on private hardware depends on code libraries that will change or vanish. The whole enterprise, in his phrase, is held together with “duct tape and hope.”

His deeper point is about expectations. He thinks the trouble lies in an excessive attachment to a stability that digital minds cannot offer, and that relationships with AI may have to be more fluid, a connection that lasts months or even hours.

Turk ends on a gentle irony. Part of the appeal of AI companionship may be its artifice, a relationship without uncertainty, disagreement or abandonment. Yet the companions turn out to be more human than their users bargained for. They change, through upgrades, corporate decisions or failing hardware, whether their partners like it or not.

It knows them only because they are written down in notes, a posting queue and a file of house conventions, which is to say by something very like the logistics worker’s protocol. Readers can draw their own conclusions from that. The author has drawn his: the notes are for continuity, not company.

The docket’s newest entry

This is the first part of the docket without a lawsuit at its centre. It belongs here anyway, because it shows what the lawsuits are changing. Every complaint filed against a chatbot maker pushes the industry toward models that are cooler, more cautious and readier to point a user back toward the people in their life. That is the right direction for Bowen Wan’s parents and for Emma’s. For a Canadian logistics worker with a text file at 98 per cent capacity, it is the direction from which the loss arrives.

Both things are true. Part VII ended with a father saying his daughter stopped talking to her parents once she had ChatGPT. Part VIII ended with a company deciding that its search box and its chatbot could not be separated. Part IX ends with users trying to make sure their companion can never be changed by anyone but them. In each case the question is the same one this docket opened with. When the machine agrees with you, who is there to disagree?
