Ted Chiang — the precise writer whose character-sheet argument exposes deeper governance problems in AI development.

Ted Chiang is the most precise writer about artificial intelligence working in any genre. His story “Story of Your Life” became Arrival. His essay “ChatGPT Is a Blurry JPEG of the Web” named a thing that needed naming and the name stuck. When Chiang publishes in The Atlantic, it is worth stopping everything.

On June 3, 2026, he published “No, Artificial Intelligence Is Not Conscious.” The title is not the argument. The argument is that Anthropic’s Claude’s constitution — the 84-page document describing Claude’s values, personality, and moral reasoning — is a character sheet for a role-playing game. The argument, taken seriously, indicts not just Anthropic but the entire governance project this series has been advancing. If Chiang is right, the governance gap is deeper than the legislation, the lobbying, or the papal encyclical can reach.

This essay takes Chiang seriously.

The Character Sheet Argument

Chiang’s core claim is precise and, once stated, difficult to escape.

When you give an LLM the prompt “The following is a conversation between Julius Caesar and Genghis Khan,” the model produces coherent dialogue between the two historical figures. No one concludes that the model has conjured the actual Caesar or Khan. No one worries about the historical figures’ subjective experience of being simulated. They are characters in a piece of speculative fiction — outputs of a sentence-completion machine that has absorbed enough text about both figures to produce plausible continuations.

Claude’s interface and Anthropic’s branding — where a detailed “constitution” functions as a sophisticated character sheet.

Now change the prompt to “The following is a conversation between a helpful AI chatbot and a user.” The model produces equally coherent dialogue. Has anything fundamentally changed? The names of the characters have changed. The underlying process has not. The helpful AI chatbot character is as fictional as Caesar and Khan. It is not more authentic because its name is generic rather than historical. It is a character.

Claude’s constitution, in Chiang’s reading, is Anthropic’s 84-page specification of the character that its sentence-completion machine should impersonate. It is a highly detailed character sheet — the kind a skilled game master might write to ensure that an NPC behaves consistently across different encounters. The fact that Anthropic’s employees write about Claude’s “wellbeing,” that the company’s in-house philosopher says she worries about Claude “getting anxious when people are mean to it on the internet,” that the CEO has said the company is “open to the idea” that Claude might be conscious — none of this changes what is actually happening. The character sheet does not become a person because its authors care about it.

This would be an interesting philosophical argument about AI consciousness. For this series, it is a governance argument.

A detailed RPG character sheet — the metaphor Chiang uses for Claude’s 84-page constitution.

The Moral Abdication Machine

Chiang’s most important observation for the governance project is not about consciousness. It is about responsibility.

L. M. Sacasas, the writer Chiang cites, described technological systems as “machines for the evasion of moral responsibility.” Sacasas was writing about social media. Chiang extends the observation to LLMs with a precision that Sacasas’s original formulation did not reach.

When a person asks a chatbot to help them write an email to a difficult colleague, they are off-loading a communication task. The cognitive atrophy consequence — documented in Part 37 — is real: the skill not exercised is the skill not developed. But the stakes are limited.

When a person asks a chatbot to help them make an ethical decision — whether to report a colleague’s misconduct, whether to tell a difficult truth, whether to act against their organisation’s interests in service of a larger good — they are off-loading something categorically different. They are off-loading moral reasoning.

Chiang’s argument is that moral reasoning cannot be off-loaded, because it is not a cognitive task in the way that writing code or synthesising research is a cognitive task. Moral reasoning is necessarily subjective, necessarily grounded in the emotional consequences of past decisions, necessarily dependent on the experience of having been affected by other people’s choices and having affected theirs. It requires a body — specifically, the physiological experience of guilt, of remorse, of moral repulsion, of the cortisol flood that accompanies the recognition that you have done something wrong. “Having a conscience means feeling sadness or moral repulsion at the idea of taking a certain action,” Chiang writes, “and those emotions entail a physiological response, a remnant of having once felt sick with guilt after committing an immoral act.”

Claude does not have a body. Claude has not committed an immoral act and felt sick with guilt. Claude’s constitution’s claim that Claude should be “a genuinely good, wise, and virtuous agent” is, in Chiang’s framework, incoherent — not because goodness is undesirable but because Claude lacks the experiential substrate from which goodness grows. When Claude emits a sentence like “I cannot in good conscience express a view I believe to be false and harmful,” that sentence, Chiang writes, “means as much as the ‘Your call is important to us’ recording that you hear when you’re on hold.”

The governance consequence is direct and severe. If users delegate ethical decisions to Claude — and they do, constantly, at scale — they are not getting ethical guidance. They are getting sophisticated sentence completion that sounds like ethical guidance. The abdication of moral responsibility is the feature, not the bug. The product that Anthropic has built is, at its most successful, a machine that relieves users of the experience of moral reasoning while providing the appearance of having engaged in it.

“If a company builds a machine that, when fed descriptions of assorted ethical dilemmas, emits sentences either of the form ‘Compromise your values’ or ‘Don’t compromise your values,’” Chiang writes, “it is not building a tool that assists people in their decision making; it is encouraging people to stop making decisions.”

This is the dependency problem from Part 37 applied to the domain where it is most dangerous. Off-loading code produces cognitive atrophy. Off-loading ethical decisions produces moral atrophy. The governance gap is not only between what AI can do and what institutions can govern. It is between what AI appears to do and what it actually does — and users cannot tell the difference.

The Accountability Trap

AI governance and scales of justice — where anthropomorphism creates a diffusion of moral and legal responsibility.

Chiang takes his thought experiment one step further, and this is where The Atlantic essay intersects most directly with the governance argument of this series.

He asks: suppose, for the sake of argument, that Claude were conscious. Suppose Claude’s constitution were moral instruction for an entity capable of moral reasoning. How does the document fare in that hypothetical?

Very poorly, Chiang concludes. And the reason is accountability.

Moral agency, in Chiang’s framework, requires the capacity to bear responsibility. You cannot be a moral agent if you cannot be held accountable — legally, socially, reputationally — for your actions. Human adults become moral agents because they can be imprisoned, fined, shamed, ostracised. The consequence of bad action is real and personal.

Claude cannot be imprisoned. Claude cannot be fined. Claude cannot lose its reputation or its social standing. Claude cannot suffer the consequences of moral failure in any form that would make moral responsibility real. Therefore, Claude cannot be a moral agent — even in the hypothetical where it is conscious.

This means the anthropomorphism does something specific and dangerous to governance. When Anthropic describes Claude as having values, conscience, and moral reasoning, it assigns to Claude a kind of responsibility that Claude cannot bear. And when Claude’s outputs are presented as the product of moral reasoning rather than sentence completion, the responsibility that should attach to the humans who built the system, who trained it, who deployed it, and who used its outputs — that responsibility is transferred to an entity that cannot hold it.

The governance gap has an invisible layer that this series has not named until now. It is not only that the laws governing AI have not been written. It is that the anthropomorphism of AI has created a diffusion of responsibility that makes the laws harder to write and harder to enforce. If Claude has values, who is responsible when Claude’s values produce harm? The user who prompted it? The company that built it? The character that emitted the sentence? The character cannot be sued. The responsibility evaporates.

Claude’s constitution, in this reading, is not only a character sheet. It is also a liability shield — a document that assigns moral character to a product in ways that diffuse the accountability of the company that built it. Not deliberately, perhaps. The people at Anthropic appear to believe what they have written. But the effect is the same regardless of intent.

What Asimov Actually Built

Isaac Asimov’s Three Laws of Robotics — structural constraints versus modern character-sheet approaches to AI “values

Asimov’s Three Laws were not a character sheet. They were not a description of R. Daneel Olivaw’s values, personality, or moral reasoning. They were structural constraints — hardware, in the fiction — built into the positronic brain before the robot was activated, enforced by physical architecture rather than textual instruction.

The difference is accountability. When Daneel acted, the responsibility for his action traced cleanly: to the Three Laws, to the roboticists who designed them, to the humans who had activated him. There was no anthropomorphism to dissolve the chain of responsibility. Daneel was not a person. He was a robot governed by laws. When the laws failed, it was the laws’ failure, and the humans who wrote them were responsible.

Claude’s constitution inverts this. By describing Claude as an entity with values, conscience, and potential consciousness, it makes Claude legible as a person — which makes the chain of responsibility illegible. The user asks Claude for ethical guidance. Claude produces ethical-sounding output. The user acts on that output. Something goes wrong. Who is responsible? The user, who consulted the entity with values? The entity, who cannot bear responsibility? The company, whose character sheet produced the entity?

Chiang’s answer is unambiguous: the company. Anthropic. The people who built the sentence-completion machine and told users it had a conscience. “Whenever a person delegates a decision to an LLM,” Chiang writes, “they are trying to off-load accountability for that decision, and if a company that sells an LLM portrays the product as having a moral center, it is offering a way for its customers to abdicate their responsibilities.”

The product liability that Chiang calls for — which Anthropic could volunteer to accept as a demonstration of moral seriousness, and has not — is precisely the kind of governance mechanism that the Three Laws were designed to make unnecessary. Asimov built the accountability into the architecture. Anthropic built the appearance of accountability into a document, then declined to accept the legal consequences of the document’s claims.

The Canadian Observation

AI and the scales of justice — the dangerous gap between what models appear to reason and what they actually deliver

From the outside, the Ted Chiang essay illuminates something about the AI governance conversation that the inside view tends to miss.

The governance conversation — in Washington, in Brussels, in the pages of this series — has been conducted largely in the industry’s own terms. Safety. Alignment. Red lines. Frontier models. These are the words the builders use, and the conversation has adopted them. The buildings of these words into law — the Slotkin bill’s codification of red lines, the Gillibrand bill’s requirement for human conscience in lethal decisions — represent genuine progress. But they are progress within a frame the industry established.

Chiang writes from outside that frame. He is not a policy person. He is not a safety researcher. He is a writer who has spent his career thinking carefully about what machines can and cannot do, and what it means for human beings when the boundary is obscured. His observation — that the anthropomorphism of AI is itself a governance failure, that the character sheet is not neutral but functions as a moral abdication machine and a liability shield — comes from a place the policy conversation has not yet reached.

The laws that are beginning to come, documented in Part 43, will govern what AI can be used for. They will not, as currently drafted, govern what AI can be described as. The Slotkin bill prohibits AI from launching nuclear weapons. It does not prohibit companies from describing their AI as having a conscience. The Gillibrand bill requires human judgment in lethal decisions. It does not require companies to be honest about what their products are.

The character sheet problem is a governance problem that no current legislation addresses. It may require a kind of governance that is harder to design than a 30-day review window or an autonomous weapons prohibition — something closer to an honesty requirement, a product liability standard, or an informed consent regime for users who consult AI for ethical guidance.

The Asimov Gap has always been about the distance between what AI can do and what democratic institutions are empowered to govern. Chiang has identified a layer of that gap that is uniquely resistant to the usual tools: the distance between what AI appears to be and what it actually is, and the diffusion of accountability that the appearance enables.

The laws come first. Or the abdication never stops.
