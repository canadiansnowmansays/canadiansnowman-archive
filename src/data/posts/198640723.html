Jawaharlal Nehru addressing the Constituent Assembly at midnight, August 14–15, 1947: “At the stroke of the midnight hour..”

At the stroke of the midnight hour, when the world sleeps, India will awake to life and freedom.

Jawaharlal Nehru spoke those words to the Constituent Assembly in New Delhi on the night of August 14, 1947, as British rule ended and Indian independence began. It is among the most celebrated speeches in the history of the twentieth century — lyrical, soaring, magnificent in its cadence and its promise.

Outside the assembly, people were dying.

I. The Decision

The Partition of British India into two sovereign nations — India and Pakistan — was the largest and most violent mass migration in recorded human history. In the space of a few months, approximately 15 million people moved across the new borders. Hindus and Sikhs fled westward from what was now Pakistan. Muslims fled eastward from what was now India. Between one and two million people were killed. Women were raped, mutilated, abducted, and traded as spoils. Trains arrived at stations on both sides of the border carrying nothing but corpses — the passengers had been massacred en route. Entire villages were emptied. Entire communities were erased.

This happened within the lifetime of people who are still alive. It happened to the grandparents and great-grandparents of my roommates. When I say that Partition is not history for Indians, I mean it literally. It is family.

The decision to partition was taken in haste by men who did not have to live with its consequences. Lord Mountbatten, the last Viceroy, arrived in India in March 1947 with instructions to transfer power. He moved the date forward from June 1948 to August 1947 — advancing the departure by ten months, reportedly because he was impatient and because the escalating violence between Hindus and Muslims persuaded him that delay would make things worse. Whether speed made things better or catastrophically worse is a question Indian historians have answered decisively: worse.

The actual border — the line that divided Punjab in the west and Bengal in the east, that determined which villages would be in India and which in Pakistan, which families would keep their homes and which would become refugees — was drawn by a single man. Sir Cyril Radcliffe, a British lawyer who had never visited India before his appointment, was given five weeks to divide a subcontinent. He worked with outdated census data and maps. He had no experience in the region. He drew lines through villages, through fields, through irrigation systems, through communities that had lived together for centuries. When he finished, he left India and never returned. He reportedly burned his papers.

I cannot improve on the judgment of history here. A British lawyer who had never seen India drew a line on a map in five weeks, and millions died. The administrative casualness of the decision — the sheer bureaucratic inadequacy of the process — is itself an indictment of the empire that had governed India for two centuries and understood it so poorly that it entrusted the most consequential geographic decision in modern Asian history to a man who could not have found Punjab on a map six weeks earlier.

The Radcliffe Line — drawn in five weeks by a man who had never visited India, dividing a subcontinent and millions of lives.

II. Punjab

The violence was worst in Punjab, and Punjab is where the story must be told most honestly.

Punjab was the heartland of three communities: Hindus, Muslims, and Sikhs. They had lived together — not always peacefully, not without tension, but together — for centuries. The Radcliffe Line cut Punjab in half. The western half, Muslim-majority, went to Pakistan. The eastern half, Hindu- and Sikh-majority, went to India. Millions of people found themselves on the wrong side.

What followed was ethnic cleansing on a scale that Europe would not see again until the Balkans in the 1990s. Muslim villages in East Punjab were attacked by Sikh and Hindu mobs. Hindu and Sikh villages in West Punjab were attacked by Muslim mobs. The killing was intimate — neighbours killing neighbours, men who had shared wells and festivals and harvest seasons turning on each other with swords, spears, and fire. The violence was not random. It was organized, often by local political leaders, by religious figures, by men who saw in the chaos an opportunity for land, for revenge, for the elimination of the other.

The Sikh community occupies a particular place in this history. Sikhs had been promised a homeland — a Sikh state, or at least significant Sikh autonomy — by both the British and the Indian National Congress during the negotiations for independence. They received neither. The Radcliffe Line divided the Sikh heartland. Sikh holy sites, including Nankana Sahib — the birthplace of Guru Nanak, the founder of Sikhism — fell in Pakistan. The sense of betrayal was existential. Some Sikh leaders organized armed militias that carried out systematic attacks on Muslim villages. Some Sikh communities were themselves victims of systematic Muslim violence. The cycle was total.

Refugee trains during Partition — one of the largest and most violent mass migrations in history

My roommate Gurneet is Sikh. He speaks Punjabi. When I tell him I am writing about Partition, I am writing about his people’s wound. The Sikh experience of 1947 — the divided homeland, the lost holy sites, the broken promises — runs through Sikh political consciousness like a fault line. It explains the Khalistan movement. It explains the tensions between Sikhs and the Indian state that culminated in the attack on the Golden Temple in 1984. It explains things that are happening between Canada and India right now, in the aftermath of the Nijjar affair. But those are later essays. For now, the point is that Partition is not one story. It is three — Hindu, Muslim, Sikh — and they do not agree.

III. Bengal

Bengal was divided for the second time — the first being the 1905 partition that galvanized the independence movement — and the consequences, while less immediately violent than Punjab, were no less devastating over time.

East Bengal, Muslim-majority, became East Pakistan — a geographically absurd arrangement that placed the two halves of Pakistan a thousand miles apart with the entirety of India between them. The arrangement lasted 24 years before collapsing in the Bangladesh Liberation War of 1971, which produced another genocide, another refugee crisis, and another redrawing of the map.

The partition of Bengal produced a refugee crisis that reshaped Calcutta and the surrounding region for decades. Millions of Hindu refugees from East Bengal poured into West Bengal, overwhelming housing, infrastructure, and social services. The bhadralok — the Bengali educated class — was split in half. Families were divided. The great literary and intellectual tradition of Bengal, which had produced Tagore, Ray, and some of the finest minds of the Indian independence movement, was fractured along a border that made no cultural sense and every demographic sense.

The refugee colonies that sprang up around Calcutta in the late 1940s and 1950s became permanent features of the city — communities of displaced people who never went home because home no longer existed, or existed in a country they were no longer welcome in. The human cost of Partition in Bengal was slower, quieter, and less photogenic than the massacres of Punjab. It was no less real.

IV. The Architects

Partition had many authors, and the assignment of responsibility remains one of the most contested questions in South Asian historiography. But three figures tower above the rest, and each bears a share.

Nehru wanted a strong, centralized, secular Indian state. He was unwilling to share power with the Muslim League in the arrangements that might have made a united India possible. His impatience with Jinnah — a personal as well as political antipathy — contributed to the breakdown of negotiations. Nehru was not wrong that a united India was preferable to a divided one. He was wrong to believe that his preference entitled him to set the terms.

Jinnah wanted Pakistan. The question of when he began wanting it — whether the “two-nation theory” (the idea that Hindus and Muslims constituted separate nations requiring separate states) was a genuine conviction or a bargaining position that spiralled beyond his control — is debated. What is not debated is that Jinnah’s demand for Pakistan, once made, could not be unmade. He bet that the British and Congress would offer enough concessions to make Pakistan unnecessary. They didn’t. He got his country. It arrived soaked in blood, divided by a thousand miles of Indian territory, and Jinnah himself died of tuberculosis barely a year after independence, leaving a nation with no institutional foundation and no leader who could replace him.

Mountbatten wanted to get out. His decision to move the deadline up from June 1948 to August 1947 — compressing the timeline by nearly a year — is the single most consequential administrative decision in the history of decolonization. Every historian who has examined it agrees that more time would not have prevented Partition but would have allowed for a more orderly transfer: planned population movements, pre-positioned security forces, functioning border administration. Mountbatten chose speed. Millions paid for his choice.

And behind all three stood Churchill, who had fought Indian independence for decades, who had presided over the Bengal Famine, and whose wartime government had made the promises to the Muslim League that Jinnah later called in. Churchill was out of office by 1947, but his fingerprints were on every surface.

V. The Women

Refugees during Partition — the women whose stories of abduction, recovery, and trauma were often silenced by national narratives.

The history of Partition is most often told as a story of men — men who made decisions, men who fought, men who migrated. The experience of women was different in kind, not merely in degree.

An estimated 75,000 to 100,000 women were abducted during Partition — taken from their families, forced into marriages, converted to the religion of their captors, and absorbed into communities on the other side of the border. Both India and Pakistan subsequently launched “recovery operations” to retrieve abducted women — programs that were framed as rescue but that often amounted to a second uprooting. Women who had spent years in their captors’ households, who had borne children, who had in some cases built new lives however coerced, were extracted by government agents and returned to families and communities they no longer recognized.

The women were never asked what they wanted. Their bodies were treated as national property — territory to be recovered, honour to be restored. The patriarchal logic was identical on both sides: our women, taken by them, must be brought back. The women’s own agency, their own trauma, their own complicated and sometimes contradictory feelings about their situations, were irrelevant to the project of national recovery.

Urvashi Butalia’s work, The Other Side of Silence, is among the most important oral histories of Partition, precisely because it centres the voices that the official narrative silenced. The women of Partition are not footnotes. They are the story — the story of what nations do to individuals, and particularly to women, when the state decides that honour matters more than humanity.

VI. The Inheritance

Partition refugees on a train — the intimate, communal violence that reshaped families, villages, and nations.

Partition left India with three inheritances that define its politics to this day.

The first is Kashmir. The princely state of Jammu and Kashmir, ruled by a Hindu maharaja over a Muslim-majority population, acceded to India in October 1947 under disputed circumstances — a Pakistani-backed tribal invasion prompted the maharaja to sign the Instrument of Accession in exchange for Indian military support. The promise of a plebiscite was made and never kept. Two wars and multiple insurgencies later, Kashmir remains the most dangerous border dispute on earth — two nuclear-armed states facing each other across a Line of Control that was supposed to be temporary in 1949 and is permanent in everything but name.

The second is the Hindu-Muslim wound. Partition did not resolve Hindu-Muslim tensions. It displaced them. The Muslims who remained in India — roughly 35 million at independence, over 200 million today — became a permanent minority in a Hindu-majority state. Their security depends on the secular constitutional framework that Nehru and Ambedkar built. When that framework is weakened — as the BJP’s Hindutva project is methodically weakening it — the largest religious minority in the world’s largest democracy has no fallback position. They cannot go to Pakistan. Pakistan was built for Muslims who left. India’s Muslims are the ones who stayed. Their fate is the test of Indian democracy’s promise, and it is being tested now.

The third is the trauma itself. Partition is not discussed in South Asian households the way the Holocaust is discussed in Jewish households — openly, ritually, as a communal obligation of memory. In many families, Partition is silence. The grandparents who survived it often did not speak of it. The violence they witnessed or committed, the homes they lost, the relatives they never saw again — these became family secrets, not family stories. The silence is itself a wound, and it has political consequences. A trauma that is not processed does not disappear. It metastasizes. It becomes communal suspicion, political resentment, sectarian voting patterns, and — at its worst — the ideology that says: never again, and if it means their turn to suffer, so be it.

VII. The Man Who Built Anyway

In the middle of this catastrophe — the killing, the migration, the trains of the dead — a small group of men and women sat in a hall in New Delhi and wrote a constitution.

They did this while refugees streamed past their windows. They did this while Gandhi was assassinated thirty minutes from their meeting place. They did this while Kashmir burned, while communal riots convulsed the cities, while the nation they were building a legal framework for appeared to be disintegrating before it had fully formed.

They did it anyway.

B.R. Ambedkar and Mahatma Gandhi — men who built a Constitution amid the ashes of Partition.

The man who led them — who chaired the drafting committee, who wrote the bulk of the document, who fought for every clause against colleagues who wanted a Hindu state, a caste-based state, or no state at all — was B.R. Ambedkar. A Dalit. An Untouchable. A man who had been denied water from the village well as a child.

He wrote the supreme law of the largest democracy on earth. And he began it with words that represent either the most audacious promise or the most magnificent lie in constitutional history:

We, the people of India.

He is the subject of our next essay. He deserves more than one.
