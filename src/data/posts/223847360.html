In the last weeks before the midterms, Jeffrey Epstein’s name was everywhere: in campaign advertisements, in stump speeches, on T-shirts at rallies and even in a scandal on a reality-television show. Two years earlier, invoking Epstein had been part of the Republican playbook. On November 3, 2026, it was part of the case against them.

Sarah Fitzpatrick spent the campaign asking why, and her answer, published in The Atlantic on November 6, should be read by everyone who has followed this series. Members of Congress, pollsters, operatives and campaign staff told her that the war with Iran and the price of gas were the issues at the front of voters’ minds. But they also told her that Trump’s handling of the Epstein files meaningfully contributed to the Republican defeat. The Republican pollster Brent Buchanan called Epstein “a proxy for corruption.” Another Republican close to the White House called it the “nuclear fuel” of a new American populism. It boosted Trump in 2024. In 2026 it worked against him.

This series has argued since its first part that the cover-up would matter more than any single document in it. The midterms are the first time voters have been asked to rule on that.

The promise and the memo

Fitzpatrick’s account begins where this series did, with the promises.

In December 2021, the Senate candidate J.D. Vance posted a question to his followers about what possible interest the government could have in keeping Epstein’s clients secret. In a 2023 podcast interview, the future FBI director Kash Patel demanded that House Republicans produce “the list.” Six months later, Trump said he would release the files, a pledge that won him praise on the right, particularly among people who do not usually follow politics.

The promise meant different things to different audiences. Epstein’s victims, and many who followed the case, wanted to know who had known about his crimes and why he had escaped justice for so long. Others were sure they already knew: that there was a client list, that it held the names of powerful men, and that anyone who failed to produce it was part of the cover-up.

Then the people who made the promise took office. In February 2025, Attorney General Pam Bondi told Fox News the client list was “sitting on my desk right now to review.” The White House handed binders labelled “The Epstein Files: Phase 1” to right-wing influencers; nearly all the material was redacted or already public. In July 2025 the Justice Department and FBI released a memo saying there would be no further disclosures and no basis for further investigation. The memo was unsigned. A person familiar with it told Fitzpatrick that no one in the senior leadership of either agency would put their name to it.

Trump responded on Truth Social by calling the affair “The Jeffrey Epstein Hoax.” His past supporters, he wrote, had bought into it, and “I don’t want their support anymore!” Several of those supporters told pollsters and focus groups that the post only deepened their suspicion.

Later that year he tried to stop Congress from voting to release the files, and gave way only when enough Republicans had defected to pass the bill without him. The Justice Department then released millions of documents in a process Fitzpatrick describes as hasty and error-filled. Parts of this series recorded each stage. None of it quieted the suspicion.

Where the bond broke

What Fitzpatrick adds is the view from inside the Republican coalition. Several Republican pollsters and operatives told her they now date the unravelling of the MAGA coalition to July 2025, the month of the unsigned memo. “That is the beginning of that bond breaking,” said Rich Baris, the director of Big Data Poll. “And then you follow it up with the Iran war, and it’s just a devastating one-two punch.”

White House officials and people close to the president also described the explosion of the Epstein story that summer as the first moment that threw Trump’s second term off its stride. Until then it had moved through Washington like a steamroller.

The Republican strategist Matthew Bartlett, a former Trump administration official, made the point more bluntly. The files had briefly been a bipartisan issue: Republicans expected them to incriminate Bill Clinton and other Democrats, and Democrats expected the same of Trump. But figures in the administration promised far more than they could deliver. “Some of them were shameful in their statements and allegations, and then came up with nothing,” he said. “That planted the seed of distrust.”

On November 4, surveying what he called “close to an extinction-level event” for Republicans, Florida’s governor, Ron DeSantis, listed among the causes that the party should have voted to release the files sooner.

The ad war

Democrats turned the issue into a campaign. AdImpact counted at least $76 million spent on advertisements mentioning Epstein, overwhelmingly by Democrats in competitive races. Epstein appeared in at least 79 federal and state races across 26 states. In Texas, James Talarico said he had “never given a sweetheart deal to a pedophile.” In Georgia, Jon Ossoff’s campaign said his opponent had “protected powerful elites” by voting against disclosure. In Ohio, Sherrod Brown accused his opponent of taking the most money from Epstein’s associates. In Maine, a Democratic-aligned super PAC said Senator Susan Collins had voted with Trump to cover the files up. Talarico, Ossoff and Brown all won, and Collins lost.

Democrats also turned “the Epstein class” into an epithet for their opponents, a shorthand for high prices, corruption and untrustworthiness at once.

Republicans spent about $3 million on Epstein ads by late October, mostly trying to turn the issue around. Senator Dan Sullivan of Alaska told voters he had voted to force the release of the files while his opponent, Mary Peltola, “took huge checks from Epstein’s associates.” In Georgia, Mike Collins told Ossoff in a debate that “the Epstein class is onstage right now. And it’s you.” The National Republican Congressional Committee aimed at Reid Hoffman, the LinkedIn founder and Democratic megadonor, whose name appears in the files. Hoffman has denied any knowledge of Epstein’s crimes and has called for more transparency. The counterattack did not work. As of November 5, Sullivan trailed Peltola by about 600 votes, with a ranked-choice count due on November 18.

The Democrats’ turn

Fitzpatrick’s most important point is about what comes next. Democrats have won. Now they are expected to deliver, and the Justice Department and FBI have shown no willingness to pursue Epstein-related investigations.

Part XL of this series covered the House Judiciary Committee Democrats’ staff report, which called for an independent special counsel to investigate Trump’s relationship with Epstein and accused his Justice Department of suppressing evidence. Jamie Raskin, who is expected to chair the committee in January, told Fitzpatrick that his priority will be pressing the department to release the roughly 2.5 million Epstein-related documents still believed to be in its possession, as the law requires, and demanding that possible crimes be investigated. The department’s position is that it has already released everything required and that the remaining files fall outside the Epstein Files Transparency Act. Several civil lawsuits are fighting over them.

Robert Garcia, who will chair the House Oversight Committee, put out a statement on election night two words long: “Lawyer up.”

Raskin was more candid about the difficulty. An administration that ignores congressional subpoenas could leave Democrats unable to meet the expectations they raised, which he called both “a great responsibility” and “a very serious challenge.” A Data for Progress poll found that 72 per cent of voters want the Justice Department and FBI to do more on Epstein, while 45 per cent do not believe any American will actually be arrested. Ro Khanna told Fitzpatrick that the Democrats who won on the issue will have to lead on sexual trafficking and accountability more broadly.

Chuck Schumer, who will lead the Senate, was asked by Michael Barbaro on The Daily for The New York Times whether he wanted to see the president impeached. “That’s going to be up to the House,” he said, and then: “Let’s see what the House does.” It was not a no.

Fitzpatrick names the risk. If the remaining documents do not bear out the darkest theories about Epstein, the Democrats who promised them may find themselves accused of joining the cover-up, exactly as the Republicans were.

The bipartisan exception

The story has one Republican chapter that does not fit the partisan frame. Sky Roberts, the brother of Virginia Roberts Giuffre, Epstein’s most prominent accuser, who died in 2025, described a meeting with James Comer, the Republican chairman of the House Oversight Committee. Comer listened to the survivors and their families, Roberts said, then sat back and said, “I have daughters.” Something changed. Comer’s committee has since conducted more than a dozen depositions and interviews, obtained information about Epstein’s bank accounts, referred two people to the Justice Department and held Leon Black, an Epstein associate, in contempt of Congress.

Roberts is clear about what the files are and are not. “It’s not political,” he said, “but it is the political state that got us here today, blue and red, and there are just as many Democrats in there as there are Republicans in the files.”

The people in the ads

The last word in Fitzpatrick’s piece belongs to the people the advertisements were about. Weeks before the election she met a small group of Epstein’s victims and their advocates in New York, at a ceremony where Giuffre received a posthumous award for speaking out. They found the campaign ads, and their abuser’s name in stump speeches, surreal. They spoke about the personal costs of their trauma becoming a political tool: therapy bills, and credit-card debt run up travelling to lobby members of Congress. Other victims told her they had taken on several jobs, or relied on donations and food banks to feed their children.

“If somebody abused your daughter or your sister,” Roberts said, “you wouldn’t ask first if he was a Democrat or a Republican.”

The ledger

The Epstein files began this series as a promise and became a cover-up. On November 3 they became a verdict. The Republicans who promised the list and then could not or would not produce it paid for it at the polls, and some of their own pollsters say the payment started the day the unsigned memo appeared.

The files now pass to the other side. Democrats will hold the committees, the subpoenas and the expectations. What they will not hold is the Justice Department, which still has the documents and still says it owes Congress nothing more. This series will follow what happens when the new majority asks for them, and whether the victims, rather than the campaigns, are finally the ones the answers are for.
