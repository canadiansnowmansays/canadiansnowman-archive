The marquee changed on November third. Gerald noted the date. The theatre does not stop being the theatre. It simply waits for the next version of itself, which will be available in spring.

The Festival closes on the first of November.

It has closed on approximately the first of November for seventy years, give or take the occasional extension for a production that warranted it, the occasional curtailment for a season that did not, and one year in which a late October ice storm persuaded the production of The Tempest that Prospero’s rough magic had been adequately demonstrated by nature and that the remaining performances should be rescheduled. The closing is not announced in any dramatic way. The final performance ends. The marquee, in the days following, goes dark. The theatre enters its winter mode, which is a mode of maintenance and preparation and planning for the season to come.

This year, on the morning of November first, Alistair did not unlock the door.

He had unlocked the door every morning of every season for nineteen years. He had done this on mornings when it was warm and on mornings when it was cold and on one morning in March when the lock had frozen and he had stood at the entrance for eleven minutes with his breath visible and a growing conviction that the building was making a point, until Gerald Fitch had arrived with a can of de-icer that he had, of course, brought from the left pocket of his cardigan, because Gerald does not go to places where locks might be frozen without de-icer.

On November first he handed the key to his stage manager, who had been with him for eight years and who had taken the key with the expression of someone receiving something that is both heavier and lighter than it appears, and who had unlocked the door herself, and who had gone in, and who had not looked back, because she understood that looking back was not what the moment required.

Alistair drove home. He sat in his kitchen. He made tea. He looked out his kitchen window at the street, which is an ordinary Elsinore street, lined with maples that have lost most of their leaves by November first, and which is, on a November morning, quiet and specific and entirely itself.

He sat there for a long time.

The marquee changed on November third.

Gerald noted the date in his personal records, because Gerald notes things, and because a marquee that says THIS ROUGH MAGIC — 2026 SEASON and then, three days later, says nothing — because the new marquee will not go up until the new artistic director is ready to announce the 2027 season — is the kind of change that benefits from being noted.

The period between what the marquee says and what it will say next is, in Elsinore, a specific quality of time. It is the time between the season and the season. It is the time when the theatre is exactly what it is without the confirmation of what it is offering, and when the town is exactly what it is without the confirmation of what the town is for. In November, Elsinore does not stop being Elsinore. It simply waits for the next version of itself, which will be available in spring.

Norman Stalker noted the marquee change in the Beacon in a single paragraph on page four. It said that the Festival’s 2026 season had concluded, that the company had produced twelve productions across the season, and that the incoming artistic director would announce the 2027 season in February.

He did not write an editorial. He had written one in October, when the season was still running, about what it meant to have had Alistair in Elsinore for nineteen years. He had said what needed saying then. The rest was in the cabinet.

The Swan Warden was on the river at five forty-five on November first, as she was every morning, as she had been every morning since 2004. She wrote in her dark green notebook what she saw: the water level, the current, the temperature by observation, the presence of four swans on the south bank and two on the north, the condition of the reed beds, the downstream visibility.

She wrote, at the end of the entry, one additional sentence that she does not usually write at the end of habitat entries. She has not shared what the sentence said. It is in the notebook. The notebook is in the system of notebooks that goes back to 2004, each one filled completely before the next begins, each one dated on the spine in her small precise handwriting.

The record goes back twenty-two years. It is the most complete account in existence of what the Avon does in Elsinore across all seasons and all conditions. She has not offered it to anyone. It is not for offering. It is for knowing.

On November first she wrote her entry and her additional sentence and closed the notebook and walked back along the bank in the early morning light, which is the light of a November morning in Ontario — thin, grey-white, specific — and she went home.

She was back the following morning at five forty-five. As she always was.

Gerald stacked the chairs at the Legion Hall after the final community event of the season, which was a gathering of the Festival volunteers — two hundred and forty of them — at which Alistair spoke for twelve minutes and said the things that needed to be said and did not say the things that did not. Gerald had attended. He had sat in the third row. He had taken notes, because he takes notes at public events, and because these were notes worth taking.

He stacked the chairs after the gathering concluded, because no one had asked him to and because he had noticed they needed doing. He turned off the hall lights. He locked the door. He drove home.

He sat at his kitchen table. Hansard was on the filing system chair. Gerald gave him the chair and made a cup of tea and looked at his notes.

He thought about what Alistair had said in twelve minutes. He thought about the nineteen years behind the twelve minutes. He thought about the first meeting of the Festival volunteers he had attended, which was in 2005, which was the year after he had begun volunteering, which was the year Alistair had been in the position for one year and had run the meeting with the intensity of a man who had one year of experience and forty years of conviction.

He filed the notes.

He went to bed.

Reverend Faye preached on November the eighth, the first Sunday after the season closed, on the text from Ecclesiastes: To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven.

She did not mention the Festival or Alistair or the marquee change. She did not need to. She has a gift, which she has had for eleven years, for finding the parable in the local situation without naming the local situation, and for trusting the congregation to find the local situation inside the parable without being told where to look.

She preached about endings and beginnings and the specific quality of the time between them, which is neither ending nor beginning but the ground of both. She said this was not nothing. She said this was the time in which things that needed to happen before the next thing could happen, happened. She said this was the time in which a community understood what it was without the thing that had been helping it understand.

She did not say how long this time was. She said it was the time it took. She said the text did not specify. She said she found this reassuring.

The congregation filed out into the November morning. Several of them stopped to speak with her at the door. One of them was Constance Hay, who said two words, which Reverend Faye received without visible response and which she has not repeated, because some things said by Constance Hay are for the recipient only.

Jim walked the dogs on November first at six-fifteen. He walked them on November second at six-fifteen, and every morning after that, because the dogs needed walking and he was there.

He had been approved to the Community Advisory mechanism in October. The first meeting was scheduled for January. He had not told many people. He had told Maggie at Sunday dinner. He had passed the bread.

He walked the labrador past Reg’s kitchen window at seven-oh-three. He did not look toward the window. Reg was at the table with his coffee. Reg watched him pass.

He had watched Jim pass every Tuesday since March. He would watch him pass on Tuesdays from now on, which was either a political instinct or a habit, and which was, in Reg’s case, probably both.

Jim went home. He made coffee. He read the Beacon.

He thought about the November morning. He thought about the marquee that now said nothing. He thought about the season that had been there and was now gone.

He walked the next dog.

On the fourteenth of November, I received a letter from Ugyen’s family in Thimphu, confirming the arrangements for our January visit. It arrived in the mail, which Ugyen’s mother prefers to email, on the grounds that a letter is a more considered document and that considered documents are worth the extra time.

I read the letter at the kitchen table on Wellington Street. Outside, the maples were bare. The street was quiet. The Festival Theatre marquee, which I can see from the kitchen window if I look down Ontario Street, said nothing.

I thought about the year the Moonbeam Chronicle had been.

I thought about Gerald in his high-visibility vest in Confederation Park in April, with sixty-two eggs and the placement record and the particular quality of morning light that makes Perth County in spring feel like the world beginning again. I thought about the sandwich board outside the Festival Theatre that said MEASURABLE OUTCOMES: TBD, and about the two swans at the proposed installation site who had not been informed and had not asked. I thought about the field at dawn and Maggie at the clinic bulletin board and the paddle boat at Waypoint 3 and the Swan Warden at five forty-five and the highway sign that said London and the Grand Theatre and Fanshawe Pioneer Village and did not say Elsinore.

I thought about everything the Notes for the Record had noted, every week since April, about the world to the south and what it was doing, and about the specific stubborn unremarkable dignity of Elsinore doing what it had always done, which was to tend its river and watch its swans and hold its meetings and publish its paper and stack its chairs and file its notes and show up, every morning, because the work needed doing and someone was there.

I thought about Ken. I thought about Leacock. I thought about what my father read in the house in London, Ontario, and what he understood about comedy and seriousness being the same thing from different angles, and I thought that the Moonbeam Chronicle had tried to find that angle for the past year, and I was not entirely sure it had found it, and I was sure that not being entirely sure was the correct state for a column written in the tradition of a man who spent his whole career being the person who looked at the place from just far enough away to see it clearly and just close enough to love it accurately.

The Avon is running through Elsinore right now. It is running in the dark, past the Festival, past the dock where the paddle boats went back to Guelph in October, past the bank where the swans will be in April. It is running through Perth County, where a man was up before dawn this morning because the work does not pause for November any more than it pauses for anything else.

The swans are wherever swans go in November. They will be back.

The marquee will say something new in February.

Alistair will be somewhere doing whatever comes next. He has had the magic and he has used it well and he has put it down. He is, for the first time in nineteen years, available to find out what happens when the island is the island without the magic, which is its own kind of knowledge and its own kind of life.

Gerald has copies of everything.

I will write to you from Thimphu.

Something is always, gently, rotten.

The Avon runs through it regardless.

The Moonbeam Chronicle will resume in spring, when the swans return to the south bank and Elsinore becomes, once again, the specific and improbable place it always is.

— Stephen Gordon, Wellington Street, Elsinore, Ontario November 2026
