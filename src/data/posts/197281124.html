By Monday of the trial’s third week, the mathematics of the witness stand had become remarkable. Satya Nadella took the stand in the morning. Ilya Sutskever followed in the afternoon. Bret Taylor concluded the day. The trial has now produced sworn testimony from Elon Musk ($300+ billion net worth), Greg Brockman ($30 billion stake in OpenAI), Satya Nadella (Microsoft CEO), Ilya Sutskever ($7 billion stake, disclosed from the stand), and Bret Taylor (OpenAI board chairman). Sam Altman — expected this week — will be the fifth billionaire to testify in a trial that is nominally about a nonprofit’s founding promise.

The crowd that lined up around the block on Day 1 has thinned to thirty people in the overflow room. The protest balloons have deflated. The inflatable tube man has been stored somewhere. What remains is the trial itself, and on Monday it produced its most analytically significant testimony since Rosie Campbell described two dissolved safety teams.

The Anthropic merger consideration. The 52-page memo. The year of waiting. The “amateur hour” assessment. And a single text message that tells the governance story more concisely than any exhibit so far.

Satya Nadella: The Partner Who Heard Nothing — And Did Something Anyway

In February 2018, the day Elon Musk left OpenAI’s board, Sam Altman sent an email to Satya Nadella. “We are planning to raise a significant amount of capital for a for-profit subsidiary to develop super capable A.I. computers,” Altman wrote. Microsoft invested $1 billion a year later. The for-profit pitch went to Microsoft’s CEO on the same day Musk walked out.

This detail reframes the lawsuit’s central timeline. Musk has argued that OpenAI’s commercial turn was a betrayal he discovered gradually, through three phases of growing alarm. The documentary record shows that Altman was pitching Microsoft on a for-profit subsidiary — in explicit terms — on the day Musk’s departure created the funding gap. Whether the pitch was a betrayal of the nonprofit mission or a consequence of its founder’s departure is the question the jury is being asked to answer.

Nadella testified that even as Microsoft’s investment grew from $1 billion to $13 billion, he never believed the venture violated OpenAI’s original mission. “It has always been my view that the nonprofit approved the creation of the for-profit so that they could pursue the mission.” On whether he ever heard from Musk with concerns: never. “We have each other’s phone numbers.” Not a call, not an email, not a text. In the years between the 2019 investment and the $10 billion round Musk described as his awakening to the charity’s theft — nothing.

Then Musk’s lawyer Steven Molo produced the texts from November 2023.

When the board fired Altman, Nadella was drawn immediately into the effort to restore him. Altman asked Nadella whether he would approve a new board that included former U.S. Treasury Secretary Larry Summers. Nadella’s text reply: “Let me call Larry first.”

Nadella called the board’s decision to remove Altman “amateur hour, as far as I’m concerned.”

The Microsoft CEO who testified he had no control over OpenAI was, in the five days following Altman’s firing, personally vetting board candidates and calling former Treasury secretaries. His “We are below them, above them, around them” podcast statement — which he characterized on the stand as merely technical — takes on a different character alongside a text message in which he personally arranged the post-firing board composition.

Molo also cited emails in which Nadella told Microsoft internally that it should have full rights to OpenAI’s intellectual property. Nadella maintained these statements did not constitute control. The jury will assess whether the distinction between influence and control is as clean as his testimony suggests.

“OpenAI had all the rights and resources they always had,” Nadella said of the partnership. The nonprofit wrapper was a structural feature of the deal. Microsoft is in the business of generating returns. He did not characterize it otherwise.

Ilya Sutskever: The 52-Page Memo and the Anthropic Option

Ilya Sutskever, seen entering an elevator, played a key role in the development of OpenAI.Credit...Jason Henry for The New York Times

Sutskever wore a blue button-down shirt. He is the man who delivered the news to Altman on November 17, 2023 — a video call at noon Pacific time — that the board had voted to fire him. He reversed that vote days later. He left OpenAI afterward. He has not spoken to Altman in over a year. He founded Safe Superintelligence Inc. On Monday, he spoke about all of it.

His stake in OpenAI, disclosed from the stand: approximately $7 billion — the second billionaire revealed by the trial’s testimony, after Brockman’s $30 billion.

On why he voted to fire Altman: he had been thinking about proposing Altman’s removal for at least one year before November 2023. He witnessed Altman manipulate high-ranking executives and provide conflicting information. Altman would tell people what they wanted to hear — “manipulative and chameleon-like in the relentless pursuit of his own agenda.” Altman’s conduct was “not conducive to any grand goal,” including the pursuit of beneficial AGI.

One or all three independent board members asked Sutskever to prepare documentation of executives’ concerns about Altman. He compiled screenshots and materials. He sent a 52-page memo to board members Adam D’Angelo, Helen Toner, and Tasha McCauley. He also sent a separate memo about Greg Brockman.

The number is 52 pages — not 70 as earlier reporting suggested. Both figures tell the same story: the concerns were extensive, documented, and curated at the board’s request by the co-founder who worked most closely with Altman on the technical side.

Then came the disclosure that changes the shape of the trial.

During the five days of chaos following the firing, the board seriously discussed merging OpenAI with Anthropic and having Dario Amodei take over as CEO.

This is the first direct confirmation of something that has circulated as rumor since 2023. The board that voted to remove Altman for governance failures was actively considering whether the right solution was to hand the company to the man who had left OpenAI over governance concerns about Altman. Dario Amodei and six colleagues left OpenAI in 2020 specifically because they did not trust its structure or leadership. Three years later, when the governance structure collapsed in the most public way possible, the board considered asking Amodei to lead the company those failures had produced.

It did not happen. Altman was reinstated. But the fact that it was seriously considered — at board level, in those five days — is now in the trial record. The Little Brother was, for a brief window, the proposed solution to the problem that created it.

On why Sutskever reversed his vote: he worried that Altman’s removal might destroy the company. “I worked really hard to create this company and I cared very much about it. I simply cared for it, and I didn’t want it to be destroyed.” The man who voted to fire Altman reversed course not because the governance concerns were wrong, but because the governance mechanism had produced a crisis that threatened the institution it was supposed to protect.

On Musk’s promises: Sutskever said he never made a promise to Musk that OpenAI would remain a nonprofit, and was not aware of any such promises by Altman or anyone else.

On Musk’s departure prediction: when Musk left OpenAI in 2018, he told Sutskever the company had “a zero percent chance of success” because it wouldn’t be able to pay for compute. “If you don’t have a big enough computer,” Sutskever added, the effort to build AGI “was not going to work.” The man suing because OpenAI raised commercial money told its co-founder, on the day he left, that the company would fail without it.

Bret Taylor: “Dire”

Bret Taylor, OpenAI's board chair. February 23, 2026

Bret Taylor, OpenAI’s board chairman, followed Sutskever on the stand. He described the period when Altman was removed as “dire” and explained OpenAI’s structure to the jury. He did not finish his testimony before proceedings concluded Monday. He returns Tuesday at 8:30 a.m. PT.

Taylor is the board chairman who oversaw the reinstatement. He is also the person who, along with Larry Summers, oversaw the non-investigation that produced an 800-word press release instead of a written report — documented in Part 28 through the Farrow-Marantz investigation. His testimony will address what the board found, what it concluded, and what it decided. What “dire” meant in his framing — dire for the mission, or dire for the commercial trajectory — is the question his cross-examination should answer. Taylor also addressed Musk's $97.4 billion bid to purchase OpenAI's assets in 2024, which he said the board rejected because "we did not feel like it was appropriate for one person to control our mission" — a direct echo of Altman's own testimony about why he refused Musk's control demands in 2017.

What the Lawyers Said

Musk’s lawyer Molo closed the plaintiff’s case with an analogy sharp enough to stick with a jury: “A museum store can’t loot the museum, steal all the Picassos and use them to turn a profit.” It is the trial’s cleanest summation of Musk’s position.

Georgetown Law professor Anupam Chander offered the trial’s most quotable outside assessment. The central awkwardness of Musk’s case, he said, is visible in the documentary record: “This is like the cheating husband complaining about the cheating wife.” Musk wanted to fold OpenAI into Tesla. He wanted 55 percent equity. He proposed the for-profit structure he is now suing over.

On damages, Chander was direct. Musk’s $38 million contribution against Microsoft’s tens of billions means the judge will likely limit what he can actually recover. “I have always been skeptical of a federal judge taking a surgical knife to corporate structure. That seems unlikely.” The case that could restructure the AI industry may produce, in the end, a modest financial settlement.

The Circus and What It Reveals

Mike Isaac, reporting from the courthouse for the New York Times, stepped back from testimony to describe what three weeks of trial have produced as spectacle.

Musk brought a squeezable stress ball. Altman spent the first three days in the gallery’s front row next to Joshua Achiam, OpenAI’s head of AI safety — positioned almost certainly deliberately in a trial about whether OpenAI takes safety seriously. Brockman took notes in red pen on a legal pad; his journals from sixteen years ago are now evidence against him.

Ari Emanuel — Hollywood superagent, Musk confidant — flew in from Los Angeles with a bodyguard carrying a green Harrods bag containing two cream-colored plush pillows. He declined to speak to reporters. The pillows remain unexplained.

Mira Murati, whose video deposition played in court, was in Manhattan for the Met Gala.

What the curtain has revealed over three weeks: a founding dispute whose bitterness was not visible in the founding documents; a governance structure that failed the one time it was tested seriously; a plaintiff whose own emails contradict his central argument; a defendant whose own texts reveal his precarity; a safety infrastructure built, tested, and dissolved within the trial’s living memory; and a Microsoft CEO who called the nonprofit board’s governance action “amateur hour” while personally arranging the post-firing board on text messages that became trial exhibits.

What the Anthropic Disclosure Means for the Series

The board’s consideration of merging with Anthropic and installing Dario Amodei as CEO is not only a trial detail. It is the most direct evidence yet that the Little Brother relationship between OpenAI and Anthropic is not a metaphor but a governance possibility that was actively on the table when OpenAI’s governance crisis peaked.

Anthropic was founded because Amodei and six colleagues did not trust OpenAI’s governance structure or leadership. Three years later, when that governance structure failed most publicly, the board considered asking Amodei to lead the company those failures had produced. The Dresser memo’s claim that Anthropic’s story is “built on fear” takes on a different character when the feared outcome — OpenAI’s governance collapsing — was the specific moment when Anthropic’s leadership was considered as the remedy.

What Remains

Witness testimony closes May 13. Closing arguments May 14. The trial is dark Friday. The advisory jury begins deliberating Monday May 18.

Taylor finishes Tuesday morning. Altman is expected Tuesday afternoon or Wednesday. Everything built over nine days — the founding documents, the 55 percent stake email, the Tesla merger proposal, Brockman’s physical fear, Zilis’s trust game text, the dissolved safety teams, McCauley’s culture of deceit, Sutskever’s 52 pages, the Anthropic merger option, Nadella’s “amateur hour,” the Larry Summers text, Musk’s “zero percent chance” prediction — all of it is preparation for the moment when the man at the centre of it sits down and answers questions under oath.

The jury has been watching for three weeks. The judge decides. The governance gap watches.
