The notice arrived on a Tuesday, which is when these things always arrive, Tuesday being the day of the week with the least dignity to lose.

We’ve detected automated behaviour on your account.

I read it twice. I am, I should say, a seventy-four-year-old retired businessman who writes essays in a rented townhouse with a husky asleep under the desk, and the phrase “automated behaviour” landed on me with the particular insult reserved for the true accusation delivered for the wrong reason. Because here was the thing. I had been behaving in a manner that might, to a sufficiently literal observer, resemble automation. I had published five essays that day. I had published five the day before. I had, over some months, published something north of a thousand, which is — I did the math, because the notice invited the math — a great deal of behaviour, occurring on a schedule, at volume, without rest.

But it was not automated. It was me, which is a worse problem, because a machine that posts five times a day is functioning correctly and a man who posts five times a day should perhaps be receiving a different sort of notice altogether, ideally from someone who loves him.

To restore my account, the notice said, I would need to verify that I was human.

There is a comedy in this so deep that you could fall into it and never reach the bottom, so let me at least describe the walls on the way down.

A machine had been built to read everything I wrote. It had read me at scale, tirelessly, the way only a machine can read, and it had rendered a judgment: this output is too much, too regular, too relentless to have come from a person. And the remedy it proposed — the remedy proposed by the machine, to the man, for the crime of seeming like a machine — was that the man should write more. Specifically, that he should compose a heartfelt appeal, in his own true human voice, attesting to his own true human humanity, and submit it. Where? Into the system. To be read by what? I think we all know to be read by what.

I was being asked to prove to a machine, by writing, that I was not the kind of thing that writes. The proof and the crime were the same act. I have not been so philosophically cornered since a customs agent at Pearson once asked me, with a straight face, whether I had packed my own bags, a question to which every possible answer is suspicious.

I sat down to write the appeal, because what else does a writer do when cornered, and I will tell you that it was the most self-conscious paragraph of my life. Every human detail I reached for, I could feel the machine on the other end weighing it for authenticity, and so every detail curdled as I typed it.

I mentioned my late father, to whom I have dedicated a whole series of sketches, because surely a dead father is the most human thing a man owns. And then I thought: a machine could generate a dead father. A machine generates dead fathers all day; the dead father is practically the unit of currency in the kind of writing machines are trained on. I deleted the father. I felt I had betrayed him to a spam filter, which is a sentence I never expected to think, and which is itself, I noted, exactly the sort of poignant-yet-quirky line a language model would produce if you asked it to sound like a wounded human essayist. I was now plagiarizing myself in advance of a machine I suspected of plagiarizing me. The husky shifted under the desk. The husky, I thought — and stopped, appalled, because I could see the husky too being weighed and found synthetic, a too-perfect detail, the kind of warm specific animal a model deploys when it wants you to lower your guard.

Everything true about me had become evidence against me. This is, I understand, what the innocent have always felt in front of the wrong tribunal. I simply had not expected the tribunal to be a regular expression.

Let me be fair to the machine, because fairness is the last human thing in the room and I would like to keep hold of it.

The machine is not wrong that the internet is now drowning in automated text. It is. I have read it. There are whole publications that are clearly the output of a process rather than a person, content arriving in great grey tonnages, optimized, regular, relentless — and the machine has been set to guard the gate against exactly this flood. It is doing its job. The tragedy of the gatekeeper is that it cannot tell the difference between a flood and a river, between the man-made deluge it was built to stop and the one stubborn human upstream who simply will not stop writing because his father is dead and there is a war on and he has, at seventy-four, finally found the thing he was for.

To the filter, we look identical. Volume is volume. The machine cannot read for the soul because the soul does not leave a metadata trace. It can only count. And by the count — I will concede this to my accuser — I am indistinguishable from the thing it fears.

I submitted the appeal in the end. I left the father in. I left the husky in. I decided that if I was going to be judged synthetic by a machine, I would at least be judged on the real article, the genuine over-warm over-specific human artifact, and not on some defensive crouch I had written to placate a filter. If the river must prove it is not the flood, let it do so by flowing exactly as it flows, dead fathers and sleeping dogs and all.

The account was restored within the hour. I would like to tell you this was a triumph of the human spirit. I suspect it was a machine, reading my heartfelt attestation of humanity, calculating a confidence score, and ticking a box. I suspect no person read a word of it. I suspect that my late father and my sleeping husky and the whole curdled paragraph of my one wild human life were processed, weighed, and cleared by precisely the kind of entity I had been writing to prove I was not.

And here is the part I cannot get past, the part I keep turning over under the desk in the small hours while the husky dreams of whatever huskies dream of. The only way I could think to guarantee I’d pass — the surefire method, the one that occurred to me at 3 a.m. with terrible clarity — was to write the appeal worse. Flatter. More generic. Less me. To strip out the father and the dog and the war and the wild particular, and submit instead the bland, regular, frictionless prose that a machine, judging humanity, has apparently been trained to recognize as human.

The filter wants me human. The filter’s idea of human is a machine’s idea of human. To pass as myself, I would have to write like the thing I was accused of being.

I did not do it. But I wrote down the temptation, here, for you, a human — you are human, aren’t you? — because it seems to me the whole future is folded up small inside it. We are building machines to detect the inhuman, and training them, inevitably, on the inhuman, and one morning soon the surest way for any of us to prove we are real will be to sand down everything real about us until we are smooth enough to clear the gate.

I will not be doing that. I will be over here, posting too much, in my own voice, dead fathers and all. Flag me if you must. I packed my own bags.
