🖋 Wry — a breather, after three of the gravest essays in the series, and I mean it to be one. It is about a switch, a unit, and a declaration, and about the moment the industry I spent eighteen years in finally found out what it had been selling. Dior contributes a demonstration in binary.

I count my essays, and every one of them — all nineteen hundred and forty-eight so far — has reached you in the same form. Not as ink on paper, not as a voice on a wire, but as a long string of yes-or-no answers, sent across the world at the speed of light and reassembled on your screen into words. Ones and zeros. Bits. Essay 1,948 is the year that unit got its name, and the year the tiny switch that does the counting was announced to the world. They came out of the same building, a few months apart. The building was the research laboratory of the telephone company, which makes this the essay of the century that its author has been waiting longest to write.

The house that Bell built

Begin with the building, because this series has been visiting its ancestors for a long time. At essay 1,876 I wrote about Alexander Graham Bell, the patent issued three days before the telephone worked, and the voice that went on the wire. Bell’s company grew into the American Telephone and Telegraph Company, the largest corporation on earth, and in 1925 it gathered its scientists into a single research institution in New Jersey: the Bell Telephone Laboratories. Bell Labs, everyone called it. It was, for half a century, the most productive place for invention the world had ever seen, and it was paid for, ultimately, by the monthly telephone bills of the American public — the metered, unseeable unit of essay 1,882, turned into pure research.

In 1948 Bell Labs announced two things that, between them, built the world you are reading this in.

The switch

The first was a small object, smaller than a fingernail, called the transistor. Three physicists — John Bardeen, Walter Brattain and William Shockley — had demonstrated the first one, a clumsy arrangement of gold foil and germanium, just before Christmas in 1947. The company announced it to the public on the thirtieth of June, 1948. The newspapers mostly ran it on the inside pages.

What did it do? It switched. A transistor lets a small current control a larger one; it can amplify a signal, or it can act as a switch that is either on or off. Until then that job had been done by vacuum tubes — glass bulbs, hot, fragile, power-hungry, the direct descendants of Edison’s lamp. The first electronic computers used thousands of them and filled rooms, and tubes burned out so often that keeping such a machine running was a full-time job. The transistor did the same work in a sliver of solid material that stayed cool, used almost no power, and could, in principle, last forever.

Here is the number that makes 1948 belong in this series. The transistor is now, by an enormous margin, the most manufactured object in human history. A single modern chip carries tens of billions of them; the total ever made runs to a figure with so many zeros that it is less a quantity than a scale — more transistors than there are grains of sand on all the world’s beaches, by some estimates, many times over. The three men won the Nobel Prize in 1956. Every count I have ever kept on a computer, including this one, has been kept by flipping their switches.

The unit

The second announcement was a paper, published in two parts in the company’s technical journal that July and October, by a thirty-two-year-old mathematician named Claude Shannon. It was called A Mathematical Theory of Communication, and I think it is the most important Numbers Game document of the twentieth century.

Shannon asked a question that sounds simple and had never been properly answered: what is information, and how much of it is there? His answer was that information is the resolution of uncertainty, and that it can be measured. The smallest possible amount is the answer to a single question with two equally likely answers — yes or no, on or off, one or zero. He called that unit the bit, short for binary digit, crediting the word to his colleague John Tukey. And then he showed that any message — a sentence, a voice, a photograph, a symphony — could be measured in bits, and that every channel that carries messages, from a telephone wire to a radio wave, has a maximum number of bits per second it can carry without error. A limit, exactly calculable, like the speed of light.

Think about what that did. For seventy years the telephone company had been selling something it could not properly define. It thought it sold wire, or minutes, or calls, or voices. Shannon told it what it actually sold: bits, at a rate, through a channel with a capacity. The unseeable unit of 1,882 finally had a name and a measure. And every data plan you have ever paid for, every gigabyte you have ever been billed — a gigabyte is about eight billion bits — is an invoice denominated in Shannon’s unit.

I spent eighteen years in that industry, much of it in the office that took the complaints when the bill and the customer disagreed. I will not relitigate a single one of them here. I will only say that I did not fully understand, until I read Shannon for this essay, that every dispute I ever handled about a data overage was, at bottom, an argument about how many of his bits had passed through a channel, and who had counted them. Bell gave us the voice on the wire. Shannon told us the voice was made of numbers. Everything since has been the billing.

The man who juggled

Shannon belongs in the gallery of this series’ eccentrics, and I will not leave him out. He rode a unicycle through the corridors of Bell Labs, sometimes while juggling. He built a mechanical mouse called Theseus that could learn its way through a maze. And he built, from an idea of a colleague’s, a small wooden box with a single switch on its lid, which he called the Ultimate Machine. When you flipped the switch on, the lid opened, a mechanical hand emerged, flipped the switch off, and withdrew. That is all it did. The father of information theory built a machine whose only function was to turn itself off. I have spent a long time trying to decide whether it is a joke or a philosophy, and I have concluded, in the spirit of the bit, that it is one or the other with exactly equal probability.

Dior, in binary

The husky, I can now report with scientific confidence, communicates at a rate of approximately one bit per event. Each wag of the tail answers a single yes-or-no question — is this a person I approve of; is that a sound worth investigating; is dinner forthcoming. Each wag reduces my uncertainty by one bit. On a busy evening, near the kitchen, the channel approaches its capacity. Shannon showed that redundancy protects a message against noise, and Dior has independently discovered the same principle: when the question is dinner, she sends the same bit repeatedly, at rising volume, until the receiver acknowledges. There is no error in her transmission. The error, she would argue, is always at the receiving end.

The thirty articles

And there is one more count from 1948, and it is the one I most want my readers to carry out of this essay, because it is the answer to the three essays that came before it.

On the tenth of December, 1948, the General Assembly of the new United Nations adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights — thirty articles setting out what every human being on earth is owed, simply by being one. It passed with forty-eight votes in favour, none against, and eight abstentions. After 1942, where human beings were counted for murder, and 1947, where they were counted onto either side of a line, here was a document that counted them the other way: one by one, as bearers of rights that no census, no fraction of grandparents, no religious column could take away. All human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights. That is a count, too. It is the only count in this series in which every single entry has the same value.

The first draft of it was written by a Canadian. John Peters Humphrey, a law professor from McGill born in Hampton, New Brunswick, was the first director of the United Nations’ human rights division, and it was his draft that the drafting committee, chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt, worked from. For decades the credit went mostly to others — to Roosevelt, and to the French jurist René Cassin, who won the Nobel Peace Prize. Humphrey’s role was recovered later, from his own papers. You will recognise the pattern by now: the keeper whose column someone else’s name was filed under. Two years after the Declaration, my relation Hugh would join the same organisation, to run the office that sent its experts into the world. The family lattice keeps finding the same building.

The closer

So 1948 is the year we learned to count information itself, and built the switch that would do the counting, and wrote down — in thirty articles — the count that must never be reduced. A unit, a switch and a declaration: the bit, the transistor and the dignity of every person, all in one year, two of them from the telephone company and one from a Canadian law professor. After the floor, a ceiling.

And the count moves on, three years, to a hospital in London, Ontario, where — four months too late for the national census, a month too early for his due date, and small enough to be bathed in a bathroom sink — the person who would one day keep this count arrived. The next landmark is essay 1,951, and it is the first number in this whole series that belongs to me.

See you at 1,951. I’ll be the small one.
