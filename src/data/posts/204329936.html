There is a particular Canadian pleasure in watching Americans turn their own birthday into a transaction. We have our own national vanities, God knows — a flag debate that lasted a decade and a half, a tendency to apologize when other people step on our feet — but we have never, to my knowledge, considered marking a sesquicentennial or semiquincentennial by handing out get-out-of-jail-free cards in round numbers. The number itself is the tell. Two hundred and fifty years, two hundred and fifty pardons. It scans nicely on a press release. It scans considerably less nicely as criminal justice policy.

According to reporting in The Atlantic, the White House has been quietly discussing exactly this: a possible Fourth of July announcement of 250 pardons or commutations, timed to the nation’s 250th birthday, under the working description “250 for 250.” The president, as of this writing, has not been formally presented with the proposal. His advisers are split. One adviser apparently believes the polling supports it; others worry it will look like exactly what it is. The Wall Street Journal flagged the number a month earlier. And in the meantime, fourteen people with knowledge of the conversations have described to reporters a process that has stopped pretending to be a process at all.

This is where Part XII of this series has to sit, and I want to be honest with readers about why. I do not yet have a list. I do not have confirmed names, confirmed sentences, confirmed restitution figures erased or preserved. What I have is something arguably more useful for the purposes of this series: a remarkably candid, on-the-record account of how the sausage is being made before a single pardon has been signed. The six criteria this series has applied since David Gentile’s thirteen days in custody for a billion-dollar fraud — connection to the president, nature of the crime, victim impact, process followed, timing, and justification — are not all equally testable right now. But two of them, process and timing, are fully testable. And what they show is not encouraging.

The Process, As Described By the People Running It

Every administration this series has examined — Republican and Democrat alike — has maintained at least the formal architecture of the Department of Justice’s Office of the Pardon Attorney: a body designed to receive applications, investigate claims, and forward recommendations up the chain. It has never been an apolitical process. It has, at various points across four administrations, been a slow and frustrating one, weighted toward the connected and the patient. But it has existed as a process, with a paper trail.

What multiple sources describe to The Atlantic is something different: an “informal network of intermediaries” who advocate for clemency seekers in exchange for a fee, operating largely outside that formal channel. One white-collar defense attorney is quoted putting a number on it directly — that for two million dollars, a client can expect a pardon, and that clients are being told by name which intermediary to retain. Liz Oyer, who held the Pardon Attorney post under the Biden administration and into the early months of this one, is more blunt still, describing what the office has become as something closer to a competition for survival than a system of justice.

I want to be careful here, because this series has never treated “Trump-critical sourcing” as self-validating, and won’t start now. Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt’s on-record response — that the president “finds it detestable that anyone would even attempt to profit off pardons,” and that requests pass through “a rigorous review process involving the Department of Justice and the White House Counsel’s Office” — deserves to be printed in full, and it has been, by the outlet that reported this story. The Justice Department’s own statement is narrower but not nothing: anyone may apply, and the president is “the ultimate decider.” Both statements are true. Neither statement addresses the specific allegation, made independently by multiple attorneys and former officials, that a parallel, fee-based market has formed around the formal one. A spokesperson denying that the White House condones profiteering is not the same thing as a spokesperson denying that profiteering is occurring.

This is, structurally, the same critique this series leveled at the prior administration’s family pardons — not that mercy was extended, but that the criteria for extending it became untethered from the nature of the offense and instead tracked proximity to power, in this case proximity purchased rather than inherited. A nepotism economy and a brokerage economy are cousins, not opposites.

Timing as Message

This series has previously examined timing as one of its six criteria on the theory that when a pardon is announced often tells you more than the text of the warrant itself. End-of-term pardons, signed in the dark on the way out the door, have historically signaled a president’s awareness that the act would not survive daylight. What’s notable about “250 for 250” is the inversion: this is a mass pardon proposed not to be buried, but to be staged — attached deliberately to the most visible patriotic occasion on the American calendar, in the most visible week of a presidency that has never once undervalued spectacle.

One former Trump associate, granted anonymity to discuss the lobbying around the plan, put it to The Atlantic in a way I found genuinely useful for this series’ purposes: that the current push is qualitatively different from the first term because, in his words, everything is now out in the open. Read generously, that is a description of transparency. Read accurately, in the context of two million dollars being a quoted going rate for access, it is a description of a market that no longer believes it needs to disguise itself as anything else.

The administration’s own internal arithmetic, as described by advisers split on the idea, treats the pardons not as individual acts of justice or mercy but as a single political instrument — a way to “shore up support” among a base ahead of the midterms, set against the risk that it reads as corrupt amid an already-weak approval rating. That is not a description of clemency. That is a description of a marketing campaign with sentencing documents as inventory.

The Names Already Circulating — and Why I Am Not Yet Scoring Them

Three names surfaced in the reporting with enough specificity that readers will likely ask why this series isn’t applying its full six-criteria test to them immediately: the fugitive financier Low Taek Jho, wanted in connection with the multibillion-dollar 1MDB fraud; Pras Michel of the Fugees, convicted of conspiring with Low and a Chinese government official in an illegal lobbying scheme; and Nicole Daedone, the OneTaste co-founder sentenced for her role in a forced-labor conspiracy.

I am deliberately not scoring these three against the series’ victim-impact and justification criteria in this installment, for a reason that itself belongs in the record: a White House official, after this story published, stated that none of the three is actually “on the radar” of the pardon team. OneTaste’s spokesperson confirmed the company is aware advocacy is happening on Daedone’s behalf but has received no official contact. Michel’s attorney stated flatly that his team has had zero discussions with the government about a pardon. Low’s representatives did not respond at all.

In other words: we have, at this stage, a list of names that lobbyists want on the list, surfacing through “advocates” with no confirmed traction inside the actual decision chain. Building a full justification analysis on speculative names — particularly a forced-labor case as distinct in character as Daedone’s, or an international fraud case as large as Low’s — risks doing this series’ analytical work on a foundation that may not survive contact with the actual announcement, if there is one. That is the entire argument for the two-part structure I am using here. Part XIII will apply the full test, criterion by criterion, the moment there is an actual warrant to test rather than a rumor to flatter.

What This Installment Is Actually Arguing

Set against this series’ established framework, “250 for 250” fails the process and timing criteria before a single name is confirmed, on the administration’s own described conduct: a formal channel that sources describe as having been substantially supplanted by a fee-based informal one; a number chosen for its commemorative arithmetic rather than its relationship to any coherent sentencing-reform theory; and an internal White House debate that weighs the idea not on the merits of the individual cases but on its midterm utility.

It is worth remembering, in fairness to the office itself, that the pardon power was written into the Constitution as a check against the rigidity of the courts — a deliberate grant of mercy outside the system, precisely because the founders anticipated that sometimes justice and law would diverge. That is not a partisan observation; this series made the same argument in defense of clemency used narrowly and well. The problem with “250 for 250” is not that it invokes mercy. It is that it has, by every account given so far by the people closest to it, organized itself around access rather than mercy — and dressed the result up in red, white, and blue for the cameras.

Part XIII will follow once there is a list to hold against the light. Until then, the country’s 250th birthday appears set to include, alongside the fireworks, an auction — conducted, as one attorney told The Atlantic with evident exhaustion after thirty years of practicing law, unlike anything he has seen before.
