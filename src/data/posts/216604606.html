by Stephen Gordon

Two essays ago we watched two revolutions open in the same year and run in opposite directions — one settling into a constitution, one spiralling toward the guillotine. I called them opposite ledgers.

A third revolution began two years later, in the richest place in the world, and it is the one the sequence usually leaves out. Nineteen seventy-six, seventeen eighty-nine, and then a gap where seventeen ninety-one should be.

I had intended to write that gap up as an omission — the revolution the ledger refused to count. I was wrong, and the correction is the essay.

They were counted. They were counted with more care, in more detail, and at shorter intervals than almost any population on earth at that time. The colony knew exactly how many of them there were, what each was worth, how fast they died and how many had to be landed each year to replace the ones who did.

They were never missing from the books. They were in the wrong column, and the wrong column is not an oversight. It is a decision, taken deliberately, and defended afterwards at gunpoint.

The richest place in the world

Saint-Domingue, the western third of Hispaniola, was the single most profitable colony in the eighteenth-century world. It produced something like forty per cent of the sugar and sixty per cent of the coffee reaching Europe. It returned more revenue to France than all thirteen North American colonies returned to Britain. Bordeaux, Nantes, and a great deal of Paris were built out of it.

Here is the population in 1789, and I want the three figures side by side because the shape of them is the whole argument.

Roughly 30,000 whites. Roughly 28,000 free people of colour — many educated, many prosperous, some of them slaveholders themselves, and most of the colonial militia. And roughly 465,000 to 500,000 enslaved people, who were nine-tenths of everybody standing on the island.

Now the figure that explains the other three.

Across the life of the colony, France landed close to 800,000 captive Africans at Saint-Domingue — nearly double the number carried to all of North America across the entire history of that trade.

Eight hundred thousand brought in. Five hundred thousand alive at the end.

That gap is not a rounding error and it is not emigration. The enslaved population of Saint-Domingue never once reproduced itself. It could not: the work killed people faster than children were born. By the 1780s the colony was importing something on the order of forty thousand human beings a year purely to stay level, and the estimated working life of a person born into that system was little more than fifteen years.

This is what I mean about the column. The planters had that arithmetic exactly. They knew the attrition rate because they budgeted for it. Replacement was not a scandal discovered later by historians; it was an operating cost, forecast annually, the way a shipping firm forecasts losses at sea.

A society that counts human beings as depreciating stock is not a society that failed to notice them. It is a society that noticed them with great precision and filed them under equipment.

August

On a night in mid-to-late August 1791, in a wooded place in the northern mountains called Bois Caïman, a few hundred enslaved people from something over a hundred plantations met in secret. A man named Dutty Boukman, a driver and a Vodou priest, spoke; a woman sacrificed a pig; those present swore an oath.

The date is given as 14 August in some accounts and 21 August in others, and the details of the ceremony come to us mostly through hostile sources and later tradition. I flag that honestly, as this series always does. What is not disputed is what happened next.

On the night of 22–23 August 1791, the northern plain went up. Within weeks the richest agricultural district on the planet was burning.

What followed took thirteen years and cannot be compressed honestly, so I will not pretend to compress it — only mark the turns. Toussaint Louverture, freed decades earlier, joined the rising and within a few years commanded an army. In 1793 the French commissioner Sonthonax abolished slavery in the colony; in 1794 the Convention in Paris abolished it across the empire. For a moment the arithmetic of 1789 and the arithmetic of 1791 agreed with each other.

The moment did not survive Napoleon.

In 1802 he sent his brother-in-law Leclerc with an expedition that began at around thirty-one thousand men and, with reinforcements, eventually ran to something near eighty thousand — the largest force France had ever sent across an ocean. Its object was to depose Toussaint and restore slavery. It took Toussaint by treachery and shipped him to a cold fortress in the Jura, where he died on 7 April 1803, alone, in a cell he had not left since arriving.

And then the expedition died. Fifty thousand or more French dead, the great majority of them killed by yellow fever rather than by any army, Leclerc himself among them in November 1802. The survivors were beaten in the field at Vertières on 18 November 1803 by Dessalines.

On 1 January 1804, the colony declared itself Haiti — the first state to be created by the people it had been built on top of, and the second republic in the hemisphere.

The bill nobody thinks to send them

Before the invoice, one entry on the other side of the ledger, because the beneficiaries of 1791 have never once acknowledged it.

Napoleon wanted Saint-Domingue because it was the engine of a French empire in the Americas; Louisiana was to be its granary. When the engine was destroyed, the granary became pointless and expensive to defend, and he sold it. In 1803 the United States bought the Louisiana Territory for fifteen million dollars and doubled in size overnight.

The United States owes the western half of itself, at a discount, to an army of formerly enslaved people who defeated Napoleon in a place Americans have never been taught to think about.

It then declined to recognise that they existed. Not for a year, not for a decade — until 1862, fifty-eight years after Haitian independence, and only once the Union was fighting a war over the same question.

The invoice

Now the number this essay was written for.

On 17 April 1825, Charles X of France signed an ordinance. That July, fourteen French warships carrying more than five hundred guns anchored off Port-au-Prince, with the king’s envoy aboard. The terms were not negotiable: France would recognise Haitian independence in exchange for 150 million gold francs, paid in five instalments, to compensate the former colonists for their losses.

Ten times what the United States had paid for Louisiana. More than ten times Haiti’s entire annual budget. On 11 July, with the guns in the harbour, President Boyer signed.

I want to be exact about what that sum was, because the exactness is the horror.

It was not a fine, and not a tribute, and not a war reparation of the ordinary kind. It was a compensation payment for lost property, and the property was the people being asked to pay. The valuation was worked from the framework of the Code Noir, the legal code that classified enslaved human beings as assets attached to an estate. So the bill was computed by pricing the bodies of the people who received it, and then handed to those same bodies, with a fleet to make sure it was read.

The winners were required to indemnify the losers for the value of the winners.

And they paid. The instalments were impossible, so Haiti borrowed from French banks to pay France, at interest, and then borrowed to service the borrowing. The principal was cut to ninety million in 1838, which changed less than it sounds. In 1911 the United States took a hand in the arrangements; by 1922 the remaining obligation had been moved to American investors, and the final payment on the whole apparatus was made to a New York bank in 1947.

A hundred and twenty-two years. Six generations of Haitians were born, worked, and died inside a payment schedule for a debt incurred by their great-grandparents for the offence of having stopped being merchandise.

What it cost is the subject of serious estimation rather than rhetoric. Around 112 million francs was actually transferred. The New York Times investigation of 2022 put the loss to Haiti’s economy over two centuries — the growth that never happened because the money left — somewhere between twenty-one and a hundred and fifteen billion dollars.

I will not dress that range up. A range that wide is itself a finding: it means the damage is large enough that reasonable people using reasonable methods land an order of magnitude apart, and every figure in it is bigger than the country.

In 2016 the French parliament formally annulled the 1825 ordinance, a gesture costing nothing. A French president has called the money the ransom of independence. The debt has not been returned.

The honest ledger

This series does not sanctify the people it defends, so:

Haiti’s own accounts have entries that will not be flattered. Dessalines ordered the massacre of the remaining French population in 1804. The colour and caste divisions of the colony survived into the republic and shaped its politics for a century. Boyer, who signed the indemnity, governed as an autocrat and imposed his own forced-labour code on the countryside.

None of that lightens the invoice by a franc. It simply means I am describing human beings who won something extraordinary and then governed like human beings, under a blockade, in an economy whose entire purpose had been to enrich somebody else, while paying instalments to the nation they had beaten.

The entry is: they were owed, and they were billed instead. Both things can be true, and only one of them was ever collected.

What the column costs

The lesson of 1791 is not that people can be left out of the count. It is the harder one, and it has been building in this series since Magna Carta.

Being counted is not protection. What protects you is which column you are counted in.

Saint-Domingue’s enslaved people were measured, valued, inventoried, insured, depreciated and replaced on schedule. Every one of those is an act of counting, performed diligently. The system did not need to ignore them; ignoring them would have cost money. It needed only to keep them on the correct side of a line drawn in a ledger — and then, when they crossed the line themselves, at a cost of thirteen years and a great many lives, to send a fleet to insist that they pay the difference.

Seventy-four essays from now, the American republic will finally retire a fraction it had used to do a version of the same arithmetic on its own people: three-fifths of a person, entered in a constitution, for the purpose of counting them without granting them anything. That number will be struck out at the cost of a war.

The fraction gets struck. The invoice gets paid. It is worth knowing which of those two the world found easier to arrange.

The closer

Haiti declared itself in 1804, was recognised by the United States in 1862, and finished paying for the privilege in 1947.

Twenty-one essays from here we come to a war in which the border did not move an inch, and three separate nations each went home and wrote themselves into the victory column — while a fourth party, who had been fighting for the only thing actually at stake, lost everything and appears in nobody’s scoreline at all.

We have just watched a people win outright and be billed for it.

Next we watch nobody win at all, and three of the four parties claim it anyway.

See you at 1812.
