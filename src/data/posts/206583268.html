There’s a particular kind of survival that doesn’t look like survival while it’s happening. It looks like submission. It looks like tribute paid, taxes collected, governors installed from a foreign capital, a foreign script imposed on the record-keeping of a people who had their own. For roughly a thousand years — from 111 BCE, when the armies of Han China absorbed the kingdom of Nam Viet, until 938 CE, when a general named Ngo Quyen finally broke that grip on the Bach Dang River — Vietnam existed as a Chinese province in every administrative sense. And yet it did not become Chinese.

This is the fact that has to sit at the front of any honest history of Vietnam, because everything that follows — the resistance to France, the refusal to be absorbed by either Cold War bloc, the current tightrope walk between Washington and Beijing — is a variation on a theme that was already a thousand years old before a single French gunboat arrived on the Red River. I’ve spent this year building out histories of North Korea and India, two countries whose relationships with larger neighbours are defined in very different registers — Pyongyang’s paranoid isolation, Delhi’s colonial rupture and reinvention. Vietnam offers something different again: a country that spent a millennium inside the administrative body of an empire and came out the other side more itself, not less. That’s a strange kind of story, and it’s worth taking slowly.

The Shape of the Occupation

Before Han China arrived, the Red River Delta was home to the Lac Viet, an agrarian, bronze-working culture organized loosely under a dynasty the later chronicles call the Hung kings — the semi-legendary rulers of a kingdom named Van Lang. The archaeological record here is Dong Son culture: elaborate bronze drums, wet-rice agriculture in the Delta, a society with enough surplus and organization to support specialized craftsmen and a warrior class. Whether Van Lang was a unified state or a looser confederation of chiefdoms is disputed among historians, but there’s no serious dispute that something recognizably proto-Vietnamese existed in the Delta before any Chinese administrator set foot there.

The bronze drums themselves are worth lingering on for a moment, since they’re among the most distinctive material artifacts to survive from this pre-Chinese period and they’ve become genuinely important symbols of Vietnamese cultural continuity in the modern era. Dong Son drums — large, elaborately cast bronze cylinders decorated with geometric patterns, scenes of boats, houses on stilts, and figures in feathered headdresses performing what appear to be ritual dances — have been found not just across northern Vietnam but distributed widely through Southeast Asia, suggesting a Dong Son cultural and possibly trade sphere considerably larger than the Red River Delta alone. Vietnamese archaeologists and nationalist historians alike have long pointed to these drums as evidence of a sophisticated, technologically accomplished civilization that predated any Chinese administrative presence — a materially verifiable rebuttal, of sorts, to any suggestion that Vietnamese civilization was fundamentally a Chinese import. Whatever one makes of the more legendary aspects of the Hung king tradition as recorded in later chronicles, the Dong Son bronze-casting tradition is real, well-documented archaeologically, and considerably older than the Han conquest that would follow it by several centuries.

The conquest, when it came, arrived in stages. The Qin dynasty pushed briefly into the region in the third century BCE. After Qin’s collapse, a renegade Chinese general named Zhao Tuo carved out an independent kingdom he called Nam Viet, straddling what is now southern China and northern Vietnam — and here’s an irony worth sitting with: modern Vietnamese nationalism sometimes claims Nam Viet as an early Vietnamese state, even though it was founded and ruled by an ethnically Chinese warlord who simply wasn’t loyal to the Han court. History rarely cooperates with tidy narratives of “us” and “them,” and Nam Viet is an early example of how thoroughly entangled these two civilizations would become. It was Han China’s absorption of Nam Viet in 111 BCE — not some earlier, cleaner moment — that began the thousand-year period of direct Chinese administrative rule the Vietnamese call Bắc thuộc, “Northern Domination.”

What Chinese rule actually consisted of evolved considerably over ten centuries — this wasn’t one static occupation but a rolling sequence of dynasties (Han, then the fractured periods of the Six Dynasties, then Sui, then Tang) each imposing its own administrative logic. The broad pattern, though, held steady enough to describe. The Delta was divided into commanderies governed by Chinese-appointed officials. Chinese became the language of law, of the civil examination system where one existed, and of elite scholarship. Confucian norms of hierarchy, filial piety, and bureaucratic merit were exported as the correct framework for organizing society. Vietnamese elites who wanted access to power had to operate, at least partially, within a Chinese idiom — reading Chinese classics, adopting Chinese administrative titles, in some cases traveling to serve at the Chinese court itself.

It’s worth noting that the degree of direct administrative control Chinese authorities exercised waxed and waned considerably depending on the strength of whichever dynasty currently held the imperial throne. During periods of Chinese dynastic weakness or transition — and there were several across the thousand years of Bắc thuộc, since Chinese history in this era was itself punctuated by dynastic collapse, civil war, and periods of north-south division — administrative control over the distant southern periphery that was the Red River Delta tended to loosen considerably, with local Vietnamese elites gaining greater practical autonomy even while nominally still operating under Chinese authority. This pattern of tightening and loosening control, tracking the rise and fall of whichever dynasty currently held the Chinese throne, is itself instructive: it suggests that Vietnamese autonomy under Bắc thuộc was less a fixed condition than a variable one, responsive to conditions in a distant imperial capital that Vietnamese local elites had little ability to influence directly but every incentive to watch closely.

This is the part of the story where a simple “occupier versus occupied” framework starts to strain. Some Vietnamese aristocratic families genuinely assimilated, intermarrying with Chinese administrators and settlers, participating in Chinese scholarly culture, and — by at least some accounts — thinking of themselves as participants in a shared civilizational project rather than a colonized people yearning to breathe free. The historian Keith Taylor, whose work has done more than perhaps anyone else’s to complicate the standard nationalist narrative of this period, has argued that framing a thousand years of Bắc thuộc as one continuous story of resistance imposes a modern nationalist logic onto people who didn’t necessarily experience their own lives that way. For long stretches, there was no unified “Vietnam” resisting anything — there were local elites, some of whom collaborated, some of whom rebelled, most of whom were simply living inside the administrative reality they’d been born into, the way people generally do.

I want to flag this scholarly tension honestly rather than smooth it over, because I think readers of this series deserve to know when they’re getting settled history and when they’re getting a contested, politically freighted narrative. The version of this period that emphasizes continuous Vietnamese resistance to Chinese rule is, in significant part, a retrospective construction — assembled and emphasized by later Vietnamese court historians, and then again by twentieth-century nationalists (both the anti-colonial movement against France and the later Communist Party) who needed a founding story of a people who had always resisted foreign domination and always would. That doesn’t make the story false. Ngo Quyen’s victory in 938 genuinely happened, and it genuinely did end direct Chinese administrative control for what would become nearly a millennium of (mostly) independent Vietnamese dynasties. But the meaning assigned to that victory — as the culmination of a thousand years of unbroken national resistance rather than one military success among many shifting political currents — is itself a historical artifact, built after the fact, serving the needs of later generations.

What Survived Underneath

Whatever the truth of elite-level assimilation, something did persist beneath the administrative overlay, and it’s worth being specific about what. Vietnamese remained a spoken language distinct from Chinese throughout the entire period of Bắc thuộc, even as it absorbed an enormous stock of Chinese vocabulary — modern Vietnamese still carries a Sino-Vietnamese vocabulary layer comparable in scale to the Latin and Greek layer in English. Vietnamese speakers used Chinese characters, chữ Hán, for writing, because there was no alternative — the Vietnamese-specific script, chữ Nôm, wouldn’t develop until centuries after independence, and the Latin-based Quốc Ngữ alphabet Vietnam uses today is a much later product of French missionary linguistics, which I’ll get to when this series reaches the colonial period.

Below the linguistic question, village-level social organization retained enormous autonomy. The làng — the Vietnamese village — functioned as a largely self-governing unit even under Chinese administrative rule, with its own customary law, its own communal land arrangements, and its own local council of notables managing day-to-day affairs with minimal interference from provincial-level Chinese officials, who were generally more interested in extracting tax revenue and maintaining order than in micromanaging peasant life. This pattern — a strong, autonomous village cell operating underneath whatever larger political structure claimed sovereignty over it — would persist through Chinese rule, through the independent Vietnamese dynasties that followed, through French colonialism, and arguably into the twentieth century. It’s one of the most durable features of Vietnamese social organization across two thousand years of documented history, regardless of who technically ruled from the top.

Religious and cultic practice tells a similar story of persistence-with-absorption. Ancestor worship, wet-rice agricultural ritual calendars, and local spirit cults continued alongside — and eventually blended with — imported Chinese Confucian, Taoist, and Buddhist influences, producing the syncretic religious landscape that characterizes Vietnam to this day, where a single household altar might honour ancestors, a bodhisattva, and a local tutelary spirit without any sense of contradiction.

Toward Bach Dang

None of this is to say Chinese rule went uncontested. There were real rebellions during the thousand years of Bắc thuộc, some of them significant enough to force temporary Chinese withdrawal or major concessions — the rebellion of the Trung sisters in 40 CE being the most famous, and one I’m dedicating a full essay to later in this series rather than compressing it here, because it deserves the space. There were other, less mythologized uprisings across the centuries, most eventually suppressed, some achieving temporary autonomy before Chinese authority reasserted itself.

What finally broke the pattern for good was less a single act of nationalist defiance than a moment of Chinese imperial weakness that Vietnamese regional leaders were positioned to exploit. By the early tenth century, the Tang dynasty — which had governed the Vietnamese region as the Protectorate of Annam — was collapsing into the chaos of China’s Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period. Central authority from the north was fraying badly enough that local strongmen across the old Tang empire’s periphery were carving out independent or semi-independent domains, and the Red River Delta was no exception. A local leader named Duong Dinh Nghe seized effective control of the region in 931. When he was murdered by a rival in 937, his son-in-law — a military commander named Ngo Quyen — moved to avenge him, consolidate power, and, critically, confront a Chinese fleet from the southern Han kingdom that was moving to reassert control over the Delta in the resulting instability.

That confrontation, in 938 on the Bach Dang River, is where this series’ next installment will begin — and it’s worth pausing here rather than rushing into it, because the battle itself deserves the room a single paragraph can’t give it: the iron-tipped stakes driven into the riverbed, the tidal trap, the way Vietnamese command of local geography turned a numerically superior invading fleet into a catastrophe. It is, quite literally, the founding battle of independent Vietnam, and it established a template — river warfare, tidal knowledge, asymmetric advantage exploited against superior numbers — that would recur across two more millennia of Vietnamese military history against opponents ranging from the Mongols to the French to the Americans.

Why This Matters Before the Story Even Starts

I raise the historiographical debate about continuity and resistance not as academic throat-clearing but because it bears directly on how the rest of this series should be read. Whether or not a unified Vietnamese national consciousness genuinely existed and resisted Chinese rule continuously across a thousand years, the story that it did became, by the time of French colonization and again by the time of the American war, a load-bearing element of Vietnamese political identity and strategic behaviour. A people — or at least a political leadership — that has internalized the belief that it has always outlasted larger empires through patience, local knowledge, and willingness to absorb catastrophic cost, will fight differently than one without that belief. Ho Chi Minh invoked the Trung sisters and Ngo Quyen explicitly in rallying resistance against France. North Vietnamese leadership during the war against the United States drew on the same well of national memory, explicitly framing American intervention as merely the latest in a long line of foreign incursions destined to fail against Vietnamese persistence.

So before this series gets to French gunboats on the Red River, before Dien Bien Phu, before Tet and the Pentagon Papers and Agent Orange and the extraordinary economic reinvention that followed reunification — it’s worth sitting for a moment with the possibility that Vietnam’s defining national trait was assembled, in part, out of a myth about a thousand years under someone else’s sky. Myths built from real events are still myths, doing real work. This one has outlasted every empire it was built to explain.
