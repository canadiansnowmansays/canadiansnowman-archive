I promised in the introduction that if I was going to ask hard questions of others in this series, I would start at home. So before I turn south and spend an essay on the most powerful man on the continent and his beautiful clean coal, let me spend one on my own country, and on the quiet death of the policy that was supposed to be our answer to all of this.

The Canadian consumer carbon tax died on April 1, 2025. It was six years old. And on the very same day, in a grim piece of symmetry that almost no one outside British Columbia noticed, the original — the grandfather of all North American carbon taxes — died with it.

Let me tell you about that grandfather first, because you cannot understand the tragedy of the death without understanding the life. In 2008, British Columbia did something genuinely radical: it implemented the first comprehensive, broad-based carbon tax in North America. And here is the detail that should stop you, given everything that has happened since — it was brought in not by some coalition of Greens and New Democrats, but by a centre-right government, the BC Liberals, under a premier named Gordon Campbell who had previously cut environmental budgets and supported offshore oil exploration. Campbell staked his political career on it. The tax was deliberately designed to be revenue-neutral: every dollar it raised came back to British Columbians as cuts to personal and corporate income taxes. It started low, at ten dollars a tonne, and rose by five dollars a year. The business community, remarkably, had asked for exactly this — something simple, economy-wide, and revenue-neutral, in preference to the regulatory alternatives.

And it worked. This is the part that matters, the part that turns the later abandonment from a mere policy reversal into something closer to a betrayal of evidence. Study after study — from the University of Ottawa, from Duke, from peer-reviewed journals — found that BC’s carbon tax reduced the province’s greenhouse gas emissions by somewhere between five and fifteen per cent, with, in the careful language of the economists, “negligible effects on aggregate economic performance.” Per capita fuel consumption fell sharply in BC even as it rose in the rest of Canada. The province’s economy kept pace with the national average and in some years outpaced it. One study found the tax was associated with a small but statistically significant increase in employment. And — this is the detail I find most poignant — public support, which started out hostile, grew over time. British Columbians came to live with the tax, to understand it, even to defend it. The international policy community came to regard it as a textbook case, “perhaps the closest example of an economist’s textbook prescription for the use of a carbon tax,” studied and copied from Washington State to Europe.

So when people tell you a carbon tax cannot work — that it must wreck the economy, that voters will never tolerate it, that it is a beautiful theory broken on the hard rock of reality — the correct answer is that we ran the experiment, right here in Canada, for seventeen years, and it worked. It reduced emissions. It did not wreck the economy. The public came around. We had the proof. We had it longer and cleaner than almost anyone on Earth.

And then we killed it anyway. That is the thing to hold in your mind as I turn to the manner of the death.

The manner of its death is what I want to dwell on, because it is a small masterpiece of the TACO method I laid out in the last essay, executed not by a blustering populist but by precisely the sort of person you would least expect to perform it. The federal carbon price was killed by Mark Carney, on his first day as prime minister, with the very first stroke of his pen in office. Before the cabinet photographs had dried, before he had done a single other thing, he signed the directive that set the consumer fuel charge to zero effective April 1 — the very date it had been scheduled to rise. By late May his government had moved to purge the thing from the law entirely, retroactively, so thoroughly that future historians will have to work to prove it was ever there. And BC, its provincial tax now pointless without the federal framework around it, repealed its own pioneering levy the same day, citing the federal retreat. The grandfather and the grandchild went into the ground together.

Now, consider who did this. Mark Carney is not a man who wandered into the climate question by accident. He ran the Bank of Canada and then the Bank of England. He served as the United Nations Special Envoy on Climate Action and Finance. He wrote a book — an actual, lengthy book — substantially concerned with the proposition that markets must price in environmental risk or court catastrophe. If there is a single Canadian who has spent more of his career arguing, in boardrooms and lecture halls and at Davos, that putting a price on carbon is the economically literate response to climate change, I cannot name him. The carbon tax was, intellectually, his policy as much as anyone’s.

And he killed it first. Not reluctantly, not after a fight, not as a concession wrung out of him by Parliament — but immediately, as his opening move, as the thing he most wanted Canadians to see him do. That is the part worth sitting with. This was not a climate skeptic defeating a climate policy. This was the most credentialed climate-finance figure in the country deciding that the most economically sound climate instrument available — the one his own country had pioneered and proven — was politically radioactive, and choosing, in his first hour of power, the politics over the economics he had spent a career defending.

Why? Because the policy had, in his own campaign’s words, become “too divisive.” And it had. For more than two years, Pierre Poilievre’s Conservatives had hammered the carbon tax with a slogan of genuine brilliance — “axe the tax” — that compressed a complicated policy into three syllables of grievance and rode it nearly to the doorstep of government. The tax had become a symbol of the cost of living, the thing you could point to at the pump, the visible federal hand in your wallet during years when the wallet was already thin. Never mind that the federal system was revenue-neutral, that the rebates were designed to leave most lower-income households ahead, that the thing you saw at the pump was largely returned to you in a cheque you did not connect to the pump. The rebate was abstract and quarterly; the price was concrete and every time you filled the tank. Grievance beats arithmetic, and grievance won.

There is a deep and uncomfortable lesson in the psychology here, and it is worth a moment because it explains far more than this one tax. The carbon price failed politically not because it failed economically but because of how human beings perceive losses and gains. The cost was salient, recurring, and attributed: you saw it every time you filled up, and you knew exactly whom to blame. The rebate was invisible, occasional, and unattributed: a deposit in your account four times a year that you did not associate with carbon at all, and that the opposition was at pains to make sure you never connected. Even when the rebate left you financially ahead — as it did for most households — you felt the loss and not the gain, because that is how the human animal is wired. BC had partly solved this by folding the offset into income tax cuts that became simply part of the fiscal landscape; the federal version, by making the rebate a separate visible cheque, accidentally created the worst of both worlds, a tax you noticed and a refund you didn’t. A policy can be economically sound and psychologically doomed at the same time, and ours was both.

Here is the cruelest structural detail, the one that turns this from a political story into a tragedy of the specifically modern kind. The consumer carbon price — the one that was killed, the one everyone hated, the one Poilievre built a near-premiership on — was never the part that did the heavy lifting. A 2024 report from the Canadian Climate Institute found that consumer carbon pricing cut far fewer emissions than the price applied to large industrial emitters. The industrial system accounted for roughly eighty per cent of the total emissions reductions from carbon pricing. The consumer tax, the lightning rod, the slogan-magnet, the thing that consumed two years of national political oxygen and ended a policy and arguably helped end a government, was doing about a fifth of the actual climate work.

So we fought a bitter, nation-defining political war over the visible, less effective half of the policy, and the invisible, more effective half survived precisely because no one could see it to be angry at it. There is a lesson in there about democracy and climate that I find genuinely bleak: the part of the policy you could feel was the part that didn’t matter much, and the part that mattered survived only by being too boring to hate. Effective climate policy, it seems, must either be hidden from the voters or be killed by them. That is not a flaw in this particular tax. That may be the central structural problem of the whole enterprise, and we will come back to it in the politics movement at the end of this series.

I should be fair to Carney, because fairness is the whole point of starting at home. He did not abolish the industrial price — he kept it, and has promised to strengthen it, and has floated a carbon border adjustment to stop dirty production from simply fleeing to dirtier jurisdictions. These are defensible, even sophisticated, positions. He may well believe, with some justification, that he sacrificed the weaker instrument to save the stronger one and to save a government that would otherwise have been replaced by one hostile to carbon pricing altogether. That is a real argument. Poilievre, after all, has pledged to axe the industrial price too, and has notably declined to say what he would replace it with beyond the magic words “technology, not taxes” — which, you will notice, is the precise Canadian dialect of the same incantation I am about to spend an entire essay dismantling in its American form. The choice between a prime minister who kept eighty per cent of the policy and an opposition leader who would scrap the rest is not a hard one, if those are the only two options on the menu.

But notice what has happened to the menu. We have arrived at a Canadian politics in which the best available outcome on climate is the quiet preservation of an industrial carbon price that no one is allowed to mention too loudly, achieved by sacrificing the consumer price on the altar of the cost of living, performed by the one man most qualified to know exactly what was being sacrificed. And just this spring, that same government suspended the federal fuel excise tax on gasoline and diesel through the summer — more relief at the pump, more accommodation, the TACO cycle turning over once again, now under the banner of energy affordability and global market disruption. The threatening stage feels very far away. The capitulating stage has become the permanent weather.

This is what I meant, in the introduction, about there being no hill for Canadians to stand on and tut. We did not lose our nerve on climate because we are villains. We lost it because the policy that worked was invisible and the policy that was visible was hated, and a democracy under cost-of-living pressure will reliably kill the thing it can see. We invented the instrument. We proved it worked. We watched the public slowly come to accept it. And then we threw it away anyway, twice over on the same April morning, federal and provincial together. The houses were built for winter. So, it turns out, was the carbon tax — a sensible instrument for a political climate that no longer exists, dismantled by the very architect who understood it best, because the weather changed and the instrument could not.

Next, south of the border, to a man who has never in his life been talked out of anything by arithmetic, and his insistence that the road forward runs straight down a coal mine.

Degrees

17 — the number of years British Columbia’s carbon tax operated, from 2008 to 2025, reducing emissions by an estimated five to fifteen per cent with negligible economic harm, before being repealed on the same April day the federal consumer price was set to zero. We did not abandon the carbon tax because it failed. We abandoned it after seventeen years of it succeeding, which is a stranger and sadder thing.
