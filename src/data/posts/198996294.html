On the morning of April 7, 2025, the United States government asked the Supreme Court to vacate Judge Xinis’s order requiring Abrego Garcia’s return. Chief Justice Roberts issued an administrative stay and referred the application to the full court.

Three days later, on April 10, the court issued its ruling. It was unanimous. No dissents. Not one. In the full text of the opinion in Noem v. Abrego Garcia, the court held that Judge Xinis’s directive “properly requires the Government to ‘facilitate’ Abrego Garcia’s release from custody in El Salvador and to ensure that his case is handled as it would have been had he not been improperly sent to El Salvador.” The court asked for clarification on the word “effectuate,” noting it might exceed the district court’s authority, and acknowledged the deference owed to the executive branch in foreign affairs. But the substance was unambiguous: the government had to bring him back.

It is worth understanding what a unanimous Supreme Court ruling means in this political climate. This is a court that has fractured along ideological lines on virtually every contested question of the past decade — abortion, guns, the administrative state, presidential immunity. The justices appointed by Donald Trump voted with the justices appointed by Barack Obama and Joe Biden. All nine agreed that the government had acted illegally, and all nine agreed that the lower court was right to order a remedy. As the Defender Services Office noted in its analysis of the decision, even the most conservative members of the court could not find a basis on which to support the administration’s position.

Justice Sotomayor, joined by Kagan and Jackson, issued a separate concurrence that contained language as close to alarm as Supreme Court justices typically permit themselves. She noted that the government’s argument — that U.S. courts lost jurisdiction once a deportee crossed the border — implied that the government “could deport and incarcerate any person, including U.S. citizens,” with no judicial remedy. The argument, she wrote, was “plainly wrong.” She cited Boumediene v. Bush, the Guantanamo habeas case, and Rumsfeld v. Padilla — cases that established the principle that the Constitution’s protections do not evaporate at the water’s edge. The government’s position, if accepted, would have meant that any president could deport any person — citizen or not — to any country, imprison them there, and then argue that the courts had no power to intervene. The implications were not theoretical. They were existential.

As Glenn Kirschner, the former federal prosecutor, put it on his Justice Matters Substack: it was a judicial clean sweep. Trial court. Court of Appeals. Supreme Court. Nine-nothing. How often do we see that on any politically charged issue?

But unanimity at the Supreme Court did not produce compliance from the executive branch. What followed was two months of what can only be described as institutional defiance dressed up as diplomatic helplessness.

The administration argued that “facilitate” did not mean they were obligated to secure his release — only that they should try. They argued that El Salvador was a sovereign nation and Bukele had refused to cooperate. A DHS official filed a sworn declaration stating that “DHS does not have authority to forcibly extract an alien from the domestic custody of a foreign sovereign nation.” A different DHS official admitted in a separate filing that the deportation was an “administrative error” and an “oversight.” As the George Washington Law Review documented in its analysis of the administration’s response, the government was simultaneously conceding that it had acted illegally and arguing that the illegality was beyond its power to correct — a position that required believing that the most powerful government on earth was helpless to undo what it had done.

The State Department told Judge Xinis that Abrego Garcia was “alive and secure” in CECOT but provided no information about efforts to return him. The phrase “alive and secure” — applied to a man being held in a prison where he had been beaten, starved, and forced to kneel for nine hours at a stretch — carries a weight of institutional mendacity that is difficult to overstate. He was alive. He was secure. He was also being tortured, but that was not the question the State Department chose to answer.

Behind the scenes, as The Atlantic reported, there had been discussions between lawyers at the Departments of Justice, State, and Homeland Security about how to bring Abrego Garcia back. The discussions included having the U.S. ambassador to El Salvador make an appeal to Bukele. But the State Department wanted more information from Homeland Security about the MS-13 allegations first. The evidence they received — the hat, the hoodie, the informant from New York — was met with skepticism. State Department lawyers were not impressed. The plan to retrieve Abrego Garcia was blocked from within the administration itself.

For two months, a man sat in a foreign prison — a prison the United States was paying for — while the branches of his own government argued about whether the word “facilitate” meant they had to do anything at all. The Supreme Court had spoken unanimously. The administration responded with a shrug.

On June 6, 2025, Abrego Garcia was finally returned to the United States. The administration announced his return as though it were a concession, a diplomatic achievement graciously offered. In the same breath, they announced something else: he had been indicted.
