There is an old rule in Canadian hockey that every kid learns before they learn to skate backwards: the team that’s up 4–1 in the third period doesn’t send out the goon. The goon goes over the boards when the scoreboard has stopped cooperating and the bench has run out of better ideas. Nobody needs to explain it. You just watch who starts the fight, and you know how that bench reads the clock.

On Tuesday, October 13, 2026, the Vice President of the United States went over the boards in Tyler, Texas.

JD Vance had flown in to rally for Ken Paxton, the Republican Senate nominee, at the old terminal of Tyler Pounds Regional Airport, with Air Force Two parked behind him for the photographs. It was, as KLTV noted, the first visit to Tyler by a sitting vice president since Dan Quayle came for the Texas Rose Festival parade in 1992. A man in the crowd called out that he loved him. Vance had the reply ready.

“I love you too, sir. In a totally platonic way, to be clear. I’m the vice president, not James Talarico.”

Then, as Katie Rogers described it for the New York Times on October 14, 2026, he paused and smiled and waited for the applause.

It is worth sitting with that pause. The second-highest elected official in the United States, a man who has just published a book about his conversion to Catholicism and who expects to be his party’s presidential front-runner by Christmas, stood in front of a military aircraft and waited for a crowd to reward him for implying that a Presbyterian seminarian with a longtime girlfriend is gay. In 2026. The joke is a schoolyard joke. It was old when “no homo” was new.

The more interesting question is not whether it was crude. It was. The more interesting question is why, three weeks before Election Day, in a race the Republicans had owned since 1988, this is what the campaign reached for.

What Happened the Day Before

Begin with the calendar, because the calendar is the essay.

On Monday, October 12, 2026, the Wall Street Journal published video of Ken Paxton ringing in 2025 on a luxury high-rise balcony in Dubai, watching fireworks over the Burj Khalifa. As Forbes summarized the Journal’s reporting, he was there with his girlfriend, Tracy Duhon, who is not the woman he is still legally married to, and with two American healthcare executives, Anosh Ahmed and Sameer Suhail, who had been indicted on federal healthcare-fraud charges and had not come home to face them.

One of them, Ahmed, had given Paxton $190,000. According to campaign finance records cited by the Journal and reported by the International Business Times, that total includes a $25,000 contribution made days after Ahmed’s indictment in July 2024. Both men are now back in the United States; Ahmed is in custody.

In fairness, and the series has always tried to be fair, the IBTimes report is careful to note that the donations establish a financial connection, not proof of wrongdoing. Being at a party is not a crime. Taking money from a donor is not a crime. But the Texas Attorney General is the state’s chief law enforcement officer, and the question of why he was toasting the new year with two men running from American prosecutors is precisely the kind of question a man who wants to be a United States senator is supposed to be able to answer.

He did not answer it. His senior adviser, Nick Maddux, told the Journal that “like President Trump, Ken Paxton has been politically persecuted for years,” and left it there. In the TACO framework this series has leaned on, that is Obfuscate in its purest form: the question was where, with whom and why; the answer was a grievance.

James Talarico answered it for him. “A party with multiple fugitives and Ken Paxton is still the sketchiest guy in the room,” he said, as FOX 4 reported.

Less than twenty-four hours later, the vice president stood in Tyler and said nothing at all about Dubai. He talked about Talarico’s sexuality instead.

The Inventory of the Unmentionable

If you want to understand an innuendo campaign, the trick is not to study what it says. It is to make a list of everything it can’t say.

Start with the debate. James Talarico has accepted four debate invitations, from CNN, Hearst, Nexstar and WFAA, and challenged Paxton to a fifth on Fox News. Paxton has accepted none, as the Texas Tribune reported on October 8, 2026, even though his campaign promised in July that “of course” he would debate. The Tribune also notes that Paxton did not debate his opponents in any of his three campaigns for attorney general. This is not a scheduling problem. It is a policy.

By October 12, Talarico was counting. “I think it’s mysterious that Ken Paxton hasn’t responded to a request for a debate in 85 days,” he told NBC’s Dallas–Fort Worth station, as Houston Public Media reported.

Then there are the tapes. Readers of “The Drag” will remember the first one: Paxton at a private fundraiser, telling donors that Trump’s midterm convention in Dallas had dropped his numbers. That was not the last. By October 11, 2026, a Fox News host was asking Paxton about five separate leaks from a single September 24 fundraiser, according to The New Republic. He said he wasn’t worried.

He might have been. In the clips, as catalogued by Newsweek, he blames the Iran war and diesel prices, claims John Cornyn privately wants him to lose, and, in the one that will matter most to retirees in Tyler, tells lobbyists that Republicans will “have to suck it up” and cut mandatory federal spending, the category that holds Social Security, Medicare and Medicaid. He has since promised to protect Social Security. He has not explained why he said the other thing to a room he thought was private.

There is his voter registration: the man who built a voter-fraud tip line kept voting from an address his estranged wife says he left in June 2024. (In fairness, ProPublica and the Tribune found Talarico voted from his parents’ address for two years after buying his own home. The difference is that Talarico is not the state’s chief enforcer of election law.)

And there is Dubai.

Even his friends struggle for words. Trump’s endorsement in September came with a disclaimer attached: “He may not dress right. He may not talk exactly perfectly, and he may not be the best-looking guy I’ve ever seen. But you know what he is? He’s the greatest attorney general in America.” Angela Paxton, his estranged wife, now appears in an ad urging Texans to vote the Republican ticket without once saying his name. Jess Bidgood of the Times had the line for it: “It’s never great when the two people most crucial to your political survival struggle to simply say nice things about you. It’s worse when one of those people is the president, and the other is your estranged wife.”

Put the list together and you have a candidate who cannot stand on a stage, cannot explain a party, cannot defend his own private remarks, cannot comfortably talk about where he lives and cannot get his own wife to say his name. That is a great many subjects to be unable to discuss with a month to go. Something has to fill the airtime.

What Fills the Airtime

What fills it is a running theme, and it was running long before Tyler.

President Trump coined “Talafreako” and hit Talarico for eating vegan food in a cattle state. Allies of Paxton promoted a false rumour that Talarico was in a mental health facility in Ohio, a state he says he has never visited; he had the flu, and the Times later obtained time-stamped security camera images showing he spent the week at home. When Talarico spent nine days off the trail recovering, Paxton’s spokeswoman demanded “proof of life,” and the campaign paid for missing-person posters around the state. Trump posted that Talarico was “MISSING IN ACTION.” Even after Talarico was back shaking hands at Austin City Limits, Paxton told Fox News he didn’t know whether to believe him about the flu, as CNN reported.

At the Republicans’ midterm convention in Dallas in September, Vance had already road-tested the darker version, suggesting that Talarico’s past comments in support of transgender children meant nobody should want to see his web browser history. When asked about it the next day, he doubled down.

In Tyler, the rest of the speech was of a piece. Vance called Talarico “a freak,” “fake and a fraud,” and “a content machine,” as The Advocate catalogued from the C-SPAN footage. Readers of “The Feed” will appreciate the last one: it is the closest thing to a compliment Talarico got all day, an admission from the other side that his clips are working. Paxton, warming up the crowd, said Talarico is a “Christian who hates Christianity,” and Mayes Middleton, the Republican nominee for attorney general, said he knew atheists who were more Christian than Talarico, according to CBS19. We covered that line of attack in “The Wrong Kind of Christian,” and it has not improved with age.

Fairness again: Talarico did once say that God is nonbinary and that there are more than two biological sexes, and he has since renounced both, as Sam Sifton noted for the New York Times on October 15, 2026. Republicans are entitled to the quotation. What they are not entitled to is the leap from the quotation to a punchline about his sexuality.

Notice what nearly every one of these attacks has in common. They cannot be disproved. You cannot produce a document showing you are man enough, Christian enough, Texan enough or carnivorous enough. You cannot release a web browser history that ends a whisper.

And the one attack that could be disproved was, security camera footage and all, and it made no difference. Paxton was still telling Fox News he didn’t know whether to believe him. Bayliss Wagner of the Times drew the lesson: “It is politically risky for a candidate, even one too ill to campaign, to disappear for nine days.” Innuendo doesn’t need to survive the fact-check. It only needs to get there first.

That is not an accident. Innuendo is the attack you choose when you don’t want an argument, because there is nothing in it to argue with. And it is the natural weapon of a candidate who will not debate. A man who won’t stand at a podium across from his opponent needs a kind of politics that never requires a podium: one that happens in a punchline, a nickname, a poster, a pause for applause.

The Case That It’s Strategy, Not Panic

The honest essay has to give the other side its best argument, and the Republicans do have one.

Ron Bonjean, a GOP strategist quoted by the Times, argued that Vance knows the news cycle forgets. He pointed out that it has been five years since Vance’s “childless cat ladies” remark, and it did not end his career. The aim in Tyler, in Bonjean’s telling, was never to persuade independents. It was to fire up the MAGA base and make sure it votes. That’s a turnout play, not a meltdown.

Take it seriously, because it is partly true. Midterms are base elections. A crowd that cheers a gay joke is a crowd that will probably vote.

But look at what the strategy concedes. A former Trump adviser, granted anonymity by the Times to talk strategy, said the quiet part out loud: Republicans might not be able to win on policy, on the candidates they picked, on the economy or by explaining the war in Iran, but they might win by painting Democrats as extreme. That is not a description of a confident campaign. That is a list of everything that isn’t working, followed by the one thing left.

There is a small, telling detail in the Tyler coverage. Vance actually came with news. As the Dallas Morning News reported, he announced that the administration would use the Defense Production Act to expand the Delek refinery in Tyler, which KLTV said would bring hundreds of well-paying jobs. A campaign that believed in its own record would have built the whole day around that. Instead, the refinery is a paragraph near the bottom of most stories, and the joke is the headline. The campaign that had a jobs announcement chose a punchline, and the punchline ate the jobs.

Senator Jon Ossoff of Georgia put it in a sentence on MS NOW on October 14, according to the Times: the remark was bigoted and offensive, he said, but also “so politically feeble and weak.” Reason’s Billy Binion, no Democratic partisan, called it “desperate and small.” When the libertarians and the Democrats land on the same adjective, it is probably the right one.

The Other Stage

The night after Tyler, on Wednesday, October 14, 2026, Talarico sat for a ninety-minute town hall in Fort Worth hosted by CNN and Jubilee Media and moderated by Erin Burnett. Paxton was invited. Burnett told the audience the invitation still stood. The second chair stayed empty.

Talarico took questions from undecided voters on housing, Israel, artificial intelligence data centres and gender, including the issue Republicans have spent millions attacking him on. Asked about the rumours surrounding his absence, he said the responsible thing to do with the flu is to stay home and not make other people sick, and then he went after what he called the “rage economy” in American politics, as The Hill reported. He also gave Texas Democrats a line they will be quoting for a while: “I’m a proud Texas Democrat, but I already have a religion and I already have a sports team.”

And at the end he said something you rarely hear from a candidate three weeks out. He hadn’t mentioned his opponent all night, he told the room, because he didn’t want people to vote only against Ken Paxton; he wanted them to vote for him.

You can find that line cloying, and some will. But set it beside Tyler. One man spent his evening answering strangers’ questions on a stage his opponent refused to share. The other side spent its afternoon on a joke about the first man’s sexuality. Talarico’s spokesman, JT Ennis, had already summed up the contrast in the Times: while Republicans “play these childish games,” Talarico is talking about gas in the tank and food on the table.

The Scoreboard

So what does the scoreboard actually say? Less than either side would like.

The Marist poll, taken October 8 to 11, 2026, has Talarico at 47% and Paxton at 45% among likely voters, with a majority of likely independents behind Talarico, as the Marist Poll reported. The Texas Politics Project has Talarico ahead 50% to 47%, within its margin of error. The UMass Lowell/YouGov poll released October 8 has them tied at 45%. And here is the line in the Marist release that should keep every Democrat humble: 53% of likely voters, whoever they’re supporting, still think Paxton will win. Robert Draper, who has covered Texas politics for three decades, put the Democrats’ problem in one sentence: Texas is “where idealistic Democrats go to be mugged by reality.” No Democrat has won statewide there in 32 years.

So this is not a 4–1 game. It is a one-goal game in the third period, in a building where the home team hasn’t lost a Senate race since 1988. Which is exactly why the choice in Tyler is so revealing. Nobody sends out the goon in a game they’re sure of. You send him out when you’ve looked at the scoreboard and the clock and the shot count, and you’ve decided that skill isn’t going to get it done.

Leacock could make an election in Mariposa funny because the stakes there were small. The stakes in Texas are not. More than half a billion dollars has already been spent on advertising in this one race, which may become the most expensive nonpresidential contest in American history. Control of the Senate may run through it. Early voting opens today, Monday, October 19, and runs through Friday, October 30. Election Day is Tuesday, November 3, 2026.

Texans now have the full set of exhibits in front of them. On one stage, a candidate who answered questions for ninety minutes. On the other, an empty chair, a party in Dubai nobody will explain, five tapes nobody can unhear, and a vice president waiting for applause.

You don’t whisper when you’re winning. You whisper when you’ve run out of things you can say out loud.
