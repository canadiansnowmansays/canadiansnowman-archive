For eight years the Kremlin told the world that the bridge to Crimea could not be touched. It cost $3.7 billion and ran twelve miles across the Kerch Strait, a road and a railway side by side on concrete piles driven into the seabed. Where the shipping lane passes underneath, both decks rise into a pair of steel arches some 115 feet above the water. Around it Russia built what became one of the most heavily defended structures on earth: long- and short-range air defences, electronic jammers, smoke generators to blind incoming missiles, and rows of sunken barges linked by booms and nets to stop sea drones.

On Monday, November 16, a wave of Ukrainian drones hit one of the central arches, bending its steel ribs until the support tilted under the weight of the roadway. Before Russian engineers could brace it, a second wave came in, this time with drones and Flamingo cruise missiles, which Ukraine builds itself. The arch buckled. On Tuesday, November 17, a section of the roadway lay in the sea, as Marc Santora and James McManagan reported for The New York Times.

“For years, the Russians have convinced the whole world that the Crimean Bridge is one of the most protected objects on the planet,” said Artem Dekhtiarenko, a spokesman for Ukraine’s security service, the SBU. “Today, parts of it lie on the seabed.”

Hours before Zelensky spoke, seven Russian ballistic missiles had slammed into the bridges over the Dnipro in Kyiv. Volodymyr Zelensky drew the line between the two events himself. “We have repeatedly offered Russia steps toward de-escalation — and every proposal has been met with loud rejection,” he said. “We will never leave terror against our cities and people unanswered.”

This was a week of bridges: the ones Russia bombed, the one Ukraine brought down, and the one three of the world’s most powerful men tried to build over Ukraine’s head.

The bridge

The Crimean Bridge was never only a road. Russia opened it in 2018, four years after it seized the peninsula, as a monument to the annexation. It became the main artery for fuel, ammunition and soldiers moving into Crimea and on to the southern front. Ukraine has gone after it before: with a truck bomb in October 2022, naval drones in July 2023, and more than a tonne of explosives against its underwater pillars in June 2025. Each time, Russia patched the damage and thickened the defences.

This time the SBU spent weeks hunting radar stations and air-defence batteries across Crimea, tearing a hole in the umbrella before the drones went in. The Kremlin’s spokesman, Dmitri Peskov, promised that the bridge “will be restored and will be even better than the previous one.” Ukrainian experts told the Kyiv Independent that rebuilding the fallen span will take a year or more.

One more thing about the wreckage. The weapons that finished the arch were Ukrainian. In the same week that Moscow demanded an end to every Western weapon reaching Ukraine, Ukraine showed it can now reach the most guarded target in Russia’s possession with weapons of its own.

The paper

What Moscow wants was spelled out in a document its officials handed round at the Abu Dhabi talks, which Part X covered. The paper is dated November 10 and carries no letterhead or signature, a “non-paper” in diplomatic language. A copy was obtained by the Times, and in places it repeats Russia’s December 2021 demands, issued two months before the full-scale invasion, word for word.

NATO would pull its troops and bases back to its 1997 borders, before Poland and the Baltic states joined. The alliance would rule out “any military activity” in Ukraine and across Eastern Europe, the South Caucasus and Central Asia. The West would end “the supply of any type of Western weapons and military equipment and products to Ukraine.” Ukraine could join the European Union only if it stayed out of the bloc’s common defence. And Britain and France would have to promise never to use their nuclear weapons to protect other European countries, a demand Russia did not make in 2021.

In Abu Dhabi, Russian officials argued that the Cold War’s spheres of influence had kept the peace. In Moscow on November 12, Putin told the American envoys that the 2021 proposals still reflected “our vision of the pillars of an indivisible security architecture,” according to his aide Yuri Ushakov. Donald Tusk, Poland’s prime minister, gave the answer most of Europe would give: “Accepting Russia’s conditions would mean a direct threat to Poland’s security.”

Europe will at least be in the room next time. On November 13, EU leaders meeting in Dublin named a team to represent them: Britain, France, Germany, Poland and Italy, plus the EU’s two leadership bodies, as Jeanna Smialek reported for the Times. “I don’t want someone to talk about our European house, if they are not living in that house,” said Luc Frieden, Luxembourg’s prime minister.

Western intelligence agencies have read the paper the same way. Putin is not ready for serious negotiations and intends to keep fighting at least through the winter, officials told the Times on November 18. He hopes his army can gain ground in the east, or that the missiles and drones falling on Kyiv will wear down Ukrainian resistance. One agency judged that he would settle only for all of the Donbas and a promise that Ukraine never joins NATO, terms no government in Kyiv could accept. Meanwhile, by Western estimates, 40,000 to 50,000 Russian soldiers are being killed or seriously wounded every month.

“We believe Russia is in a very dire situation,” said Aurélien Lechevallier, France’s ambassador in Washington. “He needs the war in Ukraine to fuel his own leadership, his own narrative.” Kaja Kallas, the EU’s foreign-policy chief, dismissed the idea that the Russian paper was merely an opening bid. Russia is “just trying to blur the picture, to say that, you know, we are just brainstorming,” she said. “They are not brainstorming.”

Shenzhen

On Thursday, November 19, Putin, Trump and Xi Jinping sat down together in Shenzhen, on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific summit. It was the first time the leaders of the three countries had ever met as a trio. Ushakov had promised that it would be held “in the spirit of the mutual understanding and alliance between our three countries during the Second World War.”

Lechevallier knew what Putin was after: a photograph. Specifically, one to hang beside the famous picture of Churchill, Roosevelt and Stalin seated together in February 1945 at Yalta. “From a Russian perspective, that would be the crowning of their diplomacy, because they want to appear as equals to the U.S. and China, which obviously they are not anymore,” he said. “But that’s the storytelling they would like to push.”

Yalta is in Crimea. It sits on the southern coast of the peninsula, at the far end of the bridge that now lies partly in the sea. In Part X, the Ukrainian MP Volodymyr Ariev warned that “Putin’s goal is to hold a new Yalta.” A week later, Putin’s aide was describing the summit in exactly those terms.

He got his picture. The meeting lasted about ninety minutes, with Xi seated at the centre of the table. The three leaders issued a four-point “declaration on peace” calling for reform of the United Nations, dialogue on nuclear risks and more refined petroleum on world markets. It also said that the International Criminal Court, which has a warrant out for Putin’s arrest, should not “abuse” its jurisdiction. It did not mention Ukraine, as the Kyiv Post reported. The historian Rana Mitter, writing in The Conversation, saw “a lot of style, but less substance.” The resemblance to Yalta, she wrote, was “largely about the imagery of the three men around the table.” In 1945 the three powers were allies. Now two of them treat the third as an adversary.

Zelensky said what Kyiv and Europe have both insisted on all autumn: questions about Ukraine have to be decided with Ukraine in the room. In Shenzhen, Ukraine was not even in the communiqué.

Hostage

“They want to come to the table holding our energy system hostage,” Zelensky said after a recent attack. It is the plainest description of Russia’s strategy this winter.

On September 1, Putin ordered a campaign of mass strikes on Ukraine’s energy system. By the end of October, Russian forces had attacked grid infrastructure more than 350 times and damaged over 800 pieces of critical equipment, Ukrainian officials told Marc Santora and Liubov Sholudko of the Times. Seventy-two hours before the Abu Dhabi talks opened, Russia fired 36 ballistic missiles and 172 drones at Kyiv and the surrounding region. The barrage severed the high-voltage lines feeding the capital and left 40 percent of Kyiv’s high-rise apartment buildings without heat before the first hard frost.

Viktoriia Bulakh, 43, lives nine flights up in a building on the edge of Kyiv that has lately been getting 18 to 26 minutes of electricity a day. With no power there is no heat, no running water and no lift. When the air-raid sirens sound at night, she carries her nine-year-old son down nine flights to a freezing basement, then back up when the all-clear comes. “All night, back and forth,” she said. “Up the stairs, down the stairs. Up, down, up again.” Her neighbour has stopped bringing her toddler down at all. “The little ones are sleepy,” Bulakh said. “They want to sleep. They cry, poor little things.”

“Winter is not yet here, and we already don’t have power,” said Maksym Timchenko, the chief executive of DTEK, Kyiv’s electricity distributor. Ukraine’s air defences are thinning just as the attacks intensify. Patriot interceptors, the only reliable shield against ballistic missiles, are running low, in part because Washington is conserving its stocks for the Middle East. Crews have abandoned the doctrine of firing two interceptors at each incoming missile. “We cannot afford such a luxury,” said Colonel Yurii Ihnat, the air force spokesman. “We are forced to use one missile on one target — but as you can see, 40 missiles are flying and five of them are being shot down.”

The people keeping the lights on have become targets themselves. In Sumy, a drone hit a repair crew’s vehicle on a busy street, and one man lost a limb. “Everyone has their own front,” said Serhii Tytarenko, who leads a power-line crew there. “We are holding our energy front.”

The easing

The week also brought a reckoning with what Washington has done this autumn. A timeline compiled by Josh Holder and Samuel Granados of the Times sets the two sides of the story next to each other, and they do not match.

In September, Putin discussed an oil deal with Trump’s envoys, Trump told Ukraine to stop hitting Russian refineries and invited Putin to the G20 summit in Miami, and air-raid alerts in Kyiv hit their highest level of the war. In October, a Russian cruise missile killed 22 people in an apartment building in Pryluky, and the next day Russia hit two buses of commuters in Kramatorsk, killing at least 30. That month Trump announced a deal to import Russian diesel, lifting sanctions to do it, said Ukraine should “get a new president,” declared an “energy cease-fire” that in practice restrained only Ukraine, and waived a sanctions law Congress had passed to restrict Russia’s war financing. “Russia, frankly, has been fine on Ukraine,” he said on his way to a rally. “They haven’t been doing much.” Steve Witkoff called Putin a “humanitarian.”

Some of Ukraine’s lifelines have flickered at the worst moments. Western intelligence sharing stopped for a time. In October, Ukrainian drone units found their links to Starshield, the military satellite network built by Elon Musk’s SpaceX, slowed down; speeds came back, reportedly after Trump called Musk. The day after the Abu Dhabi talks, the limits returned. “Without the speed we need, shooting down these Shaheds becomes much harder,” Zelensky said, according to Scripps News. SpaceX called the reports “false.” Senators Jeanne Shaheen, a Democrat, and Thom Tillis, a Republican, said in a joint statement that “Congress must determine who ordered or approved these disruptions and hold those responsible accountable.”

Washington says each step was meant to help end the war. Together they have not changed Russia’s behaviour. With wins from Washington in hand and Ukraine short of interceptors, the Times concluded, Putin “appears to believe he has the upper hand.”

The people who stay

At 9 a.m. every day, loudspeakers across Ukraine announce a minute of silence. At Lychakiv Cemetery in Lviv, the country’s oldest, Mykhailo Biliak takes off his cap, looks out over the flags on the Field of Honorary Burials and crosses himself three times. He is 62 and has dug graves since 1992. Since the full-scale invasion he has buried more than 1,200 soldiers. “The worst day was six funerals,” he told Mauricio Lima and Yurii Shyvala of the Times. “It’s a disaster. You know how it wrecks your head?”

In a basement near the front, the anaesthetist Daryna Boiko, 32, set up a drip for a soldier wounded by a drone. “It won’t be scary,” she told him. “It was scary out there,” he answered. “It’s fine here.” In Odesa, the forensic pathologist Ruslan Fedoniuk, 61, works on a station platform between refrigerated railway carriages full of soldiers’ bodies, taking DNA samples so families can bury their dead. He fled Bakhmut in 2022. “I lost everything,” he said. “All I have left is my wife, my son and my car.”

In Kyiv, the psychotherapist Hanna Buraho uses horses to help soldiers with post-traumatic stress; more than 5,000 have been through her programme. Her grandson Daniel is four. He can already tell the sound of a ballistic missile from the sound of a Shahed drone. “Don’t be afraid,” he tells her. “It’s just a Shahed.”

That is what is at stake behind the photographs and the declarations. Russia’s paper asks Europe to roll its map back thirty years. Its missiles ask Kyiv to choose between surrender and a winter in the dark. In Shenzhen, three men posed in the spirit of 1945 and left the country being bombed out of the picture.

Ukraine’s answer this week was the same answer it has given since 2022. Its people carried children down nine flights of stairs and back up again. Its line crews went out under the drones. And its engineers found a way through the most heavily defended structure in Russia’s possession, so that the bridge built to celebrate the conquest of Crimea now ends in open water.
