On Tuesday, October 20, 2026, fourteen days before Americans vote, the President of the United States posted a campaign-finance disclosure on Truth Social. He had, he wrote, spent more than $300 million on the midterm elections, and he intended to spend $100 million more by Election Day.

It is worth pausing on the verb. “I have spent.” Not “my allies have spent,” not “an independent expenditure-only committee legally barred from coordinating with me has spent.” As Theodore Schleifer noted for the New York Times that same afternoon, the money almost certainly belongs, in the legal sense, to MAGA Inc., the super PAC run by people who work for Donald Trump in every way except the one the Federal Election Commission is permitted to notice. Sixteen years after the D.C. Circuit’s SpeechNow decision invented the super PAC on the premise that independent spending cannot corrupt because it is independent, the most powerful man in the country described a super PAC’s spending in the first person singular, and nobody in Washington found the grammar remarkable.

That is roughly where this series has been heading since its Introduction. The maze no longer bothers to hide its walls. It simply counts on nobody looking at them until the game is over.

The timing of the post was not an accident either. Tuesday was a filing deadline. Thursday, October 22, brings another, the last pre-election report most federal committees will file. Between them, these two filings are the final look Americans get at who is paying for their election before they cast their ballots. After Thursday, the scoreboard goes dark. The full accounting of what gets spent in the last eleven days arrives with the post-general reports due December 3, 2026, a month after the winners have been declared and the losers have conceded. For the dark-money nonprofits we followed in Part XII, the lawyers’ advice remains what it was in August: file in mid-November 2027.

In August, I closed Part XII on a sentence I have not been able to shake. The maze’s best weapon is not secrecy forever. It is secrecy until the count is over. This Coda is about the fourteen days in which that weapon does its work, and about the new piece of equipment the Supreme Court issued to one side of the board in June.

The pellet

Anyone who played Pac-Man in an arcade in 1981 remembers the power pellet. There are four of them, one in each corner of the maze. Eat one, and for a few seconds the ghosts turn blue, the rules invert, and the hunted becomes the hunter.

On June 30, 2026, the Supreme Court placed a power pellet in the corner of the maze closest to whoever happened to have the most cash.

The case was National Republican Senatorial Committee v. Federal Election Commission, and as the law firm Skadden summarised in July, the six-justice majority held that the Federal Election Campaign Act’s limits on how much a political party may spend in coordination with its own candidates violate the First Amendment. In doing so the Court overruled its own 2001 decision in Colorado II, which had upheld those limits on the sensible ground that a party’s coordinated spending is the easiest possible route around the cap on contributions to candidates. Before June, the national party could spend $65,300 in coordination with most House nominees. After June, the number is whatever the party has in the bank.

Two details deserve a place in the record. The first is that the Trump administration’s Justice Department declined to defend the statute, so the Court had to appoint an outside lawyer to argue for a federal law the federal government would not argue for. The second is that Justice Elena Kagan’s dissent pointed to the one wall still standing: the cap on what an individual may give to a national party committee. Whether that wall survives the next case is a question for 2028. Whether the party that already had the money would profit immediately was never a question at all.

Readers who have come this far will recognise the shape. In Part IV, we watched McCain-Feingold drive soft money out of the parties in 2002, after the parties had become the great laundering machines of the 1990s. The money did not disappear. It went to 527 groups, then to super PACs, then to nonprofits that disclose nothing. The reform era’s achievement was to push money away from the institutions most accountable to voters and toward the institutions least accountable to anyone. Now the Court has reopened the front door of the party committees without closing any of the side doors. Every route remains open. One of them simply got wider.

Who ate it

Schleifer’s reporting for the Times on Tuesday showed exactly who was standing in the corner when the pellet appeared.

The Republican National Committee entered September with $126 million on hand, a sum built in large part on the fundraising of Trump and Vice President JD Vance, who has led the party’s finance operation. Between mid-August and mid-October, the R.N.C. moved more than $44 million to the National Republican Congressional Committee and the National Republican Senatorial Committee, split roughly evenly, with at least $6 million more to follow by Election Day. In the four previous cycles, its transfers to those committees ran between $8 million and roughly $31 million. By October 14, the war chest had been drawn down to $76 million.

The result shows up on television. According to AdImpact data cited by the Times, the Republican House and Senate committees have aired or booked about $152 million in coordinated ads, the largest share for Senator Jon Husted in Ohio. The R.N.C. itself is running another $5 million coordinated directly with Representative Ashley Hinson’s Senate campaign in Iowa. Democratic committees, by the same measure, have aired or booked about $102 million, their largest buy supporting Husted’s opponent, Sherrod Brown.

A fifty-million-dollar gap in coordinated advertising is not the whole story, and I will come to the limits of money shortly. But note where the gap comes from. Through August 30, the two parties’ House and Senate committees had raised money at rough parity. The difference is the R.N.C. And the Democratic National Committee, as the Times reported in July, could not afford the transfers it had planned, having reported negative cash on hand, net of debt, for almost the entire 2025–26 cycle. One party arrived at the pellet with a full stomach. The other arrived with an overdraft.

Mike Ambrosini, the R.N.C.’s chief of staff, told the Times the coordinated-spending strategy had been part of the pitch to donors: give to us, and your dollar can now go anywhere. He added that there would be no saving for a rainy day. It is a candid line, and I mean that as a compliment. The R.N.C. is doing precisely what the Court invited it to do. The scandal, as this series has argued from Part I, is rarely what is illegal. It is what has been made legal.

The white knight, on schedule

The other big number of the day came from the other side of the board.

Michael R. Bloomberg, the former mayor of New York, has given roughly $80 million to help Democrats in September and October, his team told Schleifer and Benjamin Oreskes of the Times. At least $50 million is going to House races: $24 million to Everytown for Gun Safety, the group he co-founded, and $16 million to House Majority PAC, the super PAC aligned with Hakeem Jeffries, making Bloomberg the largest disclosed donor to both. Smaller sums went to the League of Conservation Voters’ super PAC, to a group electing Democratic scientists and doctors, to Democratic gubernatorial candidates in half a dozen states, and $2.5 million to a super PAC backing James Talarico in Texas, a race that readers of Let’s Talk Texas will know well. Another $10 million for House races is still to come.

There is something almost liturgical about it. The Times described Democrats wondering each autumn where Bloomberg is, and then, like clockwork, there he is. The figure even rhymes. In June 2018, as CNBC reported at the time, Bloomberg pledged $80 million to help Democrats take the House. Eight years, one billion-dollar presidential campaign, and one Supreme Court revolution later, the cheque is the same size.

His adviser Howard Wolfson made the case plainly. Bloomberg believes resources matter in campaigns, Wolfson said, and he “expects a result.” The result he wants is not merely a Democratic House but a governable one. “The difference between 221 and 230,” Wolfson told the Times, “really matters.”

I do not doubt that it does. And I do not intend to pretend that an $80 million counterweight from a man who believes in gun safety and climate action is morally equivalent to whatever is moving through the nonprofits that will report in November 2027. Bloomberg’s money is disclosed. His team volunteered the figures before the filings landed. That is more than can be said for most of the cycle.

But Part VIII of this series was about the new nobility, and Bloomberg is one of its senior peers. Wolfson was frank that his principal remains concerned about his party’s leftward drift, that he is not funding candidates aligned with the Democratic Socialists of America, and that he will likely help shape the 2028 Democratic presidential primary. One of this cycle’s donations already backed Governor Josh Shapiro of Pennsylvania. The Democratic answer to the Republican pellet, in other words, is not reform. It is a different billionaire with a different set of preferences, deciding which Democrats are worth keeping. Elon Musk and MAGA Inc. pour hundreds of millions into the Republican side; an 84-year-old former mayor decides the size of the Democratic majority. The ghosts change colour. The game does not change.

Money that will not say its name

A smaller story from the same day belongs in this Coda because it captures something the large numbers miss.

J Street, the liberal pro-Israel group, released two ads through its super PAC on Tuesday targeting Representatives Mariannette Miller-Meeks of Iowa and Ryan Mackenzie of Pennsylvania, both Republicans endorsed by the American Israel Public Affairs Committee. As Jennifer Medina reported for the Times, it is the first time J Street’s super PAC has taken on AIPAC in a general election. J Street Action Fund has spent a record $3 million this cycle, a sliver of the tens of millions spent by AIPAC’s United Democracy Project.

Neither ad mentions Israel. Both are about health care and insurance companies.

In Part XII we met the Baldacci ad in Maine, which attacked a candidate as dark money’s choice while being paid for by dark money. The J Street ads are a gentler version of the same phenomenon, and I do not single them out as uniquely cynical; the organisers argue, fairly enough, that Israel is not the issue most voters are deciding on. But the pattern across this cycle is consistent. Interested money identifies the issue voters care about, buys airtime on that issue, and leaves the issue it actually cares about off the screen. Voters are invited to judge the advertisement. They are rarely invited to judge the advertiser’s reason for paying.

The most expensive ad of the cycle

One more figure from the day does not appear in any FEC filing, which is why it belongs here.

At a rally in Park City, Kansas, on Monday, October 19, Trump announced that the Agriculture Department would provide $8 billion to farmers hurt by high diesel prices, a programme called Harvest Fuel Relief. As Kevin Draper reported for the Times, the money will come from the Commodity Credit Corporation, payment rates will be announced next month, and the cheques will arrive later still. The announcement came fifteen days before an election in which farm-state voters may decide close races, and Trump credited it, on stage, to Senator Roger Marshall, whose seat Republican leaders did not consider competitive a few months ago and who now trails or ties the Democrat Adam Hamilton in most polls. Neither the president nor the department explained that diesel prices rose largely because the war in Iran choked oil exports through the Persian Gulf.

I am not suggesting the payments are illegal, or that farmers do not need them. I am noting that the single largest sum mentioned at any campaign event this week was public money, announced at a partisan rally, attributed to a candidate, and scheduled to be paid after the votes are counted. No disclosure rule touches it. No contribution limit applies. It is, by some distance, the most expensive campaign advertisement of the cycle, and the taxpayer is the donor.

What money cannot buy

Here the honest accounting from Part XII applies again: it does not always work.

The R.N.C.’s war chest, the coordinated-ad advantage and the $400 million Trump says he is spending are being deployed by a party playing defence. Trump’s approval stands near 37 percent. As Emily Davies and Jonathan Swan reported for the Times, his six-day swing this week runs through Wichita, Greenville, Clemson, Sioux City, Fort Myers and Macon, mostly districts he carried by double digits in 2024, because those are now the places where his party’s races are close. His advisers told the Times they have all but given up on the Senate races in Georgia and North Carolina and accepted that he cannot help Susan Collins in Maine. Kevin Madden, a Republican strategist who worked for George W. Bush and Mitt Romney, offered the line that every campaign-finance analyst should tape above the desk: the two most valuable assets in any campaign are money and time, and where you spend them in the last two weeks tells you your strategy.

Money can buy airtime in Kansas. It cannot make a war, a diesel bill and a grocery bill disappear from a farmer’s kitchen table. The cynic’s comfort, that money always wins, is not supported by this cycle. Neither is the reformer’s comfort, that money is just noise. A party that would otherwise be losing badly may, with enough money, lose by less. In a Senate decided by a seat or two, that is the whole game.

Level 256

There is a famous flaw in the original Pac-Man. The game was never meant to end, but on the 256th level a counter in the machine’s memory overflows, and the right half of the screen dissolves into a jumble of letters, numbers and broken walls. Players call it the kill screen. You can still move. You can still eat a few dots. You simply cannot see the maze any more, and nobody can finish the level.

After Thursday, October 22, 2026, the American midterm enters its own split screen. On the left, the disclosed half: Bloomberg’s cheques, the R.N.C.’s transfers, the super PACs’ independent-expenditure reports showing what was spent, if not always whose money it was. On the right, scrambled: the pop-up super PACs formed too late to report before the vote, the nonprofits filing in 2027, and every dollar raised and spent in the final eleven days, legible only after the result is in. In August, Dan Weiner of the Brennan Center told the Times this was “the least transparent midterm at least since Citizens United.” Fourteen days out, nothing has happened to make him wrong.

I write this, as I wrote the Introduction, from Toronto, where corporations and unions cannot donate to federal parties at all and third parties face spending caps when a campaign is under way. Canada’s system has its own flaws, and I would not offer it as a template to be copied wholesale. But it starts from a premise the American system abandoned in 1976: that an election is something voters own, and that the people paying for it should be visible to them before they vote, not after.

The United States began this series with twenty-one families and $783 million. It ends this cycle with a president describing a super PAC’s money as his own, a Supreme Court that handed the richer party a power pellet, a Democratic counterweight that depends on the goodwill of one billionaire, and a disclosure system that goes dark exactly when voters most need the lights on.

The dots keep disappearing. On November 3, we find out who ate them. On December 3, we find out who paid. In November 2027, we find out the rest.

Game over. Insert coin.
