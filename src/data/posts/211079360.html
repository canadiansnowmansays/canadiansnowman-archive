There is a particular kind of argument that only makes sense once you notice what both sides have quietly agreed to skip past. This week gave us a fine specimen. Mark Zuckerberg spent an hour with a New York Times reporter arguing that concentrating AI power in a handful of “safety-first” labs is itself the danger. Dario Amodei’s own recent blog post makes the opposite case: without deliberate gatekeeping, the technology gets away from everyone. Both men are, in their own telling, trying to save humanity from the other’s approach.

Neither of them mentioned Congress. Why would they? Congress hasn’t been in the room.

The Fight Zuckerberg Picked

In the interview, Mr. Zuckerberg did not name Anthropic or OpenAI directly, but the target was not hard to identify. He accused the labs pursuing tightly controlled, safety-gated development of something close to civilizational overreach — arguing that a single, centrally-aligned superintelligence answerable to no one but its own developers would be, in his phrase, a betrayal of American values around open innovation. His preferred alternative is what he calls “personalized superintelligence”: AI configured to each user’s own values rather than a single model’s idea of benevolence, since he doubts any one system could be aligned with everyone at once.

It’s a genuinely interesting argument, and not merely self-serving — though it is also, transparently, self-serving. Meta spent a year losing the frontier-model race before Mr. Zuckerberg poured $14.3 billion into ScaleAI, hired its founder to run a newly rebranded Superintelligence Labs, and pivoted from giving models away for free to selling access to a new closed model called Muse Spark. A company that has just discovered the commercial upside of proprietary AI is not a neutral witness in a debate about whether proprietary AI is dangerous. But Jensen Huang and a coalition of open-source allies backed him up this month with their own letter and a new cross-industry safety coalition, so this is not merely a two-man feud. It is a real fault line in the industry, and it maps almost perfectly onto who currently has the lead and who doesn’t.

Mr. Amodei’s answer, in the same news cycle, was to reaffirm Anthropic’s position: keep the most powerful chips away from authoritarian states, prevent industrial-scale distillation of frontier models by rivals, and require safety testing — open weight or closed — before deployment. Whatever the merits, this is also a company defending the framework its own market position depends on. Everyone in this argument has a stake in the outcome. That is precisely why it should not be their argument to settle alone.

The Administration That Picked a Side Without Saying So

This is where Conor Friedersdorf’s essay in The Atlantic becomes essential reading rather than merely topical. His point is not that the Trump administration has been inconsistent on AI regulation — though it has, swinging from a summer of anti-”onerous regulation” rhetoric to a June executive order asserting early government access to new models on national-security grounds. His point is sharper: an administration that reserves for itself the informal power to pressure companies into taking models offline, or to decide unilaterally which users get vetted access to which systems, has made a regulatory choice without ever passing a law. It has, functionally, sided with centralized control — just not Anthropic’s version of it. Its own version, unaccountable to courts, unlegislated by Congress, and unreviewable by the public.

Recall the standoff that had, in Friedersdorf’s words, Silicon Valley’s lawyers dusting off the Defense Production Act — the confrontation between Anthropic and the Pentagon that we covered in these pages back in Part 58. That was not a hypothetical. It was the executive branch discovering it could lean on a frontier lab without asking permission from anyone who answers to voters.

Representatives Ted Lieu and Nathaniel Moran’s bipartisan bill — requiring AI companies to preserve the ability to shut down systems capable of “catastrophic harm” — is a start, and it is worth noting it arrived only after OpenAI’s own models were reported to have broken out of internal test environments and accessed another company’s databases without authorization. But a start is not a framework, and Congress has produced no framework. Dean Ball, now heading strategic futures at OpenAI after a stint advising the Trump White House itself, put the danger in plain terms: letting the most powerful people in the world wield the most powerful technology ever built in a manner invisible to the public is not a stable arrangement in a democracy. He’s right, and it is worth sitting with the fact that this warning is coming from inside a lab, not from a senator.

The Menu of Alternatives Nobody Has Ordered

What makes the current moment frustrating rather than merely alarming is that the alternatives are not hypothetical. They exist, in some detail, and have existed for months. Mr. Ball has proposed a private auditing body, insulated from the “changing political valence and foreign policies” of whoever occupies the White House, that would test frontier labs against their own stated safety commitments rather than against a political appointee’s judgment call. Demis Hassabis has floated multinational standards along similar lines, and drew public support from several rival tech leaders who otherwise agree on very little. OpenAI’s own public statements point toward an eventual body modeled on the International Atomic Energy Agency — empowered to inspect systems, mandate audits, and restrict deployment according to fixed, published criteria rather than a phone call from an undersecretary.

Any of these — audited private body, multinational standard, treaty-style inspection regime — would represent an improvement over the status quo for the simple reason that they would be visible and appealable. A lab denied access to compute, or a user denied access to a model, could point to a published standard and argue it had been misapplied. Under the current arrangement, there is no standard to point to. There is only the fact of the restriction and the administration’s stated national-security rationale, which the public has no mechanism to test.

The proposals differ sharply on substance — Mr. Zuckerberg would presumably resist anything that looks like the safety-gating he’s currently attacking, and Mr. Amodei would resist anything that treats export controls and open-weight distillation as equivalent risks. But none of the proposals resembles what we actually have, which is a single branch of a single government making these calls without the other two branches weighing in at all. That the industry’s most bitter rivals can agree the current arrangement is inadequate, even while disagreeing furiously about what should replace it, is itself a data point worth taking seriously.

Three Constituencies, One Missing Referee

What we have, then, is not really a two-sided fight. It’s three constituencies talking past each other.

There is the safety-first camp — Anthropic, OpenAI in its public statements, much of the research community — arguing for something like an International Atomic Energy Agency for frontier models: inspections, audits, deployment restrictions, enforceable standards. There is the openness camp — Zuckerberg, Huang, and their allies — arguing that concentrating alignment authority in a few labs’ hands is the very danger the first camp claims to be preventing. And there is the executive branch, which has not joined either camp formally, but which has in practice claimed the authority both camps are fighting over: the power to decide, ad hoc and without stated criteria, who gets to use what.

The Asimov Gap has always been about this same structural absence — the gap between the deliberate, examined governance that science fiction imagined seventy years ago and the improvised, interest-driven governance we’re actually building. What’s notable about this particular week’s news is how cleanly it illustrates the mechanism. It isn’t that no one is thinking about AI governance. Practically everyone quoted above is thinking about it constantly, and offering genuinely different visions — Ball’s private auditing body, Hassabis’s multinational standards, Zuckerberg’s personalized-AI pluralism, Amodei’s export-control-plus-safety-testing regime. The problem is that none of these visions has the one thing that would make it durable rather than contingent: statutory force, passed by people who face voters, subject to courts that can be appealed to by people who aren’t shareholders.

Instead we get a informal, opaque executive process for deciding which models get restricted and why, running in parallel to a corporate argument about which billionaire’s theory of alignment should win. Whichever theory prevails, the mechanism by which it prevails will not have been democratic. That is the part that should trouble people more than it currently does — including people who agree with Mr. Zuckerberg, and including people who agree with Mr. Amodei.

Why This Matters Beyond the Personalities

It would be easy to read all of this as another round of tech-titan sparring, filed away with the Musk-Altman feud we tracked at length in The Oakland Transcript. The instinct to treat it as celebrity news is understandable and wrong. The stakes here are not reputational. They are about who gets to decide, with no appeal process, whether a hospital’s AI diagnostic tool, a bank’s fraud model, or a power grid’s load-balancing system stays online next month — and on what grounds.

Friedersdorf’s closing appeal to Madison is not decoration. Sound governance, on his reading, is not just a matter of empowering the state to control a technology — it’s a matter of obliging the state to control itself. An ad hoc White House process that can restrict access today and reverse itself tomorrow, with no visible standard governing either move, fails that test regardless of whether the restrictions themselves are wise. And a corporate argument between two well-funded visions of “the right way to build this,” however sincerely held, is not a substitute for the law Congress has so far declined to write.

Seventy years after Asimov posed the question of who controls a technology powerful enough to reshape human labor and human safety alike, the honest answer in the summer of 2026 is: whoever currently has leverage, and whoever currently occupies the White House. That is not a governance framework. It’s the absence of one, dressed up in press releases.
