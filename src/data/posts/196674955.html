Sarah Fitzpatrick, The Atlantic — Washington Week, February 2026. Six weeks later, the FBI opened an investigation into her reporting

CanadianSnowmanSays | May 7, 2026

There is a line this series has been tracking across twenty-two parts — a line between the abuse of law enforcement as a political weapon and something qualitatively worse. The earlier entries involved politicians, prosecutors, former officials, a Fed chair, a civil rights organization, a comedian. All of them had done something the administration could point to, however disingenuously, as justification for scrutiny. The pretext was always thin. But there was always a pretext.

Sarah Fitzpatrick had no pretext available to her enemies. She is a reporter at The Atlantic. In April, she published a sourced, reported story The FBI Director Is MIA, about FBI Director Kash Patel — interviewing more than two dozen people, including current and former FBI officials, congressional staff, law enforcement personnel, and hospitality workers — documenting concerns inside the government about Patel’s unexplained absences and visible intoxication. The story included a specific incident in which Patel’s security detail requested breaching equipment because the director had been unreachable behind locked doors.

Patel denied the story. He sued Fitzpatrick and The Atlantic for $250 million. Legal analysts across the political spectrum assessed the defamation case as weak and likely to fail.

Yesterday morning, MS NOW reported that the FBI has opened a criminal investigation focused on Fitzpatrick.

The reporter did her job. Her job is now the alleged crime.

What the Investigation Actually Is

The details of the reported probe deserve careful attention, because they reveal the mechanism.

The investigation is reportedly being run out of the FBI’s insider-threats unit — a team specifically designed to monitor federal employees or contractors with access to sensitive information who might leak it in ways that harm national security. Sarah Fitzpatrick is not a federal employee. She is not a contractor. She has no security clearance. She is, by every definition that matters, not an insider.

The FBI has not alleged that Fitzpatrick published classified information. Patel’s lawsuit does not allege she published classified information. No public statement from any administration official alleges she published classified information. What she published was a reported account of a senior official’s conduct — the kind of story that the First Amendment exists specifically to protect.

The FBI’s own spokesperson denied the probe exists, telling MS NOW: “This is completely false. No such investigation like this exists and the reporter you mention is not being investigated at all.”

And yet MS NOW reports that FBI agents assigned to the case are troubled by it. One source put it with an economy that should be read slowly: “They know they are not supposed to do this. But if they don’t go forward, they could lose their jobs. You’re damned if you do and damned if you don’t.”

Read that again. Federal law enforcement agents, assigned to investigate a journalist, telling sources they know the investigation is illegitimate — but proceeding anyway because the alternative is termination.

This is not a corrupted investigation. It is a coerced one. The distinction matters enormously. A corrupted investigation involves officials who have convinced themselves the target deserves scrutiny. A coerced investigation involves officials who know the target does not — and proceed under threat.

Patel’s Record With Reporters

Kyle Mazza / Anadolu / Getty

Fitzpatrick is not the first journalist to find herself in Patel’s crosshairs, and the pattern is worth establishing.

Last year, Patel sued former FBI official Frank Figliuzzi for stating on television that the FBI director had been “visible at nightclubs far more than he has been on the seventh floor of the Hoover Building.” A federal judge dismissed that case in April — one day after Patel filed the defamation suit against The Atlantic.

In March, the FBI investigated New York Times reporter Elizabeth Williamson after she reported that Patel had used bureau personnel to protect and transport his girlfriend. The FBI ultimately decided not to pursue the case.

In January, FBI agents executed a search warrant at the home of Washington Post reporter Hannah Natanson, seizing her phone and devices. Weeks earlier, Natanson had published a widely-read account drawing on more than a thousand sources about the administration’s overhaul of the federal government. The Post was awarded the Pulitzer Prize for Public Service this week for that body of work.

Three reporters. Three separate investigations or search warrants. All involving coverage of the FBI or the administration. All following the same pattern: a story is published, a lawsuit or probe follows, the process becomes the punishment regardless of legal outcome.

This is not coincidence. It is policy.

The Insider-Threats Tells

The use of the insider-threats unit to investigate a journalist carries a specific implication that deserves to be named plainly.

Insider-threat investigations are designed to identify the sources of leaks — the federal employees who spoke to Fitzpatrick — not to investigate the journalist herself. The unit’s mandate, its legal authority, and its investigative tools are all oriented toward finding people inside the government who may have disclosed sensitive information.

What this means in practice: the investigation of Fitzpatrick is, at minimum, also an investigation of everyone who spoke to her. Every current FBI official who described Patel’s conduct. Every law enforcement or intelligence agency employee who raised concerns. Every congressional staffer. Every hospitality worker who described what they witnessed.

The investigation is designed to find Fitzpatrick’s sources and expose them. That is what dries up the flow of information. That is what chills reporting not just on Patel but on every senior official who might one day find themselves covered unfavourably by a reporter with sources inside their agency.

The goal is not to prosecute Fitzpatrick. The goal is to make sure the next Fitzpatrick never gets the call returned.

What The Atlantic Said

Jeffrey Goldberg, The Atlantic‘s editor in chief, responded with a statement that should be read in full:

“If confirmed to be true, this would represent an outrageous attack on the free press and the First Amendment itself. We will defend The Atlantic and its staff vigorously; we will not be intimidated by illegitimate investigations or other acts of politically motivated retaliation; we will continue to cover the FBI professionally, fairly, and thoroughly; and we will continue to practice journalism in the public interest.”

That statement is precisely right, and Goldberg is precisely the editor to mean it. The Atlantic has the institutional resources, the legal infrastructure, and the editorial backbone to defend its reporters. It will not fold.

But David Graham, Goldberg’s colleague, noted something important in his own piece this morning, A Dangerous New Attack on Press Freedom: those with less ability to defend themselves may make different calculations as a result of government pressure. The local reporter. The freelancer. The journalist at a smaller outlet without The Atlantic‘s legal resources. The source inside an agency who is now watching what happened to the people who talked to Fitzpatrick.

The chilling effect is not measured in prosecutions. It is measured in stories not written, calls not returned, sources who decide the risk is not worth it. That calculation, made quietly by individuals across the country, is exactly what the administration is trying to produce.

The Enemies List, Extended

Part XXII of this series documented a qualitative shift in the administration’s approach: from stretching facts to distorting law, from seeking convictions to using the process as punishment. Former prosecutor Kyle Boynton warned that a Justice Department willing to mislead grand juries “could literally charge any American with a crime.”

Part XXIII extends the warning one step further: the target is no longer even limited to political opponents. Fitzpatrick is not on anyone’s enemies list in the traditional sense. She did not oppose Trump. She did not run against him, testify against him, prosecute him, or write opinion pieces denouncing him. She covered a government official whose conduct was a matter of significant public interest, using dozens of sources, and published a reported story.

The press freedom implications reach beyond the list entirely. When the FBI investigates a reporter for covering the FBI director, the message is not sent to politicians or prosecutors or former officials. It is sent to every journalist in the country who covers a federal agency. It is sent to every source inside those agencies who might consider speaking to one.

The enemies list began with people who had, in some sense, made themselves targets — by investigating Trump, by prosecuting him, by writing books about him, by refusing to do what he wanted. The list has now reached the reporters who simply cover what is happening.

That is not an expansion of the list. That is the elimination of the category.

The FBI Motto

David Graham’s closing observation in The Atlantic this morning is worth preserving here.

The FBI’s motto is “Fidelity, Bravery, Integrity.” The agents who are reportedly proceeding with an investigation they know is illegitimate, under threat of termination if they refuse, are embodying none of those words. They know it. They said so.

Fitzpatrick, by contrast, interviewed two dozen people, verified what she could, published what she found, stood by her reporting when Patel sued, and is now facing a federal investigation for having done so.

The FBI’s motto applies more accurately to the journalist than to the bureau investigating her.

Kash Patel sued a man for saying he was at nightclubs more than his office. A judge dismissed it. He sued The Atlantic for $250 million. That case is expected to fail. He reportedly opened a federal criminal investigation into the reporter who wrote the story.

The investigation may never produce charges. Like the Powell probe, like the Comey seashells indictment, like the SPLC case, the legal outcome may be irrelevant to the administration’s purpose. The purpose is the process. The process is the message.

Sarah Fitzpatrick did her job.

That is, apparently, sufficient.

Postscript: She Published Again Anyway

The Atlantic. Personalized Woodford Reserve bourbon bottle distributed by FBI Director Kash Patel. (The Atlantic purchased the bottle from a source who received it as a gift.)

As this essay was being finalized, Fitzpatrick published a second story.

The subject: Kash Patel’s personalized bourbon stash — custom Woodford Reserve bottles engraved with the words “Kash Patel FBI Director” and the director’s preferred spelling of his own name: Ka$h.

The story is funnier than anything a satirist could invent. Patel distributes his branded bourbon to FBI staff and civilians alike, transports cases of it on DOJ aircraft, and brought at least one case to a training seminar at Quantico where UFC athletes taught mixed martial arts to aspiring agents. When a bottle went missing at Quantico, Patel threatened to polygraph his staff. Retired agent Kurt Siuzdak, who has spent years assisting FBI whistleblowers, told Fitzpatrick that agents are contacting him for legal guidance over the bottles. His advice to current FBI employees: “I tell people to run from him.”

When a former senior FBI official was asked whether he had ever seen a previous director distribute personally branded liquor bottles, he burst out laughing.

The FBI is reportedly investigating the journalist who wrote the first story. The journalist responded by writing the second one.

The bureau’s motto is Fidelity, Bravery, Integrity. One of those words describes Sarah Fitzpatrick’s posture toward the FBI director who is investigating her. It is not the one the bureau intended as a compliment.

Ka$h Patel, FBI Director — #9, in case you were wondering — is apparently taking notes.
