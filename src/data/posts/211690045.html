Part XIII ended with a sentence I did not expect to have to revisit so soon: “The city waits.” I wrote it as a closing image, not an admission that the series itself would have to wait too. Two months later, the honest thing to do is admit that this was never going to be a thirteen-part series. It was always going to be a ledger, and ledgers do not close themselves just because the writer would prefer to move on to something with a resolution.

Two months, in this conflict, is not a pause. It is an accounting period. Here is what accrued.

The West Bank Is Being Bankrupted, on Purpose

The New York Times published a piece on August 18 that reads, in places, like the appendix Part IX should have had. It documents what Israeli financial policy has done to the West Bank economy since October 2023, and the word that keeps appearing — from Palestinian officials, from banking executives, from the Palestinian Authority’s own prime minister — is “collapse.” Not decline. Not hardship. Collapse.

The mechanism is not complicated, which is part of what makes it damning. Israel controls the flow of money into and out of the West Bank because the Oslo Accords made the shekel the territory’s currency and tied Palestinian banks to Israeli ones. That arrangement, negotiated as a technical convenience in the 1990s, has become a financial chokehold that requires no military occupation to operate — just a withheld signature. Since May 2025, Israel has confiscated the import-tax revenue it collects on the Palestinian Authority’s behalf, revenue that accounts for roughly two-thirds of a shrunken 2026 budget of about six billion dollars. The Palestinian Authority has responded by cutting the salaries of 140,000 civil servants and security officers, shortening the school week to three days, and letting pharmacies run out of medication for patients like Ibtisam Hammad, who was diagnosed with breast cancer in the spring and considers herself lucky when three of her six prescriptions are in stock.

Unemployment has hit 28 percent, more than double the pre-war rate. Roughly 5.7 billion dollars sits frozen in West Bank bank vaults because Israel restricts how much can be transferred to Israeli banks — a restriction so severe that Israeli banks themselves are now threatening to sever the relationship entirely, worried about money-laundering exposure, while the Israeli government that benefits from the arrangement does nothing to protect them from that exposure. This is a government using its own banks as leverage against a population it is simultaneously starving of currency.

None of this required a single new settlement, a single new checkpoint, or a single headline-generating military operation. It required a spreadsheet and the will to use it. Part IX called the settlement project “slow annexation.” This is faster, and it does not need bulldozers.

The Siege That Doesn’t Need a Wall: Qusra and the Civilian Policing Handover

If the financial chokehold is annexation by spreadsheet, what has been happening in the northern West Bank village of Qusra this month is annexation by mob, with the state’s blessing trailing a respectful few steps behind.

For more than a week in mid-August, Israeli settlers laid siege to Qusra, blocking roads and cutting off water and humanitarian access to Palestinian families — a siege serious enough that the United States reportedly asked to send a delegation to see it directly and asked senior Israeli defence officials to explain, in person, how they intend to address it. This is not an isolated incident dressed up as a trend. The UN’s humanitarian coordination office recorded July, jointly with March, as the deadliest month for settler-related incidents in the West Bank since October 2023 — more than 1,380 settler-related incidents since the start of the year, averaging 6.6 a day. Settler attacks now account for 55 percent of Palestinian injuries in the West Bank in 2026, up from an incident every three days in 2020 to more than three a day now. Demolitions and settler violence are displacing an average of seventeen people daily, roughly double the rate of the previous three years.

The Israeli government’s response to this has not been to rein in the settlers. It has been to formalize their position. National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir has pushed to transfer West Bank civilian policing from the Israeli military to Israeli police — a bureaucratic-sounding shift that Al Jazeera and other observers have described, correctly, as cementing de facto annexation and empowering settler militias with something closer to state authority rather than merely state tolerance. Part IX asked how many settlers it would take to make a Palestinian state physically impossible. That question has been quietly superseded by a different one: how many settlers does it take to make Palestinian civilian life in the West Bank itself impossible, without anyone in Jerusalem having to say the word “annexation” out loud.

Gaza: The Ceasefire That Kills 160 People a Month

The October 2025 ceasefire remains, on paper, in force. In practice, UN human rights experts reported this month that Israeli forces killed roughly 160 Palestinians in Gaza in July alone, bringing the death toll since the “ceasefire” began past 1,200. That is not a violation of the ceasefire in the sense of an occasional lapse. That is a ceasefire that has never actually stopped the killing — it has only changed its pace and its cover story.

Late last month, Donald Trump announced what his administration billed as a historic breakthrough: a fifteen-point plan, produced through his “Board of Peace,” for disarming Hamas and moving the ceasefire into its long-promised second phase. Within days, Netanyahu publicly rejected it — an unusually open break with a president who has otherwise given Israel nearly everything it has asked for. Israeli strikes on Gaza intensified rather than eased in the days that followed. Egypt, Jordan, the UAE, Qatar, and Turkey have jointly blamed Israel for thwarting the plan and jeopardizing what remained of the process. Meanwhile the “yellow line” — the barrier demarcating Israeli-occupied Gaza from the rest of the strip — has continued to move westward, with new concrete barriers cutting off displaced families from what few unobstructed roads remained, and killings continuing to occur well away from the line itself, suggesting it was never a meaningful constraint on Israeli operations to begin with.

Part XI closed its hostage accounting with a number that felt, at the time, like an ending: 168 recovered alive, 87 deceased, all accounted for. The number that needs adding now is not about hostages. It is about everyone else, and it has not stopped climbing.

Is “Pariah State” Now the Accurate Word?

Here is where I have to be honest about my own instinct on this, because the instinct — that Israel has become something close to a pariah state — is one I share, and a series built on “an honest accounting” owes its reader the discipline of checking an instinct against evidence rather than just restating it more forcefully.

The evidence is genuinely substantial. Since last autumn, Canada, France, and the United Kingdom have joined the roughly 150 countries recognizing a Palestinian state, a diplomatic realignment among Israel’s most significant Western partners that would have been difficult to imagine a decade ago. Belgium has announced a package of sanctions targeting settlement goods and public procurement with Israeli companies. The International Criminal Court’s prosecutor sought an arrest warrant for Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich, adding to the warrant already outstanding for Netanyahu himself; National Security Minister Ben-Gvir has been barred from entering France and Poland. Broadcasters in Ireland, Spain, the Netherlands, Iceland, and Slovenia moved to boycott Eurovision over Israeli participation — a symbolic fight, but one that a former Israeli ambassador to South Africa pointed to explicitly as following the sporting-boycott playbook that helped isolate the apartheid regime. RAND Corporation analysts, writing in The New York Times in May, described Israel’s isolation as “deepening fast,” visible in polling, social media, boycotts, and the erosion of the bipartisan American safety net that has protected Israeli policy from consequence for half a century — the very architecture Part XII spent several thousand words describing as the thing that would have to crack before anything else could change.

That architecture is cracking specifically inside the constituency that mattered most to my argument in Part XII: young American Republicans. The generational shift documented there in March polling has not reversed. If anything, the events of the past two months — a Gaza ceasefire that keeps producing bodies, a West Bank siege the United States felt compelled to send officials to witness directly — give that shift more material to work with, not less.

So: is “pariah state” accurate? Partially, and the imprecision matters. Israel remains deeply embedded in Western military supply chains, retains warm relations with several Gulf states under the Abraham Accords framework, and continues to receive the American veto whenever a Security Council resolution threatens actual consequence rather than rhetorical condemnation. A state that still receives 3.8 billion dollars a year in American military aid and an unbroken Security Council veto is not a pariah state in the way apartheid South Africa eventually became one — genuinely friendless among the powers that mattered. What Israel has become is something narrower and, in its way, more unstable: a state increasingly estranged from the cultural, diplomatic, and generational consensus of the Western democracies whose material and military support it still depends on entirely. The isolation is real. The dependency that would make isolation consequential has not yet broken. That gap — between the vote of no confidence and the funding that continues regardless — is the same gap Part XIII identified between political trajectory and political institution. It has not closed. It has just become more visible, week by week, in a way that is harder for anyone paying attention to pretend not to notice.

October 27

Which brings the ledger to its most consequential open item. Israel goes to the polls on October 27 — the first election since October 7th, the first test of whether any of this has moved Israeli domestic politics rather than only its international standing. Netanyahu is behind but not defeated. Gadi Eisenkot, the former IDF chief of staff who has emerged as the strongest opposition figure, has not secured a governing coalition, and Israeli commentators are already warning the vote may not produce a decisive outcome — that Israel could be heading back to the polls again if no bloc reaches the sixty-one seats required to govern. Security and the multi-front war remain the top issue for roughly a third of voters; nearly 30 percent of those who say they are switching allegiance cite October 7th itself as the reason.

This is the number that will tell us, more than any UN vote or Eurovision boycott, whether the isolation I have just described has actually reached the electorate whose government produces the policy. A Netanyahu victory, or a coalition that simply replaces him with an equally unyielding right-wing bloc, would confirm that international isolation and domestic political consequence remain two separate phenomena in Israel, however much they have converged in Washington. A genuine change of government would be the first evidence since Camp David that the domestic architecture Part XII described as needing to crack has actually produced a different outcome, rather than merely a different mood.

I don’t know which of those it will be. Neither, from what I can find in the polling, does anyone else with real confidence. That uncertainty is itself the honest place to end this instalment — not because uncertainty is satisfying, but because a series that has spent thirteen parts insisting on precision over comfortable narrative owes its reader the same discipline here. The compound interest is still accruing. The next payment comes due on October 27th, and whether it is paid down or simply rolled over again is not yet written.
