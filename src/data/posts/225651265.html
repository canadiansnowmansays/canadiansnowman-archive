At the opera, the reviews come out the next morning, written by critics who were in the stalls and never saw backstage. In Shenzhen there were almost no critics in the stalls. China kept tight control of coverage, a Bloomberg reporter was barred from the trilateral for shouting a question about Jensen Huang, and reporters who were not in the travelling pool never saw the American president in person. So two of the three performers wrote their own reviews, and the third flew home.

When the meeting at Kylin Villa ended on Thursday after about 80 minutes, Trump went directly to the airport, where China’s foreign minister, Wang Yi, saw him off with a military band. There was no news conference. No White House or State Department official came to the press filing centre to explain what had happened, which Peter Baker of The New York Times noted is how presidential trips used to end. The White House did not immediately put out its own account of the meeting, CBS News reported, though it suggested the president might talk to reporters on Air Force One. What it did post, according to The Associated Press, was a clip of Trump helping Xi up an escalator.

So the record of the first summit in history between the leaders of America, China and Russia comes in two editions: the one printed in Moscow and the one printed in Beijing. They are close but not identical, and the differences are instructive.

The Moscow edition

The Kremlin moved first. Its spokesman, Dmitri Peskov, told Russian state media that a joint statement would be published within the hour, and Russia then released a text it said all three leaders had agreed. The statement has four points, according to Paul Sonne of the Times: a commitment to reform the United Nations; an agreement to raise the production and supply of petroleum; a declaration that immunity for state officials is “of fundamental importance” and that the International Criminal Court must not abuse its authority; and a commitment to dialogue on reducing nuclear risks, on the principle that “a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought.”

That last line is not new. Ronald Reagan and Mikhail Gorbachev put it in their joint statement at Geneva in 1985, at a summit that opened on November 19, forty-one years to the day before Shenzhen, and the five permanent members of the Security Council repeated it in January 2022, five weeks before Russia invaded Ukraine. It is the oldest aria in the nuclear repertoire, and it commits nobody to anything.

The second point is the strangest. As Somini Sengupta of The New York Times noted, the oil prices the three leaders promised to ease were driven up by wars that two of them are fighting, Russia’s in Ukraine and America’s in Iran, and Russia’s own refineries have been damaged in recent months by Ukrainian attacks. The pledge came while the world’s climate negotiators were discussing how to burn less oil, and on the day the International Energy Agency concluded that the Iran war had permanently reduced many countries’ appetite for imported crude. It also papers over a real divide. Russia and the United States want the world to keep running on oil; China makes most of the solar panels, batteries and electric cars that are replacing it.

The third point is the one with an edge. Putin is the subject of an ICC arrest warrant over the deportation of Ukrainian children, and it has shaped his travel for three years: it kept him away from APEC summits in Peru and South Korea, both members of the court. Trump had promised in his opening remarks to propose ways for the three countries to protect their sovereignty from “rogue globalist international organizations such as the International Criminal Court, which has gotten out of control.” Peter Baker of The Times recalled that Marco Rubio had recently told the Federalist Society that if the court tried to enforce its “paper decrees” on Americans, the answer would be Pompey’s: “Stop quoting laws to men with swords.” Pete Hegseth reposted the line in capitals. Of all the things the three men could have agreed in Shenzhen, the most concrete is a statement of solidarity with the one of them who has a warrant out for his arrest.

The Kremlin’s account of the separate Trump–Putin meeting was more modest. Yuri Ushakov, Putin’s foreign policy aide, said it lasted 35 to 40 minutes, was “of a preparatory nature” ahead of the trilateral, and that “neither side made any particularly important statements,” according to a report carried by Yahoo News. Ukraine was the main topic, and Putin left in “an excellent mood.”

Then Putin held the news conference that Trump did not. He called the meeting “a new page” in diplomatic history and said he and Trump shared a common approach to “civilizational values.” Discussing democracy, he asked whether it was really the liberal order “certain circles” had tried to impose on the world, or “a liberal dictatorship, if you will.” Then he dismantled what was left of the Ukraine agenda. He said he would not negotiate any limited ceasefires, citing Ukraine’s attacks on the bridge to Crimea, cast doubt on Washington’s request to bring the Europeans into the peace talks, and was sceptical about accepting Trump’s invitation to the G20 in Miami next month. None of that appears in the joint statement.

The Beijing edition

Xinhua published the joint statement too, and David Pierson of the Times read it closely. It opens with Russia and the United States thanking China for hosting “a historic meeting,” which casts Xi as the convener of the great powers in the first line. It endorses “the principle of state consent,” the idea that countries cannot be bound by international courts without their agreement. Beijing has invoked that principle for a decade to reject the 2016 tribunal ruling against its claims in the South China Sea, a ruling won by the Philippines, the treaty ally Trump had visited two days earlier.

On nuclear weapons, the issue Trump called his top priority, Pierson observed that the three leaders agreed only to discuss reducing risks and preventing proliferation. It is a framework Beijing can support precisely because it places no limits on China’s arsenal, which is growing faster than anyone’s. Xi described the trilateral as “positive, constructive and fruitful.”

The Chinese account of Xi’s own meeting with Trump, held over lunch before the trilateral and lasting about an hour, was cooler and more specific. The two sides found the meeting “positive and constructive” and agreed to draw up an action plan for “a constructive China-U.S. relationship of strategic stability on the basis of respect, fairness and reciprocity,” according to the summary published by the Chinese embassy in Washington. Then came the warning. On Taiwan, Xi said, the United States “should insist on doing the right thing to avoid major setbacks in China-U.S. relations.” Pierson read that as a plea for Trump to keep holding back a $14 billion arms sale to the island, though American law has required Washington since 1979 to provide Taiwan with defensive arms. Xi added that keeping relations stable depended on “taking concrete actions to implement the consensus reached by the two heads of state,” which is diplomatic shorthand for not confronting China on Taiwan and trade.

In the trilateral itself, Xi was more expansive than either of his guests. “A transformation not seen in a century is accelerating,” he said, a line Edward Wong of The Times traced to Xi’s farewell to Putin in Moscow three years ago, when he added, “we are the ones driving these changes together.” Xi had used it on Trump in Beijing in May too. He called for the three countries to set up “crisis communication and prevention mechanisms to ensure that misjudgments are minimized,” something close to a three-way hotline. Washington and Beijing announced a direct channel for AI incidents last week, but Edward Wong and Dustin Volz of The Times found little progress on AI safety between the two governments, and in Shenzhen, Katie Rogers and David E. Sanger of The Times noted, none of the three leaders mentioned AI at all. Without naming the United States, Xi urged them to “abandon Cold War thinking” and avoid “decoupling and supply chain disruptions,” Beijing’s standard language against American export controls, including the chip rule Washington issued on Monday. And he described China, the United States and the Soviet Union as “the backbone of the world’s anti-fascist struggle” in the Second World War, a reference his government has used all year against Japan.

The Beijing edition also carries a risk for its author. Xi has spent years insisting that every country, “big or small,” deserves an equal voice in world affairs. A statement in which three nuclear powers announce their commitment to reforming the United Nations, with nobody else in the room, does not look like that. Nor did Beijing’s choreography treat its two guests alike. At Wednesday’s banquet, Wong noted, Trump sat beside Xi at the head table while Putin was placed farther away, next to Xi’s wife, Peng Liyuan. John Delury of the Hopkins-Nanjing Center told him that Xi and Putin see themselves as great men of history, and that “even Trump can be seen as epiphenomenal in their eyes.”

The Washington edition

There is none, at least not yet. What exists is what the American president said in front of the cameras, which was more scripted than usual and less specific than the occasion seemed to require.

In the trilateral, Trump read from prepared remarks. The three leaders had “a special responsibility” to keep talking, he said, and the United States, Russia and China were “uniquely positioned to discuss how to reduce the number of nuclear weapons on Earth.” He made the case for talking to adversaries: “No problems were ever solved under the old school, refusing to talk about it.” But he also observed that “it’s very hard to give them up as a leader of a country.” David E. Sanger of the Times pointed out that his predecessors had done exactly that. In the 1960s the United States had more than 30,000 warheads; today it has a little more than 5,000. What Trump did not offer was a mechanism. He did not propose a treaty to replace New START, which expired in February.

At his meeting with Xi, he praised their “outstanding personal relationship” and asked China to “do some more for our farmers.” “China has an insatiable appetite, I can tell you that, and we can fill that appetite,” he said. China has pledged to buy at least $17 billion of American farm goods a year through 2028; by September it had bought about $3.9 billion, excluding soybeans. He told Xi that “as long as you and I are in charge of things, we’re never going to have a problem,” an odd promise from a president whose party had just lost both houses of Congress, said to a leader who faces no such elections. That morning he had explained to Fox News how he would handle the new Congress: “They could do all the bills they want. I just go veto, veto, veto.” Baker observed that Chuck Schumer and Hakeem Jeffries have yet to be invited to the White House. Xi and Putin each got “It’s an honor for me to be with you.” And he paid his host a compliment that doubled as a complaint: “It’s too bad the media couldn’t see some of it, because every move was perfectly executed.”

The rest of the American edition has to be assembled from that Fox interview, recorded before the summit. Asked whether he would seek the other leaders’ help in ending the war with Iran, he said, “We don’t need any help.” Rogers and Sanger pointed out that American intelligence has concluded that China and Russia both supplied technology or targeting information that likely helped Iran strike American bases. On nuclear cuts, he conceded: “For me, it’s a hard thing. And it’s probably hard for them to do also.” On espionage he was relaxed: “We spy on them too.” He did not answer a Bloomberg reporter who asked whether Jensen Huang, who resigned from Nvidia after American intelligence determined that a woman he had been involved with was a Chinese asset, had been disloyal to the United States. On Ukraine, he said in the room that “things are happening.” And he marvelled that Xi had met him at the airport: “He’s never gone to the airport to meet somebody.”

The reviews

The professional critics, writing from outside the room, were broadly agreed on who won. Pierson concluded that the meeting gave Xi everything analysts said he wanted: the prestige of convening the world’s two largest nuclear powers without committing to anything that might burden Beijing, whether a Ukraine peace plan or arms control. Paul Sonne called it a big win for Putin, who stood on the same pedestal as Trump and Xi although Russia trails both on almost every measure of power except nuclear weapons. In the APEC family photo, his first at a summit since the full-scale invasion, he stood next to Xi, with Trump on Xi’s other side. Sanger noted that Trump’s first-term strategy had named Russia and China “revisionist powers.” On Thursday he called both men “my friend,” nearly two years into a détente with little to show for it. Nicole Grajewski of Sciences Po called the nuclear commitment “very empty.” In Brussels, asked about Trump calling Putin his friend, a European Commission spokeswoman said, “In general, we do not comment on friendship.”

One critic had written his review before the curtain rose. Kurt Campbell, writing in The Times, predicted that Xi, who dislikes surprises, would agree to no spontaneous deals, and that the real product would be the picture of “two strongmen and a would-be third standing together astride the planet.” Campbell was Biden’s deputy secretary of state, so he is hardly a neutral witness. But his forecast held almost to the letter.

The leaders who were not at the table had their own reviews. In his APEC speech, Prime Minister Anthony Albanese of Australia addressed Putin directly: “End your war, end it now.” Volodymyr Zelensky told NBC News that “you can’t decide about some country, even a small country, without this country.” Mark Carney said he would stand in the family photo “out of respect for the host.”

Part IV ended with Charles Kupchan’s case in Foreign Affairs that the meeting was worth having, so long as Trump held three lines: no closed-door bargain on Ukraine forced on Kyiv, no ground given on Taiwan, and no early release of Putin from the penalty box. On the evidence published so far, the first line held, mainly because nothing was agreed on Ukraine at all, and Putin ruled out even limited ceasefires within hours. The second cannot be judged, because the only account of what was said about Taiwan is Beijing’s, and it records only Xi’s side of the conversation. The third did not hold. Putin was not merely let out of the penalty box. He left with a three-power statement that courts like the one that indicted him must not overreach.

Curtain

An opera has a libretto, published in advance, and a performance, which is what the audience actually sees. In Shenzhen the libretto was written afterwards, by two of the three singers, and the third did not contribute a word. That is the real distinction between the editions. Moscow and Beijing each knew exactly what they wanted the record to say, and said it within the hour. Washington left the record to its partners and flew home with the director’s compliments.

Washington’s edition did eventually arrive, a day late, from the White House briefing room. That is Part VI.
