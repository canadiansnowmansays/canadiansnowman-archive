Sleeve notes, Third Reissue. Digitally remastered for an era that no longer requires subtlety.

In 1946, a pinball machine offered everything but control — bumpers, lights, a spring-loaded plunger, and then the ball belonged to the machine. A year later, in 1947, someone bolted flippers onto the cabinet, and the player got a say for the first time. That’s the whole of it, really: forty-six is the year you’re just along for the ride, and forty-seven is the year somebody finally lets you hit back. Donald Trump was born in the gap between those two years, which is either a coincidence or the most on-the-nose biographical detail American politics has produced since William Howard Taft got stuck in a bathtub.

We mention this because someone had to. As John Dickerson wrote in The Atlantic on September 20, 2026, the country currently experiences the presidency the way a spectator experiences a pinball table it isn’t allowed to touch — the ballroom, the war, the portraits of himself as a general and a pope, tariffs on, tariffs off, tariffs on again, all caroming past at flipper speed while the machine keeps the score. We read that, thought of a certain 1969 rock opera about a boy who goes silent after trauma and discovers he’s unaccountably brilliant at a game of lights and bells, and concluded that somebody in the greater Trump cinematic universe owes The Who a licensing fee they will obviously never pay.

So here, in the spirit of every unauthorized biopic Netflix has ever greenlit, is the treatment. Track titles and lyric fragments below are entirely original — no relation to any existing rock opera about pinball, deafness, or wizardry, living or dead, other than the concept itself, which we are stealing outright and not apologizing for.

ACT ONE: THE ACCIDENT

Our hero is not struck deaf, dumb, and blind by trauma in a mirror, as in certain other, better-known productions. He is born already unable to hear the things he doesn’t wish to hear, already unable to see the things that don’t flatter him, and — despite forty years of evidence to the contrary — congenitally incapable of going quiet. It is, the show’s programme notes concede, a somewhat harder condition to stage sympathetically.

Track 1 — “Nobody Taught Him, He Just Knows” A jaunty overture in which the ensemble marvels that the hero requires no coaching to dominate the machine — he simply arrived pre-loaded with instinct, confidence, and an total absence of interest in anyone else’s score.

He never studied the table, never learned the angles, never once asked how the old machines were wired — he just leaned in and started swinging, and somehow, somehow, the lights came on for him.

Track 2 — "Keep Him Away From the Mirror" The chorus, worried, tries to keep other reflective surfaces out of reach after what happened with the last one. As the Associated Press reported, our hero and his team have taken to generating self-portraits of himself as a Jedi wielding a lightsaber, as the pope, and as a king — a spree the chorus in our production treats not as a plot twist but as background wallpaper, the way you'd treat weather.

ACT TWO: HE SURE PLAYS A MEAN MACHINE

Word spreads. The boy — sorry, the man — is unbeatable. Not because he reads the table better than anyone alive, but because he has simply declared himself the highest score on the board and dared the machine to disagree. The machine, being a machine, has no comment. The people watching do, but their comments do not appear to register.

Track 3 — "70 Percent Gilding" A vaudeville number, tap-shoes optional, built on a single stray statistic that refused to leave us alone. As Maggie Haberman told Axios, roughly seventy percent of the hero's mindshare goes to gilding, construction, renovation, renaming, and tribute projects — a figure she says has no precedent among sitting presidents devising such endeavours in their own name. The song does not editorialize. The song simply counts to seventy and stops.

Seven in ten, seven in ten, what a use of a country’s attention, seven in ten, seven in ten — and the other three are also about him.

Track 4 — “Thirty-Seven Tweets About a Football Game” A comparative duet. One voice sings the six tweets about Charlottesville, in which the words racism, bigotry, and white nationalism never once appear. The other voice sings the thirty-seven tweets about NFL players kneeling. The two voices are not given equal stage time, on purpose, because neither was the attention.

Track 5 — “The Big Yawn (A Housing Bill Waltz)” In which a bipartisan bill to address high housing costs reaches the hero’s desk, and he calls it boring on his way to not signing it, and it becomes law anyway, without him, because the machine — infuriatingly, gloriously — does not actually require the player’s cooperation to keep running.

ACT THREE: THE URGENT AND THE IMPORTANT

Here the show pauses for its Eisenhower interlude, which test audiences found confusing and which we are keeping anyway, out of spite. As Dickerson noted, Eisenhower liked to say he had two kinds of problems: the urgent, which are never important, and the important, which are never urgent. Our hero has solved this ancient managerial riddle by declaring that everything is both urgent and important exactly insofar as it involves him, and neither urgent nor important the moment it doesn’t.

Track 6 — “The Deficit Can Wait, the Ballroom Cannot” A rousing march. The ensemble carries a wrecking ball across the stage. Somewhere off-stage, a national debt clock continues incrementing, unbothered, unphotographed, and — crucially — un-gilded.

Track 7 — “Good, I’m Glad” The show’s darkest number, and the one the director nearly cut. A death is announced. The hero does not observe the customary silence. The stage goes black for four full bars, on purpose, so the silence he refuses to give can happen anyway, just not from him.

ACT FOUR: 1947, OR, THE FLIPPER PROBLEM

The rock opera’s uncomfortable final insight — the one the marketing department begged us to soften — is that the hero was never actually playing pinball at all. He was the machine. Bumpers, lights, the whole caroming apparatus, with the rest of the country standing where the player is supposed to stand, hands on a plunger that only fires the ball once every four years.

Track 8 (Finale) — “Somebody Add the Flipper” The full company, arms raised, addressed directly at the audience — the actual audience, in the actual theatre, in 2026, ahead of an actual election.

Nineteen forty-six, you just watch the bumpers, nineteen forty-seven, you finally get a say — so don’t ask us who’s the wizard, ask yourself who’s still just watching, because the flipper’s sitting right there, and it has been the whole time.

Liner note, small print, bottom of the sleeve: This production is not endorsed by, affiliated with, or in any way connected to the 1969 rock opera it is obviously gesturing at from across a crowded room. All lyrics herein are original compositions. Any resemblance to a certain deaf, dumb, and blind kid who sure plays a mean pinball is, legally speaking, a coincidence, and, artistically speaking, the entire point. A separate, unrelated 1964 television programme about a heroic dolphin who also answered to "Flipper" is acknowledged here purely for completeness, and because someone in the audience was always going to notice regardless of what we said.
