Two men who agreed that AI could end the world, then disagreed about who should profit from it. The Oakland courthouse will sort out the details. Visual by Grok AI.

The most consequential corporate governance trial in the history of artificial intelligence opened on April 29, 2026, in a federal courthouse in downtown Oakland, under fluorescent lights, with a broken microphone.

Steven Molo, lead counsel for Elon Musk, began his opening argument. Confused looks swept the courtroom. “We can’t hear you,” said Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers. Someone fixed the microphone. Later, as Molo began to call into question Sam Altman’s integrity, the microphone cut out again and the presentation disappeared from the screens. “We are funded by the federal government,” the judge said. “The judiciary is happy to take more funds.”

The trial that could reshape the future of artificial intelligence opened with a technical glitch and a joke about underfunding. Between the two men at the center of it, Musk and Altman run technology companies worth many trillions of dollars that promise to reshape civilization. The fluorescent lights did not care.

“AI is less regulated in America than sandwiches,” Max Tegmark of the Future of Life Institute said as the trial opened. “You can’t open a sandwich shop without having your kitchen inspected. But you can release an AI girlfriend for 11-year-olds and that’s fine.” He was not being hyperbolic. He was being precise. The regulatory architecture available to address what the trial reveals is, in its entirety, roughly equivalent to the health code for a deli counter. The trial is real. The oversight is not.

The same week the trial opened, Congress offered its response to the AI governance crisis. Representative Ted Lieu of California, lead Democrat on the bipartisan House AI Task Force, introduced a bill he described as “a step forward.” It would crack down on deepfake distribution, protect whistleblowers who report AI safety violations, require US participation in international standards bodies, and establish a prize competition for AI research. Lieu was candid about what it was not: “It is not designed to be controversial.” It avoids federal preemption of state AI laws. It avoids testing requirements for AI systems used in critical infrastructure. It avoids every question that would require confronting the industry’s actual power.

In Oakland, a jury was being seated to hear a case that cannot avoid those questions. The contrast is the story.

The AI Boom’s Founding Feud

Matteo Wong, writing in The Atlantic, offered the cleanest frame for what the trial actually represents: this is not a corporate dispute. It is the AI boom’s founding feud — and its ramifications go beyond any company or executive.

The conflict between Musk and Altman has directly shaped the course of the AI industry. Disagreements over the direction of Google DeepMind led to the creation of OpenAI. Disagreements inside OpenAI led Musk to leave and eventually found xAI. A few years later, Dario Amodei and six other OpenAI employees split off to form Anthropic, trusting neither OpenAI’s structure nor its leadership to prioritize humanity over financial gain. Mark Zuckerberg, whom Musk asked in 2025 about joining forces to purchase OpenAI — according to texts released in pretrial discovery — has since spent tens or hundreds of billions overhauling Meta’s AI operation. The very schism that started with Musk and Altman keeps recurring.

The more cynical description, as Wong puts it, is that the AI boom is shaped by a very small group of men, nearly all of whom claim to be the best steward of humanity while being largely dismissive of their competition. The goal of creating an organizational structure to provide a check on a CEO has all but withered away. An independent board was supposed to govern OpenAI — but the company has basically been Altman’s fiefdom, just as Anthropic is Amodei’s and xAI is Musk’s.

Both sides made the issue of concentration of power central to their arguments. “If you have someone that’s not trustworthy in charge of AI,” Musk testified, “I think that’s very dangerous to the whole world.” OpenAI’s defense said that “one person having control wasn’t consistent with OpenAI’s core mission.” Wong’s verdict on this exchange is the sharpest sentence written about the trial so far: “Apparently, the irony was lost on everyone.”

What the Ultra Vires Doctrine Actually Means

Musk’s lawsuit rests on a legal doctrine not used in a high-profile case for approximately one hundred years. Ultra vires — Latin for “beyond the powers” — holds that a corporation is restricted to the activities defined in its founding charter. In the early nineteenth century, when the federal government was small and weak, ultra vires was the primary mechanism for constraining corporate behaviour. It fell largely into disuse as corporations developed wide-ranging charters and federal regulators took over. But there is one exception: nonprofits. A nonprofit exists to serve a defined public purpose. Its charter is a public promise. When it acts beyond that purpose, ultra vires remains available — because no other mechanism exists.

This is the governance gap made visible in a courtroom. The only law capable of holding OpenAI accountable for its founding promise is a doctrine designed for a world that existed before the SEC, before the FTC, before any of the regulatory architecture that was supposed to make ultra vires unnecessary. Congress has not passed AI legislation. Federal regulators have been hobbled. What remains is a nineteenth century legal tool, wielded by the world’s richest man, in a courthouse named after a Democratic Socialist.

Former FTC official Shaoul Sussman put it plainly: “This is the first high-profile case that I know of being pursued under these statutes for 100 years.”

What the Trial Exhibits Reveal

Before opening statements began, Musk had already posted more than two dozen times on X about OpenAI, Altman, and the trial. He owns the platform. He has 240 million followers. He called Altman “Scam Altman” and Brockman “Greg Stockman.” He promoted the Farrow-Marantz New Yorker investigation — examined in Part 28 — by boosting a weeks-old post to his feed. Judge Gonzalez Rogers called him to the bench before testimony began. “How can we get things done without you making things worse outside the courtroom?” she asked. Musk, Altman, and Brockman agreed to keep social media to a minimum. Musk’s agreement lasted approximately as long as you would expect from a man who, in March, was found partly liable by a separate jury for defrauding investors in his Twitter acquisition.

The trial has already produced material that would not otherwise have seen the light. A 2015 email from Sam Altman to Musk reads: “Do you have any objection to me proactively increasing everyone’s comp by 100-200k per year? I think they’re all motivated by the mission here but it would be a good signal to everyone we are going to take care of them over time.” The nonprofit was negotiating Silicon Valley compensation packages before the ink on its founding charter was dry.

More significant is a 2018 email from Musk himself to OpenAI leadership, saying that merging OpenAI with Tesla “is the only path that could even hope to hold a candle to Google.” This is the exhibit that cuts most directly against Musk’s central argument. He is suing Altman for concluding that OpenAI could not survive as a nonprofit — while the documentary record shows Musk reached the same conclusion two years before he left. OpenAI’s lead counsel William Savitt was direct: “Mr. Musk’s lawsuit is a pageant of hypocrisy.” He added, with less rhetorical formality, that Musk had “sour grapes.”

On the stand, Musk framed the case in the starkest possible moral terms. “It is not OK to steal a charity,” he testified. If Altman wins, he said, it would “give license to looting every charity in America.” His testimony ranged across Star Trek, The Terminator, and his brain-chip company Neuralink — a range that reflects either the genuine scope of the AI stakes or the instincts of a man who understands that juries respond to narrative. Altman, for his part, has said of Musk: “Probably his whole life is from a position of insecurity. I feel for the guy.”

A detail on the witness list deserves its own sentence. Shivon Zilis — former OpenAI board member, mother of at least four of Musk’s children, and his self-described romantic partner — is scheduled to testify. Asked about her in court, Musk described her as “my chief of staff and uh, well, yeah,” and smirked. She is also the person OpenAI claims Musk used to keep tabs on the company after he left. The governance of the most consequential technology in human history involves this cast of characters. In a courtroom in Oakland. Under fluorescent lights.

No Happy Ending

Sacha Haworth, executive director of the Tech Oversight Project, has articulated the governance problem with the trial more clearly than anyone else involved.

If Musk wins, she said, it would weaken or even destroy OpenAI — “opening up a large share of the market that an Elon Musk company can then gobble up.” The plaintiff is not a disinterested guardian of the public trust. He is a competitor with a grievance, a litigation strategy, a social media platform with 240 million followers, and a 2018 email in the trial record showing he drew the same conclusion about OpenAI’s nonprofit future that he is now suing Altman for reaching.

If OpenAI gets the suit dismissed, “it would send a signal that it’s OK to launch as a non-threatening nonprofit working for the public’s benefit and then cynically change to a for-profit without any accountability.”

“Mr. Musk’s lawsuit is a pageant of hypocrisy,” said OpenAI’s counsel. “There’s no happy ending here,” said the Tech Oversight Project. Both of these things are true simultaneously. That is the governance condition.

The judge’s question to Musk at the bench — “How can we get things done without you making things worse outside the courtroom?” — is a reasonable summary of the governance problem the entire AI industry presents to democratic institutions. The tools available to the court are real but limited. A gag order. A request for a clean slate. An agreement to keep things to a minimum on a platform the man owns and can algorithmically amplify. The institutional machinery is doing its best. The machinery was not built for this.

Max Tegmark, whose Future of Life Institute gives OpenAI a C+ for safety and xAI a D, is nonetheless watching the trial carefully. “Trials are all we have right now,” he said. “Trials provide information that is not otherwise accessible.” He is right. But evidence is not governance. A verdict is not regulation. And a plaintiff who scores worse on AI safety than the defendant is not the foundation on which a governance architecture should rest.

What Congress Offered Instead

Representative Lieu’s bill is not nothing. Whistleblower protections for people who report AI safety violations are genuinely useful — particularly given what the Farrow-Marantz investigation revealed about OpenAI’s internal culture and the absence of any written record from the board’s law firm investigation. Deepfake penalties address a real harm. International standards participation is better than isolation.

But the bill explicitly avoids the questions that matter most. No federal preemption standard. No testing requirements for critical infrastructure. No mandatory safety evaluations before deployment. No enforceable accountability for companies whose models can, in Tegmark’s phrase, be released to eleven-year-olds without a kitchen inspection.

Lieu explained the avoidance: the bill is “based on bipartisan legislation that other members have introduced.” Bipartisan, in the current Congress, means acceptable to both parties’ donors. The AI industry has flooded Washington with money — OpenAI co-founder Greg Brockman gave millions to a PAC specifically designed to knock down AI regulation. The bill that emerges from a legislature swimming in that money is, predictably, a bill about deepfakes.

The sandwich inspector does not work for the sandwich shop. The AI regulator, such as they are, increasingly does.

The Asimov Frame

Asimov’s governance insight was not that robots needed laws. It was that the laws needed to be explicit, public, and prior — established before the capability existed, not improvised after the damage was done.

The Three Laws were not perfect. Asimov spent his career writing stories about their edge cases and unintended consequences. But they were knowable. Every person in his fictional universe could read them, debate them, hold the robots accountable to them. The drama came from the gap between the letter of the law and the complexity of reality — not from uncertainty about whether any law existed.

In Oakland, a federal jury is being asked to apply a nineteenth century doctrine to a twenty-first century technology because no twentieth century law was written to cover the gap. In Washington, a bipartisan task force has produced a bill about deepfakes. In San Francisco, OpenAI is preparing its IPO. On X, the plaintiff is posting about the trial he agreed to stop posting about. In the courthouse, both sides are arguing about concentration of power while each man runs his own personal AI fiefdom.

Sam Altman arrives at the Ronald V. Dellums Federal Courthouse, Oakland, May 12, 2026 — his first day of testimony in the trial his co-founder brought against him. Photo: Benjamin Fanjoy / Getty Images.

Sam Altman took the witness stand for the first time in his life today. Musk’s lead counsel asked him directly: “Are you completely trustworthy?” Altman answered: “I believe so.” There was an audible gasp. The question is the question this series has been asking for thirty-one parts. The answer is what you get when there are no structures capable of producing a better one.

Asimov would have recognized the shape of the problem. The technology arrived. The laws did not. And the people most responsible for building the technology are now arguing about it in a courthouse named for a man who believed corporate power was the primary obstacle to human flourishing.

The noblest Roman of them all, in this production, is the Tech Oversight Project director who said there is no happy ending. She is right. But unhappy endings can be instructive. The trial will produce a record. The record will outlast the verdict. And the question it is asking — whether a public promise made to build technology for humanity’s benefit can be broken without consequence — will not be resolved by whatever Judge Gonzalez Rogers decides.

It will be resolved, if it is resolved at all, by the laws that do not yet exist.
