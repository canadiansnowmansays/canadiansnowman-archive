Twenty-five years is a long time to keep revising a memory. On September 9, 2026, at a Tunnel to Towers Foundation event honouring police officers, firefighters, and other first responders, Donald Trump told the story again, and this time it grew a new scene. He recalled leading a crew to Lower Manhattan in the immediate aftermath of the September 11 attacks, standing near a creaking United States Steel Building he feared was about to collapse, and being physically lifted off his feet by two firemen who carried him to safety while he insisted, needlessly, that he could run on his own.

It is a vivid scene. It is also, by the available evidence, one that did not happen — not on the day he implied, and not in the form he described it. This series exists to count patterns, not to adjudicate history, so the point here is not really whether Trump was carried by firemen on September 11, 2001. The point is what happened in the twenty-four hours after he said it, because that sequence is the clearest single specimen this project has yet collected of a behavioural loop it has been describing piecemeal since Part I: invent, get caught, double down, cite evidence that does not exist.

The correction cycle, timed

CNN’s Daniel Dale published a fact-check within hours of the speech, noting that Trump was several miles away at Trump Tower on the day itself and was not evacuated from any building near Ground Zero. New York magazine’s Nia Prater and Chas Danner filed a similar piece the same evening. A 2019 PolitiFact investigation, resurfaced in the coverage, had already put the claim to a more direct test: PolitiFact’s reporters asked Richard Alles, the FDNY battalion chief who arrived at the site roughly twenty minutes after the second tower fell and remained there for months, whether he had any knowledge of Trump or a hundred-worker crew being present. He said he did not, and added that a deployment of that size would have left a record, since everyone on site operated under direct fire and police supervision.

Trump’s response, posted to Truth Social that same night, did not retreat from the story. He called it exactly accurate, said he was attaching video clips of himself in Lower Manhattan with “numerous of my people,” and named his former head of security, Matthew Calamari, as a corroborating witness who had confirmed the trip to journalists “many times.” He then adjusted the timeline without appearing to notice the adjustment was a concession: he had not been there on September 11 itself, he explained, but went “as soon as the Law allowed.” A story that began as an eyewitness account of imminent collapse had, within one Truth Social post, become an account of a visit on some unspecified later date, defended with evidence — video, a security chief’s say-so — that by its own description could not confirm the version originally told.

That collapsing-timeline defence is not new to this series. It is the same three-beat structure this project logged around the East Wing demolition in Part XX: assert something specific and vivid, get contradicted by people who were actually there, and respond not by narrowing the claim but by re-describing it just enough to survive the contradiction while insisting nothing has changed. What is new in the September 9 episode is the subject matter. Part XX was about a building. This is about a memory of the day nearly three thousand Americans died, retrofitted to place him inside it as a nearly-injured hero rather than, as his own contemporaneous television appearance that day showed, a man four miles uptown remarking to a local news station that one of his own buildings had briefly been the second-tallest in Lower Manhattan.

A subtype worth naming

This series has used “confabulation” as a catch-all for two years’ worth of misremembered dates, invented figures, and garbled briefing material — the North Korean “fifty-seven” disclosure in Part XXI is the clearest recent example, a specific number produced with total confidence and no traceable source. The 9/11 firefighter story belongs to the same family but is not quite the same species. The North Korea number was material that plausibly passed through him once, in some briefing, and came out the other side distorted. The rescue story is not distorted intelligence; it is a scene with a protagonist, a threat, and a resolution, and the protagonist is always him. Confabulation fills a gap in memory with whatever detail feels true. What September 9 demonstrates is a version of that mechanism that reliably fills the gap with himself, cast as the person who survived something, defeated something, or was singled out for danger and endured it. Call it self-mythologising confabulation: not simply a false detail, but a false detail with a plot, and the plot is always heroic.

Once that subtype is named, a great deal of adjacent material this series has been treating as separate stories starts to look like the same story. Thomas B. Edsall’s September 15, 2026 New York Times essay collected several of them under a single heading, and it is worth pausing on the collection rather than any one entry in it, because the pattern is in the accumulation. Edsall’s newsroom colleague Peter Baker had already documented, in a September 9 piece, the roughly sixty posts Trump published to social media over a single ten-hour stretch that Labor Day weekend — among them AI-generated images depicting Trump menacing Canada’s prime minister with a hockey stick, personally piloting bombing runs against unnamed enemies, and patrolling darkened streets alongside robotic security forces. Baker’s framing, that the volume and content of the posts function less as messaging strategy than as a visual manifestation of how Trump wants to be seen, describes exactly the same impulse driving the firefighter story, just executed in a different medium. One is a synthetic image of a war he did not fight. The other is a synthetic memory of a rescue that did not happen. Both put him at the centre of danger he did not actually face, and both were produced and distributed within days of each other.

The body keeps its own ledger

While the story was being told and re-told, the body doing the telling kept producing its own, smaller data points. At the September 11 Pentagon commemoration two days after the Tunnel to Towers speech, Trump paused for close to three full seconds mid-sentence before finishing a reference to the year of the attacks, rendering it as “twenty-O-one” in a halting, syllable-by-syllable delivery — a stumble captured on video and widely circulated the same day. It followed a similar episode a month earlier: on August 12, 2026, during an Oval Office executive-order signing, Trump was observed by outside clinical commentators to lose the thread while addressing Health Secretary Robert Kennedy Jr., then appear to drift toward sleep as Kennedy spoke, before catching himself and snapping back to attention.

Neither of those moments proves anything on its own, and this project has never claimed otherwise; the “grip that won’t close” ledger opened in Part XIX exists precisely because no single stumble is evidence of anything except a stumble. But a ledger’s value is cumulative, and by September 2026 the entries are arriving close enough together that the interval between them has become as notable as the entries themselves: a three-second freeze at a solemn national commemoration, a month after an on-camera episode of apparent drowsiness in front of the health secretary, in the same ten-day window as an invented rescue story and a ten-hour AI-generated self-mythologising binge. The categories this series built separately — confabulation, disinhibition, the physical stumbles catalogued since Part XIX — are not behaving like separate categories anymore. They are behaving like symptoms clustering around the same underlying event.

Worth noting, too, is the asymmetry in how each episode was handled by the people around him. The Pentagon freeze was allowed to stand uncorrected, absorbed into a news cycle that had bigger stories competing for attention that week. The firefighter story, by contrast, prompted an immediate and specific rebuttal from the president himself, complete with named witnesses and promised video evidence. That is backwards from what a White House confident in his fitness would produce: the harmless physical stumble drew no response at all, while the self-flattering fabrication drew an elaborate defence. It suggests a communications operation calibrated to protect the mythology rather than the man, which is its own kind of data point about where the institutional priorities currently sit.

The letter that got there first

Readers of Part XIX and Part XXI will recall Representative Jamie Raskin’s still-unanswered public request for a formal accounting of the president’s cognitive fitness. It turns out Raskin’s letter was not the only formal document raising the alarm, and the other one had been sitting in the public record for longer. On April 30, 2026, Senators Sheldon Whitehouse and Jack Reed entered into the Congressional Record a statement signed by thirty-six medical specialists, who were careful to note they had not examined Trump directly, but who described a pattern of “objectively observable signs of serious medical concern”: disorganised and tangential speech, unexplained reversals on major strategic questions, and, notably for this essay, grandiose and delusional self-conception — the specialists specifically cited imagery of Trump casting himself as a warrior hero and a combat pilot, output that reads, in September 2026, less like clinical speculation and more like an accurate advance description of the Labor Day AI images Baker would document four months later.

That is the detail this series had not fully registered until now. The specialists were not simply flagging a general risk of cognitive decline. They flagged, in April, the specific fantasy content — the self-as-warrior imagery — that would surface as literal AI-generated pictures by September, and the specific narrative confabulation — self-insertion into a scene of danger he did not survive — that would surface as the 9/11 firefighter story the same week. A prediction that specific, sitting unanswered in the Congressional Record for four and a half months, is no longer merely a warning this administration has chosen to ignore. It is a warning this administration’s own subsequent behaviour has gone some distance toward confirming.

What the ledger holds now

Craig Malkin, a Harvard Medical School lecturer quoted in Edsall’s piece, made the fair point that some baseline of narcissism is close to a job requirement for anyone who runs for the presidency in the first place. This series has never disputed that, and does not dispute it now. The distinction Donald Moynihan drew in the same essay is the one that matters here: the same untethering that produces a charming story about oneself at a memorial event also produces confident, ungrounded claims about inflation, about public health, about the reliability of an election, about the size of a hostile state’s nuclear arsenal. The 9/11 story and the North Korea number are not different phenomena wearing different clothes. They are the identical mechanism — self-referential confabulation, delivered with total confidence, unconstrained by an audience able to correct it in the moment — aimed at two different targets, one merely embarrassing and one genuinely dangerous.

Twenty-two parts in, the file no longer needs a new category so much as it needs to acknowledge that its existing categories have started to merge. The confabulation is self-mythologising. The disinhibition is now visibly rendered as AI imagery rather than only spoken aloud. The physical stumbles are arriving close enough together to read as a cluster rather than isolated incidents. And the clinical warning that predicted all of it — specifically, months in advance — is still sitting in the Congressional Record, unanswered, alongside Raskin’s letter, while the country waits for a threshold this series has never pretended to know the location of, only that the evidence keeps arriving closer to it.

The Baseline Problem is an ongoing series answering Lawrence O’Donnell’s question — if Donald Trump gets dementia, how will we know? — through pattern-counting rather than diagnosis.
