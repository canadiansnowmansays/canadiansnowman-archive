Every biography of a living, still-contested figure ends the same way a hearing does: without a verdict, because the subject is still alive to complicate whatever conclusion the writer would prefer to reach. Anthony Fauci is eighty-five years old, was held in contempt of Congress by a party-line committee vote on August 6, 2026 — a largely symbolic action almost certain to go nowhere given the sixty-vote threshold required for the full Senate to act on it — and faces a legacy that two irreconcilable coalitions of Americans have already decided, with total confidence, in opposite directions. This series was never going to resolve that disagreement, and it was not built to try. What it was built to do — across an introduction and nine essays tracing a Brooklyn pharmacy delivery boy through Jesuit schooling, three obscure decades of vasculitis research, a war with AIDS activists that remade American drug policy, a global program that has saved something on the order of 25 million lives, service to seven presidents of both parties, a catastrophic collision with an eighth, a strange and uncomfortable brush with sainthood, a diary that revealed more vanity than criminality, a Senate hearing conducted mostly in silence, and the contempt vote and constitutional fight that followed it — is put the actual record in one place, honestly enough that a reader on either side of the current argument has to reckon with the parts that complicate their preferred story.

There is also a concrete institutional lesson buried in this record, distinct from the question of Fauci’s personal character, and it is worth stating plainly because it is the one part of this ledger that has genuine forward-looking value rather than merely settling accounts with the past. American pandemic response, across both the AIDS crisis and Covid, has relied enormously on the personal credibility of individual scientists standing at podiums, rather than on institutional structures durable enough to carry public trust independent of any single person’s reputation. That arrangement works remarkably well right up until the individual in question becomes, for reasons only partly within his control, a symbol too politically charged for either side to evaluate on the merits — at which point the entire apparatus of public trust that a country needs functioning smoothly during a genuine emergency collapses along with the reputation of whichever scientist happened to be standing closest to the microphone. A public health system that depends this heavily on one trusted face is not a resilient system; it is a single point of failure wearing a lab coat. Whatever else the next several years of investigations and hearings settle about Fauci specifically, the country would do itself a genuine favor by drawing the more durable lesson: build institutions capable of earning public trust on their own structural merits, rather than borrowing it, as the country did for decades, from one man’s accumulated credibility.

There is a pattern worth naming explicitly before turning to the ledger itself, because it runs through nearly every essay in this series and it is, I think, the single most useful thing a biography like this one can offer that a news cycle cannot: Fauci’s entire career was built on a bet that expertise, patiently explained and consistently nonpartisan, would eventually earn durable public trust regardless of which party held power. That bet paid off, spectacularly, for the better part of five decades — through the AIDS crisis, through PEPFAR, through six presidencies of both parties that each found him useful precisely because he refused to be anyone’s partisan instrument. It began to fail, for the first time in his career, only when he encountered an administration willing to treat the underlying premise of his entire professional identity — that facts exist independently of who is inconvenienced by them — as itself negotiable. Everything that has happened to Fauci since 2020, the fame and the backlash and the diary and the hearing, reads differently once you see it as the collision between an old model of expert authority and a genuinely new kind of politics that no longer accepts that model’s basic premise. That collision did not happen because Fauci changed. It happened because the ground he was standing on did.

So here, as plainly as I can state it, is the ledger.

On the credit side:

Fauci’s early work on vasculitis syndromes turned a near-certain death sentence into a survivable diagnosis for the great majority of patients, a genuine medical achievement that predates his fame by decades and would alone justify a respectful entry in the history of twentieth-century American medicine. His confrontation with ACT UP and Larry Kramer in the late 1980s, however belated and however uncomfortable for him personally, produced a rebuilt federal drug approval system — parallel track access, community advisory boards, accelerated approval pathways — that has outlasted the crisis it was built for and continues to shape how American medicine handles every subsequent public health emergency, this pandemic included.

PEPFAR, which Fauci helped design and which a Republican president chose to announce as one of his signature achievements, is credited by independent researchers with preventing roughly 25 million deaths, primarily across sub-Saharan Africa, and stands as close to a consensus success story as American foreign policy has produced in the past quarter-century. His capacity to serve seven presidents of two parties across five decades without any of them, or his own staff, ever quite discerning his politics is a genuinely rare institutional achievement, evidence of real discipline rather than mere convenient obscurity. And whatever the final legal reckoning over the gain-of-function funding question turns out to be, nothing in the released diary, the emails, or the public record establishes that Fauci deliberately concealed a known lab origin for the virus or lied under oath about it — the accusation that has driven Senator Paul’s pursuit of him for the better part of a decade remains, on the evidence currently public, unproven.

On the debit side, in fairness: Fauci’s diary reveals a man considerably more consumed by his own sudden fame than his carefully cultivated public reputation for scientific humility would have suggested, cataloging celebrity phone calls and awards with an enthusiasm that reads badly set against contemporaneous death tolls, whatever sympathy the strangeness of that fame deserves. His 2022 claim to have had “nothing to do” with school closures does not match his own contemporaneous diary account of having personally urged specific closures on officials in New York and California — a real discrepancy, not merely a matter of evolving scientific understanding. His public communication around the mask guidance reversal in 2020 was, by the account of his own former colleagues, handled less clearly than it should have been, leaving an impression of inconsistency that a fuller explanation at the time might have avoided. And his comment to MSNBC in 2021, that criticism of him amounted to criticism of science itself, was a genuine overreach — a collapsing of his personal reputation into the institution he represented that handed his critics an argument more useful than anything they could have constructed on their own.

Neither list cancels the other out, and I want to resist, in closing, the temptation to average them into some falsely tidy midpoint verdict — “a complicated man, neither hero nor villain” — that would flatter my own even-handedness more than it would serve the truth. The honest position, I think, is closer to this: Fauci’s professional record, taken across the full fifty years rather than the four most recent ones, is genuinely exceptional, and the specific character flaws this series has documented — the vanity, the discomfort with challenge, the school-closure discrepancy — do not erase that record any more than Fauci’s decades of competence excuse those flaws from honest scrutiny.

What has happened to him since January 2025 — the stripped security detail, the removed mural, the dismantled PEPFAR funding, the ousted successor, the diary weaponized by a political enemy for the specific purpose of embarrassment, and now a Senate hearing built around a tie color rather than an actual accounting of gain-of-function policy — reads, on the full record this series has assembled, less like accountability finally catching up to a man who evaded it for decades than like a targeted campaign of retribution dressed in accountability’s language, aimed at the single most visible symbol of an administration’s predecessors that the current government has decided to punish regardless of what the underlying facts actually support.

That is not a claim that Fauci is above legitimate criticism, and this series has tried, essay by essay, not to write past the parts of his record that earn it. It is a claim that the specific form his current reckoning has taken — a diary seized from a government server and handed deliberately to his fiercest enemy, a hearing built around demonstrating the absurdity of a legal privilege rather than establishing any actual fact, a contempt vote that functions chiefly as a political marker given how unlikely full Senate adoption actually is, a program that saved 25 million lives being quietly dismantled by the same government now interrogating the man who helped build it — bears very little resemblance to the kind of accountability a democracy is actually supposed to produce. Senator Andy Kim named the real casualty of that hearing more precisely than anyone else in that room: not Fauci’s reputation, which was already thoroughly contested before the gavel came down, but the country’s own capacity to hold anything resembling a shared, good-faith argument about what happened during the worst public health crisis in a century, and what should be different the next time one arrives. On that question, this series does not think the hearing, or the vote that followed it, brought the country one inch closer to an answer. It brought the country a clip, several dozen more, and a contempt resolution that even its own sponsors know is unlikely to survive contact with the full Senate — nothing that will help the next Anthony Fauci, whoever and wherever they are, make a single decision more wisely when the next pandemic, inevitably, arrives.

He gave the republic, by any fair accounting, considerably more than he took from it. Whether the republic manages to remember that, once the clips stop circulating and the next crisis arrives to demand its own reluctant expert, is a question this series cannot answer either. Future historians, writing with the distance none of us currently have, will likely find both the sainthood and the demonology equally embarrassing artifacts of a country that had briefly lost its capacity to hold a complicated man in mind as anything other than a symbol. They will probably also find, buried underneath both exaggerations, roughly the man these nine essays have tried to describe: gifted, vain, genuinely consequential, imperfectly self-aware, and unlucky enough to have his one true collision with an unreasonable government arrive at the very end of a long and largely honorable career rather than at its beginning, when he might have had the decades left to outlast it. That, in the end, is the only verdict this series is prepared to offer with any confidence at all.
