There is a particular kind of silence that follows a man getting exactly what he asked for and discovering it is the last thing he wanted.

On the first of March — fifteen weeks ago, which in the chronology of this war feels like a geological epoch — President Trump posted to Truth Social that “there will be no deal with Iran except UNCONDITIONAL SURRENDER.” The capital letters were his. They usually are. They are the typographical equivalent of a man standing on a chair so that everyone can see him.

On Wednesday evening, at the Palace of Versailles, in a building Mr. Trump admired chiefly for not being gold leaf but “the real deal,” the President of the United States signed his name to the document that was supposed to contain that surrender. And on Thursday the world finally got to read it.

It is not a surrender document. It is the opposite of a surrender document. It is, as the New York Times put it with the dry precision of people who have covered the Iranian nuclear program for twenty years, a memorandum that read “nothing like a surrender document” — under which “the Iranians emerged from a confrontation with the world’s most powerful military having not only survived, but with much to celebrate.”

I have been keeping this diary since the last day of February. I have watched the President threaten to end Iranian civilization on a Tuesday and announce that peace was at hand on a Wednesday, more than three dozen times. I have learned to distrust every announcement, including the announcements that the previous announcements were wrong. But I want to be careful here, on the day it actually happened, to say the true thing first.

The war is over. The bombs have stopped. Whatever else is in the fourteen paragraphs of the Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding, that much is real, and it is good, and a great many people who would otherwise be dead next week will now be alive. The mediators — Pakistani, Qatari, Omani — did patient, thankless, genuinely skilled work, shuttling between capitals while two governments postured for their domestic audiences. The Iranian and American negotiators who actually wanted this stopped a thing that should never have started. For that, before anything else, relief.

And then you read the document.

I. The Special Day, Revisited

President Trump at the U.F.C. fight at the White House on Sunday, on the evening of the deal’s announcement. Credit...Kenny Holston/The New York Times

Last week, in Part LXXXI, I argued that the timing of this deal — announced just past midnight Tehran time on the President’s eightieth birthday, hours before a cage match on the White House lawn — was not a coincidence but a courtship. I called it the Birthday Deal. I suggested that the mediators had learned what every courtier of an aging king eventually learns: that the surest way to move him is to make the moving feel like a gift.

I would like to report that I was being too cynical. I cannot.

This week the New York Times published a reconstruction of the final hours — six reporters across Tel Aviv, New York, Islamabad, and Washington — and it confirms the thesis more completely than I would have dared to claim. Pakistan’s army chief, General Asim Munir, “wanted the deal to be signed on Mr. Trump’s birthday.” Qamar Cheema, who runs an Islamabad research institute that was privy to the mediation, put it without ornament: “The birthday was a way to woo him.” The mediators believed the birthday and the U.F.C. match together “could put Mr. Trump in the mood to sign.” The Qataris told the Iranians, in so many words, to give their final sign-off before the start of the fight.

There is a detail here that I find almost unbearably elegant, and it is the Iranian half of the story. Two Iranian officials told the Times that Tehran deliberately waited until the clock had passed midnight, local time, so that the agreement would not coincide with Mr. Trump’s birthday. The seven-and-a-half-hour time difference let each side claim the date it preferred — the President his birthday, the Iranians a day that was conspicuously not his birthday.

Think about what that means. The Americans wanted the deal wrapped in birthday paper. The Iranians, understanding this perfectly, made sure to mail it the day after, so that no one in Tehran could accuse them of having sung anyone “Happy Birthday.” Both governments were managing the same clock to tell their own people opposite stories about the same event. It is the entire war in miniature: two leaderships negotiating not with each other but with their own reflections.

II. What the Document Actually Says

For three days, the administration refused to release the text while complaining that everyone was mischaracterizing it. This is a novel theory of public relations — that the cure for misunderstanding is more secrecy — but on Wednesday a senior official finally read the fourteen paragraphs aloud on a conference call, and Iranian state media released an identical copy, which rather settled the question of whether the two sides agreed on what they had signed.

The reporters at the New York Times annotated each paragraph, and the annotations are where the document quietly comes apart.

The United States will lift its naval blockade. It will issue waivers immediately for the export of Iranian crude oil, “including banking transactions, insurances, transportation.” It will “terminate all types of sanctions,” including those imposed by the United Nations Security Council and the I.A.E.A. It will make available Iran’s frozen assets — twenty-four billion dollars or more — “upon the implementation of this M.O.U.,” payable, the text specifies, to “any ultimate beneficiary designated by the Central Bank” of Iran. It commits, with regional partners, to a reconstruction fund of “at least U.S.D. 300 billion.”

And what does Iran give in return? It will allow ships through the Strait of Hormuz “with no charge for 60 days only.” It “reaffirms that it shall not procure or develop nuclear weapons” — a promise it first made in 1970, when it signed the Non-Proliferation Treaty, and repeated in the opening lines of the 2015 agreement that Mr. Trump tore up. It will “down-blend” its enriched uranium on site, under inspection — but it will not, as it did under Barack Obama in 2015, ship that uranium out of the country. In 2015, ninety-seven percent of the stockpile went to Russia. This time it stays in Iran, buried under the rubble of the very strikes that were meant to eliminate it, available to be dug up and re-enriched whenever the mood takes a future government.

Nicole Grajewski, who studies Iranian foreign policy at Sciences Po, rendered the verdict that almost every serious analyst reached independently this week: “On balance, the memorandum appears to favor Iran.” She added the sentence that should be carved over the entrance to the next sixty days of negotiations: “Judged solely on the memorandum itself, the immediate and concrete benefits accrue disproportionately to Iran.”

The phrase “no charge for 60 days only” deserves its own small monument. Iran never charged anyone to pass through the Strait of Hormuz before this war. The waterway was free. It was, in fact, the entire job of the United States Navy to keep it free. The memorandum now establishes, in writing, that free passage is a temporary courtesy with an expiration date — and Iran’s lead negotiator, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, confirmed on Wednesday that after the sixty days, fees will apply. “The Strait of Hormuz will not return to the conditions before the war,” he said. He is correct. It will be worse. The United States went to war to keep a road open and signed a document making it, for the first time in history, a toll road.

III. The Bookend

Antony Blinken, who helped build the 2015 deal and then spent a decade listening to Mr. Trump call it the worst agreement in human history, allowed himself a moment of professional satisfaction this week. “The only ‘achievement’ of the ceasefire,” he wrote, “is the likely re-opening the Strait of Hormuz — which was open before the war started. And we will apparently pay Iran to do so.”

That is the bookend. Begin with “UNCONDITIONAL SURRENDER” in capital letters in March. End, in June, with a memorandum in which the United States pays Iran to reopen a strait that Iran closed, releases Iran’s money, lifts Iran’s sanctions, helps rebuild Iran’s economy, and postpones — to a sixty-day negotiation that the President himself now says is not a hard deadline — the single question the war was supposedly fought to answer.

This is not my characterization. It is the remarkable, almost vertiginous fact of this week: the verdict is unanimous across a political spectrum that agrees on nothing else. From the left, Chuck Schumer called Mr. Trump “a terrible negotiator” who had produced “one of the worst deals” in the history of American foreign policy. From the right, Senator Bill Cassidy — a Louisiana Republican freed by his primary defeat to say what he actually thinks — wrote that “Reagan is rolling over in his grave” and called the war “the worst foreign policy blunder in decades.” Senator Roger Wicker, the Republican chairman of the Armed Services Committee, complained that the deal “negotiates away the victories of Operation Epic Fury” and that the reconstruction fund would make Obama’s 2015 payout “look like a pittance by comparison.” Nikki Haley: “a huge mistake to pay to rebuild the threat we just destroyed.” Marjorie Taylor Greene, sarcastically: “This, apparently, is what winning looks like.” The New York Post — the New York Post! — ran a cover reading “LOVEBOMB.”

When Schumer and Wicker, Haley and Greene, Blinken and the New York Post all arrive at the same conclusion by different roads, it is worth noticing that the conclusion is probably where the roads were always heading.

IV. Words Don’t Matter

On Thursday, Vice President Vance walked to the White House podium to defend the deal, and in the course of doing so produced the single most clarifying sentence of the entire war.

A reporter pressed him on where, exactly, some of his confident claims about Iran’s commitments were written down in the document. Mr. Vance replied: “Words don’t matter, ladies and gentlemen. We’re about verification.”

Words don’t matter. This was said by the Vice President of the United States, in defense of a fourteen-paragraph written agreement, to explain why the words in the agreement should not be held against the administration’s description of it. I have read that sentence perhaps forty times and it does not get less astonishing. It is the perfect companion to the President’s own assessment of his deal earlier in the week: “Nobody knows what it is, but it’s very strong.”

Graeme Wood, writing in The Atlantic, caught the same disease in a second administration voice. An anonymous American official told CNN that “people shouldn’t read too much into the language” of the agreement, because the real deal lay in “understandings we have with each other.” Wood’s reply belongs in a textbook: “This statement goes against all legal advice ever given. (Read the contract; ignore what the other guy tells you while he watches you sign it.)”

There is a pattern in this administration that I have spent this series trying to name. In my own writing on the President’s habits of mind — what I have elsewhere called the baseline problem — I have described a decision-making loop that has come unhooked from consequence, in which the announcement of a thing has replaced the achievement of it, and each new statement erases the last one without apparent memory that the last one was ever made. “Words don’t matter” is not a gaffe. It is the operating principle, stated aloud, by the man assigned to defend the words.

And the trouble is that the words do matter — they just matter against the people who wrote them. Mr. Vance claimed the lifted oil sanctions were “not a new benefit” for Iran; the Times’ own fact-checkers noted that Iran can now sell its oil at market price, to a wider range of buyers, in stronger currencies, instead of smuggling it to Chinese refineries at a steep discount. He claimed Iran’s military had been “destroyed”; a classified American intelligence estimate, reported last month, found that Iran retains roughly seventy percent of its prewar missile stockpile. He said the financial benefits would flow only “if they change their behavior”; the memorandum says the assets unfreeze “upon the implementation of this M.O.U.” He insisted “we have all the cards.” And then his colleague Jonathan Swan reported the quiet part: the President’s own advisers say privately that it is hard to imagine him returning to military action against Iran before the midterms — “And the Iranians know that. So the United States has likely given up a substantial amount of leverage.”

We have all the cards, said the man holding none of them, to an audience that had already seen the hand.

V. Humiliation Is Not the Same as Defeat

Here I want to slow down, because the easy thing to write this week is “Iran won,” and the easy thing is not quite the true thing.

Graeme Wood drew a distinction I have not been able to stop thinking about. “Humiliation,” he wrote, “is distinct from defeat. Only the United States was humiliated; both countries have experienced a catastrophic loss.”

The American humiliation is the loud part. A rich and overwhelmingly powerful country could not bend a poor and stubborn one to its will, and the President said so himself, boasting that he could have kept bombing “for another three weeks, two weeks, four weeks, two years” and still “you would never have the Hormuz Strait open.” Keeping that strait open is one of the chief purposes of the United States Navy. The President was, without noticing, announcing that the most expensive military on earth cannot do one of its defining jobs.

But Iran’s loss is real, even if it is quiet. Its neighbors no longer regard it as a difficult acquaintance; they now know it for an outright threat, and they are arming themselves and digging pipelines to route their oil around the strait entirely. Its economy, broken for fifteen years, is now wreckage. More than three thousand of its people are dead. Its supreme leader was killed in the war’s opening hour. To call this a victory is to misunderstand what victory costs.

What Iran won is not the war. What Iran won is the humiliation — the spectacle of getting the United States to sign a document that Americans themselves called degrading, mortifying, a capitulation. As Wood observed, drawing on officials from several administrations, there is a strain in the Iranian leadership that regards the exasperation of the Americans “as an end in itself.” For a regime that has never won a war, that has struck at its enemies only through proxies and in the dark, a piece of paper humiliating to America is a kind of capital no Iranian government has held since 1979. Whether they can spend it on anything but their own survival is the question the next sixty days will not answer.

VI. The Bad Divorce

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu of Israel in Jerusalem on Monday.Credit...Ronen Zvulun/Reuters

If Iran was the unexpected beneficiary, Israel was the party that woke on Thursday to find the locks changed.

The reaction in Jerusalem, the New York Times reported, played out “with disbelief and largely in silence.” The deal, its bureau chief wrote, “accomplishes none of Israel’s war aims and arguably leaves the country in worse shape on each of them.” Regime change: the government in Tehran emerged harder-line and more entrenched. Ballistic missiles: untouched by the agreement, and the President now says Iran is entitled to them because “other people have some.” The nuclear program: postponed to the sixty-day talks. And worst of all, from Israel’s vantage, the deal requires Israel itself to stop fighting Hezbollah in Lebanon and withdraw — handcuffing a country that was not even allowed to read the document before it was signed.

The Israeli vocabulary this week was the vocabulary of a marriage ending badly. David Horovitz, editor of The Times of Israel, headlined his column “a catastrophic capitulation.” A Channel 12 analyst called it “a diplomatic October 7th.” Chuck Freilich, a former deputy national security adviser, said flatly: “Iran came out stronger, and I believe is now the regional hegemon. This is an Iranian victory over the U.S. and Israel.” A Likud lawmaker filmed himself removing a red MAGA hat and replacing it with a blue one reading, in Hebrew, “total victory” — the saddest costume change of the year. And a columnist at Israel’s largest newspaper wrote that Mr. Trump “reneged on every promise, turned Iran into a power, strengthened Hezbollah, and as a final flourish, gave Israel a kick and humiliation.”

Mr. Vance’s response to all this was not consolation but threat. “Donald J. Trump,” he told the Israeli critics from the White House podium, “is the only head of state in the entire world who is sympathetic to the nation of Israel at this moment in time.” If he were in the Israeli cabinet, he added, he “might not be attacking the only powerful ally that I have anywhere left in the entire world.” This is the patron reminding the client who pays for the furniture. It is not the language of an alliance. It is the language of a landlord.

And then, because nothing in this administration stays still for forty-eight hours, the landlord changed his tone. On Friday, unveiling the new Air Force One — a plane gifted to the United States by Qatar, a detail I will simply leave sitting here — the President who had spent the week calling Mr. Netanyahu excitable and possessed of “no fucking judgment” suddenly praised him as a “warrior prime minister” who deserved credit. The affection switches on and off like a porch light. And while it flickered, the Times of Israel reported, officials in the same administration were quietly opening back-channels to Israel’s opposition — to Naftali Bennett and Gadi Eisenkot, the men most likely to replace Mr. Netanyahu at the ballot box this fall. This is the truest portrait of the alliance there is: the President flattering the prime minister in public while his staff shops for the prime minister’s successor in private. The client is told he is beloved. The client is also, simultaneously, being replaced.

The columnist David French, in the New York Times, found the deepest frame for the whole catastrophe. Both Israel and the United States, he wrote, possess militaries strong enough to defend themselves against any conceivable threat — and not strong enough to force their will on a determined enemy. This leaves both nations with “security without safety,” walking what an economist once called “an infinite corridor with an invisible pit.” Each success breeds the confidence to walk faster. “The worst possible outcome,” French wrote, “is the Trump approach — to try to dominate and fail.” He reached, as a Canadian raised on the King James cadence might also reach, for Proverbs: “Pride comes before destruction, and an arrogant spirit before a fall.” A year ago, defending the war’s opening strikes, Mr. Vance had sneered that previous administrations were run by “dumb presidents,” whereas now America had “a president who actually knows how to accomplish America’s national security objectives.” The dumb presidents, it turns out, understood the corridor. They knew where the pit was. They walked slowly.

VII. The Strait That Would Not Revv, and the Peace That Would Not Hold

The single most fitting image of this entire war is what happened — or rather, what conspicuously did not happen — when the strait reopened.

Mr. Trump, ever the impresario, posted: “Ships of the World, start your engines. Let the oil flow!” The world’s shipping industry responded with a collective shrug. The numbers wobbled day to day — twenty-five ships on Thursday, then only five on Friday as the whole arrangement began to teeter — but the larger picture held steady at roughly nothing. Before the war, about a hundred and thirty vessels a day passed through the strait. Now a handful crept along the Iranian coastline, the same furtive route the sanctioned ships had used all along, while some five hundred commercial vessels and eleven thousand seafarers remained stranded in the Gulf, their hulls growing barnacles. A maritime analyst at Argus put it with a perfect deadpan that I could not improve upon: “President Donald Trump’s cry to shippers to ‘start your engines’ is unlikely to be met with much revving. Wait-and-see seems to be the order of the day.”

This is what status quo ante looks like in practice. Not triumph, not surrender — a man shouting “start your engines” at an empty parking lot, where a handful of ships that should never have been stopped now resume a journey that should never have been interrupted. Iran, meanwhile, has already created a new agency — the Persian Gulf Strait Authority — through which ships must now request passage. Free for sixty days. By Friday the authority had issued a directive requiring every vessel to carry an approved insurer and to sail only past Larak Island, declaring any deviation a “violation.” The rules for what comes after, it says, will be “described in the future.” The toll booth is built. It is simply not yet charging. And lest anyone mistake the mood in Tehran for contrition, a news agency tied to the Revolutionary Guards spent Friday calling for the strait to be closed again entirely.

For the new agreement was, by Friday, already wobbling — on its first business day. The next round of talks, which were to begin at a Swiss resort on Lake Lucerne, was abruptly postponed; Vice President Vance, who was to have flown there, cancelled his trip the night before; and the Swiss were left to announce that “preparations are continuing” at a resort with no one coming to it. The reason was Lebanon. The memorandum had declared a “permanent termination of military operations on all fronts, including in Lebanon” — and within four days, the all fronts were on fire again. A Hezbollah ambush killed four Israeli soldiers; Israel answered with more than a hundred and fifty strikes that killed at least forty-seven people in Lebanon, the deadliest flare-up since the deal was announced. Iran, which had insisted from the start that the fighting in Lebanon could void everything, pulled out of the Swiss talks. A second ceasefire was brokered by Friday afternoon — but Israel’s own ambassador said his troops would “remain” in southern Lebanon “until the mission is accomplished,” and American intelligence concluded flatly that Israel is likely to keep striking Hezbollah regardless of any deal. The clause Iran fought hardest for was a dead letter before the ink was dry. That story — Lebanon, the clause that could sink the whole thing, and the talks set for Washington next week — is its own chapter, and I will give it one. For now it is enough to note that the peace was unravelling in the same news cycle it was meant to be celebrated.

Holly Dagres of the Washington Institute asked the question that hangs over the whole enterprise: “It’s difficult to escape the conclusion that these negotiations could have taken place without a three-month war.” The strait was open. The talks were possible. Everything in the memorandum could have been reached by diplomacy, and instead it was reached by diplomacy — after the war, at the cost of the war, with the war added on top for no discernible gain.

VIII. The Son Who Buried His Father

I have saved the last word, as I often do, for the dead, and for the man who will preside over them.

On Thursday, Mojtaba Khamenei — the new supreme leader of Iran, the son of the Ayatollah Ali Khamenei who was killed in the war’s opening strike, himself injured and out of public sight — issued his first statement since the signing. He said that President Trump had made the deal “out of desperation.” He said Iran “will not submit to excessive demands.” He said that the negotiations to come “do not mean accepting the enemy’s view.”

Consider the symmetry the war has produced. The United States and Israel opened the conflict in February with a decapitation strike, killing the supreme leader on the first day, on the theory that removing the head would collapse the body. Four months later, the head has been replaced by his son — harder-line, more emboldened, propped up rather than toppled — and that son’s first act as leader is to call the American president desperate.

The President could not let it stand. By Friday he had fired back on Truth Social: “We didn’t meet out of desperation, Iran did. They are FINISHED! We’ll play out the 60 days. They get no money, not ten cents!” And so the war ends as it was waged — two leaders, on the same day, each calling the other desperate, each performing strength for an audience at home, neither speaking to the other so much as past him. The whole conflict, from the first all-capital-letters threat to this last all-capital-letters boast, has been two men shouting into separate mirrors and mistaking the echo for a reply.

And there is a date. The funeral ceremonies for Ali Khamenei begin on the fourth of July. America’s two hundred and fiftieth birthday. The man killed to win the war will be buried, by the son the war installed, on the anniversary of the nation that killed him — a nation that is, that same week, quietly paying his country to reopen a strait, releasing his country’s money, and lifting his country’s sanctions.

I did not arrange these facts. History arranged them. I am only a Canadian with a notebook, writing them down in the order they happened, which is the only honest order there is.

The Numbers

Brent crude: about 77 dollars a barrel, down roughly ten percent on the week — approaching prewar levels for the first time since February.

Gasoline: below four dollars a gallon for the first time in months, and still about a third higher than before the war began.

Diesel: 5.13 dollars a gallon, more than thirty-five percent above the prewar price.

The reconstruction fund: at least 300 billion dollars, of which the President insists the United States will pay nothing, and of which, a diplomat told Reuters, half has already been pledged.

Iran’s frozen assets, to be unfrozen “upon implementation”: 24 billion dollars or more.

Iran’s enriched uranium, to be diluted but not removed: roughly 11 tons, including 970 pounds enriched to 60 percent — just short of bomb grade.

Iran’s retained missile stockpile, per classified U.S. intelligence: roughly 70 percent of prewar levels — the arsenal the war was launched to destroy.

The Strait of Hormuz, before the war: about 130 vessels a day. Two days after reopening: 25 on Thursday, 5 on Friday, and some 500 still stranded.

American service members killed in the war: 13.

Iranians reported killed: more than 3,000.

Children killed in the first hours of the war, when American strikes hit the Shajarah Tayyebeh elementary school in Minab using targeting imagery seven years out of date: 175. It was the worst civilian-casualty incident caused by the U.S. military since 1991. Asked this week whether anyone would be held accountable, the President said: “Nobody did that on purpose. Mistakes are made. War is nasty.”

Indian sailors killed aboard the tanker Settebello during the blockade: 3. Their families are still waiting for more than condolences.

People killed in Lebanon on Friday, four days into a deal that promised “permanent termination of military operations on all fronts, including in Lebanon”: at least 47.

Days the negotiators say they now have to settle Iran’s nuclear future: 60 — which the President says is “not hard,” and which the last comparable deal took twenty months to reach.

Days until Ali Khamenei’s funeral, and America’s 250th birthday: counting.
