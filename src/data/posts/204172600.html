David Brooks, writing in The Atlantic, opens with a fact that should complicate every assumption this series has carried about how the AI transition would unfold. AI was supposed to give people more free time. Researchers at ActivTrak analyzed the digital activity of more than ten thousand workers and found the opposite: when people adopted AI, their work lives became more intense, not less. Time spent on email, messaging, and business software more than doubled for early adopters. Researchers at UC Berkeley’s Haas School of Business found people using AI to take on tasks they had previously outsourced, squeezing in extra work bursts during evenings, weekends, and waiting rooms. The hours saved did not become leisure. They became more hours, more crowded, and — there is now a name for the resulting mental state — “AI brain fry.”

Brooks’s diagnosis of what this means for the species is the most consequential single argument this series has encountered on the human-cognition side of the gap, and it deserves the full treatment Doctorow’s reverse centaur framework received in Part 50.

I. Need for Cognition

Brooks’s central claim is precise and falsifiable: what will differentiate people in the AI age is not intelligence, but their relationship to mental effort — a trait psychologists call need for cognition. Some people have a high need for cognition; they enjoy thinking hard, the way some people enjoy running marathons for no practical reason. Cognitive misers find sustained thought unpleasant and avoid it whenever possible. Most people sit somewhere in between, willing to work hard when they care enough but not seeking out difficulty for its own sake.

This distinction predicts three different relationships to AI, and Brooks names each group with the precision this series has come to expect of its strongest sources.

The Productive Passengers — people with low need for cognition — get an immediate gain and a slower-arriving loss. AI makes them more productive because it makes everything easier. It also diminishes their underlying capacities, for exactly the same reason. Nataliya Kosmyna’s team at the MIT Media Lab found that brain connectivity declines by as much as 55 percent when people use ChatGPT compared with doing the same task unaided. Vivienne Ming, co-founder of Possibility Sciences, found gamma-wave activity — a marker of cognitive effort — dropping roughly 40 percent during AI use. Michael Gerlich’s research at SBS Swiss Business School documented a significant negative correlation between frequent AI tool use and critical thinking ability. Researchers at Carnegie Mellon found that after just ten minutes of AI-assisted problem-solving, people who then lost access to the AI performed worse and gave up more frequently than people who had never used it at all. A study of endoscopy physicians found detection rates for precancerous lesions falling from 28.4 percent to 22.4 percent after the doctors had used AI assistance and then had it removed. The skill had not been supplemented. It had been quietly replaced, and the replacement was not permanent.

The Reluctant Optimizers — the medium-need-for-cognition majority — understand the risk intellectually and resolve not to fall victim to it. They fail anyway, gradually, under the ordinary pressure of a crowded day. Brooks’s account of the mechanism is sharp: the technology does not just save time, it shifts the user from a cultivation mindset, in which difficulty is the price of becoming more capable, to an optimization mindset, in which the goal is removing friction entirely. In a GoTo-commissioned survey, 43 percent of workers admitted submitting AI-generated content they suspected contained errors or was low quality — not because they were deceived, but because the optimization mindset had already redefined success as throughput rather than excellence. Researchers at Wharton built an AI deliberately programmed to occasionally give wrong answers; the humans working with it accepted the errors as correct 80 percent of the time. Brooks calls this cognitive surrender, and it is the precise psychological mechanism behind the automation blindness this series documented through the radiologist case in Part 50 — except here it is not a structural feature of a deployment, but a default outcome of ordinary, well-intentioned use.

The Mental Marathoners — high-need-for-cognition people — will, Brooks predicts, do what marathoners always do with a technology that makes their preferred difficulty unnecessary: train harder anyway, on purpose, because the difficulty was never the obstacle. It was the point. This group, in Brooks’s accounting, will not avoid AI. They will use it adversarially — asking for hints rather than answers, writing a first draft before consulting the bot at all, treating AI as what Brooks calls “a brilliant librarian rather than an oracle.”

II. The Industrialization of Detachment

Brooks’s most striking piece of testimony comes from Chris Sibben, head of school at a small private school in Northern Virginia. Sibben showed his students a film that took more than two hundred artists over five years to make. Their response was not awe. It was confusion: why would anyone do that, when “AI could have done it in five minutes”?

Sibben’s analysis of that single sentence, published in an essay for Mere Orthodoxy, is the sharpest formulation this series has yet found of what is actually at stake beneath the labor and governance questions Parts 47 through 53 have addressed. The comment, he argues, is not really about speed. “It is a moral revaluation. It assumes that what matters is the output, not the ordeal; the image, not the seeing; the product, not the person becoming capable of making it.” A student who internalizes that frame, in Sibben’s words, “does not become lazier; he becomes less formed, less present, less able to bear the weight of difficulty without reaching for a prompt.” AI, on this account, offers “competence without apprenticeship. Fluency without understanding.”

This is the same apprenticeship collapse this series documented through Marcus Chen in Part 47 — but Sibben’s framing locates the damage one level deeper than a labor-market or skills-pipeline problem. The apprenticeship pipeline does not just train workers in a trade. It trains a self capable of tolerating difficulty without immediately escaping it. Remove the apprenticeship, and you do not just lose a generation of skilled radiologists or junior developers. You lose, Sibben argues, the formation process by which a person becomes solid enough to bear weight at all.

III. Why Bots Cannot Fix the Motivation Problem They Created

The clearest empirical test of whether better AI design can solve the motivation problem comes from education, and the result is not encouraging for anyone hoping a sufficiently well-built tutor bot resolves the issue.

Sal Khan, the founder of Khan Academy, told a TED audience in 2023 that AI was about to deliver “probably the biggest positive transformation that education has ever seen,” via Khanmigo, an “amazing personal tutor” for “every student on the planet.” By this spring, per Jenny Anderson and Mike Goldstein’s reporting in The Atlantic, Khan himself admitted the rollout had been “a non-event” for many kids. Access scaled from forty thousand students in 2023 to nearly a million. Actual uptake — whether students chose to use it — stagnated.

The diagnosis from Khan Academy’s own chief learning officer, Kristen DiCerbo, restates Brooks’s argument from inside the industry trying hardest to solve it: “Learning is hard work. It’s cognitively effortful and not experienced as fun. How do we get kids to want to do that?” AI, she said, cannot be expected “to bridge that motivation gap.” A Stanford review of available K-12 research found educational benefits from AI tools generally limited. Laurence Holt’s “5 percent problem” — only about one in twenty students who get access to ed-tech tools use them enough to see real gains — means these tools risk supercharging the already-motivated minority rather than lifting anyone else, widening exactly the cognitive polarization Brooks predicts rather than narrowing it.

What the research keeps pointing back to is something no chatbot redesign addresses: learning is fundamentally social. Ron Ferguson’s research at Harvard found that effective teachers motivate students by pressing them to think rigorously and persist through difficulty, inside relationships and group dynamics a bot cannot replicate. Justin Reich of MIT’s Teaching Systems Lab put it most plainly: most students “do not care about the things we are teaching them.” What they care about is “impressing their teachers and cooperating and competing with their peers. They care about the people.” Mary Burns’s observation about pandemic-era remote learning — “we saw a psychic break” when students were isolated from peers and teachers — is direct evidence that the relational infrastructure Brooks’s mental marathoners rely on to sustain difficult effort is not optional scaffolding. It is the mechanism itself.

This matters for the series’ broader argument because it forecloses an easy out. If the answer to cognitive polarization were simply “build better AI tutors,” the problem would eventually solve itself through product iteration, the same way better search ranking improved information access. The education evidence says otherwise: the motivation gap is not a product design problem that more capable models will eventually close. It is a structural feature of how humans learn, and no amount of model capability addresses it, because the bottleneck was never information delivery. It was always the human relationship that makes a person willing to sit with difficulty in the first place.

IV. What AI Cannot Want

Brooks’s closing argument is the essay’s philosophical core, and it connects directly to the meaning-of-life disagreement Emma Pierson raised with her own mentor in Part 52.

Before AI, Brooks argues, many people assumed reason and intelligence were the qualities that made humans distinct from animals. That assumption cannot survive contact with entities that will soon be more intelligent than any human. What AI categorically lacks, in Brooks’s account, is not intelligence but hunger — desire, want, a structure of cares, a personal history of wounds and joys that generates aspiration rather than mere prediction. “A bot doesn’t have a past person that it used to be or a future person that it wishes to become,” he writes. Life, on this view, is not primarily about solving problems — any sufficiently capable computer can do that. It is a pilgrimage: growing from experience, reaching for a possibility not yet possessed.

This reframes the entire cognitive-polarization thesis as a question this series can finally state with precision: the AI transition will not be won or lost on whether the technology is capable. It will be won or lost on whether human institutions — schools, workplaces, families — can cultivate desire itself, the thing AI cannot manufacture on a person’s behalf. Brooks’s prescription draws on Edward Deci and Richard Ryan’s self-determination theory: autonomy, competence, and relatedness are what generate the motivation to do hard things, and all three are eroded by exactly the kind of frictionless optimization the AI industry has built its entire product philosophy around. Amit Singhal’s 2013 description of Google’s mission — “maniacally focusing on the user to reduce every possible friction point” — is, in Brooks’s telling, the precise design philosophy that, generalized to cognition itself, produces cognitive misers at scale.

The stakes Brooks identifies are higher than this series has previously stated them. Economic inequality divides people by what they have. Cognitive polarization, on Brooks’s account, risks dividing people by what they have become — sorting society, in his words, into “what might begin to look like two different species.” That is not a metaphor this series should accept casually. But it is also not, on Brooks’s own argument, inevitable. Desire is trainable, even if it cannot be commanded directly — shaped by admiration, by apprenticeship, by institutions willing to ask what a person truly wants rather than simply what they can be made to produce. Whether American schools, workplaces, and AI companies build toward that cultivation, or continue optimizing away the friction that cultivation requires, is the next chapter of the gap this series exists to track.
