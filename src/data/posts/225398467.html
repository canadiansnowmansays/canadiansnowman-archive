I. The Chapter We Left Out

This series began with a document. On March 5, 2026, while Washington was counting the cost of the first week of the Iran war, Beijing published the 141-page outline of its 15th Five-Year Plan. The five essays that followed walked through what was in it: artificial intelligence, humanoid robots, quantum networks, the rare-earth processing that sits under every American missile. The last of them argued that China was winning a peace that the United States had not noticed it was fighting.

There was one chapter we did not give an essay of its own. The plan treats biotechnology as a top national priority, level with artificial intelligence, and it sets a target that sounds modest until you think about it: China is to produce at least 25 per cent of the world’s “first in class” innovative medicines over the next five years. First in class means a drug that works by a mechanism nobody has used before. Not a copy and not a cheaper version, but a new idea.

Eight months on, that chapter has come due. As Gina Kolata reported for The New York Times on November 17, 2026, after speaking to 18 biotech executives and investors, the American industry has responded to that target in a way nobody would have predicted a decade ago. It has stopped talking.

II. Going Dark

For as long as there has been a biotech industry, its habits came from the university. A company with a promising molecule wanted the world to know. It presented at meetings, posted preprints, put its early data on its website, and went to conferences to argue about it over bad coffee. Publication was how a young firm got noticed by investors, partners and the large drug makers that might one day buy it.

That is ending. At Alnylam Pharmaceuticals in Cambridge, Massachusetts, the rule is now unwritten but firm: early-stage studies are not revealed and unpublished data is not discussed, according to its founding chief executive, Dr. Christoph Westphal. Jacob Becraft of Strand Therapeutics deliberately delays filing patents, because a patent filing is a public description of what you have, and he is guarded about what he discloses even to his own investors. Alexis Borisy, a co-founder of Curie.Bio, which supports early-stage companies, gives scientists blunter advice. Go dark, he tells them. Don’t publish. Don’t present posters. Don’t even go to the meetings.

“This is the world we’re in now,” Borisy told the Times.

His reasoning is not paranoia, or at least it is not only paranoia. He describes Chinese investigators using A.I. agents that read every conference presentation and every patent as it appears. Anything made public is tracked. When a new idea surfaces, he says, a crowd of Chinese companies will chase it. Most will fail. But it takes only two or three to succeed, and those can start a clinical trial far sooner than the Americans who had the idea.

This is the A.I. chapter and the biotech chapter of the five-year plan working together. Part I counted 52 mentions of artificial intelligence in those 141 pages. Some of that intelligence is now being pointed at the American scientific literature, and the American scientists have noticed.

III. The Numbers Behind the Fear

It would be easy to dismiss all this as an industry looking for someone to blame for a bad funding market. The numbers make that harder.

A recent paper in JAMA, cited by the Times, traced where early-stage drug development happens. In 2015, 48 per cent of it was in the United States and 8 per cent in China. By 2024, the American share had fallen to 37 per cent and China’s had risen to 32. That is not a gap closing slowly. That is two lines about to cross.

John Crowley, who runs the industry group BIO, said 30 to 35 per cent of innovative medicines now in development originate in China, and he thinks Beijing’s 25 per cent target is conservative. He added the figure that matters most to anyone with a molecule and a bank balance: Chinese companies can complete clinical trials about three times faster than American ones, at half the cost. Dr. Steven Paul of Third Rock Ventures went further, predicting that China will “leapfrog” the United States in biotechnology before long.

None of this should have surprised Washington. In April 2025, the National Security Commission on Emerging Biotechnology, a body chartered by Congress, warned that the United States would lose its lead in biotech to China within about three years unless the government acted quickly, as BioCentury reported at the time. Its report made 49 recommendations: incentives for research, faster clinical trials, less risk for investors. It also contained a sentence that has aged into a slogan: China does not have a right to American research, period.

The trouble is the other half of the equation. Cyriac Roeding, an investor, points out that small American biotechs live on private money, which comes and goes in booms and busts, while a drug takes 10 to 12 years to reach patients. China’s companies are funded by a state that has named their industry a national priority. One side is running a marathon on venture capital. The other is running it on a five-year plan.

IV. Fast Follower, Design Around

The industry has a name for the tactic it fears most: fast follower, design around. An American company invents a drug and patents it. A Chinese company changes the design just enough to get around the patent, then races it into trials. The original inventor does not lose the idea, exactly. It loses the years of market exclusivity that were supposed to pay for the idea.

Revolution Medicines says that is what happened to it. Its scientists spent years on a drug for patients whose tumours carry mutations in the RAS family of genes, the drivers behind nearly all pancreatic cancers. The drug, daraxonrasib, rests on a design the Times called stunningly effective. Then, seemingly from nowhere, Chinese scientists licensed a nearly identical molecule to a San Diego company, Erasca. Revolution says its trade secrets were misappropriated. Erasca denies it. The courts will sort out who is right, but the pattern is the one Borisy describes: a breakthrough appears, and an almost-twin appears soon after on the other side of the Pacific.

Lisa Roessler of Amyris describes something cruder. Amyris engineers specialised yeasts whose products end up in vaccines and cosmetics; each of its 15 commercial products took years and millions of dollars to develop. At recent trade shows, she says, Chinese companies have been advertising the same products backed by the same data, down to the same number of people in the same clinical trials with the same results. That is not a design-around. It is a photocopy.

Then there are the investors who are not investors. Becraft says representatives of Chinese firms have approached him at meetings promising millions, and some turned out to have no money at all. What they wanted was a look at his discoveries. He has stopped talking to them.

Asked about the accusations, China’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs said that China has always attached great importance to protecting intellectual property and that the United States should stop making unwarranted accusations. It is the same answer Beijing has given about semiconductors, about software and about wind turbines. It may even be partly true, in the sense that China now has its own valuable inventions to protect. But a ministry statement does not explain a brochure carrying somebody else’s clinical data.

V. The Rare Earth Question

Part II of this series was about rare earths, and the biotech story keeps circling back to them. The security commission’s warning is that if China comes to control access to an important drug, say a cancer treatment, it could use that access as leverage, the way it has rationed rare-earth minerals during its trade fights with Washington. The fear is not only a lost industry. It is a medicine cabinet with a foreign lock on it.

Not everyone accepts the comparison, and the strongest objection comes from inside the industry. Peter Kolchinsky, managing partner of RA Capital Management, which has backed more than 200 companies, most of them American, thinks the “China hawks” have the analogy wrong. Drugs, he says, are not commodities. A rare-earth chokehold works because one country processes nearly all of a material that everyone needs. The equivalent in medicine would be the United States relying on China to manufacture all its drugs. That is not the situation, he argues, and with planning it need never become the situation for any single drug.

Kolchinsky’s second point is the more uncomfortable one. Cutting off collaboration with and investment in Chinese firms, he says, would hurt American companies more than help them, because American investors would lose sight of what is happening in China. They would not know when a Chinese rival had something better. You cannot win a race you have chosen not to watch.

It is worth taking seriously. Walls are satisfying to build. They also stop you seeing over them.

VI. The Room With Eight People in It

Part of the reason this story has been slow to surface is that few companies want to tell it in public. Melissa Roberts Chapman, president of KC Biohub, explained why with a single conditional: if you make an allegation and later want to sell a drug in China, you might have trouble. Roessler adds that most American biotechs depend on China to manufacture their drugs and to run early clinical trials, and that companies which criticise Beijing can be, and have been, punished with loss of access.

Becraft did speak out. He says cyberattacks on his company’s systems and his personal accounts rose five- to tenfold afterwards, and that a U.S. official advised him it would be best not to travel to China.

The clearest measure came in May 2026, when the U.S. International Trade Commission held a hearing in Washington on China’s threat to American biotech. It invited 297 people and organisations to testify. According to Chapman, eight showed up.

Two hundred and eighty-nine empty chairs is a finding in itself. An industry that is afraid to testify before its own government about the competition it faces has already conceded something, even if it has not yet lost anything.

VII. The Price of Silence

Here is the bind. Going dark may well be the rational choice for any single company. A founder who keeps her molecule secret until the patent is filed and the trial is running is protecting her investors, her employees and possibly her patients. Nobody can fairly tell her to publish and be copied.

But an industry is not a single company. American biotech did not become the world leader by keeping secrets. It became the leader because ideas moved: from the National Institutes of Health to the university lab, from the lab to the poster session, from the poster session to the start-up, from the start-up to the giant that could afford a Phase III trial. Publication, as the Times put it, is the lifeblood of science. Remove it and you do not only slow down the Chinese reader of American papers. You slow down the American one too.

That is the larger point this coda wants to add to the series. The 141 pages did not need to steal anything to change American behaviour. A credible threat of copying, backed by speed and state money, was enough to make the most open scientific culture in the world start to close. That is what a quiet war looks like when it is going well for one side. The other side starts doing the damage to itself.

It is also happening while the federal science that fed this industry is being cut back, a story this masthead is following in a separate series, The Endless Frontier. Vannevar Bush’s 1945 promise was that open, publicly funded research would keep America ahead. The cuts are one strain on that promise. Self-imposed silence is another.

Caitlin Frazer, the security commission’s executive director, told the Times that none of this is an accident. It is the product of 20 or 25 years of deliberate Chinese strategy, redoubled in the latest five-year plan. That is a long game, and the American answer so far has been a report with 49 recommendations and an industry hiding its homework.

Crowley, for his part, insists it is not too late. Unlike chips, where he says America lost the race outright, biotech is still an American lead. His warning is that there is no time to waste if the country does not want to wake up one morning and find the industry has withered.

From up here, the view is a familiar one. Canada has spent a long time watching discoveries made in its labs turn into companies, and then into fortunes, somewhere else. It is a strange thing to watch the United States start to worry about the same thing. The 141 pages said China would make a quarter of the world’s new medicines. Few in Washington noticed the line in March. In November, American scientists are skipping their own conferences because of it.
