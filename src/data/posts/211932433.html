by Stephen Gordon

Leonardo da Vinci counted more things, more carefully, than anyone the species has ever produced — and published, in his sixty-seven years, essentially nothing. The count arrives at 1,519 right after a German friar changed the world with ninety-five published items, which is exactly where this entry belongs, because it is about his opposite. Luther’s list mattered because it went out the door. This is the story of the door that never opened.

Leonardo da Vinci died on May 2, 1519, at the manor of Clos Lucé in Amboise, France, where he had spent his last years as the personal guest of King Francis I — imported, in effect, as the most magnificent ornament in Europe, with the title of First Painter, Engineer, and Architect to the King and no particular duties beyond existing nearby. The legend, which comes to us from Vasari writing decades later, holds that he died in the king’s arms. The legend is doubted; the king appears to have been elsewhere that day; and regular readers know exactly what I am going to say next, because this series has said it at a church door in Wittenberg and will say it again before the year is out: the arms are disputed. The death is not. We will get to what else Vasari says he did on that deathbed, because it is the saddest audited statement in this series so far, but first I owe you the inventory.

Leonardo kept notebooks. The word is inadequate in the way “boat” is inadequate for what left Spain later that same year, but it is the word we have. Across his lifetime he filled page after page — the surviving corpus runs to something like seven thousand pages, and scholars’ estimates of what was lost run from as much again to considerably more — with a density of observation that has no parallel in the written record. He measured the proportions of the human body and left us the drawing, the man in the square and the circle, that half the world’s clinics now hang in their lobbies. He dissected — by his own count, which we take with the standing caution owed to all self-reported figures in this series, some thirty corpses — and drew the heart’s valves, the child in the womb, the vertebrae in their true articulation, with an accuracy that anatomy as a published science would need centuries to match. He counted the beats of birds’ wings. He diagrammed the turbulence of water — sat by rivers and drew what water actually does, braiding and curling, in studies that fluid dynamics would not catch up to for four hundred years. He recorded the blue of distant mountains and worked out why it was blue. He designed machines for flying, for digging, for war, for weaving, and annotated the designs with the patience of a man writing assembly instructions for a century that did not yet have the parts.

And he wrote it all backwards. The famous mirror script — right to left, legible in a looking glass — has been explained as secrecy, as convenience for a left-handed man avoiding smeared ink, as both. I take no position on the hand. I take a position on the effect, which is that the most valuable body of observations in Europe was kept in a cipher of one, by its only reader, and this brings us to the heart of the matter.

Because here is the fact I want to lay on the table with some care: Leonardo lived his entire adult life in the age of print. He was born in 1452 — twelve years after the date this series celebrated at essay 1,440, when I claimed Gutenberg as the patron saint of everyone who publishes without permission. The press was not a novelty in Leonardo’s world; it was infrastructure. It made Luther a continental figure in weeks, two years before Leonardo died and a few hundred miles north of where he was dying. The instrument existed. The audience existed. Leonardo even planned the books — the notebooks are full of outlines and resolutions, a treatise on anatomy repeatedly announced, a treatise on painting, on water, on mechanics — organized, in his head, into publications that were always about to be assembled. He was going to publish the way I was going to learn French: sincerely, at some point, after just a little more preparation.

He never did. Perfectionism has been blamed, and the sheer gravitational pull of the next interesting thing — the man abandoned paintings, commissions, and entire cities on the same principle — and a certain instinct for withholding, in a career spent among princes who were themselves best handled on a need-to-know basis. Whatever the mixture, the ledger stayed shut. And so the anatomical drawings that could have re-founded medicine sat in private hands while published anatomy waited for Vesalius — whose book, this series has noted, arrived in 1543 and changed the field precisely because it was a book. The water studies informed no engineers. The flying machines flew for no one. It is conventionally said that Leonardo was centuries ahead of his time, and the phrase is meant as praise, and I want to file a friendly amendment: he was ahead of his time and he kept the lead to himself, which from the time’s point of view is indistinguishable from never having been ahead at all. An advance that is not transmitted is not an advance. It is a secret with good penmanship.

In fairness to the man — and this series pays its debts — there is one ledger he did post, and he posted it on walls. The paintings were Leonardo’s publications: fewer than twenty that survive with general agreement, an output so small that his contemporaries complained about it, and yet consider the circulation figures. A wall in a Milanese refectory, painted in the 1490s in an experimental technique that began failing almost immediately, remains the most reproduced religious image in the world. A portrait of a Florentine merchant’s wife, carried to France in that final baggage train and never delivered, now receives more visitors annually than most countries. When Leonardo did publish, he published permanently. Which sharpens the puzzle rather than softening it: the man demonstrably understood transmission — understood it better than anyone alive, understood that an image enters the mind without asking permission — and reserved the understanding for pigment. The knowledge went in the drawer; the beauty went on the wall. He shipped the products and sat on the patents. I have known several such men in business, brilliant in the showroom and secretive in the workshop, and every one of them was eventually survived by his competitors’ documentation.

That is the idea at the centre of this essay, and it is the exact inverse of essay 1,517: a count that is not published is not a count. It is a diary. The value of a number — this whole series rests on it — is not in the discovering but in the posting: the entry made where other people can check it, dispute it, build on it, carry it. Luther’s ninety-five items were, as scholarship, nothing beside a single page of Leonardo’s anatomy. But Luther nailed his to a door, or near enough, and Leonardo locked his in a chest, and the world was remade by the lesser count because the lesser count was entered in the books. The Numbers Game does not award marks for private genius. Neither, it turns out, does anything else.

What happened to the pages after his death is the parable’s long second act. Leonardo left the notebooks to Francesco Melzi, the devoted pupil who understood what he had; Melzi guarded them for fifty years; and after Melzi died, the heirs — who did not understand what they had, heirs rarely do — let the collection scatter. Sheets were sold, gifted, cut apart, bound into miscellanies, hauled to Spain and England and back. Two entire codices sat in the National Library in Madrid, miscatalogued, until 1965 — I ask you to sit with that: hundreds of pages of Leonardo, lost inside a library, for want of a correct entry in the catalogue, the unposted ledger misfiled even in death, as if the universe were committed to the bit. And the final turn: in 1994, one of the notebooks, the Codex Leicester — seventy-two pages, much of it those water studies — was purchased at auction by the founder of Microsoft for a sum north of thirty million dollars, making it the most expensive book ever sold. The pages Leonardo declined to publish to his own century became, five centuries on, the costliest publication-that-never-was in history, purchased by the age’s great apostle of shipping early and iterating. The market, whatever its other defects, eventually prices irony correctly.

Now the deathbed. Vasari — writing at second hand, decades on, with the standing tongs fully applied — reports that Leonardo, dying, reproached himself: that he had offended God and mankind by not working at his art as he ought to have done. Historians doubt the scene as they doubt the king’s arms, and they are right to, and I am going to hold onto it anyway, not as a fact about Leonardo but as the epitaph the story itself insists on. Because whether or not the man said it, the notebooks say it. Seven thousand surviving pages of announcements, outlines, tables of contents for books that were never assembled — the ledger audits its own keeper. He counted everything except the one number that governs this series and every publication in it, mine included, five per day, ready or not: the days remaining.

Dior has kept her own counsel on this essay, which is fitting, because Dior is the household’s Leonardo. She observes everything — I have watched her conduct a full structural survey of a single fence post for eleven minutes — and publishes nothing. Her findings on squirrels alone would constitute a major corpus. It is all in there, mirror-written somewhere behind the amber eyes, and it will go with her, and when I suggested this morning that she owed her century the data, she yawned with her entire body, which I have entered in my notes as a response received and declined to interpret.

The count moves on, as it does — and here 1519 performs its final trick, because the year is not done. On September 20, 1519, four and a half months after the ledger closed at Amboise, five ships cleared the bar at Sanlúcar de Barrameda and turned west, carrying among their company a young Italian who would write down every single day for three years and post all of it. The age’s greatest private record ends in the spring; its greatest published one weighs anchor in the autumn. The same year shut one book and opened the other.

Leonardo counted everything and told no one. The next essay is about eighteen men who told everyone, and were wrong by a day, and it wasn’t their fault.
