East India Company ships on the high seas in the 17th–18th centuries. What began as a modest trading venture with £68,000 capital and a royal charter for pepper would, within two centuries, rule more people and territory than most nations — a private corporation that became an empire.

On December 31, 1600, Queen Elizabeth I granted a royal charter to a group of London merchants, giving them a monopoly on English trade with the East Indies. The company they formed was called the Governor and Company of Merchants of London Trading into the East Indies — later shortened to the East India Company. Its initial capital was £68,373. Its initial ambition was pepper.

Within two centuries, this trading company would govern more territory and more people than most nations in history. It would maintain a private army larger than the British state’s. It would fight wars, sign treaties, collect taxes, administer justice, and determine the fates of millions — all while paying dividends to its shareholders in London. It would become, by any reasonable measure, the most powerful corporation in the history of the world.

And it would break India.

I. The Spice and the Sword

Captain William Hawkins at the court of Emperor Jahangir, early 17th century. The Mughals ruled an empire of 150 million with revenues that dwarfed England’s. The East India Company arrived not as conquerors, but as petitioners seeking trading rights in a civilisation that had dominated global trade for centuries.

The East India Company did not arrive in India as a conqueror. It arrived as a supplicant. In 1608, Captain William Hawkins sailed into the Mughal port of Surat and presented himself at the court of Emperor Jahangir — Akbar’s son, a man who ruled an empire of perhaps 150 million people and whose annual revenue dwarfed that of the English crown. Hawkins came asking for trading rights. The Mughal court received him with the bemused condescension of a civilization that had been trading with the world for a thousand years receiving a merchant from a small, cold island at the edge of Europe.

This is important to establish because the narrative the British later told themselves — and taught to generations of Indian schoolchildren — was that they brought civilization to India. The reality is that when the Company arrived, India’s share of global GDP was approximately 24 percent. When the British left in 1947, it was less than 4 percent. The decline was not coincidental. It was engineered.

But that engineering took time. For its first century in India, the Company was a trading operation — establishing “factories” (trading posts, not manufacturing facilities) at Surat, Madras, Bombay, and Calcutta. It traded textiles, spices, tea, and opium. It navigated the Mughal court system, paid local taxes, and operated within a framework of Indian sovereignty. The Company’s servants learned local languages, married Indian women, adopted Indian customs, and existed — however self-interestedly — within the Indian world rather than above it.

What changed was the Mughal collapse. The death of Aurangzeb in 1707 set in motion a slow disintegration of central Mughal authority. Regional powers — the Marathas in the west, the Nizams in the Deccan, the Nawabs of Bengal and Awadh — asserted increasing independence. The subcontinent fractured into competing sovereignties, each seeking advantage, each willing to ally with outside powers for tactical gain.

The Company saw the opportunity. And it had a tool that the Indian powers did not fully understand until it was too late: the joint-stock corporation. The Company could raise capital in London, deploy it in Calcutta, absorb losses across a diversified portfolio, and wait. Indian rulers operated on the logic of personal sovereignty — their power rose and fell with their lifetimes, their treasuries, their military fortunes. The Company operated on the logic of institutional continuity. It could lose a battle, replace a governor, raise new capital, and return. No Indian ruler had faced an adversary that could not die.

II. Plassey

Robert Clive meets Mir Jafar after the Battle of Plassey, 1757. A leveraged betrayal more than a battle: one general bribed, a Nawab overthrown, and Bengal — the richest province in India — delivered into Company hands. The template for corporate conquest was set.

The battle that turned a trading company into an empire was fought on June 23, 1757, at a mango grove called Plassey, about 150 kilometres north of Calcutta. Robert Clive — a Company clerk turned military commander, a man whose personal courage and personal corruption were equally extraordinary — faced the army of Siraj ud-Daulah, the Nawab of Bengal.

The battle was, in military terms, a fraud. Clive had bribed Mir Jafar, one of the Nawab’s generals, to defect with his troops. When the fighting began, Mir Jafar’s forces stood idle. The Nawab’s remaining army, confused and leaderless, collapsed. The “battle” lasted a few hours. British casualties were minimal. Siraj ud-Daulah fled and was murdered days later. Mir Jafar was installed as the new Nawab — a puppet who owed his throne to the Company and would pay for it in territory, treasure, and sovereignty for the rest of his life.

Plassey was not a conquest. It was a leveraged buyout. And it established the template for everything that followed: find a local dispute, back the weaker side, install a client ruler, extract the revenue, repeat. The Company did not need to conquer India. It needed to franchise it.

The Bengal Famine of 1770 killed an estimated 10 million people — roughly one-third of the population. The Company continued tax collection while people starved, having restructured the economy for maximum revenue extraction. This was not misfortune. It was a business model.

Within a decade of Plassey, the Company controlled Bengal — the richest province in India, home to roughly 30 million people, producing the finest textiles in the world. The revenue extraction was immediate, systematic, and devastating. The Company’s annual tribute from Bengal financed its wars elsewhere in India, its tea purchases in China, and its dividends in London. Bengal’s weavers — whose muslins and silks had been the envy of Europe — were subjected to forced contracts, price controls, and physical coercion. A British parliamentary inquiry later described the Company’s early rule in Bengal as “a system of plunder.”

The famine of 1770 killed an estimated ten million people in Bengal — roughly a third of the population. The Company did not cause the drought that triggered the famine. But it had restructured Bengal’s agricultural economy to maximize revenue extraction, replacing food crops with cash crops, stripping the province of its reserves, and continuing to collect taxes while people starved. When the dying was done, the Company raised taxes on the survivors to compensate for the reduced tax base.

This was not an aberration. It was a business model.

III. The System

By the late eighteenth century, the Company had developed a governing apparatus of remarkable sophistication and remarkable cruelty. It operated through a system called the “subsidiary alliance” — Indian rulers were offered British military protection in exchange for paying the cost of the troops stationed in their territory, ceding control of foreign policy, and accepting a British Resident at their courts. Rulers who refused were conquered. Rulers who accepted were slowly strangled: the cost of the troops inevitably exceeded the ruler’s ability to pay, at which point the Company would annex territory “in lieu of debts.”

Lord Wellesley, Governor-General from 1798 to 1805, perfected this system. His brother Arthur — the future Duke of Wellington — provided the military muscle. Between them, they transformed the Company from a trading power with territorial possessions into a territorial power with trading interests. By 1818, the Company directly or indirectly controlled most of the subcontinent.

The intellectual justification evolved alongside the territorial expansion. In the Company’s early decades, its servants had often admired Indian civilization — William Jones, who founded the Asiatic Society of Bengal in 1784, translated Sanskrit texts and argued that Sanskrit, Greek, and Latin shared a common ancestor, a linguistic insight that reshaped European understanding of human history. But by the early nineteenth century, a new ideology had taken hold: Utilitarianism, Evangelicalism, and what we would now call liberal imperialism. James Mill, who wrote a massive History of British India without ever visiting the country, divided Indian civilization into periods of “Hindu” and “Muhammadan” rule and declared both inferior to the British present. Thomas Babington Macaulay, in his infamous 1835 Minute on Education, argued for replacing Indian classical education with English-language instruction, declaring that “a single shelf of a good European library was worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia.”

Macaulay’s Minute is not merely a historical curiosity. It created the English-speaking Indian professional class that would eventually run the independence movement, staff the postcolonial bureaucracy, and — two centuries later — power the IT industry that employs my roommate Rikesh. The colonizer’s language became the colonized’s instrument of liberation and, later, of global economic mobility. India’s relationship with English is one of the great ironies of imperial history, and it is not resolved. It is contained — which, as we are learning, is what India does with its contradictions.

IV. The Destruction of Indian Industry

Indian weavers and artisans, once producers of the world’s finest textiles — ‘woven wind’ and ‘running water’ muslins — were systematically ruined. Punitive tariffs, forced contracts, and deliberate de-industrialisation turned India from the world’s largest manufacturer into a supplier of raw materials for British mills.

The story that matters most for understanding what the Company did to India is not political. It is economic.

When the Company arrived, India was the world’s largest manufacturer of textiles. Indian cotton and silk were traded from Jakarta to London. The quality of Indian muslins was so fine that European merchants described them with names like “running water” and “woven wind.” Bengali weavers, Gujarati dyers, and South Indian silk producers were among the most skilled artisans in the global economy.

The Company destroyed them. Not through competition. Through policy.

Indian textiles were subjected to punitive tariffs in British markets — as high as 70 to 80 percent — while British-manufactured textiles entered India duty-free or at nominal rates. Indian weavers who attempted to sell to the Company’s competitors were physically punished; accounts exist of weavers having their thumbs cut off to prevent them from practicing their craft. The deliberate strategy was to reduce India from a manufacturer to a supplier of raw materials — raw cotton shipped to Lancashire, manufactured into cloth, and sold back to India at prices the destroyed Indian industry could no longer undercut.

This was not Adam Smith’s invisible hand. This was a visible boot. And it accomplished exactly what it was designed to accomplish. By the mid-nineteenth century, India’s textile industry was devastated, its artisan class displaced, and its economy restructured to serve British manufacturing. The drain of wealth — what Indian economists later called “the drain theory” — transferred an estimated £9.2 trillion (in modern terms) from India to Britain over the course of colonial rule, according to the economist Utsa Patnaik’s calculations. That figure is a high-end estimate, and other scholars put the number considerably lower. The methodology is contested; the phenomenon is not. Even conservative estimates run into the trillions. The direction of the flow — out of India, into Britain — is beyond dispute.

When you hear the phrase “developing country” applied to India, remember this history. India did not fail to develop. It was de-developed. The poverty that the British later cited as evidence of Indian civilizational inferiority was, in substantial part, a product of British economic policy. This is not a polemical claim. It is an accounting one.

V. 1857

The Indian Rebellion of 1857 (Sepoy Mutiny). What began with greased cartridges exploded into a widespread uprising against Company rule. Brutal on both sides, it ended the Company’s direct governance — but the extractive system it built passed intact to the British Crown.

The Company’s rule ended the way most extractive enterprises end — in a catastrophe it had created the conditions for but could not foresee.

The Indian Rebellion of 1857 — called the Sepoy Mutiny by the British, the First War of Independence by Indian nationalists, and a complex, multi-causal uprising by historians — began with a grievance about rifle cartridges. The Company’s Indian soldiers, called sepoys, were issued new Enfield rifles whose cartridges were rumoured to be greased with cow and pig fat. Loading the rifle required biting the cartridge open. For Hindu soldiers, cow fat meant ritual pollution. For Muslim soldiers, pig fat was haram. The insult was either a genuine blunder of cultural ignorance or a calculated provocation — historians disagree — but the response was unambiguous.

On May 10, 1857, the sepoys at Meerut mutinied. They marched to Delhi and proclaimed the aging Mughal emperor, Bahadur Shah Zafar, as their sovereign — a gesture that was more symbolic than strategic but that electrified the subcontinent. The rebellion spread across northern and central India. Lucknow, Kanpur, Jhansi, Gwalior — city after city rose against the Company. The violence on both sides was extreme. At Kanpur, Indian rebels massacred British women and children. At Delhi, the British retaliated with mass hangings, the destruction of mosques, and reprisals against the civilian population that contemporaries described as indiscriminate.

The rebellion was suppressed within a year, though pockets of resistance continued into 1859. The consequences were seismic. The British Parliament dissolved the East India Company and transferred governance of India to the Crown. Queen Victoria was proclaimed sovereign of India, and the era of the British Raj — direct imperial rule — began formally on November 1, 1858.

The Company was gone. But what it had built — the administrative apparatus, the revenue extraction system, the racial hierarchy, the economic dependency, the deliberate fracturing of Indian society along religious and caste lines — was inherited wholesale by the British state. The Raj did not start from scratch. It started from the Company’s ledger books.

VI. The Lesson of the Company

East India Company trading posts (‘factories’) like this one at Surat symbolised the transition from commerce to control. Through subsidiary alliances, debt, and military force, a joint-stock company governed millions, paid dividends in London, and rewrote India’s economy to serve its shareholders.

I dwell on the East India Company at this length because its relevance extends far beyond Indian history.

The Company was the prototype for a form of power that the modern world knows intimately: the corporation that becomes a state. It maintained armies, conducted diplomacy, administered justice, and governed populations — all while operating under the legal fiction that it was a private enterprise pursuing legitimate commercial interests. Its officers enriched themselves beyond imagination. Its shareholders collected dividends while millions starved. Its abuses were periodically investigated by parliamentary committees that expressed horror, recommended reforms, and changed almost nothing.

My great-uncle, Hugh Keenleyside — a Canadian diplomat who helped build the postwar international order — understood this pattern. The institutions he worked to create, from the United Nations Technical Assistance Board to the frameworks for international development cooperation, were attempts to build a world in which the exploitation the Company represented could never happen again. That those institutions have been imperfect does not diminish the clarity of the diagnosis. The East India Company was not an anomaly of history. It was a warning about what happens when profit operates without accountability, when corporate power exceeds sovereign authority, and when the people being governed have no voice in how they are governed.

India learned that lesson the hardest way possible. It spent two centuries as a corporation’s balance sheet. And when it finally broke free, the first thing it did was write a constitution that placed sovereignty in the hands of the people — all the people, including those the Company and the Raj had spent two hundred years treating as line items.

But between the Company and the Constitution, there was the Raj. And the Raj had ideas of its own.
