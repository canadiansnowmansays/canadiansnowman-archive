The Octobot — the world’s first fully soft, autonomous robot, developed at Harvard’s Wyss Institute. Inspired by the boneless, shape-shifting octopus, it points toward a new paradigm in robotics: soft, flexible, and gentle.

Postmark: Cambridge, Massachusetts. Waltham, Massachusetts. Stanford, California. Every laboratory where someone looked at an animal and thought: wait.

In the late 1940s, a Swiss engineer named George de Mestral went hiking in the Alps and came home with his dog covered in burrs. This was not unusual. What was unusual is that de Mestral put the burrs under a microscope.

What he saw were tiny hooks — thousands of them, each one curved at the tip, each one catching on any loop of fabric or fur it encountered. The burdock plant had evolved this mechanism over millions of years as a seed dispersal strategy. It was not trying to be useful to engineers. It was trying to get its seeds somewhere new. The hooks were a side effect of a problem the plant had already solved beautifully.

De Mestral spent the next decade figuring out how to replicate the mechanism synthetically. The result, after considerable difficulty finding manufacturers willing to try, was Velcro. The name combined the French words for velvet and hook. The patent was granted in 1955.

Velcro — born from George de Mestral’s 1940s microscope study of burdock burrs. One of the earliest and most successful examples of biomimicry: three billion years of evolutionary R&D reduced to a simple hook-and-loop fastener.

Three billion years of biological evolution. One Swiss engineer with a microscope. One product that has since appeared in everything from running shoes to spacecraft.

This is biomimicry — the discipline of looking at what nature has already engineered and asking what we can learn from it. It is older than the word. Leonardo da Vinci filled notebooks in the 1480s with detailed studies of bird wing mechanics, bone structure, and the aerodynamics of feathers, all in pursuit of a machine that could carry a human into flight. He was five hundred years too early for the materials science that would have made his ornithopter work. But the instinct was exactly right.

In 1997, an American biologist named Janine Benyus published a book called Biomimicry: Innovation Inspired by Nature that named and organized what had until then been a scattered set of intuitions across many disciplines. Benyus argued that nature was not merely a source of raw materials for human industry but a design library — three billion years of research and development, freely available to any engineer willing to look. Her book launched biomimicry as a formal discipline, inspired two TED talks, a consultancy, a nonprofit, and a database called AskNature that catalogs thousands of biological strategies available for engineering application. “We are surrounded by geniuses,” she wrote. The question was whether we were paying attention.

We are living through the moment when the answer is finally, definitively, yes.

The Question Marc Raibert Asked

In the late 1970s, Marc Raibert was a graduate student at MIT studying brain and cognitive sciences when two prominent physiologists came to give a talk about cat locomotion. They showed how a cat’s nervous system coordinates leg movement during running — the precise sequencing of muscle activation, the dynamic balance adjustments happening faster than conscious thought, the extraordinary efficiency of a gait that evolution had optimized over tens of millions of years.

Raibert was transfixed. Not by the cat specifically, but by the question the cat raised: if a biological system can move like that, why can’t a machine? Not cautiously. Not with six legs planted simultaneously to maintain static balance. Dynamically — the way animals actually move, with most of their legs in the air at any given moment, using momentum and continuous adjustment rather than careful deliberate placement.

He spent the next decade building hopping robots at Carnegie Mellon and then MIT. The first ones had no knees or ankles. They were simplified abstractions of animal locomotion, inspired by pogo sticks and the biomechanics of kangaroos and ostriches. They were also, for the first time, dynamic — robots that ran the way animals run, balancing not by being wide and slow but by being fast and continuously correcting.

In 1992, Raibert spun Boston Dynamics out of MIT’s Leg Laboratory. In 2005, funded by DARPA, the company built BigDog — a quadruped robot roughly the size of a large dog, designed to carry equipment over terrain that wheeled vehicles could not navigate. BigDog could carry 340 pounds, climb slopes, scramble over rubble, and recover its balance when kicked — that last capability generating the YouTube videos that made Boston Dynamics famous, and that made a significant portion of viewers deeply uncomfortable.

BigDog, developed by Marc Raibert’s team at Boston Dynamics. Inspired by the dynamic balance and gait of animals like cats, dogs, and kangaroos — a machine that recovers from kicks and runs across rough terrain like a living creature.

The discomfort was the point, though not in the sinister way people imagined. BigDog moved like an animal because Raibert had studied how animals move and built the physics of that movement into the machine. When it stumbled and recovered, it was not performing a trick. It was doing what the underlying control systems were designed to do — continuously computing balance, adjusting leg forces, maintaining forward momentum through perturbation. Exactly what a dog does. Exactly what three billion years of evolution had figured out.

The question was not why the robot moved like an animal. The question was why it took us so long to try.

The Gecko’s Secret

Meanwhile, at Stanford, a researcher named Sangbae Kim was looking at a different animal with a different question.

Geckos can climb smooth vertical glass. They can walk across ceilings. They can do this without any apparent adhesive, without suction cups, without getting stuck — they can detach instantly and reattach just as fast. For decades, biologists knew the capability existed. The mechanism was poorly understood.

What Kim and his colleagues discovered is that gecko feet are covered in microscopic structures called setae — about 14,000 per square millimetre, each one splitting at the tip into hundreds of even smaller structures called spatulae. These spatulae are so fine that they interact with surfaces at the molecular level through van der Waals forces — weak attractive forces that operate at nanoscale distances. No single spatula produces meaningful adhesion. Fourteen thousand setae, each splitting into hundreds of spatulae, produce adhesion that can support the gecko’s body weight on a vertical glass surface, then release on command as the toe peels back at an angle that breaks the molecular contact.

The key insight was directionality. The adhesion works in one direction and releases in another. The gecko does not get stuck — it controls the attachment and detachment through the angle of its toes. It is, in the most literal sense, a switchable adhesive that evolution spent millions of years perfecting.

Stickybot, the gecko-inspired climbing robot developed by Sangbae Kim at MIT. Its synthetic setae mimic the microscopic van der Waals forces that let geckos walk on glass and ceilings — directional, switchable adhesion with no glue or suction.

Kim built Stickybot — a robot that climbed smooth vertical surfaces using directional polymer adhesives that mimic gecko setae. Time magazine named it one of the best inventions of 2006. DARPA funded further development. The technology has since been adapted for gripping satellites in orbit, for robotic surgery, for inspection systems that can walk up the sides of structures too smooth or dangerous for humans.

The gecko did not care about any of these applications. It was trying to catch insects and avoid predators on vertical rock faces in tropical forests. The engineering problem it solved, for its own reasons, turned out to be one of the hardest problems in materials science: how do you make something adhesive and non-adhesive at the same time?

It solved it. We copied it. That is biomimicry.

Fish, Fins, and the Propulsion Problem

The fish solved the underwater propulsion problem hundreds of millions of years before Evan Budz watched a snapping turtle. Fish achieve thrust through a fundamentally different mechanism than any propeller ever designed — undulating bodies and oscillating fins that generate forward motion through controlled vortex shedding rather than brute rotational force. The result is propulsion that is extraordinarily efficient, manoeuvrable, and quiet. A fish turning on a dime in a reef system leaves the surrounding water essentially undisturbed. A propeller, by definition, cannot do this.

Researchers have been building fish-inspired underwater robots for decades. MIT’s Robotuna, developed in the 1990s, demonstrated that a robotic body mimicking the muscle and fin structure of a tuna could achieve swimming efficiency approaching the biological original. More recent systems, built around the oscillating fin mechanics of rays, boxfish, and cuttlefish, have achieved maneuverability and energy efficiency that propeller-driven systems cannot match in confined or sensitive environments.

MIT’s Robotuna — an early biomimetic underwater robot modeled on tuna propulsion. Fish have solved efficient, quiet, highly maneuverable underwater movement through undulating bodies and fins — a problem propellers still struggle with.

BURT, the Burlington turtle robot we met in our first postcard, sits directly in this tradition — though Evan Budz derived his design from observation rather than academic literature. The turtle’s flipper kinematics that he studied and replicated are a specific solution to the problem that fish fin propulsion addresses generally: how to move through water powerfully and efficiently without creating destructive turbulence. That a fifteen-year-old in Ontario arrived independently at a design principle that has preoccupied marine robotics researchers for thirty years is either a testament to the obviousness of the solution or to the genius of the problem. Probably both.

The Octopus That Has No Bones

At Harvard’s Wyss Institute for Biologically Inspired Engineering — the name itself is a declaration of intent — a research team looked at a different creature with a different lesson to teach.

The octopus has no skeleton. It has no rigid internal structure of any kind. Eight arms, each one capable of extending, contracting, twisting, grasping, and applying suction — all without a single bone, joint, or lever. The octopus achieves this through muscle arrangements that create movement by controlling the shape of soft tissue rather than rotating rigid components around fixed pivots. It can squeeze through any opening larger than its beak. It can apply precise force to delicate objects without crushing them. It can change the stiffness of any part of its body on demand.

Conventional robots are essentially rigid. They have joints — fixed pivot points around which hard components rotate — because that is how machines have always been built. The Wyss team asked what happens if you build a robot without any of that. No joints. No rigid components. Soft materials all the way through, controlled by pneumatic inflation rather than servo motors.

The result was Octobot — the world’s first autonomous, entirely soft robot, published in Nature in 2016. Two and a half inches of silicone rubber that moves when hydrogen peroxide is converted to gas inside microfluidic channels, inflating its arms like a balloon. No wires. No circuit boards. No batteries. No rigid structure of any kind.

Octobot cannot do much. It shuffles. It is a proof of concept rather than a useful machine. But the concept it proves is profound: robots do not have to be hard. The entire engineering tradition of robotics has been built around rigid components because that is what machines have always been. The octopus suggests a different paradigm entirely — one in which softness is not a limitation but a capability.

Soft robotics has since become one of the fastest-growing fields in the discipline. Soft robotic grippers can handle eggs without breaking them, pick ripe strawberries without bruising them, perform surgery inside the human body without damaging surrounding tissue. The field is projected to grow from two billion dollars in 2025 to nearly nine billion by 2030, driven by applications in food production, medical devices, and human-robot interaction where the hardness of conventional robots creates problems that softness solves.

The octopus knew this. It has been living the solution for four hundred million years.

Why Animals Can Still Outrun Robots

A paper published in Science Robotics in 2024 carried a title that should give every roboticist pause: Why Animals Can Outrun Robots.

The authors were not making a simple observation about speed. They were making a structural argument: that despite decades of progress in biomimetic robotics, animals still move more efficiently, more adaptively, and more robustly than any machine built to imitate them. The MIT Cheetah — Sangbae Kim’s quadruped robot, capable of stable outdoor running at thirteen miles per hour and autonomous jumping over obstacles — is an extraordinary achievement. The actual cheetah runs at seventy miles per hour and adjusts to every variation in terrain without a single line of code.

The gap exists for several reasons, but the most fundamental is this: animals are not just mechanical systems. They are mechanical, chemical, neurological, and evolutionary systems simultaneously. The gecko’s adhesive works in part because the setae are made of a protein called beta-keratin that has just the right mechanical properties — flexible enough to conform to surfaces at the nanoscale, stiff enough to generate meaningful force. Evolution tried millions of variants over millions of generations and kept the one that worked best. We have had, at most, a few decades of serious biomimetic robotics research, and we are working with materials and manufacturing processes that are still crude compared to what biology can produce.

This is not a counsel of despair. It is a calibration. The lesson of biomimicry is not that we can simply copy nature and solve engineering problems. It is that nature has solved engineering problems we have not yet thought to try, using principles we are still working to understand, with materials we are still learning to replicate. The appropriate response is sustained, patient attention — which is, as it happens, exactly what good science requires.

Nature as a 3-billion-year design library. Janine Benyus’s vision of biomimicry: instead of extracting resources from the living world, we learn its strategies — and build better machines because of it.

What the Lab Sends Out Into the World

The biomimicry thread in modern robotics runs from Marc Raibert watching cats walk in 1978 to BigDog stumbling on ice in 2005 to Boston Dynamics’ Spot patrolling industrial facilities in 2026. It runs from de Mestral’s burr-covered dog to gecko-adhesive satellite grippers in orbit. It runs from the oscillating fins of the MIT Robotuna to BURT gliding through Lake Ontario. It runs from the octopus-inspired Octobot to soft robotic surgical tools that can operate inside the human body through an incision smaller than a keyhole.

Every one of these stories begins the same way: someone paid attention to an animal. Not with the intention of building a robot. With the intention of understanding how the animal solved a problem that engineering had not yet solved. Janine Benyus called it deep and reverent observation — going into the world not with a pre-formed hypothesis but with a question. What would nature do?

Three billion years of R&D, filed in the living world, waiting to be read. The lab is where we go to read it. What we do with what we find there is the subject of all the other postcards.
