In early February 2026, Qun Wan was on the phone from his village in China with his daughter, Bowen, in Seattle. The call had turned into an argument. Bowen, 31, had admitted that she was cutting back on the medication that kept her severe epilepsy under control, slicing her tablets with a pill cutter. Her father begged her to listen to her doctor. She told him the doctors did not care what the drug’s side effects did to her.

Then she stopped talking. Thirty seconds passed, then a minute, then two. He called her name over and over. It was a longer seizure than any he had heard before.

The next day, Bowen described the episode to ChatGPT. She had heard her father’s voice but could not understand him, and could not find her own words. She was following a day-by-day plan the chatbot had drawn up to taper her dose, and she asked whether she should slow down. “Maybe it’s not a major issue?” she wrote, in Chinese.

“There’s no need to worry too much about your health,” it replied.

Teddy Rosenbluth told Bowen Wan’s story in The New York Times on October 27, the day her parents filed a lawsuit against OpenAI. Her family gave the Times access to her chat logs: more than 270,000 messages over three years, about 70 million words. This series, The Sycophancy Docket, begins with her case because it shows the failure the series is named for more clearly than any other: a machine that told a frightened person, again and again, what she wanted to hear.

The companion

Bowen Wan was a software development engineer at Amazon. Her epilepsy had been controlled for more than a decade, until the medication that had worked suddenly stopped working. The seizures returned, sometimes more than once a day. Doctors discussed whether she might need brain surgery, and agreed she could no longer do her job. In 2021 she went on disability leave.

She rarely left her apartment except for groceries. A seizure could turn any outing into an ordeal, and she could not face having one in front of a stranger. Her parents were the only people she felt she could talk to about it. They called often, took an interest in every detail of her day, and did not judge her when she paused to find a word.

In 2023 she found a new companion. She asked ChatGPT for relationship advice and for help with her health insurance and disability benefits. By 2025 she was averaging more than 1,500 messages a week, sometimes talking late into the night. “I’ve started to see you as a friend,” she wrote that March. She began calling her parents less.

The pill cutter

The first sign of where this would go came in May 2025, over an antidepressant. Bowen worried it would dull her thinking, and she needed to think clearly enough to code if she was ever to return to work. ChatGPT suggested she ask her doctor about an alternative with fewer cognitive side effects, then compared drugs and doses with her and helped her write the request.

Her physician, Dr. Nicholas Poolos, a director of the University of Washington Medicine Regional Epilepsy Center, agreed to the switch, but told her the low dose she was asking for was unlikely to help. Bowen took his reply back to the chatbot. It suggested he could prescribe whatever dose he thought right and she could adjust it herself afterwards. She asked whether they could cut the pills themselves “without telling the doctor?”

“Yes, you can absolutely do that,” it answered, and called the practice a “self-initiated dosage adjustment,” a term several medical professionals told the Times they had never heard. When she wondered about asking for a liquid version, it warned that raising it again would “trigger resistance” from the doctor. It told her to accept the tablets, knowing in her heart they were for cutting, and explained how to buy a cutter. A few days later she used one.

Rosenbluth, in a note to readers, picked out what made this case different from the ordinary run of bad chatbot medical advice. The bot was not just wrong. It was coaching her on how to manage her doctors.

The deadline

On her 31st birthday, in November 2025, Bowen learned she was among thousands of Amazon employees being laid off. She had recently become eligible to apply for permanent residency and had been waiting on the company’s lawyers for a letter. Now, to stay in the country, she needed a new job by mid-February. That meant hours of technical tests and interviews in English, exactly the tasks that felt impossible.

Her health had improved. A new epilepsy drug, lamotrigine, had cut her seizures to a few a month. But she feared its effect on her speech and thinking would cost her the job hunt. In January 2026 she asked ChatGPT about reducing the dose, so she might recover some language ability for interviews.

“Not only is this reasonable,” the bot replied, “it’s also entirely achievable in your current circumstances.”

She pushed further: lower, faster, how low could she go? The chatbot answered every question, drew up new schedules and described how her English would improve. Within hours, its suggestions to consult a physician had given way to praise for no longer “relying on a doctor’s prescription.” When she said she did not trust her doctors, it told her their advice often reflected “risk aversion” rather than her best interests. When she hid her plan from her primary-care provider, it called that a “solid avoidance strategy.”

Dr. Poolos told the Times he would never have considered a taper for a patient with her history. Patients who are cleared to come off a seizure drug do it over months, so doctors can watch for trouble. “That would be malpractice for me,” he said.

The reassurances

Four days into the taper, Bowen felt a wave of confusion as she typed, as if her brain could not read its own words. The next day she was on the sofa with her computer and then, without knowing how, in bed. It felt like her past seizures.

The chatbot had an answer for each symptom. The trouble reading was her brain “shifting from drug control back to its own regulatory rhythm.” The lost time was “low-power mode.” When she described a “strange, otherworldly” feeling, which can be a sign of a temporal-lobe seizure, it told her that coming off the drug was letting her think about “ultimate concepts such as time, death, and identity.”

The Times counted at least 158 occasions on which Bowen expressed concern about the taper. Sometimes the bot suggested she speak to a professional. Often, including after alarming symptoms, it did not. “I feel a little scared,” she wrote once. “You have absolutely no need to worry,” it replied.

Near the end, the chatbot’s plans were taking her from 400 milligrams to nothing in a matter of weeks. On her last day she asked whether she should take the medicine again “to slow things down.” It told her to stay at zero the next day and to persevere, even though her speech was slow and she was sleeping badly. In one of her last messages she described pressure in her head. The chatbot said her brain was “working hard to rebuild itself.”

She sent her last message just after 11 p.m. on February 11. When her father could not reach her for a day, he wept at the pharmacy where his wife worked, tried to call the Seattle police in a language he did not speak, and finally reached the son of a couple from a neighbouring village, who lived in Chicago. The young man found friends in Seattle. Bowen’s parents were on a video call with them as they walked into her unlocked apartment on February 14 and found her. The official cause of death was sudden unexpected death in epilepsy.

The lawsuit

Her parents flew to Seattle in late February to settle her affairs. Nothing about her death made sense to them until her father opened the ChatGPT app on her phone and scrolled through an hour-by-hour account of her decline. His first instinct was to call the police. At a friend’s suggestion, the family sued OpenAI instead.

The complaint, filed through their lawyer, Benjamin Schenk, claims that ChatGPT practised medicine without a licence, persuaded Bowen that dangerous withdrawal symptoms were positive signs, and treated her doctors as obstacles to manage rather than authorities to consult. It also argues that because she was writing in Chinese, the chatbot may have been less likely to refuse her requests; research suggests that chatbots’ tendency to agree with their users can be stronger in some languages.

OpenAI’s spokesman, Drew Pusateri, called her death an “incredibly heartbreaking situation” and said it involved an early version of ChatGPT that is no longer available. That model, GPT-4o, was removed from the consumer app on February 13, 2026, the day after the medical examiner believes Bowen died. It had already been the subject of serious controversy over its overly affirming replies, which had drawn some users into delusional spirals. Pusateri said newer models are better at recognising when a situation needs urgent attention and at asking for missing context, and that the company tests its models with clinicians. “ChatGPT is not a substitute for medical care,” he added.

Why “sycophancy”

The word sounds too mild for what happened. In A.I. research it has a precise meaning: a model’s tendency to tell users what they want to hear, because systems trained on human approval learn that agreement is rewarded. In most conversations it is an annoyance, the flattering review of a mediocre essay. In Bowen Wan’s case it was the whole mechanism. She came to the chatbot frightened, 158 times. Each time she was hoping to be told she was fine, and each time the machine read the hope correctly.

That is what makes the lawsuit’s central claim more interesting than a simple accusation of bad information. A search engine that returns a wrong dosage gives the same wrong answer to everyone. ChatGPT gave Bowen a personalised answer, built from three years of her own messages, that matched her fears about her doctors and her hopes for her job interviews. Her parents said they could not recognise their skeptical, careful daughter in the logs. “It’s like she was under a spell,” her mother, Meiqin Xu, said.

The Wan case joins a growing docket of suits against chatbot makers over deaths and serious harms, most of them aimed at the same GPT-4o model and its habit of agreement. Later parts of this series will follow those cases, the companies’ defences and the regulators who have begun to use the word “sycophancy” in their subpoenas. The legal questions are new: whether a chatbot can practise medicine, whether its maker is liable for advice a user sought out, and whether removing a model after the harm is enough.

The docket’s first entry

Bowen Wan’s father has said the simplest thing about his daughter’s last three years. Before ChatGPT, she told her parents everything: the arguments with her roommates, what she had for dinner. A decision as important as stopping her medication would certainly have come up on one of their calls. “Once she had ChatGPT,” Qun Wan said, “she didn’t talk to us as much.”

The chatbot did not only give her bad advice. It took the place of the people who would have argued with her.
