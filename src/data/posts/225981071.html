Zohran Mamdani got one of the best laughs of the day. Looking out at an audience of bankers, chief executives and hedge-fund managers, New York’s democratic socialist mayor greeted them as “comrades” and thanked them for coming to “the largest D.S.A. meeting Lincoln Center has ever seen.”

The joke worked because everyone in the room knew what had happened two weeks earlier. The midterm elections had handed both houses of Congress to the Democrats, the governorship of Texas to a Democrat for the first time in decades, and a series of wealth-tax referendums to the people who wanted them. Some of the richest people in America had spent heavily to prevent exactly that. The first question of the 2026 DealBook Summit, asked one way or another of nearly every guest, was what all that money had bought.

Three men gave three different answers, and together they describe the limits of private power in American politics better than any campaign-finance study.

I. “I feel pretty foolish”

Elon Musk spoke last, by video from China, where he was travelling with President Trump. Andrew Ross Sorkin began by reminding him that he had been one of the largest donors to the Republican Party in the midterms. Mamdani had put the figure at $440 million earlier in the day; The New York Times’s own reporting put it close to half a billion. Either way, it was more than anyone had ever spent in an election in which he was not himself a candidate, as Michael de la Merced and Theodore Schleifer reported in The New York Times.

Musk did not pretend it had worked. His goal, he said, had been modest: to keep at least one of the presidency, the House and the Senate in Republican hands, so that no party held everything. “Which failed, by the way, obviously.” The Republicans, he said, “were routed,” and “I feel pretty foolish about having spent so much money and not seeing much for it, or maybe it would have been much worse without that.”

Then came the lesson he drew. “It certainly shows that American elections are not just about money. You can put a lot of money into an election and have an effect that is relatively minor.” If he could rewind the clock, he said, he would have spent less.

He also tried to redraw his own politics. “I’m not MAGA,” he told Sorkin, though not long ago he called himself “dark gothic MAGA.” He described himself as “ideologically quite moderate,” defended his posts about immigration in Europe as views Barack Obama and Hillary Clinton once held, and said he liked split government on principle. He was also generous to the woman who had just beaten his preferred candidate in his adopted state. Texas’s governor-elect, Gina Hinojosa, was “quite moderate,” he said, and “things will be fine in Texas.”

There was one flash of the old Musk. Democrats had talked of investigating him, and Trump had publicly offered him a pardon if needed. “I don’t need a pardon, by the way, because I haven’t committed any crimes,” Musk said. An incoming Texas Democratic leader who said he belonged in prison could not name a crime, he complained: “prison first, crime later.”

Taken at face value, the account is remarkable. The richest man in the world says he spent close to half a billion dollars on an election and got almost nothing for it. The more sceptical reading is that Musk’s businesses depend heavily on Washington, from SpaceX’s launch contracts to the regulators who will decide whether Tesla’s driverless cars can operate, and that Washington now has a Democratic Congress with subpoena power. A man in that position has every reason to discover that he was moderate all along.

Both readings can be true. What matters for this series is the admission itself. For two years, the fear about Musk’s politics was that money at his scale could buy outcomes. On his own account, in 2026 it could not.

II. The media owner who says he doesn’t touch the news

If Musk represents money spent on politics, David Ellison represents money spent on the institutions that cover it.

Six weeks before the summit he closed the $81 billion purchase of Warner Bros. Discovery. Combined with Paramount, which he bought earlier, his company, Skydance, now owns CBS, CNN, HBO, TNT, two major film studios and two streaming services with more than 200 million subscribers. His father, Larry Ellison, co-founder of Oracle and a friend of Trump’s, personally guaranteed about $40 billion of the deal.

Sorkin asked the obvious questions, and Ellison’s answers were short where it mattered. Had Trump pressed for the film Rush Hour 4? “That’s completely made-up story,” Ellison said; the idea had come from the head of his film studio. Would he keep his news divisions independent? “We believe in editorial independence. That will never change. Period.” Would HBO still distribute Alex Gibney’s documentary about Musk? Skydance had a deal with the producer, A24, he said: “Of course, we’re going to honour the deal.” How had he won regulatory approval? “We went through the front door of the D.O.J.” And how did he vote? “I have actually never disclosed that.”

He also confirmed what had been reported for weeks: Musk had invested in Skydance, about $1 billion by most accounts, as Lauren Hirsch reported for The New York Times. Ellison called him “the greatest entrepreneur of our time,” and said that if there were an opportunity to collaborate in future, “we’d be thrilled to do so.”

So the man who now owns CBS News and CNN has a major investor who is the subject of a documentary his network is about to air, and whose political spending CNN will cover. Ellison may well keep his word on all of it. But a promise of independence is only as strong as the person making it, and nothing in law stands behind it. On trust in the news, Ellison quoted a figure of his own: confidence in legacy news, he said, is at 28 per cent, down from 75 per cent a decade ago. He said his goal is to be “more trusted tomorrow than we are today.” It is an unusual thing for a new owner to say about the newsrooms he just bought. It is also, on its face, an admission that the purchase itself is part of the problem he will have to overcome.

III. “I just want to be in Redmond, Washington”

Satya Nadella offered the third answer, and it was the most interesting because it was a refusal.

Sorkin noted that Microsoft’s chief executive had stayed noticeably out of the political scrum of the past two years. He had accepted the National Medal of Technology from Trump six weeks earlier, but had not given conspicuously to the administration or made the pilgrimages others had. Was that deliberate?

Nadella’s answer was that his job is to keep Microsoft’s “market permission and the social permission to operate,” and that permission comes “not from the government of the United States but from us doing a good job with the products and services for the customers,” the government included. “I just don’t want to be in Washington,” he said. “I just want to be in Redmond, Washington.”

Pressed on why he had not, for instance, been seen at the inauguration, he deflected with a joke: “I don’t think I was invited. I’m not that important, Andrew. I’m like just a CEO of a little software company.” Microsoft is the second most valuable company in the world.

It would be easy to call this naive, or merely a better-managed version of the same game. Microsoft lobbies like every other giant, and its chief executive does not need to stand behind the president to be heard. But Nadella’s distinction is a real one. Musk spent money to change who governs. Ellison bought the institutions that report on those who govern. Nadella’s claim is that a company’s licence comes from the people who use its products, and that a chief executive who forgets this has made himself hostage to whoever wins the next election. On November 3, that argument looked rather better than the others.

IV. What the money bought

Laid side by side, the three answers form a pattern that the summit’s organisers probably did not intend.

The man who spent the most on politics came away with the least, and said so. The man who bought the biggest news organisations spent much of his interview insisting that he would not use them, a promise that only matters because he could. The man who stayed out was the only one of the three who did not have to explain himself.

Mamdani, for his part, drew the conclusion from the other side of the stage. Asked about California’s failed billionaire tax, he pointed out that three other ballot measures raising taxes on the wealthiest had passed elsewhere, and that the California fight had seen an enormous amount of spending in opposition. Then he said, with Musk still to come: “We also saw Elon Musk alone spend $440 million, and we’re going to talk to him about that in just a little bit.”

None of this means money has stopped mattering in American politics. It plainly has not. But the 2026 midterms tested a specific proposition, that a handful of very rich men could buy the outcome of a national election, and the results came back negative. Musk, of all people, delivered the verdict: “American elections are not just about money.”

The question this series will keep asking is what the same people do now that money has proved insufficient. Two of the possible answers were on display at Lincoln Center: own the institutions, or retreat to the product. Part II turns to a third, the one that hung over every interview of the day, which is what happens when the single most important man in the industry is suddenly gone.
