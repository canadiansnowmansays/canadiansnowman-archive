Flip phone vs smartphone. Kaitlyn Tiffany’s experiment showed how devices quietly become mandatory infrastructure.

In March 2026, Kaitlyn Tiffany, a staff writer at The Atlantic, traded her iPhone for a flip phone for a month. She did it for a story. The experiment was not particularly dramatic. She managed. She found some things easier — the frantic feeling of constant availability dissipated — and some things harder. She could not enter Citi Field without her smartphone because the stadium requires a mobile ticket. There is no paper option. There is no PDF. There is no workaround.

She described this detail in passing, as a minor inconvenience. It is not a minor point.

Citi Field has not banned smartphones. It has restructured its operations around the assumption of smartphone ownership, such that attendance at a baseball game now requires the device. The stadium did not ask permission to make smartphones mandatory infrastructure. The decision was made incrementally, by many parties, for reasons of operational efficiency, and by the time it was complete the option to attend without the device had simply ceased to exist.

This is how technological dependency works. Not through mandate. Through restructuring.

The AI version of this process is already underway. It is further along than the smartphone version. It is moving faster. And unlike the smartphone dependency — which primarily affects how you navigate the world — the AI dependency that is accumulating concerns how you think.

The Skill That Is Not Being Exercised

Isaac Asimov spent fifty years imagining what happens to human capability when intelligent machines do the hard work.

In The Naked Sun, the Spacer civilization had delegated so completely to robots that its people had lost the capacity for basic physical and social functions. Solaria’s humans could not tolerate being in the same room as another person. Aurora’s humans could not function without robotic support. Asimov was not writing about robots becoming dangerous. He was writing about humans becoming helpless.

The helplessness did not arrive through catastrophe. It arrived through comfort. Each delegation was individually rational. Why learn to cook when a robot cooks for you? Why navigate when a robot navigates? Why maintain social skills when a robot manages your calendar, your correspondence, and your relationships? The accumulation of individually sensible delegations produced, over generations, a civilization incapable of the tasks it had delegated.

This is the dependency problem. Not the governance failure at the top — the deleted AGI clause, the dissolved safety teams, the Pentagon contracts. The dependency problem is quieter and more personal. It is the set of skills that are not being exercised because AI is exercising them instead.

The list is already long and growing. Code that is written with AI assistance that no human fully understands. Medical diagnoses suggested by AI that doctors increasingly ratify rather than generate. Legal briefs drafted by AI that lawyers review rather than compose. Research syntheses produced by AI that students submit rather than conduct. The skill is not being stolen. It is being rendered unnecessary, one delegation at a time, until the moment it is needed and no longer present.

What the Dumb Phone Movement Understood

The people showing up to Month Offline meetings in Bushwick, New York — twenty-six to thirty-two year olds in creative class jobs who felt their time being consumed by a device they chose to carry — understood something intuitively that takes longer to articulate analytically.

The smartphone did not replace their social skills. It changed the conditions under which those skills were exercised. The constant availability, the Find My Friends location sharing, the Instagram reels at bedtime — none of these prevented human connection. They changed its texture, reduced its friction, and in doing so altered the experience of connection itself.

Kaitlyn Tiffany described running into people she knew more often when she walked without earbuds. Not because the earbuds prevented the encounters — they were on the same streets regardless — but because without the audio cocoon she was present to the environment in a way that made the encounters register. The skill of noticing was not absent. It was suppressed.

AI suppression operates at a different level of cognition. When you ask an AI to synthesize research, the synthesis still arrives. The conclusion may be correct. The sources may be appropriate. What does not arrive — what is specifically not exercised — is the process of reading across sources, holding contradictions in tension, noticing that one author’s framing changes the meaning of another’s argument, arriving at a conclusion that is yours because you built it from parts you assembled yourself. The output is similar. The cognition is absent.

This would matter less if the cognition were simply offloaded — if humans retained the capacity and chose not to exercise it, the way a person who takes taxis retains the ability to walk. The concern is that cognition not exercised is cognition not developed. A generation that grows up using AI to write their essays does not merely outsource the writing. It outsources the thinking that writing produces. The essay is not the product. The thinking is the product. The essay is the evidence that the thinking occurred.

Symbolic representation of AI dependency: When machines do the thinking, what skills do humans stop exercising?

The Infrastructure Restructuring

The smartphone dependency and the AI dependency differ in one critical structural respect.

The smartphone became mandatory infrastructure because businesses restructured their operations around it incrementally, without any single decision that could be contested or reversed. Citi Field did not announce a policy requiring smartphones. It announced a digital ticketing system that happened to require them. The accumulation of such decisions produced a world in which opting out of smartphone ownership means opting out of significant portions of civic and commercial life.

The AI restructuring is happening at a faster pace and at a deeper layer of cognition. The IRS Fight Club, examined in Part 35, was doing AI work so carefully and incrementally that it required a cover story to avoid political interference. What DOGE interrupted was not a policy. It was a capability — a carefully developed institutional competence in AI-assisted modernization that, once dismantled, will not simply reassemble when a different administration arrives. The people who developed that competence have taken buyouts and walked out the door. The competence walked with them.

This is the dependency problem applied to institutions rather than individuals. Organizations that build competence around AI-assisted processes become dependent on those processes in ways that are not immediately visible. The IRS learned to use AI to translate COBOL. When DOGE arrived, the AI capability was paused. The COBOL engineers who were retiring before the AI arrived did not un-retire. The dependency that had developed — on AI as a bridge between the old infrastructure and the new — was interrupted precisely as it was becoming load-bearing.

The same dynamic operates in medical diagnosis, legal research, financial analysis, and any domain where AI assistance has become embedded in professional practice faster than the underlying human capability has been allowed to atrophy. The capability atrophies gradually. The AI is interrupted suddenly. The gap between them is the dependency problem in its most dangerous form.

The Governance Dimension

The dependency problem is not separate from the governance argument this series has been making. It is its deepest expression.

Every part of this series has been about the distance between capability and accountability — between what AI can do and the structures available to govern how it is done. The dependency problem adds a third term: what humans can do without AI. If that third term shrinks — if the skills necessary to understand, evaluate, and govern AI systems are themselves being delegated to AI systems — the governance gap does not merely persist. It becomes self-reinforcing.

The people who are supposed to regulate AI need to understand AI well enough to regulate it. The people who are supposed to evaluate AI’s outputs need to understand the domain well enough to know when the output is wrong. The people who are supposed to hold AI systems accountable need to maintain the cognitive independence to form judgments that are not themselves AI-mediated. If the cognitive skills required for democratic governance of AI are being eroded by the same AI systems they are supposed to govern, the governance gap is not just a political failure. It is an epistemic one.

Asimov’s Three Laws failed not because they were poorly designed, but because the humans applying them lost the capacity to apply them well. The robots were too capable, too embedded, too economically necessary to question. The humans who were supposed to provide oversight had delegated so much to the robots that the oversight itself became robotic — the application of rules without the judgment to know when the rules were being gamed.

The 4chan gamers who discovered chain-of-thought prompting in 2020 — examined in Part 29 — were being more scientifically precise than the researchers who later claimed credit for the finding. They had not delegated their understanding of what the AI was doing. They had maintained the cognitive independence to notice when the AI performed differently than expected. That independence is a capability. It can be lost.

Senator Kirsten Gillibrand, introducing legislation in June 2026 to restrict how the Pentagon uses AI, put the governance dimension in terms that do not require a philosophy degree. “Lethal decisions require a conscience, not just an algorithm,” she said, “and a machine shouldn’t make those types of decisions.” At the Air Force Academy commencement the week before, Vice President JD Vance had invoked Pope Leo XIV’s encyclical — the same document examined in Part 41 — saying that “decisions over life and death must be made by humans and not machines.” When the Vice President of the United States is citing a papal encyclical to make the case for preserving human decision-making capacity, the dependency problem has moved from philosophy seminar to the policy agenda.

The precaution the legislation is trying to codify is precisely the precaution the dependency problem requires: the maintenance of human judgment as a structural requirement, not a preference that can be optimised away.

The Precaution That Isn’t Being Taken

The dumb phone movement is a voluntary, individual response to a voluntary, individual dependency. People are choosing to limit their smartphone use because they have noticed what the smartphone is doing to their attention, their social presence, their experience of time.

No equivalent movement exists for AI cognitive dependency, because the dependency is less visible. The smartphone’s effects are legible — you notice when you have been doing phone for forty-five minutes instead of drinking your coffee. The AI’s effects are not legible — you do not notice that the thinking you did not do because AI did it was the thinking that would have produced the insight you did not have.

The governance precaution that is not being taken is the precaution of preserving human capability alongside AI capability. Not through a ban on AI use — that is the defensive crouch Tyrangiel identified as ineffective in Part 35. Through deliberate cultivation of the skills that AI is displacing: the ability to write without AI, to synthesize without AI, to diagnose without AI, to code without AI, not as a permanent restriction but as a maintained capacity. The way a pilot who uses autopilot for most of a flight is still required to be able to fly the plane.

The governance question is not whether to allow AI. The governance question is whether the people who live with AI will retain the cognitive independence to govern it, evaluate it, and replace it if necessary. That question is not being asked by the Lieu bill, the Oakland trial, or the Pentagon contracts. It is not being asked by the industry that is restructuring cognition around its products. It is being asked, quietly and individually, by the people in the Bushwick meeting who noticed something was happening to their attention and decided to do something about it.

They are, in Asimov’s terms, the resistant humans. The question his stories always asked was not whether the resistance was right. It was whether it was in time.
