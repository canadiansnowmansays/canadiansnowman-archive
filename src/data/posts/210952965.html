Every financial era gets the parable it deserves. This one arrived, fittingly, three days before a wedding.

The Essay That Became a Hedge Fund

As David Wallace-Wells and Natasha Sarin discussed on The New York Times’s “The Opinions” , Leopold Aschenbrenner is 24 years old, has no formal finance background, and until Monday ran one of the most closely watched AI-focused hedge funds in the country. The fund, Situational Awareness, took its name from the essay Aschenbrenner wrote in 2024, which predicted labs would reach something like superintelligence by 2027 and that getting there would require capital investment at a scale the market hadn’t yet priced in. Investors including Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, and the Collison brothers of Stripe read that essay and handed him money to bet on it. For a while, spectacularly, it worked — Sarin cited returns near 1,000 percent at the fund’s peak.

Then, over roughly 36 hours this week, it nearly didn’t survive. Aschenbrenner had leveraged the fund four-to-one, per Sarin’s account — for every investor dollar, four more borrowed from public and private markets, riding both long positions in AI infrastructure plays and short positions against legacy software firms like Adobe. When AI-adjacent holdings like the South Korean chipmaker SK Hynix wobbled and his short positions turned against him simultaneously, the banks that had happily extended credit on the way up issued margin calls on the way down — Rob Copeland reported for the Times that the fund put out an emergency call to rivals seeking to offload more than $10 billion in stock, and that Kenneth Griffin’s Citadel won a brief overnight bidding war to buy the position at a discount. Wallace-Wells and Sarin’s framing is the one worth keeping: this is simultaneously a story about AI specifically and a story we’ve seen before, structurally identical to Long-Term Capital Management in the late 1990s — leverage, a margin call, a forced sale that depresses the very price it’s trying to protect, and a spiral that feeds itself.

“A Kind of Narrative Reset”

What makes the Aschenbrenner episode more than a Wall Street morality tale, in Wallace-Wells’s phrase, is that his original thesis was never a fringe position. It was, almost word for word, the story every major AI lab has been telling investors and the public for years: the technology is improving faster than anyone appreciates, a takeoff point is close, and whoever crosses the finish line first captures profits at a scale never before seen. Aschenbrenner didn’t just believe that story — he built a fund on the premise that it was true enough to justify skipping the conventional guardrails around leverage. Sarin’s read is sharp: the size of his bet was itself a bet that no ordinary risk management applied, because the underlying transformation was too large for ordinary rules to matter. That premise held right up until a margin call proved it didn’t.

Half a Trillion Dollars, Announced the Day Before

The timing here is worth sitting with. One day before Aschenbrenner’s fund nearly folded, Rob Copeland and Kalley Huang reported that six of the largest names in finance — BlackRock, Goldman Sachs, KKR, Apollo, Blackstone, and Brookfield — announced a coordinated effort to raise $500 billion specifically to lend to Nvidia’s customers for chips and data centers. Larry Fink, BlackRock’s chief executive, put the urgency plainly on air: “We need to raise this money as fast as possible.” He went further, calling the moment “a next future for financial engineering” and comparing it to his own start in mortgage-backed securities in the 1970s — a comparison that will read very differently to different readers depending on how the 2008 story ended for them.

Jensen Huang, whose company sits at the center of the arrangement, declined to name which AI labs or startups would actually receive the financing, or whether Nvidia itself stood to profit from the deal it brokered — a detail that matters given Nvidia has already faced criticism for what a Financial Times report described as circular financing arrangements with its own customers. The mechanics disclosed so far are thin: a joint release referring only to “memorandums of understanding” and executives alluding, per Copeland and Huang’s reporting, to “yield-based products” and securitization without specifics. Nvidia’s own stock dipped modestly on the news.

What the Independent Numbers Add

Set alongside outside research, the picture gets both bigger and more specific than either the Aschenbrenner story or the consortium announcement alone suggests. JPMorgan now projects global AI-related capital expenditure will reach $5.5 trillion through 2030, with debt financing accounting for $4.1 trillion of that total — a figure that has climbed each time the bank has revised it. Alphabet posted negative free cash flow in the second quarter of 2026, the first time in the company’s history as a public entity, while its long-term debt more than doubled to $98 billion in six months. Amazon’s long-term debt jumped 81 percent, to $119 billion, in a single quarter. Hyperscaler capex intensity — capital spending as a share of revenue — has reportedly climbed past 30 percent, more than double the peak reached during the dot-com buildout of the late 1990s.

None of this proves Aschenbrenner’s thesis wrong. JPMorgan’s own analysts remain bullish, projecting hyperscaler operating cash flow will exceed $900 billion by 2027, and framing the shift toward debt as a deliberate, rational choice to finance construction now while credit is cheap and deleverage later. That’s a coherent argument. It coexists uneasily, though, with a detail Moody’s flagged that neither the Aschenbrenner coverage nor the consortium announcement mentioned: hyperscalers are collectively sitting on roughly $662 billion in signed data-center lease commitments that haven’t yet commenced, and which sit off balance sheet under current accounting rules — a shadow liability larger than those same companies’ combined on-the-books debt. We’ll come back to that number in Part II, because it deserves its own accounting rather than a footnote here.

The Whiplash Is the Point, Not the Exception

It would be a mistake to read the Aschenbrenner collapse as a single dramatic break in an otherwise steady market. The steadier pattern this year has actually been the opposite: violent, fast reversals that make the Situational Awareness story look less like an aberration and more like an extreme version of what’s already normal in this sector. Karen Weise’s reporting for the New York Times on Q2 hyperscaler earnings captured one such swing directly — Microsoft’s shares jumped more than 15 percent on a single day’s results, while Meta’s sank more than 7 percent the day after its own capex disclosure, and Google’s stock fell over 6 percent following its first-ever negative free cash flow report. Nvidia’s stock dipped on the consortium news this week, then the broader market shrugged it off within days. Wall Street gets spooked by a data point — a downgrade, a earnings miss, a margin call at some hedge fund nobody outside finance had heard of a week earlier — sells off hard, and then, often within days, decides the underlying story still holds and buys back in.

This isn’t unique to AI. It’s the standing behavior of Nasdaq-heavy, growth-narrative-driven markets generally — the same rhythm showed up during the dot-com run and, in a different register, through crypto’s cycles. What’s different this time is scale: the swings are happening on top of trillions in committed capex and hundreds of billions in fresh leverage, so a “normal” bad week now moves more real money, more real debt, and more real jobs than the same-sized swing would have moved five years ago. Erin Griffith’s reporting for the New York Times on Silicon Valley’s own venture capitalists is instructive here — many of them told her, more or less in these words, that treating any given downswing as the beginning of the end is usually the wrong trade, because bubbles, on the historical record, tend to run longer than the people calling them expect. That’s not comfort so much as a warning shape: the swings will likely keep happening, in both directions, and neither the panic days nor the relief-rally days should be read as the final verdict on their own.

This series’ companion, The Asimov Gap, has spent its recent parts documenting a governance vacuum — no fixed framework deciding who gets to build or restrict what, and at what pace. The Capex Ledger exists because there’s a parallel vacuum in how the money underneath that buildout is being priced and risked, and the two are not unrelated. An industry racing ahead with no governing framework and an industry racing ahead with historically unprecedented leverage are, at minimum, drawing on the same underlying confidence that the growth story is too big and too fast for ordinary caution to apply. Aschenbrenner’s fund tested that confidence against a margin call and needed a bailout within 36 hours. The $500 billion Wall Street just committed is a bet that the same confidence will hold at a considerably larger scale, for considerably longer.

Whether that bet is right is not a question this series can answer yet. What it can do is keep the ledger.
