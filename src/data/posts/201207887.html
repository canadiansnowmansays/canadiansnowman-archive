On June 8, 2026, Chinese President Xi Jinping stepped off a plane at Pyongyang’s international airport and was greeted by Kim Jong-un, his wife Ri Sol Ju, and the full ceremonial apparatus of a state that had spent seventy years constructing rituals of sovereignty. Children carried balloons and hopped in choreographed welcome. A military honour guard snapped to attention. The leaders of the two countries — the world’s second-largest economy and one of the world’s most heavily sanctioned — shook hands and smiled for cameras that captured what both governments understood to be a carefully managed political signal.

The signal was directed outward and inward simultaneously. Outward, to Washington and Seoul and Tokyo: North Korea is not isolated. Its allies come to it. Inward, to twenty-six million North Koreans who had survived pandemic closures and market crackdowns and food shortages and the grinding weight of the most comprehensive surveillance state on earth: your sacrifice was worth it. The supreme leader is recognized. The hermit kingdom has ascended.

This is Part XI of a series that began at the same moment — with the same image, the same balcony, the same three men clapping. We have travelled from the ancient peninsula to the present one, from Gojoseon to the jangmadang generation, from the Joseon dynasty’s deliberate walls to the shoot-to-kill orders at the Yalu River. We have arrived at the moment the series was designed to reach. This is what the history was building toward. This is what “both sweets and bullets” means.

The Phrase

The Workers’ Party congress in 2025 used the phrase that became this essay’s title: North Koreans could now hope to have “both sweets and bullets” — both economic recovery and military prowess, both the satisfactions of a improving material life and the security of a state that no one can threaten with impunity. It was, as propaganda phrases go, unusually honest about the trade-off it was celebrating. The Korean people had endured the bullets for a long time. The sweets were being promised now, in the same breath, as compensation.

The phrase contains the essential tension of Kim Jong-un’s present moment. He has achieved, by the measures that matter most to his regime, a genuine triumph. North Korea’s nuclear arsenal is larger and more sophisticated than it has ever been. Its strategic position is stronger than at any point in the last thirty years. Its diplomatic isolation has cracked. Its economy is growing for the first time in a decade, powered by the war dividend from Ukraine. And the internal crackdown of the pandemic years has substantially reasserted the state’s monopoly over the information and economic lives of its people.

But the sweets remain elusive. The rice prices are still double what they were before the pandemic. The won has lost most of its value. The markets that kept people alive during the 1990s famine are operating under the tightest state control since they spontaneously emerged. The jangmadang generation, now in its thirties and forties, has not forgotten what the state failed to provide during the Arduous March or what the state took from it during the pandemic. The fracture that the famine opened has not been repaired. It has been papered over, with force and fear and the careful management of information, but the substrate remains unstable.

The Nuclear State

At the Supreme People’s Assembly in March 2026, Kim Jong-un spoke with the confidence of a man who has decided that a particular argument is over. He pledged to “irreversibly” cement North Korea’s status as a nuclear weapons state, called South Korea the “most hostile” state, and accused the United States of global “state terrorism and aggression.” He made no overture toward diplomacy. He offered no concessions. He described a five-year economic development plan focused on industrial modernization and electricity generation that said, with the quiet authority of a man who believes he no longer needs to apologize for anything, that North Korea was moving forward on its own terms.

The nuclear programme that Kim described as irreversible is not rhetorical. It is real. North Korea has conducted six nuclear tests since 2006, the most recent in 2017, producing a device estimated at approximately 250 kilotons — seventeen times the size of the bomb that destroyed Hiroshima. Its arsenal is estimated to contain somewhere between forty and one hundred nuclear warheads, with ongoing production. Its missile systems now include intercontinental ballistic missiles theoretically capable of reaching the continental United States, intermediate-range missiles capable of targeting Japan and Guam, and submarine-launched ballistic missiles that would complicate any adversary’s first-strike calculations. In April 2025, Kim launched North Korea’s largest naval destroyer, equipped to carry nuclear-capable ballistic and cruise missiles — a visible demonstration that North Korea is expanding its nuclear capability into new domains.

Russia has been providing technology transfers that are making these capabilities more sophisticated. The US Intelligence Community’s 2025 Worldwide Threat Assessment noted that Russia had offered assistance with North Korea’s space programme, including satellite launch vehicles and training — capabilities that are dual-use in the most direct sense, since the same rocket technology that launches satellites can be adapted for intercontinental missile delivery.

When Kim told his parliament that North Korea’s nuclear status was irreversible, he was not speaking of aspiration. He was describing a physical reality that exists in silos and submarines and warheads, that has been built over thirty years of sacrifice and sanctions and international pressure, and that cannot be unwound by any diplomatic process that Kim is prepared to enter. The days immediately before Xi’s June 2026 arrival in Pyongyang made the point with characteristic directness: Kim visited a missile factory and a newly operational weapons-grade uranium enrichment facility, vowing, as the New York Times reported, to “beef up our state’s nuclear forces at an exponential rate.” It was a message timed for maximum visibility — delivered on the eve of a state visit from the leader of the country that officially opposes North Korean nuclear development. The question of North Korean denuclearization — which consumed the attention of every American administration from Clinton to Biden — has been answered. The answer is no.

The Visitor

Xi Jinping’s decision to visit Pyongyang in June 2026 — his first trip in seven years, his first overseas visit of the year — was not a gesture of warmth between old allies. It was a calculated response to a strategic problem.

The problem was straightforward: North Korea had spent the previous four years developing an alternative patron in Russia, and that alternative had proven more generous than China had been. Russia provided food and fuel without the conditions and periodic cooling-of-relations that characterized Beijing’s management of Pyongyang. Russia provided military technology that China, cautious about international reaction and its own strategic interests, had been reluctant to supply. Russia provided a mutual defense treaty. Kim Jong-un had turned up in Vladivostok to meet Putin before he appeared in Beijing, a sequencing that was itself a message.

Xi came to Pyongyang to reassert China’s primacy before it was lost entirely. He brought, by analyst expectations, economic aid packages — grain, fertilizer, investment commitments — and the implicit promise of a Chinese group tourism programme that would bring hundreds of hundreds of Chinese visitors to North Korea, providing foreign currency earnings that the state could direct without the complications of private market activity. He also brought what Kim most wanted from China: the tacit acknowledgment that Beijing had moved from publicly advocating denuclearization of the peninsula to simply calling for “peace and stability” — a formulation that stops meaningfully short of demanding that Kim give up the weapons he has built. In a letter published in North Korea’s main state newspaper, Rodong Sinmun, Xi declared that relations between China and North Korea were at a “new historical starting point” — language that acknowledged, without stating explicitly, that the relationship had fundamentally changed.

The shift in China’s posture has been building for years. As Hong Min, an expert on North Korea at the Korea Institute for National Unification in Seoul, told the New York Times: “China’s approach to North Korea has shifted markedly over the past seven years, from the role of a mediator for North Korea–U.S. denuclearization talks to that of a strong strategic partner in countering the United States.” The change may reflect China’s desire to placate North Korea. But some analysts suggest it could also mean that Beijing has begun to view a nuclear-armed North Korea as leverage over Washington and Seoul — a geopolitical asset rather than a liability to be managed.

Kim received Xi’s visit from a position of confidence that would have been difficult to imagine a decade earlier. He was not the desperate supplicant dependent on Chinese goodwill for his regime’s survival. He was the leader of a nuclear state with a Russian alliance and a war dividend, entertaining a suitor who needed the visit as much as he did.

The new road bridge being built across the Yalu River — a modern structure to supplement the aging Sino-Korean Friendship Bridge — encodes the relationship in concrete and steel. China wants the connection. North Korea is in a position to receive it.

What the Experts Got Wrong

For thirty years, the most consistent prediction about North Korea was that it was about to collapse.

The collapse school had reasonable foundations. North Korea was economically irrational by almost every measurable standard. Its population was malnourished. Its industrial base had essentially stopped functioning. Its ideology was internally contradictory and externally discredited. Every state that had attempted the Soviet model had either reformed or dissolved. North Korea seemed to be the last holdout of a system that history had conclusively rejected.

The collapse school was wrong because it made a specific error: it assumed that economic failure and political failure would move together. They did not. The Kim regime survived the 1990s famine — the worst economic catastrophe in its history — without losing control of the instruments of power that mattered: the military, the security services, the border, the information environment. It emerged from the pandemic crackdown having reasserted those instruments in ways that would have seemed impossible in 2011, when the Western consensus assumed that Kim Jong-un was too young and inexperienced to consolidate power.

The collapse school also consistently underestimated the extent to which the Kim dynasty’s legitimacy rested not on economic performance — which it had never successfully delivered — but on the foundational mythology of anti-imperialist resistance that this series has traced from the Joseon dynasty through the Japanese colonial period through the Korean War. A government that frames its existence as the barrier between its people and foreign subjugation does not need to make people rich. It needs only to make the threat feel real and itself feel indispensable. Kim Jong-un has done both with considerable skill.

He has also benefited from the failures of his adversaries. The United States spent thirty years pursuing a denuclearization framework that North Korea was never going to accept. It spent those same years maintaining an alliance with South Korea while periodically undermining that alliance’s credibility through transactional demands and expressed doubts about its value. It watched Russia dismantle the sanctions architecture without mounting an effective response. It watched China decline to enforce restrictions while being unable to offer Beijing sufficient incentive to change that calculation. The failure to contain North Korea is not only Kim Jong-un’s achievement. It is also the West’s failure.

The Balance

The honest accounting of Kim Jong-un’s present position is neither the triumphalist version his state propaganda produces nor the dismissive version that treats North Korea as an irrational basket case that will eventually fail.

He has built a nuclear arsenal that is, for practical purposes, permanent. He has consolidated domestic control to a degree that exceeds his father’s and approaches his grandfather’s at his grandfather’s peak. He has found, in the Russia partnership, a revenue stream and a strategic alignment that have broken the pressure framework that his adversaries spent decades constructing. He has been received in Beijing as a peer rather than a dependent. He has survived every prediction of his imminent collapse and is now presiding over the fastest economic growth his country has seen in eight years.

He has also presided over the execution of people for listening to pop songs. He has returned a twenty-two-year-old American to his family in a vegetative state from which that young man died within days. He has maintained a system of political prison camps that hold an estimated one hundred thousand people in conditions that constitute crimes against humanity. He has closed his border in a way that killed people trying to cross it and left his population without medicine or adequate food during a pandemic. He has spent the wealth generated by the Russia partnership on warships and missiles while rice prices remain triple their pre-pandemic levels.

Both things are true simultaneously. The assessment of whether this constitutes success depends entirely on which of his objectives you take as primary. If the objective is the Kim family’s survival in power, the present is a success. If the objective is the welfare of the twenty-six million people whose lives are entirely subject to his decisions, the present is a catastrophe.

There is a third frame that this series has largely set aside in its focus on the North, and that deserves acknowledgment in the penultimate essay: the view from Seoul. South Korea — whose existence was the premise of the Korean War, whose economic miracle was the implicit rebuke to everything North Korea built, and whose culture is the “vicious cancer” that Kim spent the pandemic years trying to burn out of his people’s minds — lives with North Korea’s nuclear arsenal pointed at it from approximately thirty miles away, at its closest point. The DMZ that separates the two Koreas is the most heavily fortified border on earth, yet Seoul, a city of ten million people, sits within artillery range of North Korean guns that have been there since 1953.

South Koreans have lived their entire national existence under this shadow with a pragmatism that outside observers frequently mistake for indifference. They have built one of the world’s great economies, one of its most vibrant democracies, and one of its most influential popular cultures while this shadow has fallen across all of it. The dramas that North Koreans watch under blankets at night are South Korean. The pop music that gets people executed in Hyesan is South Korean. The shining half of the Korean Peninsula at night — the half that blazes in the satellite images where the North is dark — is South Korea’s achievement, built in the seventy years since the armistice while the North spent the same period building nuclear weapons.

The question of what North Korea’s present strength means for South Korea is therefore not only a security question. It is a question about whether two peoples who share a language, a history, and four thousand years of civilization on the same peninsula will spend another generation — or a century — pointing weapons at each other across the 38th parallel, or whether some other arrangement is possible.

Kim Jong-un has already answered, in his March 2026 parliament address and in the constitutional amendment that removed all references to eventual reunification: there is no other Korea. There is only the Democratic People’s Republic, sovereign and nuclear-armed and permanent. Whether South Korea — and the United States, and China, and the world — can find a way to live with that answer is the question this series ends with.

North Korea’s history — the history this series has traced — is in large part the history of this gap: between the stated purpose of the state, which has always been the dignity and security of the Korean people, and the actual function of the state, which has always been the preservation of the Kim dynasty’s power. The gap opened in the 1990s, when the jangmadang generation learned to see it. Kim Jong-un has spent fifteen years working to close it — not by delivering on the stated purpose, but by making the gap invisible again, through force and fear and the systematic elimination of any source of information that might illuminate it.

He is succeeding, by the narrow measure of his own survival. He is failing, by every other measure there is.

Final essay: Part XII — The Unfinished Peninsula. What does a nuclear North Korea mean for the next twenty-five years? Reunification as a dead concept. The generational change Kim cannot control. The information war he is winning but has not won. And the question that the whole history of this peninsula has been building toward: is there any outcome, for either Korea, that could be called good?
