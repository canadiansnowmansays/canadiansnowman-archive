A quiet Sunday morning in Toronto — the ordinary Canadian backdrop from which the smoke across the southern border looks especially concerning.

I am writing this from Toronto, on a Sunday morning, at a kitchen table that looks out on a street where the most dangerous thing that happened last week was a disagreement about recycling bins. Dior — my husky, who has no opinion on American politics but strong opinions about breakfast — is pacing behind my chair. Ugyen is making tea. The CBC is on, running a segment about interprovincial trade barriers that has all the dramatic intensity of a lawn bowling tournament.

This is not a boast. It is a coordinate. It is the location from which I have been watching my southern neighbour attempt to process the recent shooting at its annual celebration of press freedom, and it is a location that grants a particular kind of clarity — the clarity of distance, which is not the same as the clarity of understanding, but which has its uses.

Canadians watch American political violence the way people who live near a volcano watch the smoke. With attention. With concern. With the nagging awareness that proximity is not protection and that the forces at work across the border do not respect the line on the map.

But also — and this is the part that is harder to admit — with a degree of incomprehension that borders on denial. Because what is happening in the United States simply does not correspond to anything in Canadian political experience, and the gap between the two countries’ relationship with political violence is so vast that it resists casual explanation.

Thomas D’Arcy McGee assassinated 7 April 1868 in Ottawa / Wikipedia Commons

Let me establish the baseline.

Canada has had one assassination of a sitting federal politician in its entire history. Thomas D’Arcy McGee, a Father of Confederation, was shot outside a boarding house on Sparks Street in Ottawa in 1868. One hundred and fifty-eight years ago. The suspect, Patrick James Whelan, was hanged — the last public execution in Canada. The event remains a historical curiosity, studied in schools and referenced in Heritage Minutes, not a living trauma. Most Canadians, if pressed, could not name him.

No Canadian prime minister has ever been assassinated. No Canadian prime minister has ever been the target of a serious assassination attempt. The closest thing we have to a notable physical confrontation involving a sitting prime minister is the so-called “Shawinigan Handshake” of 1996, when Jean Chrétien grabbed a protester by the neck and chin during a Flag Day event in Ottawa. It made headlines for weeks, spawned endless jokes, and became a minor piece of political folklore — precisely because it was so unusual. In any other country it might barely register. Here, it was national news.

The “Shawinigan Handshake” incident from February 15, 1996

The most notable security incident in modern Canadian political history was the 2014 Parliament Hill shooting, in which a gunman killed Corporal Nathan Cirillo at the National War Memorial and entered the Centre Block before being shot dead by the Sergeant-at-Arms, Kevin Vickers. It was a genuine act of political violence, and it shocked the country. It also remains, a decade later, essentially singular.

The current Prime Minister of Canada — whoever holds the office at any given moment — rides a bicycle to work. They walk through downtown Ottawa with minimal security. They attend public events without magnetometers or counter-sniper teams. As a young man in London, Ontario, I once attended a private gathering at the London Press Club where John Diefenbaker — a former Prime Minister of Canada — was the guest. It was an informal affair. No security perimeter. No magnetometers. No counter-sniper team on the roof of the neighbouring building. Just a former head of government in a room with local journalists and a few curious onlookers, talking and taking questions as if the idea that someone in the room might wish him harm was simply not a category that existed. Because it wasn't. The RCMP protective detail exists, but it operates at a scale that would be laughable by American standards.

This is not because Canadian leaders are braver or less important. It is because the threat environment is fundamentally different. And the threat environment is fundamentally different because the structural conditions that produce political violence in the United States either do not exist in Canada or exist at a scale so different that the comparison barely holds.

The structural differences are well-documented, but it is worth listing them plainly because the conversation in America so often treats political violence as a cultural phenomenon — something rooted in attitudes and rhetoric — while ignoring the material conditions that make it possible.

Guns. The United States has approximately 400 million civilian firearms, roughly 120 guns per 100 people. Canada has approximately 13 million, roughly 35 per 100 people. More importantly, the regulatory frameworks are different at a fundamental level. In Canada, firearms ownership requires a licence. Obtaining a licence requires a safety course, a background check, a waiting period, and ongoing eligibility. Handguns are restricted. Many types of semi-automatic weapons are prohibited. The system is imperfect — gun violence exists in Canada, and the 2020 Nova Scotia massacre proved that our own security architecture has catastrophic gaps — but the baseline accessibility of firearms capable of killing a political leader is dramatically lower.

Cole Tomas Allen walked into the Washington Hilton with a shotgun, a handgun, and knives. The guns were, in the American context, unremarkable. They were the kind of weapons that millions of Americans own legally and without incident. But they were also the instruments that allowed a lone individual to exchange gunfire with the Secret Service inside a hotel hosting the president. The accessibility of the hardware is inseparable from the frequency of the outcome.

Media ecosystem. Canada has partisan media. Canada has political talk radio. Canada has social media. But Canada does not have a media ecosystem of comparable scale or intensity to the American one. There is no Canadian equivalent of the right-wing talk radio infrastructure that reaches tens of millions of Americans daily. There is no Canadian equivalent of the social media amplification system that converts mainstream political rhetoric into extremist content at industrial scale. The CBC — whatever its flaws, and they are numerous enough to fill a separate series — operates as a shared national information space that the United States has not had since the decline of the network news era.

Political culture. This is the hardest variable to quantify but perhaps the most important. Canadian political culture is, for better and worse, institutionally deferential. Canadians tend to resolve political disputes through institutions — courts, regulators, elections, parliamentary committees — rather than through extralegal means. This deference produces its own pathologies. It can shade into passivity, into a willingness to tolerate mediocrity, into a reluctance to challenge power directly. I grew up in London, Ontario — what I’ve called “the Mariposa on the Thames” — and I can tell you that Canadian politeness is not always kindness. Sometimes it is just a convenient way of avoiding difficult conversations.

But it also produces a political culture in which the idea of shooting at a politician is not just illegal but genuinely alien — something that happens in other countries, not here.

Americans, by contrast, were born in revolution. The founding mythology of the United States is the violent overthrow of an illegitimate government. The Second Amendment is not just a legal provision; it is a cultural totem that encodes the idea that an armed citizenry is the ultimate check on tyranny. That mythology has produced extraordinary things — a republic, a constitution, a tradition of individual liberty that remains, despite everything, genuinely inspiring. But it has also produced a political culture in which violence against government is not just thinkable but, for some, defensible.

The question Canadian readers will ask — the question I ask myself, sitting at this kitchen table while Dior stares at me with the moral clarity available only to huskies — is whether any of this can cross the border.

The honest answer is that it already has, in attenuated form. The convoy protests of 2022, which occupied downtown Ottawa for weeks, were directly inspired by American political movements and amplified by American media and social media platforms. The rhetoric of those protests — the framing of government as tyranny, the invocation of freedom as an absolute rather than a negotiated value, the hostility toward mainstream media — was imported wholesale from the American political ecosystem.

The 2017 Quebec City mosque shooting was motivated by the kind of anti-Muslim radicalization that operates across borders through online networks. The rise of far-right movements in Canada tracks, with a lag, the rise of similar movements in the United States.

Canada is not immune. We are downstream. The question is not whether the American crisis of political violence will affect us. It is how much, and whether we have the structural resilience to absorb the pressure without cracking.

The answer, I suspect, depends on the things we are least willing to examine: whether our gun laws remain robust, whether our media ecosystem retains enough shared space to resist tribal fragmentation, and whether our political culture can maintain its institutional deference without collapsing into the kind of performative rage that has consumed our neighbour.

These are not comfortable questions for a Sunday morning in Toronto. But the smoke from the volcano is visible now, and pretending otherwise would be a different kind of Canadian failing — the failing of politeness mistaken for safety.
