Wayne reserved all four paddle boats and printed laminated Korean-language route maps, which took him four evenings. Mr. Park described them as thorough and mostly accurate. Wayne filed this as a professional assessment.

Wayne had been learning Korean for fourteen months.

He had begun in January of the previous year, shortly after the paddle boat photograph went viral in Seoul, on the grounds that a tourism operator whose primary emerging market spoke Korean and who did not speak Korean was, in Wayne’s assessment, leaving value on the table. He studied on his phone for thirty minutes each morning before the tours began, using an application that graded his pronunciation with the particular honesty of software that has no incentive to spare feelings.

His pronunciation had been described by his Korean-Canadian clients as sincere. He had chosen to receive this as a compliment. They had intended it as one.

By November he could greet clients in Korean, thank them in Korean, describe the Festival Theatre marquee in Korean, explain the paddle boat incident in Korean, and say, in Korean, that the swans belong to no one, which was a phrase he had specifically learned because it had been the most frequently asked question from Korean visitors following the Question of the Swans coverage that Carly had filed for the Beacon and which had been translated, in its entirety, by a member of the Seoul Facebook group and posted with the heading Why Elsinore Is The Most Canadian Place on Earth.

The group now had two thousand three hundred members.

Wayne checked it every morning after his Korean lesson and before the ferry tour schedule review.

The delegation arrived on a Saturday in late October.

There were forty-two of them.

Wayne had anticipated thirty, which was the number confirmed in the booking, which had been made six weeks earlier by the Facebook group’s moderator — a retired high school English teacher from Gangnam named Mr. Park who had visited Elsinore three times, redeemed the Presidential Package twice, and who communicated with Wayne in English of sufficient precision that Wayne had described their correspondence, to Gerald, as “among the most professional client communications I have received,” which Gerald had filed under Elsinore Heritage Tours — Korean Market — Client Relations — Park, Mr.

The additional twelve had joined, Mr. Park explained, when the group’s announcement of the trip had been shared beyond the Facebook group to a broader community of Canadian literature enthusiasts who had been following the Moonbeam Chronicle since a Korean literary magazine had published a piece, in September, comparing the series to the work of Yi Kwang-su, who is considered the father of modern Korean literature and who had written, in the early twentieth century, about small communities navigating cultural change with a combination of dignity and gentle absurdity that the magazine’s critic felt was specifically echoed in Gerald Fitch’s handling of the Easter egg hunt documentation.

Wayne had not known about Yi Kwang-su. He added him to his phone’s note-taking application under Cultural Context — Korean Market — Literary Parallels. He looked him up. He read two paragraphs of a summary. He felt that the comparison was accurate and that he should have thought of it himself.

The logistics of forty-two visitors in Elsinore required modifications to the standard tour structure that Wayne had planned over three evenings and a Sunday afternoon.

The paddle boats could accommodate twelve. Wayne had reserved all four for the morning session and arranged two sessions, which meant the forty-two would divide into groups of twenty-one, the first group taking the river tour while the second group visited the Festival Theatre box office and the Elizabethan Gardens, then switching. He had arranged this with the Festival’s front of house manager, who had agreed with the particular goodwill of an institution that has been told forty-two Korean literary enthusiasts are coming to see the theatre.

Alistair heard about the delegation on a Thursday.

He rearranged his Friday morning to be at the theatre when they arrived.

He did not tell Wayne he was doing this. He simply arranged to be there, in the lobby, when the first group came in. He spoke to them for twelve minutes through Mr. Park, who translated with the smooth unhurried precision of a man who had been translating literature his entire professional life and found live theatre director somewhat easier.

Alistair talked about This Rough Magic. He talked about why he had titled his farewell season after Prospero’s speech — the speech in which the magician chooses to put down his staff rather than continue to use it. He said that power used past its proper moment becomes something else, and that knowing when to stop was the hardest thing a person in any position of authority could learn, and that he had been trying to learn it for nineteen years.

Mr. Park translated this. The group was quiet for a moment.

Then one of the group — a woman in her sixties who turned out to be a professor of comparative literature at Yonsei University and who had been invited by Mr. Park because she had written the literary magazine piece comparing the Moonbeam Chronicle to Yi Kwang-su — asked, through Mr. Park, whether Alistair thought the best art was made by people who knew they were almost at the end of their time with it.

Alistair was quiet for four seconds.

He said yes.

The professor nodded. She wrote something in a small notebook she had been carrying. She thanked him. The group moved on to the Elizabethan Gardens.

Alistair stood in the lobby for a moment after they left. His stage manager, who had been present, did not speak. She understood when not to speak.

The river tour proceeded across both sessions without the incident that Gerald had, privately, considered possible given the combination of forty-two visitors, four paddle boats, and Waypoint 3. Wayne had briefed the groups extensively on the Avon Narrows situation. He had distributed laminated copies of Carly’s original incident report, now in its fourth print run. He had positioned himself in the lead boat with the particular alertness of a man who has learned, in a specific way, what happens at Waypoint 3.

Both sessions completed without grounding.

The professor from Yonsei watched the Swan Warden for several minutes without taking a photograph. She said: She is keeping the record. This is the most important work.

The Swan Warden, who had been informed by Wayne — he had learned, after the Prospero incident, the importance of advance consultation — that forty-two Korean visitors would be on the river, had noted this in her habitat log and had positioned herself, during both sessions, at the bench near the second waypoint. She did not participate in the tour. She attended to her notebook. She was simply there, as she always was, noting what the river was doing.

Several of the visitors photographed her.

She did not appear to notice. She was writing.

The professor from Yonsei University, who was in the second session, watched the Swan Warden from her paddle boat for several minutes without taking a photograph. Afterward, through Mr. Park, she asked Wayne who the woman with the notebook was.

Wayne explained the Swan Warden: the twenty-two years, the five forty-five mornings, the dark green hardcover notebooks, the habitat survey, the navigational advice that had become, in Seoul, the most quoted moment of the entire paddle boat story.

The professor listened to Mr. Park’s translation. She nodded slowly.

She said, in English: “She is keeping the record.”

Wayne said yes.

She said, still in English: “This is the most important work.”

She went back to her paddle boat. Wayne noted this in his phone under Presidential Package — Cultural Observations — Notable.

The Bieber Heritage Walk took place in the afternoon, in three groups of fourteen with Wayne leading each in rotation. He had printed laminated Korean-language route maps, which had taken him four evenings to produce with the help of the translation application and Mr. Park’s corrections, and which Mr. Park had described, in his written feedback, as “thorough and mostly accurate,” which Wayne had received as a professional assessment and filed accordingly.

The groups were attentive and warm. They photographed the Tim Hortons, which Wayne had begun including in the route as a heritage site on the grounds that it had been there since 2001 and that its window table on the right had a documented relationship to the Festival marquee that the Moonbeam Chronicle had established in its Introduction. The groups photographed the proposed installation site marker, which had achieved by this point the status of an Elsinore landmark in its own right. Two of the visitors had brought small gifts for the marker — a K-pop badge pinned to the stake, and a small hand-drawn illustration of a swan, left at the base.

Wayne photographed these additions. He was not sure whether to file them under Bieber — Heritage — International Response or Swans — Cultural — Korean Market. He filed them under both.

The delegation left on Sunday morning after attending the final performance of the season’s run of Something Rotten!, which they had bought out the last twelve rows of on Mr. Park’s initiative and which had produced, in the theatre, a quality of laughter that Alistair’s stage manager described afterward as the kind of laughter you only get from an audience that has understood the joke at two levels simultaneously.

Alistair, who had been in the building, heard the laughter from his office.

He looked out the window at the Avon. The morning light was on the water. The swans were on the river, proceeding.

He thought about what the professor had asked him.

He thought about whether the best art was made by people who knew they were almost at the end.

He sat with this for a while. Then he went back to work.

Wayne updated his spreadsheet on Sunday evening. The delegation had generated the highest per-group revenue in Elsinore Heritage Tours’ history. The Facebook group had gained sixty-three members since the trip was announced. Mr. Park had confirmed a return visit for the following spring, when the new artistic director’s inaugural season would be opening.

Wayne noted this under 2027 Forward Bookings — Korean Market — Presidential Package.

He sent the spreadsheet to Gerald, because Gerald had asked him, after the Prospero incident, to begin sending the spreadsheets. Gerald had not explained why. Wayne had not asked. He sent the spreadsheet.

Gerald filed it under Elsinore Heritage Tours — Revenue — Korean Market — 2026.

He noted, in his personal records, that the Korean market had become, in the space of fourteen months, the fastest-growing segment of Elsinore’s cultural tourism economy. He noted that this had begun with a photograph of a man in a grounded paddle boat reading a laminated route map. He noted that the photograph had been taken by Carly Meadows, who had known it was the right photograph and had not known immediately why.

He filed the note.

He went home. Hansard was on the filing system chair. Gerald gave him the chair and read the news on his phone, because someone had to, and because the news required reading whether or not the chair was available.

A Note for the Record

Cultural tourism is built on specificity. People travel to see the particular thing — the particular river, the particular theatre, the particular moment captured in the particular photograph. They do not travel to see something generic. Generic is available everywhere. Specific is only available here.

Elsinore is specific. It has a Swan Warden who has been on the bank at five forty-five every morning for twenty-two years. It has a volunteer coordinator who has copies of everything and moves at his own pace. It has a paddle boat incident documented in laminated format and distributed internationally. It has a man learning Korean to serve a market that came to him because a photograph of a man reading a map in a grounded boat was more compelling than any marketing Elsinore Heritage Tours had produced.

To the south, the question of international cultural relationships has taken on a different quality. Tariffs have been imposed on friends and allies. Cultural exchanges have been reduced. The State Department has cut funding for international arts programmes. The message being sent is that America’s cultural engagement with the world is a transaction rather than a relationship, and that transactions can be adjusted, suspended, or terminated based on current policy priorities.

In Elsinore, forty-two Korean visitors came because a retired English teacher read a column about an Easter egg hunt and thought: this is a place worth seeing. They came back because a woman with a dark green notebook was on the river at five forty-five doing what she had always done, and a professor of comparative literature watched her from a paddle boat and said: she is keeping the record. This is the most important work.

Nobody sent them. Nobody funded their visit. Nobody issued a cultural exchange directive.

They simply came, because Elsinore was specific, and because specific things have a way of finding the people who need to see them.

The river runs through the evening. The swans proceed. The Facebook group has two thousand three hundred and sixty-three members and is growing.

Wayne is learning Korean.

He is getting better.

The Moonbeam Chronicle: Sketches from Elsinore publishes every Sunday morning. Mr. Park has confirmed a return visit for spring. The laminated maps are in their fourth print run. The professor’s note has not been shared. The Swan Warden was keeping the record.
