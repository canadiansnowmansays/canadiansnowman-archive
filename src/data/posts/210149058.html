Postmark: A garage, Vancouver Island. A twelve-foot solar-powered hull takes shape where a car used to park. Somewhere off the coast, an orca surfaces, unwatched, in water nobody is currently monitoring.

Part I of this series told the story of a fifteen-year-old in Burlington who watched a snapping turtle and built a robot to monitor coral reefs without disturbing them. This postcard belongs to the same address and, in its way, to the same origin story — except the person who noticed the ocean’s blind spots this time had already spent five months alone on it, with nothing but oar strokes and horizon, and came home determined to build something that would keep watching after she left.

Five months on the water, and an idea that followed

Julie Angus is, by the plain record of her own accomplishments, not an ordinary founder. As her company’s own team page states, she holds a master’s degree in molecular biology and was the first woman to row across the Atlantic Ocean from mainland to mainland — a five-month crossing from Portugal to Costa Rica in a small wooden boat, undertaken with her partner and eventual co-founder Colin Angus, whose own résumé includes being the first person to circumnavigate the globe using only human power. As Business in Vancouver reported, it was during that Atlantic crossing that the two of them first arrived at the idea of an autonomous ocean vessel — an insight that could only have come from the specific, unglamorous experience of being the only two people for hundreds of miles in every direction, watching an ocean that nobody else was watching at all.

As West Coast Now recorded, the company that idea became — Open Ocean Robotics — started in 2018 in the couple’s own garage on Vancouver Island, the same democratic-invention tradition this series traced through BURT’s backyard pool in Part I and e-NABLE’s puppeteer-and-carpenter origin in Part XXV. Julie Angus described the founding motivation in terms this series has learned to recognize as the truest kind: one of the biggest challenges with our oceans, she has said, is that they are extremely difficult to monitor — not a market opportunity identified from a spreadsheet, but a problem she had lived inside for five months with an oar in each hand.

The boat that rights itself

What emerged from that garage is a twelve-foot uncrewed surface vessel called the Data Xplorer, built for exactly the punishment the open ocean delivers as a matter of course. As Forbes reported, the vessels are entirely solar-powered, capable of remaining at sea for up to a year at a stretch, continuously collecting data and relaying it in real time over satellite communication — and built with a self-righting hull specifically so that when a fifty-foot storm wave capsizes the boat, which it will, the vessel simply rolls back upright and continues operating rather than requiring rescue.

Julie Angus, describing planned sea trials in genuinely rough winter conditions, put the design philosophy as plainly as an engineer could: the boat will repeatedly capsize, and its self-righting design will let it keep going anyway.

The sensor payload turns each vessel into what the company itself calls a piece of a “digital ocean” — cameras, ship-tracking AIS transponders, remote-sensing lidar, hydrophones for underwater acoustic monitoring, and a full suite of oceanographic instruments measuring everything from wave height to water temperature to barometric pressure. As Douglas magazine reported, the company now operates out of the Vancouver Island Technology Park, a University of Victoria facility, having grown from a two-person garage operation to a genuine engineering team spanning mechanical, electrical, and software disciplines alongside the oceanographic science the platform exists to serve.

What the boat actually watches for

The applications this fleet has been put to are worth stating individually, because together they sketch a picture of exactly what an ocean looks like when nobody is watching it at all. As West Coast Now documented, the vessels have been deployed for Fisheries and Oceans Canada research on the southern resident killer whale population in the Salish Sea — an endangered orca community whose survival depends on exactly the kind of sustained, unobtrusive, long-duration monitoring a fifty-foot storm would otherwise make impossible to maintain with a crewed research vessel. The same platform has been used to detect illegal fishing activity inside marine protected areas, work that traditionally required an expensive crewed patrol boat physically present at the right place at the right moment — a needle-in-a-haystack problem that a solar-powered vessel loitering silently for months at a time solves simply by never leaving.

The company’s client list has, in the years since that Vancouver Island garage, grown to include national governments, ocean industries, and — as West Coast Now reported — militaries, including NATO, a detail that earned Julie Angus her own formal appointment as NATO’s Director for its Maritime Unmanned Systems Initiative. As the company’s own mission page records, Open Ocean Robotics has since launched the first purpose-built uncrewed vessel specifically designed for marine carbon-dioxide-removal monitoring, expanded sales into Iceland, Singapore, and South Korea, and led a $3.8-million federal Ocean Supercluster grant in partnership with four other Canadian companies to build an integrated air-sea ocean monitoring system. None of this reads like the kind of thing that starts in a garage. All of it did.

The industry this garage helped start

Open Ocean Robotics did not stay alone in this space for long, and the broader uncrewed surface vessel industry it helped pioneer is itself worth a moment’s attention, because it shows how quickly a garage-scale idea can seed an entire commercial category. Comparable companies have since emerged pursuing overlapping missions — American firm Sofar Ocean, descended from the open-source underwater robotics community this series briefly considered as its own Garage candidate, now runs a global network of ocean-sensing buoys used by navies and shipping companies alike; British firm Online Oceans has built its own uncrewed surface vessel, Spotter Scout, specifically to extend that kind of network into deeper water. The industry comparison sites now used by investors to evaluate marine robotics startups treat Open Ocean Robotics and its peers as an established sector rather than a curiosity — a sign that the garage-born idea has, within less than a decade, become a genuine and competitive commercial category rather than a single company’s novelty.

What distinguishes the Vancouver Island company within that now-crowded field is the origin story this postcard has centred throughout: a founder who did not identify the market gap from a spreadsheet but lived inside it, oar in hand, for five months. That distinction shows up in small but telling details — the self-righting hull specifically engineered for the storms Julie Angus had already personally survived in a rowboat, the choice to build for months-long unattended deployment because she knew, from direct experience, exactly how long an ocean can go unwatched. Competitors can copy the sensor payload and the solar panels. The five months on the water are not for sale.

There is a further dimension to the design worth naming, because it distinguishes these vessels from almost every conventional research platform they replace. A crewed research vessel monitoring the same stretch of ocean burns diesel, requires a rotating human crew, and returns to port after days or weeks at most. A solar-powered USV produces no emissions at all while it works, needs no crew to rotate, and can remain on station for the better part of a year — which means the very act of studying climate change and ocean health no longer has to add its own carbon footprint to the problem being studied. That closed loop, watching the ocean without contributing to what threatens it, is not an incidental feature of the design. It is close to the entire point.

The quiet, unglamorous kind of watching

This is, notably, not the flashy demonstration-driven robotics this series has often found itself reporting on — no dancing, no marathon record, no viral video of a fall. As one profile of Julie Angus observed, the sensors on these vessels quietly measure the ocean conditions that matter to the people who need to know them: seismic activity, oil-spill extent, water quality, water temperature, and the slow accumulating signature of a changing climate — the unglamorous, continuous, patient data-gathering that conservation and climate science actually run on, rather than the spectacle that draws a television audience. The boats look, in the company’s own affectionate description, like the offspring of a surfboard and a kayak — sleek, low, entirely unremarkable to glance at, doing work that would otherwise require a full crewed research vessel and a budget most conservation programs simply do not have.

There is a quiet symmetry worth naming between this postcard and the one this series filed about BURT in Part I. Both machines exist because someone spent real time in or on the water and noticed, specifically, what a noisy, disruptive, expensive human presence costs an ecosystem trying to be studied. A fifteen-year-old watched a turtle move through water without disturbing it. Two adventurers spent five months rowing across an ocean and came home asking how to keep watching it without a crew, a fuel tank, or a greenhouse-gas footprint attached. Different scales, different oceans, the same instinct — and, tellingly, both born entirely outside a corporate lab, one in a backyard pool and one in a Vancouver Island garage.

What the balcony sees

From the balcony, Open Ocean Robotics is one of the cleanest illustrations this series has found of the Garage territory’s founding premise: that the most durable robotics companies often begin not with a market analysis but with a specific, lived encounter with a problem nobody else in the room has actually experienced firsthand. Julie Angus did not read about ocean-monitoring gaps in a report. She rowed through five months of them, alone with her co-founder and an unbroken horizon, and came home to a garage with an idea that a fifty-foot wave could not sink.

A Canadian reads this postcard with a particular pride, because Open Ocean Robotics is that rarest thing in this series’ coverage: a garage-founded company that stayed a garage-founded company’s own country, growing not by relocating to a larger market but by building a genuine ocean-technology cluster around a University of Victoria research park, monitoring the same coastline Julie and Colin Angus once rowed away from. This series has traced Canada’s robotics thread from a Toronto clean room to a Burlington backyard pool to a Halifax e-NABLE chapter recycling weed-container lids into prosthetic hands. Vancouver Island’s contribution is a boat that rights itself after every wave that tries to sink it, built by two people who already knew, better than almost anyone alive, exactly how empty and how watched-over an ocean needs to be.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one came back from five months at sea, and then went back out, alone, to keep watching.
