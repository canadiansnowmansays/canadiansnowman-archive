At around 1:30 in the afternoon, Madrid time, on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, Kilmar Armando Abrego Garcia walked out of the arrivals hall at Madrid–Barajas Airport, past a scrum of waiting reporters, raised his fist, said nothing at all, and climbed into a blue minivan. As Alan Feuer and Hari Raj reported for The New York Times, that was the whole of his arrival statement in Europe. He had already said what he wanted to say the night before, in a statement released through We Are CASA, the Maryland advocacy group that stood beside him for twenty months: “I am leaving happy. I am leaving with my freedom.”

Before he left American soil, Immigration and Customs Enforcement agents met him at the airport in Washington and removed the ankle monitor he had worn for nearly a year. He flew commercially, with his wife Jennifer, his brother, his lawyer Simon Sandoval-Moshenberg and representatives of CASA. He entered Spain, CNN reported, on a tourist visa good for ninety days. Madrid is the first stop on a speaking tour of several countries.

When this series closed in May with Part VII: The Ruling, I ended on a line I still believe: the pupusa maker’s son beat them all. He beat them in Judge Paula Xinis’s courtroom in Greenbelt. He beat them at the Fourth Circuit. He beat them nine to nothing at the Supreme Court. He beat them in Nashville, where Judge Waverly Crenshaw threw out the smuggling indictment as vindictive. Six months on, I owe readers an honest accounting of what beating them has turned out to cost, because the man who won every round has just left the country, and the government that lost every round is calling it a victory.

Both of those things are true. That is the problem this Coda has to sit with.

The Seventh Destination

Readers of Part VI: The Coercion will remember the itinerary the Department of Homeland Security drew up for Mr. Abrego Garcia after it was forced to bring him back from El Salvador in June 2025. Costa Rica on a Thursday, as the reward for a guilty plea. Uganda on the Friday, minutes after he walked out of a Tennessee jail on August 22, 2025, when he declined to plead. Then Eswatini. Then Ghana. Then, when none of those governments would take him, Liberia.

Liberia was supposed to be the one that stuck. In March 2026, ICE Director Todd Lyons asked Judge Xinis to dissolve her injunction so the government could remove him there swiftly, warning, as Fox News reported on March 23, 2026, that walking away from the Liberian negotiations could damage the diplomatic reliability of the United States. Meanwhile Mr. Abrego Garcia’s lawyers kept repeating the same offer they had made for more than a year: he would leave voluntarily for Costa Rica, which had offered him refugee status. In April, The Independent reported that the administration had rejected that offer because allowing it would be prejudicial to the United States.

Pause on that for a moment. A government that insisted, at the top of its voice, that this man must not remain in the country was offered his departure, at no cost, to a willing democratic neighbour, and refused it. The objection was never to his leaving. The objection was to his choosing.

Judge Xinis saw it plainly. In February 2026, ruling that he could not be re-detained, she wrote that the government had made “one empty threat after another,” a line Al Jazeera carried in its headline on February 17, 2026. On May 12, 2026, keeping the Liberia block in place, she pointed out the contradiction at the heart of the government’s double-tracked strategy, which the Washington Examiner captured in a single sentence from the bench: “You need Mr. Abrego Garcia here to try him.” The Justice Department wanted him in Nashville in the dock and in Monrovia on an ICE Air flight at the same time. It could have neither.

So the seventh destination turned out to be one the government never proposed at all. Spain. A country in the European Union, with a large Latin American community and a common language, where a Salvadoran construction worker can walk out of an airport as a visitor rather than a deportee. Of all the places on earth the administration might have wanted Kilmar Abrego Garcia to land, a European capital with a press corps waiting at the gate cannot have been near the top of the list.

Two Press Releases

The terms of the departure are not fully public. CBS News reported that Judge Xinis issued an order on Friday, November 20, 2026, that remains sealed, and that a hearing on the Liberia effort had been abruptly cancelled several weeks earlier. NBC News, citing a U.S. official, reported that he had been ordered by a federal judge to leave the country by Tuesday, November 24. What is public is how each side chose to describe the same airport.

The Department of Homeland Security went first, and went loudly. Markwayne Mullin, the homeland security secretary, announced on X, as NPR reported, that the department was “DELIGHTED” to report that Mr. Abrego Garcia had self-deported to Spain, and repeated, without offering evidence, the gang and trafficking accusations that no court has ever accepted. A follow-up post from the department, reported by CBS News, insisted that he left under a removal order, paid for his own flight, received no incentives, and would never be allowed back. The capital letters were the department’s.

Mr. Abrego Garcia’s lawyers went second. Mr. Sandoval-Moshenberg called it a temporary departure and said the retaliatory deportation campaign against his client had come to an end. He told NPR that the government had wanted to send him to Liberia in shackles. Senator Chris Van Hollen of Maryland, who flew to El Salvador in April 2025 to sit across a table from him when the administration was still insisting he would never come back, said his constituent was travelling to a country of his choosing, unharmed and with his dignity intact.

Run both statements through the TACO framework and they describe the same event from opposite ends of the acronym. The administration Threatened (Uganda, Eswatini, Ghana, Liberia), was forced by the courts to Accommodate (the return from CECOT, the release from detention), and in the end Capitulated on the point it had fought hardest to win, which was who would choose the destination. Then it Obfuscated. “Self-deported” is the administration’s own term of art, the label it attaches to its programme of encouraging people to pack up and leave on their own. Applying it here turns a defeat into a policy success. The government did not deport Kilmar Abrego Garcia. It could not. It spent twenty months, two federal judges, a court of appeals, the Supreme Court and the Attorney General’s personal attention trying to, and he left anyway, on his own ticket, to his own choice of country, with his lawyer in the next seat and a reporter from The Washington Post on the same plane.

The insistence that he received no incentives is the most revealing line in the whole exchange. Nobody had asked. A department confident of its victory does not issue a clarification that the vanquished paid his own fare.

The Prosecution That Outlived Its Defendant

There is one more thread the administration has not let go of, and it says more about the machinery than anything said at Madrid–Barajas.

When Judge Crenshaw dismissed the Tennessee indictment on May 22, 2026, he found that the prosecution was a case of “picking the person first and the crime second,” and he named the man he held responsible. Todd Blanche, then the Deputy Attorney General and now the Attorney General, had, in the judge’s finding, reawakened a dormant investigation into a 2022 traffic stop because Mr. Abrego Garcia had embarrassed the government in court. Readers of The Blanche Files will know how little that finding has slowed Mr. Blanche’s rise.

The Justice Department filed notice of appeal on June 22, 2026, as the Tennessee Lookout reported, and filed a 68-page opening brief at the Sixth Circuit on August 17, 2026, which Maryland Matters summarised the next day. The brief accuses Judge Crenshaw of second-guessing career prosecutors and intruding on executive authority. According to CBS News, oral argument is scheduled for February.

So the United States government is now asking a federal appeals court to reinstate criminal charges against a man it has just announced, in capital letters, will never set foot in the country again. If the Sixth Circuit agrees, the department will hold an indictment against a defendant it has barred from appearing. Judge Xinis’s sentence from May has aged into prophecy: you need Mr. Abrego Garcia here to try him. The appeal is not about trying him. It is about overturning the finding of vindictiveness, because that finding names the Attorney General, and in this administration the record of who was right matters more than the case itself. That is the thread connecting this series to The Enemies List: the prosecution was never really about the defendant, and it does not need him to continue.

What Winning Looks Like

I wrote in Part VII about the president who cannot lose. The Coda supplies the corollary. A president who cannot lose does not need to win. He needs only to be able to say that he did. Mr. Abrego Garcia’s departure gave the administration that sentence, and it took it gratefully.

But the honest answer to who won is not a press release from either side.

Kilmar Abrego Garcia is free. He was not sent to Liberia in chains. He was not tried on charges a federal judge found were brought to punish him. He is not in CECOT, where he says he was beaten, deprived of sleep and psychologically tortured for nearly three months in 2025. Every court that examined his case found in his favour, including a unanimous Supreme Court and a conservative appellate judge, J. Harvie Wilkinson III, who wrote in April 2025 that the government was claiming a right to hide residents of the country in foreign prisons without due process. That record is permanent, and no social media post can amend it.

And yet. He is a man with a 2019 immigration court order barring his deportation to El Salvador, an American wife, an American child, a union apprenticeship and a home in Maryland, and he has just had to leave all of it behind to stop the government from shipping him to a West African country he has never seen. His lawyers say he has many routes back as the spouse and father of U.S. citizens. Mr. Sandoval-Moshenberg told NPR that his client will decide where to settle while he waits out the remaining two years of this administration, and that the family expects the next administration to bring him home. That may well happen. It is also a remarkable thing to hear a lawyer say out loud: that the remedy for his client is not the law but an election.

That is the cost I could not see in May. The rule of law held in every courtroom it was tested in. It did not hold at the border, because at the border the government does not need to win the argument. It only needs to make staying more dangerous than leaving, and wait. Twenty months of Uganda, Eswatini, Ghana and Liberia, of check-ins that might end in detention, of a criminal appeal hanging over him in Cincinnati, is its own kind of sentence, handed down without a trial. The courts kept saying no. The administration kept asking. Eventually a man with a family decided that a tourist visa in Madrid was safer than one more Friday-morning appointment at the ICE office in Baltimore.

From Toronto, this looks less like a deportation than an exile, and this city knows something about American exiles: it took in tens of thousands of young Americans who chose to leave rather than fight in Vietnam. People do not usually leave a country where they have won every case. That he did is the clearest verdict yet on the government he left.

The pupusa maker’s son beat them all. He is also, as of Wednesday, November 25, 2026, living out of a suitcase in Spain, waiting to see whether his own country’s next election will let him go home to his wife and child. Both of those sentences belong in the record. A Coda is supposed to resolve the theme. This one can only note that the theme has moved: from what the state can do to a man in court, to what it can do to him by simply refusing to stop.
