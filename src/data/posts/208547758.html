On July 20, 2026, in the same San Francisco courtroom where seven objectors once got two minutes each, Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín did what most observers had expected her to do since the fairness hearing in May: she signed the order. Final approval, $1.5 billion, “fair, reasonable, and adequate” — the phrase copyright lawyers have been waiting to type into headlines for the better part of a year.

It took ten weeks between hearing and order. It resolved exactly what it was always going to resolve, and not one dispute more.

What Got Decided

The arithmetic held steady from May: a 91.3 percent claims rate, 350 valid opt-outs covering roughly 1,802 works — three-hundredths of one percent of the total. All fifty-three objections were overruled, including the two that mattered most as arguments rather than grievances. Robert Jackson’s math — that $3,000 amounts to about 2 percent of the statutory ceiling — was correct and irrelevant; the court’s job was never to certify that the number felt large enough. Randy Hoopman’s objection — that a one-time payment while the model keeps running forever doesn’t feel like justice — met the same fate, and for a more interesting reason. The court’s order states plainly that the settlement releases claims about past conduct only: pirating and copying books “up to the point of any AI output,” as of the settlement’s effective date of August 25, 2025. It does not release claims about future outputs, or about anything Anthropic does going forward. Hoopman was not wrong that a one-time payment can’t buy permanent absolution. He was wrong that this settlement was trying to.

That is worth sitting with, because it quietly answers the essay’s own closing question from May: the settlement does not, in fact, address whether those seven million pirated books remain inside Claude’s models. It never claimed to. The release covers the theft. It leaves the ongoing use of what was stolen entirely on the table — which is precisely the ground on which the opt-out plaintiffs are now standing.

On the late opt-outs, the court split the difference Anthropic asked it not to. Two of the five untimely filers were let in: Laura Esquivel, the Mexican author of Like Water for Chocolate, and her co-author nephew Castells, who declared he’d never received notice at all and only learned of the case from his aunt. The court found their few-days delay past the February 9 deadline forgivable — “excusable neglect,” in the term of art. The other three late filers were turned away. A modest ruling, but not a nothing one: it means Esquivel’s objection about the missing Spanish-language notice did, in the end, buy her something.

What Didn’t Get Decided — Because It Was Never This Case’s Job

While Bartz sat under submission, the authors who’d already opted out kept moving on their own separate track. Carreyrou v. Anthropic — once an omnibus complaint against six AI companies — got cut down to size on June 8, when Judge Pitts severed the claims against OpenAI, Google, Meta, xAI, and Perplexity, leaving Carreyrou’s group suing Anthropic alone. A hearing on Anthropic’s motion to dismiss that case, originally set for March, has been rescheduled twice and now sits on Judge Trina Thompson’s calendar for July 7. Cruz v. Anthropic — the Eggers-and-Vida group demanding the permanent injunction — spent early June bouncing from magistrate to district judge assignment, the ordinary furniture-moving of federal litigation, with the underlying question (can a court order Claude to stop generating in the genres of authors who never agreed to any of this) still entirely untested.

That question is the one Bartz was never going to answer, no matter which way Martínez-Olguín ruled. Judge Alsup’s fair-use finding on lawfully trained content stands undisturbed. The settlement bought Anthropic peace on the piracy count. It did not, and structurally could not, buy peace on the injunction theory — “dilution,” the Copyright Office’s newly minted phrase for the argument that an AI model harms authors merely by being able to generate competent knockoffs of their genre. Alsup rejected that theory in Bartz. A different judge, Chhabria, entertained it in dicta in the Meta case without ruling on it. Cruz v. Anthropic is where it will actually be tested, and it is not close to trial.

The Front That Escalated Instead of Concluding

If Bartz spent its final act clarifying scope, the New York Times‘s fight with OpenAI spent July doing the opposite — widening it. On July 9, the Times, joined by the Daily News, the Center for Investigative Reporting, and Ziff Davis, asked a federal judge in Manhattan to sanction OpenAI outright, on the grounds that the company had misrepresented, under oath, its own ability to search itself.

The specifics are the kind that make a discovery dispute worth reading past the docket number. An OpenAI data-privacy engineer, deposed in April, reportedly acknowledged that the company had already built an internal database of roughly 78 million de-identified ChatGPT conversations — used to gauge its own infringement exposure — and had deployed a detection tool, reportedly nicknamed “Project Giraffe,” that tracked when the model regurgitated source material in its outputs. All of this, the newspapers say, while OpenAI told the court that searching its own logs and training data for copyrighted journalism would be too technically burdensome to attempt. You cannot simultaneously build the tool and claim the tool is impossible to build. That is the sanctions motion in one sentence, and it is a considerably sharper allegation than the piracy question Anthropic settled. Piracy is “we took something we shouldn’t have.” This is “we told the court we couldn’t check whether we took something we shouldn’t have, while checking.”

The Front That Got Quieter Than Reported

Some coverage this month collapsed two separate music-industry deadlines into one moment, so it is worth being precise. Germany’s Munich Regional Court will deliver its verdict in GEMA v. Suno on July 31 — that date is confirmed and unmoved. The parallel American fight, Sony and Universal’s case against Suno in Massachusetts, is not reaching a fair-use ruling this month or anything close to it. Fact discovery in that docket now runs through September 30, 2026; dispositive motions aren’t due until April 9, 2027. The “July ruling” that several outlets promised readers this summer was never on Chief Judge Saylor’s actual calendar.

What did happen in Boston was smaller and more tactical: on July 2, Suno pointed the court to a favorable result in the parallel Udio case in New York, where a judge had just denied Sony’s bid to add roughly 30,000 more tracks to that complaint, and asked Saylor to deny Sony and Universal’s near-identical request to expand the Suno case from 560 works to 61,026. That expansion fight — not the fair-use question — is the live battle in Boston right now, and it will not resolve before the fall at the earliest.
