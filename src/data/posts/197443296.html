I. 2.8 Miles

West Palm Beach City Hall sits 2.8 miles from the former Epstein estate on El Brillo Way. It sits four miles from Mar-a-Lago, where Virginia Roberts Giuffre was recruited by Ghislaine Maxwell in the summer of 2000, when she was sixteen years old and had just finished tenth grade.

It sits steps from the federal courthouse where, in 2008, then-US Attorney Alexander Acosta signed the most notorious non-prosecution agreement in American legal history — a secret deal that branded fifteen-year-old victims as prostitutes, granted immunity to unnamed co-conspirators, and allowed a convicted sex offender to leave jail on “work release” to continue raping women in the very mansion down the road.

On May 12, 2026, Democratic members of the House Oversight Committee walked into that city hall and convened a field hearing. They invited survivors of Jeffrey Epstein’s abuse to testify. They invited attorneys who had spent decades fighting for those survivors. They invited the public.

They did not invite Jeffrey Epstein’s co-conspirators, because those men’s names remain redacted in files that the Department of Justice continues to suppress.

The Republican members of the Oversight Committee were invited. Not one attended.

The legacy media was welcome. Jim Acosta — who left CNN, who was standing in the Washington Hilton ballroom two weeks ago when a man with a shotgun charged the Secret Service checkpoint — broadcast the hearing live on Substack and YouTube from the sidewalk outside city hall. He noted, as the hearing began, that no major network had yet sent a crew.

And Donald Trump? He started the morning at the White House, where he has previously called the Epstein investigation a “Democratic hoax” and said the files are “not a matter of national interest.” Then he departed for China to meet Xi Jinping. He left the country on the same morning that women he once partied alongside Jeffrey Epstein with were testifying about being raped as children, four miles from his house.

The distance between Mar-a-Lago and city hall is four miles. The distance between the president and accountability, one suspects, is rather greater.

II. The Women in the Room

There were four survivors at the witness table: Courtney Wild, Jena-Lisa Jones, Dani Bensky, and a woman identified only as Roza, who testified under her first name for her own safety. Maria Farmer, the woman who first reported Epstein to the FBI in 1996 — thirty years ago — testified by video from a hospital bed, where she has spent twenty-three of the last thirty nights, including multiple stays in the ICU. She has Hodgkin’s lymphoma, a brain tumor, and Addison’s disease. She attributes the collapse of her health to three decades of fighting a government that refused to listen.

Sky and Amanda Roberts appeared on behalf of Sky’s sister, the late Virginia Roberts Giuffre, reading her words from a sworn 2016 deposition — testimony in which Virginia named Prince Andrew, Glenn Dubin, Alan Dershowitz, and others. Sky Roberts stood at the podium and said what his sister would have said if she were alive to say it: “For years, survivors have been asked the same question. Who were the names? The truth is, many survivors stay silent because many of these individuals still hold power, wealth, and influence in our society. Point blank. Period. That is dangerous.”

Spencer Kuvin, the attorney who represented the first victim to come forward in the original Palm Beach investigation, testified about the mechanics of the sweetheart deal. Lauren Hersh, CEO of World Without Exploitation, testified about the systemic failure to protect survivors at every stage of the process.

This was a shadow hearing — meaning Democrats, who are in the minority, have no subpoena power. Every witness appeared voluntarily. Every survivor who spoke did so by choice, knowing their words would be recorded, broadcast, and scrutinized. They showed up anyway.

The Republicans who could have been there — who have subpoena power, who could compel testimony, who could issue contempt citations — sent empty chairs.

III. What Roza Said

Roza, an Epstein survivor who testified under her first name for safety, speaks at the House Oversight Committee Democrats’ field hearing on the Epstein investigation. West Palm Beach City Hall, Florida, May 12, 2026. (C-SPAN)

Roza’s testimony was, by any fair accounting, the most devastating three minutes of the hearing. It may be the most devastating three minutes in the history of this series.

She was eighteen when she met Jean-Luc Brunel at MC2 Model Management. She was promised a career. Within six months, the agency had obtained an O-1 visa for her — a visa reserved for individuals with extraordinary abilities, which she did not have the documentation to support. “It’s a miracle,” she said. “You try to do that right now. You won’t be able to.”

She arrived in New York. The agency claimed she owed them over ten thousand dollars. She was given a hundred dollars a week. “If any of you go to New York City today and try to live on $100,” she told the members of Congress, “good luck.”

Less than a month after arriving, she was brought to the home of a registered sex offender. She met his girlfriend, who was described as a photographer who would help build her portfolio. She was introduced to Epstein at his mansion in West Palm Beach. He used the names of powerful politicians to demonstrate his influence. He bragged about girls visiting his cell. He offered her a job at his science foundation.

Then the rapes began.

“Jeffrey Epstein was under house arrest for the molestation of underage girls,” Roza said, “at the exact same time he was abusing me.”

Read that sentence again. Roza was raped by Jeffrey Epstein while he was supposed to be in a jail cell. She was raped during the period that Alexander Acosta’s sweetheart deal was ostensibly designed to produce justice. She was raped while Courtney Wild, who had been abused starting at age fourteen, was simultaneously fighting the federal government to find out why the deal had been made in the first place. She was raped because the system worked exactly the way it was designed to work: it protected the man with the money and discarded the women without it.

And then the government released the files. And Roza’s name appeared in them. Five hundred times. While the names of the rich and powerful who raped children remained hidden behind black bars of redaction.

Roza closed her testimony with a story about Ignaz Semmelweis, the nineteenth-century doctor who discovered that physicians performing autopsies and then delivering babies without washing their hands were killing the women in their care. He had the evidence. He had the solution. The medical establishment refused to listen because admitting the truth would have required admitting their own complicity. Semmelweis was ridiculed and cast out.

“I see the same patterns happening today,” Roza said. “Releasing my name while redacting the names of the powerful is not a mistake. It’s a choice. It’s a choice to prioritize the comfort of an institution over the safety of survivors.”

She paused. “I am no longer a Jane Doe hiding in the files. I’m a woman. I’m a fighter. And I’m a witness.”

Then she looked at the members of Congress. “And I’m begging you. Please wash your hands.”

IV. What Courtney Wild Said

Courtney Wild, a survivor of Jeffrey Epstein who was first abused at age 14, testifies at the House Oversight Committee Democrats’ field hearing on the Epstein investigation. West Palm Beach City Hall, Florida, May 12, 2026. (C-SPAN)

Courtney Wild was fourteen years old when Jeffrey Epstein began abusing her. She was abused until she was seventeen. She then spent the next decade of her life — from 2008 to 2019 — fighting the federal government in court under the Crime Victims’ Rights Act, trying to understand how a secret deal had been made that extinguished any possibility of federal prosecution for the man who raped her.

She won. In 2019, a federal judge ruled that her rights, and the rights of at least forty other victims, had been violated by the non-prosecution agreement.

And then nothing happened.

“The court found that the government violated the law,” Wild told the committee, “and nothing happened. That means the law — the Crime Victims’ Rights Act, 18 USC 3771 — does not matter. And if the law has no consequences, it doesn’t protect anybody.”

She described the emails between Epstein’s lawyers and federal prosecutors that she and her attorney Brad Edwards spent years fighting to obtain through the Eleventh Circuit. “The government wasn’t trying to negotiate with Epstein’s lawyers,” she said. “It looked like the government was trying to make him happy. They were trying to make sure that the punishment they were going to give him was okay with him. It seemed like they had forgotten that there were forty of us kids who had been abused by him.”

Then she said this: “I was so confused because where I come from, if you commit a crime, you go to jail. I have never heard of the feds letting you help them decide which crimes they’re going to charge you with, or if you’re cool with how long you’re going to spend in jail.”

A pause.

“But I thought — what do I know? Maybe that’s how it works for rich guys.”

Another pause.

“That’s how it did work for Jeffrey Epstein.”

Wild closed by asking Congress to fix the Crime Victims’ Rights Act — to define what “meaningful” consultation with victims actually requires, to create enforceable penalties when those rights are violated, and to provide for attorneys’ fees so that survivors can find lawyers willing to fight for them. “Don’t let this work be in vain,” she said. “Make the Crime Victims’ Rights Act matter.”

When a member of Congress later asked Wild what justice would look like, she was quiet for a moment. Then she said: “I don’t know what justice looks like anymore.”

V. What Maria Farmer Said from a Hospital Bed

Maria Farmer reported Jeffrey Epstein, Ghislaine Maxwell, and Les Wexner to the FBI in 1996. She was the first person to do so. The commanding officer at her local precinct told her to take the full story to the FBI because it spanned multiple states. She did. The FBI asked questions, said they were familiar with some of the perpetrators, and gave her every reason to believe they would act.

They did not act.

Eight years later — eight years during which Epstein continued to recruit, traffic, and rape girls across multiple states and countries — the FBI came back to Farmer. They said they had searched for her because they knew she had reported Epstein in 1996 and no one else had. They urged her to testify in a federal criminal trial. They promised they would get him this time.

They did not get him. They gave him the sweetheart deal instead.

From her hospital bed, Farmer listed the names of women who were abused in the years after her 1996 report was ignored: Virginia Giuffre, Anushka DeGiorgio, Shante Davies, Marika Cherton, Dani Bensky, Jena-Lisa Jones, Ashley Rub, Jennifer Rose. “Each survivor represents a life changed forever,” she said. “Suffering that could have been spared if the FBI had done their job in 1996.”

She has been trying to obtain her own FBI file — the record of the report she filed thirty years ago — for years. Her FOIA requests have been met with delays. One response told her the government would get back to her in November 2027 — a three-year wait. When the Epstein Files Transparency Act finally produced a partial document confirming a small portion of her report, it was incomplete. “Where is the evidence I provided of my sexual assault?” she asked. “And of my sister’s?”

She has received death threats from Maxwell’s associates, including a threat to burn down her apartment. The harassment has not stopped in three decades.

“Had the FBI done their job,” Farmer said, “thirty years of child sex abuse and trauma could have been avoided.”

She demanded that Maxwell not be pardoned. “She sexually abused me, my minor sister, hurt so many girls and other young women, and personally threatened to kill me. She has shown no remorse and has demonstrated no basis on which to believe she would ever be truthful.”

She is still fighting. She described that fact as a miracle.

VI. What the Attorneys Said

Spencer Kuvin has spent two decades representing Epstein’s earliest victims. He told the committee that the non-prosecution agreement was “the worst ever executed by a US attorney” — not merely because it was secret, not merely because it extinguished federal charges, but because it gave immunity to four named co-conspirators and then added the phrase “unnamed co-conspirators.”

“I have never in thirty years doing this,” Kuvin said, “ever seen a non-prosecution agreement so broad as to say that they’re giving immunity to unnamed individuals. It was a gift.”

He described walking through Epstein’s Palm Beach mansion after the original investigation and seeing mounting brackets where cameras had been. CPU units had been removed. He confirmed that in 2003, Sarah Kellen — one of Epstein’s assistants — had shown internal surveillance footage to police during a separate incident, proving that cameras were operational inside the home. That footage has never been released. He described a client in Manhattan who saw the full camera network in Epstein’s New York mansion. That footage, too, has never been released.

The lead prosecutor at the time, Maria Villafaña, had identified the camera evidence as a smoking gun. Acosta refused to pursue it.

And then there was the question of the emails between Acosta’s office and Epstein’s lawyers. Kuvin told the committee what the documents showed: prosecutors and defense attorneys meeting at Starbucks, corresponding like friends, negotiating not the terms of justice but the terms of comfort for the defendant. “It was all scripted,” he said. “It was all set up.”

Lauren Hersh, the former prosecutor, made a point that deserves to be tattooed on the wall of the Oversight Committee hearing room: “When individuals and institutions surface repeatedly in the files, it’s a pretty strong indicator that there’s some there there. When there are millions and millions of dollars flowing between an individual and Epstein and there doesn’t seem to be a business relationship, but instead allegations of sexual abuse — that’s not noise to ignore. That is an investigative lead.”

She noted that the Senate Finance Committee had identified $1.5 billion in suspicious wire transfers in Epstein-related suspicious activity reports (SARs) held by the Treasury Department — transfers that include the names of women and girls he may have trafficked, as well as the names of potential clients. Those SARs have not been released.

One point five billion dollars. And the acting attorney general says there is “no case against anybody.”

VII. What Garcia Revealed

Rep. Robert Garcia (D-CA), Ranking Member of the House Oversight Committee, speaks at the Democratic-led field hearing on the Epstein investigation. West Palm Beach City Hall, Florida, May 12, 2026. (C-SPAN)

Ranking Member Robert Garcia opened the hearing by announcing that Oversight Democrats were publishing a new report based on evidence obtained through their investigation — including bank records showing that Acosta’s sweetheart deal did not merely fail to deliver justice. It actively enabled Epstein to build a global trafficking network using the financial infrastructure the deal left intact.

“This report,” Garcia said, holding bank records aloft, “is just the beginning of numerous reports and information that we intend to put out over the course of the months ahead.”

He also made a disclosure that should have been front-page news in every newspaper in the country: through their investigation, committee Democrats have been conducting depositions and interviews with individuals who were never contacted by law enforcement in the thirty years since Maria Farmer’s first report. Lawyers, accountants, private investigators, assistants — people who surrounded Epstein, who facilitated his operation — had never once been questioned by the FBI or the DOJ.

“How could Les Wexner have never been interviewed by our own government?” Garcia asked. “How could Darren Indyke and Richard Kahn — who were essentially the brain trust of Jeffrey Epstein’s organization — how could they never have been questioned? That is not a mistake. That is a cover-up.”

He was equally direct about partisan culpability. “Why wasn’t Merrick Garland more engaged and focused on prosecutions?” he asked. “This is not just about partisan politics. This is a continued pattern of abuse and not seeking justice.” Then he pivoted: “But we’re here now, at this moment, in this Congress, with this administration, and this cover-up must end.”

Garcia closed by noting that Pam Bondi’s deposition is scheduled for late May. He demanded that it be conducted under oath, on camera, and released to the American public.

VIII. What Nobody Said (Because Nobody Was There)

Not a single Republican member of the House Oversight Committee attended the hearing. Not Nancy Mace, who led the bipartisan subpoena of Bondi. Not Anna Paulina Luna, the Florida Republican who has been publicly vocal about congressional misconduct. Not James Comer, the chairman who has subpoena power and has used it for everything from investigating Hunter Biden’s laptop to requesting documents about the president’s son’s art sales.

The survivors noticed.

When Rep. Jasmine Crockett observed that only Democrats had shown up, she did not equivocate. “I’m going to be full-on partisan,” she said, “because only the Democrats decided to show up today.”

She also did something that no other member did. She named the legal term for what the DOJ has been doing. “My colleagues have used words like ‘cover-up,’” she said. “’Choice.’ For me, it’s obstruction of justice. That’s just the lawyer in me. Anyone else that would engage in this type of behavior would be charged with obstruction of justice. Yet somehow, because it’s the Department of Justice — the ones that are supposed to enforce justice and law in this country — we’re not calling it the thing.”

Rep. Melanie Stansbury of New Mexico made a structural argument that deserves to be the epigraph of this entire series: “The system didn’t fail. The system was designed to protect the wealthy. The system was rigged from the beginning. It was a system of exploitation, a system of abuse, and a system of power.”

She pointed out that this was the first congressional hearing on the Epstein case ever held in West Palm Beach — the community where the crimes began more than twenty-five years ago. She noted that her state, New Mexico, is the first and only state to pass a law to investigate the Epstein case at the state level.

Rep. Dave Min mentioned the $1.5 billion in suspicious activity reports. He called for Treasury to release the SARs immediately. He called for subpoenas of twenty-one additional banks, beyond the Deutsche Bank and JP Morgan subpoenas already issued.

And then Roza was asked what justice would look like. She looked at the members of Congress. “I come from a different country,” she said. “I appreciate you asking that question, but I don’t know — that’s your job. You guys have to figure out how to make justice, not me.”

The room went quiet.

“You keep on saying this is the worst case in the United States. It’s not. It’s the worst case in the world. This never happened to me in other places, and I traveled as a model all over the world. This didn’t happen to me there. It happened to me here.”

IX. The Convergence

I published Part 45 of The Trump Accountability Project this morning. It is titled “How War Buried Epstein (Again)” and it documents the way the Iran crisis has functioned, since February, as a mechanism for pushing the Epstein story out of the news cycle. The thesis is that war is the most effective suppression tool available to a president who needs the country looking somewhere else.

And then I watched the hearing.

The war is still doing its job. While four women testified in West Palm Beach about being raped as children, about having their identities exposed by their own government, about thirty years of institutional betrayal, the president was en route to Beijing for a summit with Xi Jinping. The networks that might have covered the hearing were covering the departure. The pundits who might have analyzed the testimony were analyzing the trade implications. The country that might have listened was listening to something else.

Jim Acosta — who was literally under fire at the Correspondents’ Dinner two weeks ago, who was pushed out of legacy media and now broadcasts independently on the platforms the establishment considers beneath it — was the journalist who showed up. He stood outside city hall in West Palm Beach with a camera and a microphone and did the job that the institutions built to do that job could not be bothered to do.

There is something structurally important happening here. The survivors are testifying. The independent media is covering it. The Oversight Democrats are investigating without subpoena power, without majority status, without the cooperation of the DOJ. And they are producing more investigative leads, more depositions, more documentary evidence, and more testimony than the FBI and the Department of Justice have produced in thirty years.

The institutions designed to do this work have failed. The people who were never supposed to have to do it are doing it anyway.
