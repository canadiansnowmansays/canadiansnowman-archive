Of the three slogans on the Ministry of Truth’s wall, the first is the one that has aged into usefulness.

Orwell meant it as the purest form of the lie: the state calling a thing by the name of its opposite and daring the citizen to notice the substitution. What he did not anticipate was the variant in which the slogan turns out to be an accurate operational description, arrived at honestly, by an administration that would be startled to learn it had said anything at all.

For three days at the end of July, the war stopped.

No American strikes on Iran from Friday night, July 24, through Monday, July 27. No Iranian strikes on American allies either. As Aaron Boxerman and Sanam Mahoozi reported for the New York Times on July 26, Omani officials came to Tehran over the weekend to discuss the Strait of Hormuz, and Iran’s foreign ministry said the talks were useful and that progress had been achieved. Brent crude fell 11.3 percent on Monday to close at $85.87, its steepest single-day decline since April 8. Stocks opened higher. Somewhere in the commentary a phrase surfaced about diplomacy being given space.

It was not diplomacy. It was inventory.

I. What the Pause Was For

As Eric Schmitt and Jonathan Swan reported for the New York Times on July 25, the president set aside his plans for a sharp escalation after a Friday meeting with his cabinet, and the governing concern in the room was not the Iranian position, the Omani channel, or the price of oil. It was the Pentagon’s stock of Patriot interceptors.

General Dan Caine, chairman of the Joint Chiefs, told the president privately that resuming major combat operations was doable but would dangerously deplete the interceptors available to Central Command.

We now know roughly what he meant. A report published on Monday, July 27, by Mark Cancian and Chris H. Park of the Center for Strategic and International Studies — under the mild title Renewed Iran War Would Test Diminished Interceptor Inventories — estimates that the American stock of high-end Patriot interceptors has fallen from 2,330 before the war to 1,030 when the April ceasefire took hold, and now stands somewhere between 759 and 827. That is a decline of at least sixty-five percent in five months. THAAD, the higher-altitude system and the harder one to replace, is down by roughly thirty-eight percent, leaving an estimated 234 to 278.

The United States manufactures 183 of the high-end Patriots a year, and it takes about three and a half years from the signing of a contract to the delivery of a missile. Cancian, a retired Marine colonel, has said the depletion could reach a level that would affect America’s capacity to fight China, or even North Korea. Three sources familiar with the stockpile told CNN that the CSIS estimates track closely with the government’s own internal figures.

And then the detail that tells you what a munitions shortage actually looks like at the level of a battery crew. NBC News reported that American commanders, anxious not to waste interceptors, have begun declining to shoot down Iranian projectiles judged likely to land in unpopulated parts of U.S. bases in the region.

Three American soldiers died in Jordan when a ballistic missile slipped through the air defences that those interceptors are. The Pentagon’s chief spokesman, Sean Parnell, responded to the stockpile reporting on July 28 with a statement declaring the American military the most powerful in the world, in possession of everything it needs to execute at the time and place of the president’s choosing. It was word for word the statement his office had issued twice before on the same subject since April. An unchanged sentence, deployed three times across four months, against a two-thirds drawdown.

Few if any in the president’s inner circle thought escalation was wise. One senior official offered the assessment that ought to have ended the argument in March: the strikes keep Iran cohesive and give its leaders an external enemy to point at.

And then Admiral Brad Cooper, who actually runs Central Command, gave the president the advice that makes this essay necessary. As Julian Barnes, Helene Cooper and Eric Schmitt reported for the Times on July 29, the admiral told the president that if he was not going to resume major combat operations, the United States should stop the bombing campaign around the Strait of Hormuz, because it has reached the limit of its effectiveness.

Read that again. The officer commanding the war told the commander-in-chief that the campaign has two rational options — escalate or stop — and the president has spent the week choosing the third one, which his own commander has already told him does not work. A spokesman for Admiral Cooper declined to comment, citing the confidentiality of the discussions.

So the pause was not a decision about Iran. It was a decision about warehouses. And a pause taken to preserve munitions is not a pause; it is a reload.

War is peace. Not as a lie this time — as a logistics schedule. The quiet was the war’s, and the war took it back on Tuesday. (The Pentagon’s parallel innovation in bookkeeping — the casualty ledger split across two webpages, as Helene Cooper reported for the Times on July 26 — is the subject of Ministry of Truth, Part 11.)

II. The Three Days Ended on A Tuesday

On Tuesday evening, July 28, Iran fired multiple ballistic missiles at American forces in Jordan. Central Command called it an attempted surprise attack and said all the missiles were intercepted — which is both true and the point, since interception is the resource in question.

The American answer came Wednesday evening as a “powerful response,” and again Thursday morning as a “heavy wave” against command centres, missile and drone facilities, coastal surveillance sites and maritime capabilities. Jordan’s military said it shot down five more Iranian missiles early Thursday. Iranian state media reported a strike on Piranshahr, on the Iraqi border.

The Revolutionary Guard’s own outlet then claimed its aerospace unit had destroyed three American F-35s at Al-Azraq Air Base in Jordan and damaged three more. The Guard exaggerates, there is no American confirmation, and I would not print the claim except for its stated justification: retaliation, the Guard said, for an American strike on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz that killed three members of one family, including a child.

That is the war now. A family on an island, three aircraft that may not exist, and a barrage of missiles which everyone agrees were all intercepted, fired to prove that the firing can continue.

The family has since acquired an official American answer, and it is worth recording precisely. Iran’s parliament speaker, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, said the United States soils its hands with a new crime every day, and called the strike on civilian homes on Qeshm Island a continuation of the atrocities at Minab and Lamerd. Captain Tim Hawkins, the Central Command spokesman, told ABC News that the American military never targets civilians — unlike Iranian forces, he added, which have fired thousands of missiles and drones at innocent people across the Middle East and killed more than fifty thousand of their own citizens earlier this year.

Set aside that the fifty-thousand figure is Captain Hawkins’s, offered without a source, and considerably larger than any independent count of the winter crackdown I have seen. Notice the grammar instead. Asked about a two-year-old, Central Command said the United States does not target civilians and then produced a larger number of dead attributable to someone else. That is not a denial. It is a comparison, and a comparison is what an institution offers when it cannot manage a denial.

And then, on Wednesday night, the bombing stopped again. As of this writing on Friday, July 31, there have been no American strikes on Iran since. Two pauses inside eight days, both beginning immediately after a heavy expenditure of interceptors, both described by officials as space for diplomacy. At Camp David on Friday, in a televised cabinet meeting, the president said he was losing faith in Iran, because they lie and they misrepresent, and they always want to talk but break their word so often. Iran, he predicted, would get weaker, then perhaps a little stronger, then weaker again, and then peter out.

Losing faith. Five months into a war he began, over a document his own negotiators wrote ambiguously enough that both governments could sign it while meaning opposite things.

III. Saudi Arabia Stops Pretending

Late on Tuesday, July 28, Saudi and American aircraft struck Iranian-aligned militias across Iraq. The Popular Mobilization Forces — a coalition of mostly Shiite armed groups written into Iraqi law as part of the state’s own security apparatus — said the strikes hit its official headquarters in Baghdad, Wasit, Nineveh, Basra, Kirkuk, Karbala and Diyala, killing twenty and wounding thirty-two. Washington’s position is that the dead were government security forces taking their orders from Tehran. It was the first time Saudi Arabia has claimed responsibility for striking inside Iraq since the war began in February. Riyadh called it lawful self-defence; Iraq’s national security council called it an act of aggression.

Hesham Alghannam, who directs the Security Research Center at Naif Arab University for Security Sciences in Riyadh, called the Saudi acknowledgement a pivotal development. It is worth being plain about why. For five months Saudi Arabia has been the exposed ally — the country whose oil terminals and tankers absorbed the consequences of an American war it did not start. It is now a co-belligerent, which is the precise outcome the second phase of this campaign was supposedly designed to avoid.

The following day the Saudi defence minister, Prince Khalid bin Salman, sat with the president and the vice-president in Washington to convey his brother’s message that the kingdom is not looking to escalate.

At a group funeral on Wednesday, mourners carried coffins draped in the Iranian flag alongside coffins draped in the Iraqi one — the Iranian ones, as the Washington Times reported on July 29, for Iranian advisers killed in the strikes. If that holds, the United States and Saudi Arabia killed Iranian military personnel on Iraqi soil this week, and it reached print as a caption under a photograph of the coffins.

What the strikes actually accomplished is the opposite of American policy. Baghdad had spent months struggling to meet Washington’s demand that it disarm the militias. Popular pressure to disarm was building. The strikes ended that. The militias now hold the sovereignty argument, and they have used it: one leader promised a bombing for a bombing; another suggested the government’s weapons should be transferred to the resistance. They gave the Iraqi government a deadline of Tuesday to respond, and two commanders privately conceded the deadline is theatre — they know Baghdad cannot act against Washington or Riyadh, which is precisely why the demand was made.

Iraq’s presidency called the strikes a flagrant violation of its sovereignty and, in the same statement, rejected the use of Iraqi soil for settling regional scores. Both halves are true, which is Iraq’s whole predicament in a sentence.

No group has claimed the drone attacks the strikes were retaliation for. Central Command counts more than thirty of them in seventy-two hours against American forces and Saudi energy sites. The Iraqi militias deny responsibility — a departure, since they claimed attacks readily before April, and the Houthis claim everything they do and occasionally things they don’t. Iraq’s army spokesman noted that the strikes have obstructed the investigation into whether the attacks came from Iraq at all, and asked for the evidence. None has been produced.

Two analysts supply the pattern this series has been describing since March. Renad Mansour of Chatham House: the more the United States attacks these groups, the more it fragments their chains of command, producing more rogue actors. Ramzy Mardini of Geopol Labs: Iran has for decades cultivated proxies that deliberately obscure who pulled the trigger, and Saudi Arabia stands to lose the most.

Decapitation produces incoherence. It did to Iran’s leadership in February, and Washington is now applying the same method to the militias with the same predictable result. An enemy with a chain of command can sign something. A fragmented one cannot.

IV. Egypt, and Nobody’s Fingerprints

On Wednesday, July 29, two vessels caught fire at Damietta, on Egypt’s Mediterranean coast near the mouth of the Suez Canal: the Energos Winter, an American-owned gas storage tanker, and the Greek-operated LNG carrier GasLog Salem. Zane Irwin, Peter Eavis, Farnaz Fassihi and Pranav Baskar reported for the Times that maritime trackers attributed the fires to a drone. Egypt confirmed the drone finding on Thursday. There were no injuries. Nobody has claimed it.

Two Iranians told the Times the strike was designed to demonstrate that global shipping could be disrupted far more deeply if Iran chose. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi, meanwhile, posted that Egypt is an important friend and partner whose security matters greatly to Tehran, and warned against false-flag operations. The Houthis denied it outright.

And Vaughn Cockayne and Tom Howell Jr. of the Washington Times report a third reading that nobody else is running: that Damietta was Iranian retaliation against Ukraine. On July 25, Ukraine struck an Iranian-linked cargo vessel in the Caspian, killing a sailor. Tehran called it a hostile and criminal act and vowed a response, and having no way to reach Ukraine, Iranian state media implied that energy shipments to Europe would answer for it.

If that is right, then the two wars this series has insisted on covering together have finally been plumbed into one another in the most literal way available. Ukraine strikes a ship carrying Iranian materiel to Russia; Iran burns an American-owned tanker in Egypt; the Suez Canal, which became the last safe route for Saudi oil after Hormuz closed, acquires a risk premium. Damietta’s regasification unit handles roughly seven percent of Egypt’s daily gas consumption, at the start of peak summer demand.

The president was asked about it in the Oval Office and said it was Iran-related, that he had been briefed, that it was more of the same, and that it would be straightened out. Then: “we’re going to be hitting them very hard, because it’s our turn to hit them.”

Our turn. Five months, eighteen American dead, and the doctrine has arrived at the rules of a schoolyard.

V. Three Registers in Seven Days

Assemble the president’s public statements about bombing Iranian infrastructure and you will find no policy, but you will find a man.

On Friday, July 24, asked in the Oval Office whether he hesitated to strike power plants and bridges because it might constitute a war crime, he said he would not respond to that question. David Sanger reported the exchange for the Times on July 26, in an analysis arguing that a president who believed there were no limits on his power has spent five months discovering several.

On Tuesday, July 28, on Fox, weighing the same targets: “And who am I hurting there? I’m hurting the people. So I’m not looking to do that.”

On Wednesday, July 29, on the same network, he vowed — as CNBC reported, citing Fox — to beat a considerable quantity of excrement out of Iran.

He declined to say whether it would be a crime, then declined to do it because it would hurt people, then promised violence in the vocabulary of a parking lot — all within six days, all about the same bridges, which were being destroyed throughout by an air force that did not require him to settle on a reason.

The same week produced the finest example yet of TACO applied to his own casus belli. Before meeting Prime Minister Netanyahu on July 28, the president told Fox that he did not need Netanyahu to tell him about Pickaxe Mountain, that Netanyahu raises it because he wants America to stay involved, and that he knows exactly what is happening there and it is not a big problem.

Six days earlier he had called Pickaxe a possible target for a nice big fat shot right in its front door. Four days earlier, Julian Barnes had reported for the Times that American intelligence believes the new supreme leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, is far more interested in a nuclear weapon than the father America helped kill. The Israeli meeting was described by a participant as extremely positive, and both men reaffirmed that Iran must never obtain nuclear weapons.

So: the facility is not a problem, the leadership is more determined to build a bomb than the leadership that was assassinated to prevent one, and the two leaders agree entirely. Danny Citrinowicz, a former Israeli military intelligence commander, put the irony to Isabel Kershner of the Times more economically than I can. Netanyahu told Trump that Ayatollah Khamenei had to be killed to stop Iran getting a weapon. There is now a new Ayatollah Khamenei, and Israel is saying Iran must be stopped from getting a weapon.

VI. Six Accounts of the Same Four Days

The president says the United States halted its strikes at Iran’s request.

Iran’s foreign ministry spokesman says Tehran will never allow Washington to determine the timing of war and peace, that whatever this is cannot be called a ceasefire, and that Iran has no negotiations with the United States at all — only with Oman, about a strait which remains closed. Iran’s deputy foreign minister says his government has submitted no request to negotiate with America in fifteen days. Tehran denied reports that it had agreed to a ten-day truce. The American ambassador to the United Nations said the pause was giving diplomacy space. Pakistan’s foreign ministry said negotiations were ongoing to normalize the situation, a statement that survived the resumption of bombing by about six hours.

Six official descriptions of four days, no two of them compatible. In June this series called the ceasefire document a memorandum of misunderstanding. The diplomacy has now caught up to the paperwork.

VII. The Chamber That Faces Voters

On Thursday, July 23, the House of Representatives voted for the second time to direct the president to end the war or seek explicit authorization to continue it, with enough Republicans to carry it. The Senate has now declined for the second time. CBS News, tracking the votes alongside the polling, puts the president’s overall approval at 33 percent — marginally below where he stood at the same point in his first term.

The stated reasoning of the Senate skeptics deserves preserving. Because the president notified Congress on July 10 that hostilities had resumed, the sixty-day clock under the War Powers Resolution restarted — so the administration should be given more time. The clock written to constrain the war has become the argument for extending it.

Senator John Kennedy of Louisiana, still the only Republican in that chamber reliably willing to say so out loud, spent his time with Secretary Hegseth at appropriations asking for the objective, the timeline, the ramifications and the endgame, and got a hypothetical waved away. The House then approved a framework providing roughly $70 billion for the war and left town for over a month.

VIII. By the Numbers

  • 18 American service members killed, listed across two Pentagon webpages that must be added together.

  • 653 American wounded, as of the database update on Thursday, July 30 — up from 624 four days earlier.

  • 759–827 high-end Patriot interceptors remaining, down from 2,330 before the war — a decline of at least 65 percent, per CSIS.

  • 234–278 THAAD interceptors remaining, down roughly 38 percent.

  • 183 high-end Patriots produced per year, with a delivery lag of about three and a half years.

  • 13,629 munitions fired at Iranian targets between February 28 and the April ceasefire, per Bloomberg.

  • $58.6 billion the ceiling on the Patriot production contract awarded to Lockheed Martin this week, running through fiscal 2032.

  • 29 verified transits of the Strait of Hormuz between July 24 and 26. About 100 a day was normal before the war.

  • 24 tankers waiting at Kharg Island on Sunday, July 26.

  • 20+ American warships enforcing the blockade the president calls a steel wall.

  • $85.87 Brent crude at Monday’s close, down 11.3 percent on three days of quiet, and climbing again since.

  • $4 a gallon for gasoline in the United States.

  • 80%+ of American military families reporting elevated stress from this war, per Blue Star Families — reported by Bernard Mokam and Georgia Gee for the Times on July 29, alongside counsellors describing tripled caseloads.

  • 33% approval for the president overall; 29% for his handling of this war.

  • 1 Iranian family on Qeshm Island, three of them killed, one a child.

  • Narges Mohammadi remains in Evin Prison, where she has been throughout, while the country that imprisons her is governed by a man too injured to appear in public and too hunted to communicate.

IX.

The pause was three days long and cost fifteen dollars a barrel to end. That is the cleanest measurement anyone has taken of this war: the world’s energy markets will pay a fifteen-dollar premium for seventy-two hours in which two governments merely refrain.

Admiral Cooper told the president the campaign had reached the limit of its effectiveness. He was describing the bombing around the Strait of Hormuz. He might as well have been describing the fifth month of a war launched to end a nuclear program that is now more likely to produce a weapon, to reopen a strait that was open when it started, and to replace a government that has been replaced by a worse one.

Every party to this conflict is now doing something it has been told does not work, for reasons it has stopped explaining, at a price it declines to total on any single page.
