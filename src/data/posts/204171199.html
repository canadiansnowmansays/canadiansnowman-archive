At a conference last October, Emma Wiles, a Boston University professor who studies how AI affects workers, listened to two human resources executives describe something that would have sounded like satire two years earlier: their companies had begun listing AI agents as “employees” on their organizational charts. The executives framed this as cutting-edge productivity practice. Wiles, with three collaborators from Boston Consulting Group, decided to test what actually happens once an AI system occupies a position previously held by a person.

What they found, reported by Noam Scheiber in The New York Times, is the first experimental confirmation this series has had access to of a mechanism Part 50 described through Doctorow’s accountability sink framework — and the confirmation is more precise, and more unsettling, than the original theory.

I. The Experiment

Wiles and her colleagues surveyed more than a thousand corporate managers. About a third said their organizations referred to AI as a “teammate or employee.” Nearly a quarter said their employer listed AI agents on its actual organizational chart, alongside human staff. One manager described the arrangement to researchers without apparent irony: “We call it Scout. It’s technically an equivalent peer on your team.”

The researchers then gave every manager in the survey the same five documents, each containing planted errors, and twenty minutes to review as many as they could catch. Some managers were told an AI employee had produced the documents. Some were told an AI tool had produced them. Some were told a human had. Across most of the sample, the stated source made little difference in how carefully managers checked the work.

But at companies that had specifically placed AI agents on their org charts — treating them as something closer to colleagues than tools — managers caught substantially fewer errors when told an AI employee had done the work. Wiles’s explanation is the accountability sink mechanism stated with experimental precision: managers who supervise humans operate on the assumption that a subordinate’s mistake reflects on the manager. Managers who supervise an AI “employee,” for reasons Wiles’s research suggests are specifically tied to the anthropomorphized-peer framing, do not extend the same sense of ownership. “It’s not your problem,” Wiles said, describing the mental shorthand. The mistake becomes, in the manager’s own accounting, the tech team’s fault, or the fault of the executives who pushed for AI adoption in the first place — anyone’s responsibility except the person whose job was specifically to catch it.

This is the precise mechanism this series named in Part 50 through the Air Canada chatbot case, except Wiles’s research demonstrates something the Air Canada case could only suggest: the accountability sink is not simply a feature companies exploit deliberately to deflect blame after the fact. It is a psychological default that activates automatically, in real time, the moment a worker is told they’re vetting an AI’s output rather than a human’s — measurable, reproducible, and apparently invisible to the managers experiencing it.

II. The Bias Against Being Human

A second strand of research, independent of Wiles’s workplace study, identifies a different and in some ways more alarming defect: AI systems’ own measurable preference for AI-generated material over human-generated material.

A 2025 paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences found that several major large language models rated text written by humans lower than comparable text written by AI, a pattern the authors termed a “potentially consequential form of implicit ‘anti-human’ bias.” Jane Yi Jiang, an operations professor at Ohio State University, and her collaborator Jiannan Xu, a doctoral candidate at the University of Maryland, extended this finding into a domain with direct economic consequences: AI systems used to screen résumés favor candidates whose résumés were written with AI assistance over candidates who wrote their own, independent of the actual underlying qualifications being described.

The mechanism here compounds quietly rather than dramatically. A job applicant who used AI to polish their résumé gains an advantage over an equally or better qualified applicant who wrote their own — not because the AI-screened résumé reflects superior judgment or experience, but because the screening AI has a documented, measurable preference for the statistical fingerprint of AI-generated prose. Jiang and Xu found this specific bias is correctable — instructing the evaluating model to focus strictly on the substantive quality of the material, rather than considering authorship at all, reduced the effect. Some recruiting firms have already adjusted their processes after the finding reached them. But correction requires first knowing the bias exists, and Jiang’s own assessment of the broader pattern is blunt: “People are moving so fast to use LLMs without thinking too much about the implications, biases.”

III. The Rationality That Isn’t

A third defect, documented by the same research team, has consequences that extend well beyond hiring into the basic commercial decisions companies are increasingly delegating to AI: pricing, market entry, competitive strategy.

When humans negotiate or compete, they frequently cooperate and seek mutually acceptable outcomes, even when a narrowly self-interested move is technically available. AI models, by contrast, tend to evaluate the same situations through a colder, more strictly game-theoretic lens — and, per Xu’s research, they tend to overestimate how rational human counterparts and competitors actually are. “Most of the LLMs we test think that human beings are more rational than they actually are,” Xu said. “But the most rational response leads to a bad situation for all” in a meaningful share of the scenarios the team modeled — the textbook case being an AI system recommending an aggressive price cut against a competitor, on the theory that a coldly rational rival would respond predictably, when real competitors often respond with an equally aggressive and mutually destructive countercut precisely because they are not behaving like the model assumes.

This is a different register of harm than anything else this series has documented about AI’s economic effects. Parts 47 through 50 were substantially about AI displacing or degrading human labor. This is AI actively mis-modeling human behavior in ways that produce specifically bad outcomes for the humans relying on its judgment — not because the AI lacks data, but because its baseline assumption about how people behave is measurably wrong, and that wrongness gets exported directly into business decisions with real financial consequences.

IV. The Loop That Closes on Itself

The most consequential finding in Scheiber’s reporting is also the most abstract, and it concerns what happens to all of these uncorrected biases over time.

Shayne Longpre, founder of the Data Provenance Initiative, which monitors AI infrastructure, raised the specific risk of a self-reinforcing loop: if future AI models are trained on data substantially produced by today’s models, without sufficient care taken to filter or correct for the biases already present, “the tendency to consolidate on existing perspectives and behaviors seems likely.” Each generation of models would not just inherit the prior generation’s blind spots. It would compound them, since the training data itself increasingly consists of AI output rather than the more varied distribution of human-generated material the earlier models learned from.

Cecilie Steenbuch Traberg, a psychologist at Copenhagen Business School, documented a parallel effect already operating among human researchers who consult AI at every stage of their own work — generating research questions, seeking methodological advice, analyzing data, drafting findings. No individual researcher notices the effect on their own output. “You’re sparring with a chatbot, it’s helping you come up with ideas, you might think it sounds great,” Traberg said. “But at the collective level, it looks pretty similar. Everyone is sounding alike.” This is the homogenization risk this series gestured toward in Part 54’s discussion of cognitive polarization, but inverted: where Part 54 worried about a society splitting into two cognitively distinct populations, Traberg’s finding describes the opposite failure mode happening simultaneously, within the population of people most actively using AI to think — a narrowing of the range of ideas being produced, invisible at the individual level and only visible once you compare outputs across an entire field.

Both findings point toward the same long-run risk: a feedback loop in which AI-generated content increasingly trains the next generation of AI, and AI-assisted thinking increasingly homogenizes the next generation of human research, with neither loop currently subject to any institutional check designed to interrupt it.

V. The Room Going Silent

Wiles’s closing anecdote is worth ending on, because it is the clearest signal available of where this trajectory is actually headed, ahead of any of the research catching up to measure it properly.

At the same October conference where she first heard HR executives describe AI agents as employees, one executive went further: her company would soon have AI employees managing human employees. Wiles recalled the room’s reaction precisely. “A hush went over the room.” Even an audience of professionals already comfortable enough with anthropomorphized AI colleagues to put them on an org chart found the next step — an AI accountable for a human’s performance review, raise, or termination — to be a different category of proposition entirely.

Wiles’s own assessment of the underlying problem applies to every finding in this essay, not just the management question that started her research: “We’re going out there blind.” Companies know, broadly, that AI models can carry demographic bias, can answer confidently while being wrong, and can leak information that should stay private — the well-documented failure modes this series has covered since its earliest parts. What Scheiber’s reporting demonstrates is that the well-documented failures are very likely a fraction of the total defect surface, and that the undocumented remainder is not a fixed, discoverable list waiting to be checked off. It is a moving target, growing as adoption accelerates faster than the research that would explain what adoption is actually doing — which means the gap this series exists to track is not narrowing as the technology matures. By this accounting, it may be widening, simply because nobody has yet built the institutional capacity to find the unknown unknowns before they have already changed how a company hires, prices, competes, and is run.
