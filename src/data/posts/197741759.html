Billionaire and industry spending in U.S. elections exploded after Citizens United. The AI industry replicated decades of campaign finance architecture in just 18 months — faster than oil, tobacco, or finance ever managed.

The ten-part history that preceded this essay followed eighty years of accumulated mechanism — from Sidney Hillman’s workaround in 1943 to the dark money layering of the 2020s, each escalation building on the one before it, each reform followed by a more sophisticated circumvention, the maze resetting with better graphics every cycle.

The artificial intelligence industry replicated the entire architecture in a year and a half.

Not a piece of it. Not a tentative first step into political spending with a modest PAC and a few cautious donations to incumbents on the relevant subcommittees. The whole thing: the super PAC, the dark money nonprofit, the bipartisan spending arms, the 501(c)(4) that doesn’t disclose its donors, the ads that never mention the industry they are designed to protect, the candidates selected not for what they believe but for what they will not regulate. All of it, built from scratch, funded at a scale that took the oil and gas industry decades to reach, operational before the first comprehensive federal AI bill had been introduced in Congress.

If you have read the preceding ten parts of this series, you will recognize every mechanism that follows. If you have not, this essay will make less sense — not because the facts are complicated, but because the pattern is the point, and the pattern requires the history to see clearly.

The AI industry did not invent anything new. It did not need to. The maze was already built. It just moved in.

The Two Networks

The AI industry’s political money infrastructure is organized around two rival networks that are, beneath their policy disagreements, structurally identical.

The first is Leading the Future, a super PAC network that raised $125 million in 2025 and began the 2026 election cycle with $70 million in cash on hand. Its principal funders include Greg Brockman, co-founder and president of OpenAI; Marc Andreessen and Ben Horowitz of the venture capital firm Andreessen Horowitz; Joe Lonsdale, co-founder of Palantir; and Ron Conway, the Silicon Valley angel investor. Its policy position is that AI should be regulated at the federal level through a single national framework — which sounds reasonable until you understand that the purpose of a single national framework is to pre-empt the state laws that are currently the only laws that exist, and that the people funding the framework are the people whose companies would be constrained by those state laws.

Marc Andreessen and Ben Horowitz of Andreessen Horowitz, together with Greg Brockman (OpenAI) and other backers, funded Leading the Future — the $125 million super PAC network pushing for federal pre-emption of state AI laws.

Leading the Future operates through three vehicles. Think Big is its Democratic spending arm. American Mission is its Republican spending arm. Build American AI is its advocacy arm — a dark money organization running a $10 million campaign for uniform national AI policy that does not disclose its donors.

Super PAC. Bipartisan spending arms. Dark money 501(c)(4). If you have read Part IX of this series, you are looking at Karl Rove’s American Crossroads and Crossroads GPS architecture, rebuilt for a different industry with a larger initial capitalisation.

The second network is anchored by Public First Action, a 501(c)(4) social welfare organization backed by a self-reported $20 million donation from Anthropic, the AI company founded in 2020 by researchers who left OpenAI over governance and safety concerns. Public First Action also received $30 million from undisclosed sources — undisclosed because 501(c)(4) organizations are not required to reveal their donors, as this series documented in Part IX. Public First Action funds two PACs: Jobs & Democracy, which supports Democratic candidates, and Defending Our Values, which supports Republican candidates.

Dario Amodei, co-founder and CEO of Anthropic, whose company backed Public First Action — the parallel dark money and PAC network favouring stronger AI safety and opposing federal pre-emption.

Anthropic’s policy position is the opposite of Leading the Future’s: it favours stronger safety oversight, transparency requirements, and has opposed federal pre-emption of state AI laws. The two networks are, in the language of political strategy, on opposite sides of the AI regulation debate.

They are also, in the language of this series, structurally identical. Both operate through the same vehicles. Both use the same dark money architecture. Both deploy bipartisan spending arms to avoid being identified with a single party. Both fund candidates through mechanisms that route money away from its source and toward its destination with the minimum possible transparency.

The policy disagreement is real. The mechanism is the same. The maze does not care what the money wants. It cares only that the money moves.

The classic dark money architecture: 501(c)(4)s and super PACs moving hundreds of millions with minimal disclosure. Both Leading the Future and Public First Action networks use this exact structure — one “pro-safety,” one “pro-innovation,” same vehicles.

The Ads That Don’t Say AI

In February 2026, NBC News reported that super PACs backed by the AI industry were pouring millions into the year’s first primaries. There was, the report noted, one thing missing from their advertisements: any reference to artificial intelligence.

This is worth pausing on, because it is the most revealing detail in the entire AI campaign finance story, and because it connects directly to the mechanism this series has been tracking since Buckley.

The ads run by Think Big — the OpenAI-aligned Democratic spending arm — in New York’s Twelfth Congressional District attacked Alex Bores, a Democratic assemblyman who had led the push for New York’s AI regulation law. The ads did not mention AI regulation. They attacked Bores for his technology company’s work with ICE. “It’s black and white: Alex Bores’ tech company works for ICE,” a narrator says in one ad.

The ads were funded, in part, by money traceable to Joe Lonsdale — co-founder of Palantir, the data analytics company whose federal contracts include work with Immigration and Customs Enforcement. The ads attacking Bores for his company’s ICE connection were funded by a network backed by a man whose company’s ICE connection is one of its most prominent government relationships.

Think Big spent $2.9 million opposing Bores. On the other side, Jobs & Democracy — the Anthropic-aligned PAC — spent approximately $745,000 supporting him. The two companies most publicly identified with the question of whether AI should be governed democratically or left to the market were funding opposite sides of a Manhattan congressional primary, spending nearly $4 million combined, and neither side’s advertising mentioned artificial intelligence.

This is the pattern from Part III, updated for 2026. The soft money machine of the 1990s funded party activities that were technically distinct from candidate support and practically indistinguishable from it. The AI super PACs of 2026 fund advertising that is technically about immigration and healthcare and practically about who will regulate artificial intelligence. The form of the message is maintained. The substance is hollowed out. The voter who watches the ad about ICE does not know they are watching an ad about AI governance. That is the point.

Political advertising data flows. In 2026, AI-backed super PAC ads spent millions attacking or supporting candidates while almost never mentioning “artificial intelligence” — classic issue-ad strategy updated for the new industry.

The Map

The AI industry’s political spending is not confined to a single congressional district. It is a national campaign, targeting races in both parties, across multiple states, with a strategic logic that is visible once you see the pattern.

In Texas, American Mission — the Republican arm of the OpenAI-aligned network — spent $2.8 million across multiple congressional races, including $747,000 supporting Chris Gober, a Republican candidate who had served as a legal adviser to Elon Musk’s political operation in 2024. Jessica Steinmann, a Republican who won her primary in Texas’s Eighth Congressional District, received approximately $511,000 in ad spending. In Gober’s case, American Mission accounted for nearly half of all advertising spending on his behalf during the primary. None of the ads mentioned artificial intelligence. They mentioned Trump, China, and technology investment.

In Georgia, Montana, and Iowa, Leading the Future pledged $1.5 million supporting Republican primary candidates in safely Republican districts — races where outside money could prove decisive in primaries that the general election would not contest.

In Illinois, Think Big spent more than $1 million each supporting two former members of Congress — Jesse Jackson Jr. and Melissa Bean — in their comeback bids. The ads touted their records on the Affordable Care Act. The funding came from an AI industry network.

In North Carolina, Public First — the Anthropic-aligned network — spent more than $1.6 million boosting an incumbent in a race where the data center debate was the local issue but the advertising mentioned immigration raids and holding Trump accountable.

The strategic logic is the logic of Part VIII of this series — the New Nobility chapter. The AI industry is not trying to purchase all of American politics. It is trying to purchase the specific parts that affect its regulatory future. It is selecting candidates who will support a federal AI framework that pre-empts state regulation, opposing candidates who have demonstrated willingness to regulate the industry at the state level, and doing so through advertising that discusses everything except the actual reason the money is being spent.

Polls show that a majority of voters remain sceptical of AI and do not want data centers in their communities. The ads know this. That is why they do not mention AI.

The Irony

I have spent considerable time in another newsletter — The Asimov Gap — examining the distance between what artificial intelligence can do and what democratic governance has figured out how to manage. That gap is, it turns out, also a campaign finance gap. The same companies whose governance models are in active democratic dispute — one of them the subject of a three-week federal trial in Oakland that ended on May 18 when a jury rejected the plaintiff’s claims in ninety minutes without reaching the merits — are simultaneously funding congressional races to shape the composition of the body that might one day regulate them.

This is the Super PACman story applied to the newest and fastest-growing industry on earth.

The mechanism is not new. Leading the Future is American Crossroads with a different donor list. Public First Action is a 501(c)(4) social welfare organization that exists to fund political activity while concealing its donors, exactly as described in Part IX. The bipartisan spending arms are the same architecture the Koch network pioneered. The ads that don’t mention AI are the same technique the soft money machine used when it ran “issue ads” that mentioned candidates by name without using the magic words “vote for” or “vote against.”

What is new is the speed. The tobacco industry took decades to build its political influence infrastructure. The financial industry took a generation. The AI industry did it in eighteen months, with more initial capital than any of its predecessors, at a moment when the technology it is spending to protect is moving faster than the political system has ever been asked to respond.

And the two companies at the centre of the spending — OpenAI and Anthropic — are the same two companies whose governance models represent the defining dispute in artificial intelligence. One was founded as a nonprofit to build AI for the benefit of humanity. The other was founded because its researchers did not trust the first company’s governance. Both are now funding congressional races through dark money vehicles, opposing and supporting the same candidates, running ads that do not mention their industry, and deploying the full architecture of the post-Citizens United campaign finance system to ensure that whatever regulation emerges will have been shaped by their spending before it was shaped by the public’s vote.

The maze has a new player. The player arrived with $125 million in one pocket and $50 million in the other, hired the same lawyers, built the same vehicles, ran the same plays, and moved faster than anyone who came before.

The dots are disappearing.
