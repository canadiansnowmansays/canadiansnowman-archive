I count my essays. Readers of this series know that by now, and know that every so often the count lands on a number that was already a year before I got to it, and I stop whatever I was doing and write about it.

This is essay 1865, and it is one of the grave ones. I said at essay 1835 that I would write it the way I wrote 1633, and I will: plainly at the top, so you know what kind of essay you are holding, with my hands as steady as I can keep them. In the single month of April 1865, the deadliest war in American history ended and the American president was murdered, five days apart, and between those two events — enclosing them, underwriting them, giving them their meaning — sits the ugliest number in the history of political arithmetic, which that same spring was finally struck from the books. This essay is about that number. It is a fraction. It is three-fifths.

Let me lay the fraction out first, because everything in April 1865 is the settling of its account.

In 1787, the framers of the American Constitution — the operating charter of essay 1789’s quiet ledger — faced an accounting question: how were enslaved people to be counted in apportioning representation and taxes? The enslaving states wanted them counted fully — not as citizens, not as persons with rights, but as population, because population conferred seats in Congress and votes in the Electoral College. The non-enslaving states objected to granting political weight for human beings held as property. The compromise, written into Article I, Section 2, counted each enslaved person as three-fifths of a person. I want to be precise about what this fraction did, because its cynicism is routinely misremembered as mere insult. It was worse than insult; it was leverage. The three-fifths clause gave the enslaving states congressional seats and electoral votes in proportion to the very people they held in bondage — the enslaved counted toward the political power of their enslavers while possessing none themselves, their bodies entered in the census column and their humanity in no column at all. It is the most consequential single act of dishonest counting in this series’ eighteen centuries of files: a fraction, agreed by gentlemen, that weaponized the counted against themselves. And on that fraction the arithmetic of American power ran for seventy-eight years — presidencies decided by it, laws passed under it, the whole ledger of the republic tilted by three-fifths of four million people who had never been asked.

The war that came due on that account killed, by the modern demographic reckonings, something on the order of three-quarters of a million soldiers — the estimates were long carried at 620,000, and scholars working from census data have since revised them upward, and I give you the range rather than the false precision, as this series does. Add the civilians, the refugees, the freedpeople dead in the contraband camps, and the toll defies the tidy figure the textbooks want. Two percent of the entire American population. The equivalent, in today’s United States, of roughly seven million dead. It was, per capita, the costliest ledger entry in the nation’s history, and no war since has approached it.

And here is where I must bring in the one text this essay has been waiting for, because the accounting I have just performed — the war as the settling of the fraction’s account — is not my conceit. It is Abraham Lincoln’s, stated by him, in public, five weeks before Appomattox, in the greatest piece of theological bookkeeping ever spoken in English. The Second Inaugural Address, delivered March 4, 1865, is seven hundred words long, and near its end Lincoln does something no head of state at the edge of victory has done before or since: he presents the war as an invoice — as the possibility that a just God intended the conflict to continue until all the wealth piled by two hundred and fifty years of unrequited toil was sunk, and every drop of blood drawn by the lash repaid by another drawn by the sword. Blood entered against blood. Toil entered against treasure. Two and a half centuries of theft, carried on the books unacknowledged, and the war as the audit that a higher Auditor had ordered — with the nation, both nations, North and South alike, named as the audited party. I have read the address more times than I can count and it still takes the air out of the room. The man presiding over the winning side stood on the steps of the Capitol and declined to claim the victory, entering it instead as a debt payment — and then closed with malice toward none, with charity for all: the only creditor in this series’ files who, holding the ledger and the army both, chose to mark the account settled rather than to keep collecting. Whether the books of Heaven read as Lincoln proposed is beyond my beat. That a president proposed it — that the greatest speech in American history is an accounting document — is squarely on it.

Five weeks later, the settlement. On April 9, 1865, at the McLean house at Appomattox Court House, Virginia, Robert E. Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia to Ulysses S. Grant, and the terms Grant wrote out — in his own hand, in a spare room, in about ten minutes — belong in this essay because they are bookkeeping of a particular kind, and the kind matters. Officers to keep their sidearms. Men claiming a horse or mule to take it home — for the spring ploughing, Grant said, the armies dissolving back into the planting calendar. Rations issued to the starving surrendered army from the stores of the army that had starved it. And for each man, a printed parole pass — the presses worked through the following days producing them, some twenty-eight thousand paroles, each one a small paper entry converting a soldier of a broken rebellion back into a farmer with a mule — so that the largest surrender of the war was executed not as a spectacle but as a paperwork exercise, deliberately unhumiliating, receipts issued, accounts closed man by man. When the Union gunners began firing salutes, Grant ordered them stopped. The war ends in this series’ files not with a ceremony but with a print run, and I confess I find the print run more moving than any ceremony could have been. It was the Ghent instinct, essay 1,812, raised to its highest form: the refusal to gloat in the documentation.

Five days. That is the interval this essay turns on, and I will keep the account of it brief and exact, as the register requires. On the evening of April 11, Lincoln spoke from a White House window about reconstruction, and said aloud, for the first time in public, that he favoured the vote for at least some Black men — the freedmen who had fought, the educated. In the crowd below stood a twenty-six-year-old actor named John Wilkes Booth, who heard the sentence, understood the arithmetic — the counted becoming counters — and told a companion that this was the last speech the man would ever give. Three days later, on the evening of Good Friday, April 14, at Ford’s Theatre, Booth shot the president in the head from behind, and Lincoln died the next morning in a boarding house across the street, in a bed too short for him. I set down the plain sequence because the sequence is the point: he was not killed for the war, which was over, nor for emancipation, which was accomplished. He was killed, on the evidence of his killer’s own stated trigger, for a franchise proposal — for suggesting that the men lately counted as three-fifths for their masters’ benefit might now count as one, each, for their own. The first American president to be assassinated was murdered over the arithmetic of the ballot. This series has spent eighteen hundred essays establishing that the count is never merely the count. April 14, 1865, is the entry where that finding stops being an aphorism.

And the fraction itself? It was already dying when its author’s republic buried him. The Thirteenth Amendment had passed Congress on January 31, 1865 — Lincoln pressing for the votes with every instrument a president holds — and it was ratified in December, eight months after his death: slavery abolished, and with it the three-fifths clause rendered a dead letter, formally superseded when the Fourteenth Amendment rewrote the apportionment arithmetic three years on. Four million human beings moved, in the space of that ratification, from the property columns to the person columns — the largest single reclassification in the history of counting, the fraction retired, five-fifths at last, at least on the page. I am obliged, because this series corrects for premature triumph, to note what the following century did to the page: the counting of those citizens — their votes suppressed, their districts redrawn, their names purged from rolls — became the long unfinished war after the war, and it is not finished now, and I cover its current engagements under other datelines more weeks than not. The fraction was retired. The instinct that wrote it was not. It merely changed columns, and it is the most patient adversary in these files.

There is no full Dior section in this essay; the register does not permit one, and the veterans know the convention by now. I will say only that she was present for the writing of the Second Inaugural paragraphs, chin on knee, and that when I read the lash-and-sword sentence aloud to the empty room — a thing I do, testing weight — she raised her head at whatever she heard in the voice, and kept it raised until I went on. Some accounts are kept off the books. That one is now on them, I suppose. Let it stand.

The count moves on, as it does — and now I must tell you where it is going, because the next number is the one I have been protecting longest of all. Essay 1867 is my country’s birthday: the year three British provinces, watching the republic to the south count its three-quarters of a million dead, decided that there had to be another way to build a country, and wrote peace, order, and good government where their neighbours had written happiness, and did addition — essay 1,707’s rarest operation — by committee, in winter, without a single battlefield. It is the most personal essay on the map save two, and those two follow close behind it, and I will write all three slowly, in my own time, at my own desk, which is to say: the next few entries will not be rushed for anyone, including the count. It has waited eighteen hundred and sixty-six essays. It can wait for me to get my country right.

Three-fifths, retired. Seven hundred words on the Capitol steps that entered a war as a debt. Twenty-eight thousand parole slips and a bed too short. April 1865 is the month the American ledger was audited in full, and the auditor’s fee, as it so often is in these files, was the auditor.

See you at 1867. I will bring my whole country with me.
