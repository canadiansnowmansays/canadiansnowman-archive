Last night, Trump got the kind of platform most leaders would kill for: a primetime emergency broadcast from the White House, C-SPAN carrying it live, the gold curtains and the presidential seal doing all the visual work a speech like this is supposed to do. The subject was election security. Somewhere in the middle of it, with a war entering its sixth straight day of strikes, a collapsed ceasefire, a naval blockade, and a diesel price crossing $5 a gallon, he found four words for Iran: “We are winning big in Iran.” No elaboration followed. The broadcast moved on.

That’s the whole week in miniature, and it’s worth sitting with before anything else. This is a government that will not stop talking about this war in bursts — Truth Social posts, radio hits, an aside to Fox News, a line dropped mid-sentence at the Army War College — and says almost nothing about it when actually handed the microphone on purpose. I closed Part LXXXVII asking where the off-ramp comes from when nobody can afford to build one. A week later, I have an answer, and it isn’t encouraging: nobody’s building one. They’re building strikes on bridges instead — the literal kind — and everything else this week has been noise around that one fact, including, apparently, an entire primetime address that found time for everything except it.

This is a long one. It has to be. Six days produced a formal death of a peace deal, a presidential reversal inside 24 hours, a mediator being mocked in his own capital, a threat against Saudi oil infrastructure, a released hostage, a stalled defense bill, and a billboard with the American president lying in a coffin. None of it happened in isolation.

I. The Ceasefire Dies Three Times

It’s worth being precise about how thoroughly dead the June memorandum actually is, because it didn’t collapse once — it collapsed in three separate, almost bureaucratic ways, days apart.

First, rhetorically. As Peter Baker reported for the New York Times, Trump told Hugh Hewitt’s radio audience the MOU “don’t mean much,” dismissing the very deal his own White House had called a “historic breakthrough” a month earlier. “It was sort of a test,” he said, “and they basically broke the deal... they are extremely unreliable people.”

Second, institutionally. Trump formally notified Congress on July 7 that hostilities had resumed — a letter obtained by the Times — triggering a war-powers fight that was already simmering. Both chambers had already voted to direct him to end the war or seek authorization; he maintains he doesn’t need it.

Third, legislatively, and on Iran’s side this time. The Times’ live coverage of Iran’s escalating threats reported that roughly 180 members of Iran’s Parliament formally declared “there is no longer any memorandum of understanding,” accusing Washington of violating it, and called for retaliation, a pause in diplomacy, and legislation to formalize Iran’s own administration of the strait. That’s not a spokesman’s talking point. That’s a legislature.

Three governments, three different mechanisms, all arriving at the same conclusion within one week: the deal Trump signed at Versailles is dead, and everyone involved has now said so through their own institutional channels.

II. The Toll That Lasted a Day

If you want the cleanest single artifact of how incoherent this administration’s Iran policy has become, look at the 20 percent toll.

Monday: Trump announces the U.S. will become “THE GUARDIAN OF THE HORMUZ STRAIT,” charging a 20 percent fee on all cargo transiting the strait — the exact policy his own Secretary of State had called illegal weeks earlier. Michael Crowley and Edward Wong, reporting for the Times, laid out the contradiction plainly: “No country is allowed to charge tolls or fees on an international waterway,” Rubio had said in June. Vance had said the same. The U.S. had signed a joint GCC statement rejecting tolls outright.

Tuesday: reversal. As Anushka Patil and Yan Zhuang reported, Trump scraps the fee, replacing it with vague, undefined “Trade and Investment Deals” from unnamed Gulf states — no mechanism, no countries confirmed, no numbers. “I don’t think anybody should be able to charge a fee for the strait,” he said, the day after proposing exactly that.

Iran’s foreign minister Abbas Araghchi didn’t miss the opening. “POTUS is absolutely right,” he posted, in Trump’s own capitalization style. “Iran has always been the GUARDIAN of the Strait and will remain so FOREVER. 20% is of course too much. We will be fair.” Tehran didn’t need to argue against the toll. It just had to agree with the principle and let Washington’s own contradiction do the work.

Robert Kagan’s assessment cuts clean: “Trump undermines whatever legitimacy we could have claimed. We were posturing as the defender of a global public good. We ask the Europeans to take huge risks to help us gain control of the strait so we can charge them for it?” The IMO passed a formal resolution the same week reasserting free passage — a rebuke aimed at both governments simultaneously.

III. The Threat Became the Target

Tuesday also brought Trump’s clearest statement yet of intent, as the Times’ coverage of his renewed civilian-infrastructure threats documented: “Next week, it gets really bad for them, because next week comes the power plants, next week comes the bridges.” Iran’s military spokesman Ebrahim Zolfaghari answered in kind: “All infrastructure in the region will be crushed” if Washington followed through. Both threats describe conduct that could be considered a war crime under international law.

It didn’t take a week. By Thursday night, Iranian state media reported two bridges struck near Bandar Khamir — the Kahurestan and Gariveh bridges, on the road linking Bandar Abbas to Lar and Shiraz — killing at least three and wounding nine, alongside a railway junction outside Bandar Abbas splitting freight and passenger lines toward the country’s largest commercial port. The Times’ Friday live coverage reported the list had grown further: bridges, rail lines, power and water facilities, and a port. Kuwait took its own hit that same day — a power plant and water desalination facility damaged by Iranian retaliation, fires set, generators knocked out.

Here’s the detail that should stop you cold. U.S. Central Command’s Friday statement described the strikes as hitting “dozens of Iranian military targets such as coastal surveillance and air defense sites, military logistics infrastructure and maritime capabilities.” No mention of civilian infrastructure. At all. Iranian officials say bridges and a wheat silo and a water plant were hit earlier in the week too; CENTCOM denied hitting the wheat silo or water facility specifically, insisting its targets were military. Either the Pentagon doesn’t consider bridges worth acknowledging, or it isn’t acknowledging them on purpose. Friday's confirmed toll: eight killed, twenty wounded, per Iran's state news agency — including three civilians killed in the Bandar Khamir bridge strike specifically, one of them, per a photo posted by Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi, appearing to be a child. IRNA put the cumulative death toll from U.S. strikes since the start of July at 38. Nobody in Washington has confirmed or denied the specific sites, though CENTCOM did confirm separately destroying surveillance towers at the ports of Chabahar and Shahid Kalantari — infrastructure it was willing to claim, unlike the bridges.

IV. The Silence at the Pentagon, the Megaphone at the White House

While bridges were coming down, the Defense Secretary’s public agenda ran to testosterone testing for troops and a new task force to hunt down leakers. Helene Cooper’s reporting for the Times lays out the gap in stark numbers: the Pentagon held more than thirty press briefings in the first five weeks of the 2003 Iraq invasion. This war has had eight in six weeks. The last substantive strategy briefing was in May, about an escort mission — Project Freedom — that Trump personally abandoned within 48 hours after Saudi Arabia refused U.S. forces its airspace.

Into that vacuum steps the president himself, doing the Pentagon’s talking for it: “We’re going to hit them very hard tonight, we’re going to hit them hard tomorrow, and there’s not a damn thing they can do about it... They have nothing going other than they have big mouths.” Days later, at the U.S. Army War College of all places, he went further, framing the entire war as a coin flip he hadn’t called yet: “We’ll find out whether or not we settle with them or we just finish them off.” A Pentagon spokesman’s defense of the silence — that Central Command has provided “comprehensive, real-time updates” — doesn’t survive contact with the actual math. This isn’t a wartime government explaining its strategy. It’s a government letting one man narrate a war in place of having one.

V. Existential War, Open Door

Iran’s own contradictions sharpened on cue. Wednesday brought the first public address from Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf — Parliament speaker, top negotiator, both feared by and answerable to hardliners — since the funeral. Farnaz Fassihi’s reporting on the speech captured him calling it an “existential war,” accusing Washington of seeking regime change, and in the same breath insisting “negotiations at this stage do not amount to capitulation... Alongside war, they form part of the strategy of resistance.” He acknowledged being a target of hard-line attacks for negotiating at all, but said he did it under the new supreme leader’s guidance — guidance Mojtaba Khamenei has still never delivered in his own voice, in public, to his own people.

Lawmaker Mohammad Reza Bahonar put the same posture more bluntly Friday: "Dialogue and negotiation is also a continuation of the war and part of it." Not a hedge — a doctrine. Talking and shooting aren't opposites in Tehran's current framing. They're the same instrument.

The contradiction runs deeper than Ghalibaf alone. Not long before his speech, Iran’s own foreign ministry spokesman said the country had no intention of returning to talks. One government, two irreconcilable public positions, hours apart. Analyst Gregory Brew’s read holds: Ghalibaf is “trying to unite the battlefield and the negotiating table,” which is another way of saying Iran hasn’t decided which one it’s actually standing on.

Washington offered its own version of the same move. Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt told reporters Iran “very much continues to talk to the United States... because they are suffering devastating blows” — an official confirming back-channel contact exists, on the same day CENTCOM’s strike list said nothing about the bridges Iran says had just fallen.

VI. The Coffin in Enghelab Square

Some images do more work than any paragraph of analysis. Wednesday, a building-sized billboard appeared in Tehran’s Enghelab Square: Trump’s body, eyes and mouth closed, resting in an open black coffin, hands folded over a red tie and a protruding stomach. White chalk-style graffiti sprawled across the lower half read “We Will Kill Trump,” in Persian and English, dedicated “In memory of Minab’s children” — the elementary school killed by a U.S. targeting error on the war’s opening day. Ordinary street life moved underneath it: cyclists, a motorbike delivery driver, pedestrians on their way somewhere, treating state-sponsored assassination fantasy as background scenery for a Wednesday commute.

This is the same square where, in January, a different billboard showed a strike on an American warship leaving a wake shaped like the stripes of the U.S. flag. Threatening imagery there is routine. What’s new is the target, the specificity, and the fact that it now sits directly beneath the same vengeance rhetoric coming out of Iran’s own senior clerics and its supreme leader’s first public words.

VII. The Mediators Nobody’s Listening To

Three separate countries have tried and failed to insert themselves into this week’s chaos, and it’s worth naming all three, because their failure is its own story.

Qatar, the primary mediator, had its foreign minister meet Iran’s Araghchi mid-week — officially to pay respects after the death of the former emir, but also plainly an attempt to keep some channel open. Qatar also got struck directly by Iran this week and had its own airbase, Al Udeid, claimed as an Iranian target — the mediator is now also a combatant, which makes mediating rather difficult.

Pakistan, which helped broker the original ceasefire, is faring even worse. Elian Peltier’s dispatch from Karachi captured spokesman Tahir Andrabi’s understatement — the deal is facing “challenges” — which barely registers next to the reality: Pakistan’s own civilian and military leadership have spent months trying to protect a peace process now openly collapsing, while at home, rising prices and Iran-bound exports rotting at the border have left ordinary Pakistanis openly mocking the effort. The business community’s nickname for Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif — “Showbaz,” a jab implying performance over substance — tells you how much credibility the mediation currently has left domestically, let alone internationally.

Yemen’s fragile truce cracked open too, in a way that threatens an entirely new front. Shuaib Almosawa and Vivian Nereim’s reporting from Sana and Riyadh describes how Saudi Arabia struck Sana’s airport, reportedly to block an Iranian plane; the Houthis retaliated with missiles toward Saudi Arabia and, by week’s end, their leader Abdul-Malik al-Houthi threatened Saudi oil infrastructure directly if Riyadh escalated further — “all Saudi oil installations and highly vital facilities are targets for our missiles and drones.” A four-year truce, arguably the most durable peace this region has produced in a decade, is now genuinely at risk because of a dispute over a single flight carrying medical patients and funeral mourners.

VIII. The Sea, the Ground, One Woman’s Kitchen

The blockade is doing exactly what it was built to do, and the cost is landing on people with names now, not just tonnage figures. Ship traffic through Hormuz has cratered — eight vessels Thursday, the lowest in over a month, against a prewar daily average above 130. Diesel crossed $5 a gallon in the U.S. for the first time since June, up roughly a third since the war began. CENTCOM has redirected, disabled, or boarded a growing list of vessels defying the blockade, including physically boarding a Cook Islands-flagged tanker to enforce compliance, and firing on one near Kharg Island — the first commercial vessel targeted since the blockade resumed.

But the number that matters most this week came from a teacher in Bandar Abbas named Marzieh, who gave only her first name, for fear of reprisal. “The ground was shaking,” she said of the strikes near her city. “It is truly terrifying.” Daily power cuts now. Roads too damaged for people to travel. Fishing boats destroyed alongside the naval vessels the strikes were aimed at. “Our lives depend on the sea,” she said. “How are people supposed to survive now?”

That’s not a rhetorical question anyone in this war has bothered to answer.

IX. One Release, Several Still Held

Amid all of it, a rare cooperative note: Farnaz Fassihi’s separate report on Dena Karari — a dual American-Iranian citizen barred from leaving Iran since December 2024 on espionage charges — confirmed her release and return to the U.S. Trump called it a “gesture of goodwill.” Worth sitting with the fuller picture, though: at least three other Americans remain detained in Iran, including Kamran Hekmati, a 70-year-old jeweler sentenced to two years for attending his son’s bar mitzvah in Israel, and journalist Reza Valizadeh, serving ten years on charges of collaborating with a hostile government. Goodwill, in this war, still comes with an asterisk.

X. Washington’s Own War at Home

The war isn’t just being fought in the Gulf. It’s fracturing Congress too. Robert Jimison’s reporting from Capitol Hill documented Senate Democrats blocking the National Defense Authorization Act outright this week — a bill that almost never fails to pass with bipartisan support — specifically over Trump’s conduct of the Iran war. Schumer called supporting it “a permission slip for that recklessness.” Duckworth was blunter: “not strategy; it’s a recipe for a forever war.” Even Republican Bill Cassidy said the war “needs to end” and called for another classified briefing. A defense bill authorizing over a trillion dollars in Pentagon spending is currently stuck, because nobody in the Senate can agree on how to talk about the war it would help fund.

XI. Four Terrible Choices

Brynn Tannehill’s Atlantic analysis this week lays out, more starkly than anything else published so far, exactly how few paths remain open to Washington — and how bad every single one of them is. Stay the course: keep bombing, keep failing to move Iran’s position, the way an eerily similar incremental strategy failed to move Hanoi’s in Vietnam — a war Tannehill notes is also draining American munitions stockpiles the Pentagon says will take at least three years to replace. Make Iran unlivable: target the water and electrical infrastructure Iran’s own leadership calls existentially vulnerable, the way the U.S. targeted Japan’s civilian infrastructure at the end of World War II, a strategy that starved millions. Declare victory and go home, the way the collapsed MOU already tried and failed to do. Or invade — something the U.S., per Tannehill, lacks the manpower and logistics to sustain, and which Trump has never fully ruled out.

Tannehill’s most chilling observation isn’t about Iran at all. It’s about who’s left inside the Pentagon to say no. Previous administrations, she notes, could rely on senior military lawyers to balk at deliberately targeting civilian infrastructure as the “cleanest” path to victory. This administration has purged many of them. The guardrail Tannehill describes isn’t hypothetical — it’s the same silence Helene Cooper documented from a different angle this same week. Nobody’s saying no, because increasingly, nobody whose job it was to say no is still in the room.

XII. What Four Diplomats Would Tell Both Men

Lara Jakes rounded up four veteran negotiators this week — spanning the Obama-era nuclear file and Republican administrations going back decades — and none of them are optimistic, though they don’t agree on why. Catherine Ashton says both sides need clarity on the actual endgame before talks mean anything. Federica Mogherini’s advice reads like a diplomat’s polite way of describing Trump’s entire week: “a more rational, consistent approach, less impulsive reactions and random contradictory public statements.” Elliott Abrams — no dove, veteran of three Republican administrations — argues against rushing back to any table at all, on the grounds that Iran emerged from the last one “feeling triumphant.” Condoleezza Rice’s advice is blunter still: let Iran “sit there and stew in their lousy economy.” Robert Malley warns that if talks resume, there’s no grand bargain coming, only “painstaking talks over every last clause,” which is exactly the kind of process an administration that “prizes speed and simplicity” is constitutionally unsuited to survive.

Four people who’ve spent careers negotiating with Tehran, and the only thing they agree on is that neither government currently knows what it actually wants badly enough to get it.

XIV. What This Actually Comes Down To

Every option Tannehill lays out ends in the same place: neither government can compel the other, and neither can walk away without looking like it lost. That was true a week ago. It’s truer now that the war has moved from missile sites to bridges, from soldiers to teachers describing shaking ground, from a Pentagon that briefs the press to one that doesn’t bother, from a single memorandum to three separate funerals for it.

I don’t think this war ends because someone finds the clever diplomatic formula four negotiators spent this week describing. I think it ends, if it ends, the way Tannehill’s framework suggests wars like this usually do — not with a deal, but with one side running out of something the other isn’t running out of. Right now, that’s an open question on both sides of the strait. Iran’s economy is bruised but functioning. America’s munitions stockpile has a three-year repair clock on it. Both governments are, this week, more publicly incoherent than they’ve been at any point in this series — Iran’s foreign ministry and its own chief negotiator contradicting each other within hours, Washington reversing a major policy within a day, a defense bill stalled over a war nobody in uniform will fully explain.

A war run this incoherently doesn’t usually end on purpose. It ends when the incoherence itself becomes unaffordable — to an economy, to an alliance, to a population asking, out loud now, how they’re supposed to survive.
