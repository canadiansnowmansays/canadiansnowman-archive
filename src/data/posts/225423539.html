On Sunday, November 15, 2026, my fight with the telecom industry went out on national radio. On Monday, November 16, 2026, the telecom industry announced it might be about to get considerably smaller. I would like to say the two events were connected. They were not. But they belong in the same essay, because together they show the gap between how Canadians are invited to think about their phone bills and how the people who send those bills actually think about them.

Start with the radio.

The Kvetch of Living

Jennifer Keene, senior producer of CBC Radio's Cost of Living, the programme hosted by Paul Haavardsrud, interviewed me on November 10, 2026 about my intervention in the CRTC's consumer-code proceeding. The segment aired five days later in an episode titled, with a cheerful Yiddish wink, the "Kvetch of Living." The premise was that listeners had sent in their pet peeves and the show would air them. No gripe, the episode description promised, was too petty.

My segment arrived after a listener who resents paying soda-pop prices for sparkling water, and a man who dislikes the new voice on Google Maps. It was followed by a woman who objects to tipping on sales tax and a massage therapist asking for an online review halfway through the massage. I was, in other words, filed under the miscellaneous drawer of modern irritation, somewhere between fizzy water and an untimely request for five stars.

I should be fair before I am anything else. The show told my story accurately. Fido billed my partner the full $42.38 for a month of service he had used for four days. Once he had ported his number to Koodo, the chatbot would not let a former customer through to a human being, and nobody would call back. The amount actually owed was about $4.10. When I refused to pay the inflated bill on time, Fido added a $1.22 late fee calculated on the money we never owed. The program got all of that right. It also made the central comparison cleanly: we pay for hydro, water and gas after we have used them, in arrears, and those are essential services too. That is the heart of my case, and it reached a national audience. I am grateful for that.

But the frame matters, and the frame was condescending.

Time on his hands

The reporter introduced me by explaining that most of us complain to friends or rant on Reddit, but occasionally someone comes along with grit, determination and “the time on their hands that comes from being retired.” That was the credential. Not eighteen years in the Office of the President at Rogers, handling the escalated complaints that came up from the front lines and down from regulators, the media and the executive floor. That fact did make it into the piece, a little later, delivered as a punchline: get this, he dealt with customer complaints.

I have been retired since 2021, so I recognise the genre. In Canada, a man who spent nearly two decades inside a carrier’s complaint machinery is an expert right up until his last day of work. The morning after, he becomes a pensioner with a grievance and an afternoon to fill. The expertise does not evaporate at the retirement party. It just stops being mentioned in the same breath.

The episode closed my segment by calling my supporters, as near as I can make out from the recording, “a small contingent of annoyed people.” I would gently point out that the small contingent has a paper trail. The Commission received roughly 140 interventions in this proceeding. Of those I have read, well over 60 support ending advance billing or its worst effects, and I have found one clear dissent. The Public Interest Advocacy Centre, which does this for a living, quoted my intervention by name in its own filing and cited other consumers who raised the same issue independently. The CRTC itself confirmed in a procedural letter on July 30, 2026 that advance billing is within the scope of the proceeding. None of that made the episode.

Nor did the most telling number of all. According to the Commission for Complaints for Telecom-television Services, its 2024–25 annual report logged 23,647 complaints, up 16 per cent, and 46 per cent of them were about billing. Billing is not the miscellaneous drawer. It is the biggest drawer in the cabinet. Calling it a petty grievance is like calling the weather a minor feature of February.

The case against me, and why it does not hold

What troubled me more than the tone was that the arguments against my position were given the authority of experts and then left standing.

The show spoke to David Dunbar, a former Competition Bureau official now practising law in Toronto. His point was that nothing in law requires service before money, and that we pay up front for plenty of things. His example was the grocery store: you do not take your groceries to the car and wander back in five minutes later to pay.

No, you do not. You pay at the till and walk out with the groceries in the same minute. Nobody at Loblaws asks you to pay in November for December’s groceries, then charges you for the whole of December when you move away on the fourth. The grocery store is a simultaneous exchange. Advance billing is a loan from the customer to the carrier, renewed every month, with no interest paid to the lender and a late fee charged to him if he disputes the terms.

The other examples offered were mortgages and insurance. Canadian mortgage interest is generally paid in arrears: your payment covers the interest on money you have already had the use of. Insurance is paid in advance, but cancel a policy and the insurer returns the unused portion of the premium. Both examples, examined for more than a sentence, argue my side.

Then came the industry’s own position, via the Canadian Telecommunications Association: telecom is really a subscription service, rather like Netflix, and billing in advance spares carriers the cost of chasing delinquent accounts.

The Netflix comparison deserves a moment of our time, if only because it collapses so quickly. When you cancel Netflix, you keep watching until the end of the period you have paid for. Netflix does not bill you for a month after you have gone, and it does not add a late fee to a debt you do not owe. More to the point, Netflix is not licensed under the Telecommunications Act, does not use public spectrum, and nobody has ever needed it to call an ambulance. A phone line is closer to the hydro meter than to a streaming queue, and the hydro meter bills in arrears.

As for bad debt, every utility in the country manages it while billing after the fact, with deposits, collection rules and disconnection procedures. And my intervention offered a fallback that costs the carriers almost nothing: a final bill prorated to the day the service ends, and late fees calculated only on what is actually owed. Even a retired regulatory director from the industry side, in a long exchange we have had on LinkedIn, conceded that the fallback seems reasonable. The radio audience heard the objection. It did not hear the answer.

Monday morning

Then Monday arrived, and with it a reminder of whose interests the industry actually organises itself around.

The Globe and Mail reported on November 16, 2026 that Quebecor, Canada’s fourth-largest telecom company, had made a non-binding $31-billion proposal to acquire Telus. As The Canadian Press laid out, Telus shareholders would receive $6.35 in cash and 0.17 of a Quebecor class B share for each Telus share, which Quebecor values at $19.45. Bloomberg calculated that as a 60 per cent premium and one of the largest domestic mergers in Canadian history. The Globe noted that Telus shares had fallen more than 40 per cent over the past year, and that the deal would be the second major test of federal competition law in five years.

The first test, of course, was Rogers and Shaw, announced in March 2021, the year I left Rogers. That deal went through only after Shaw’s Freedom Mobile was sold to Quebecor’s Vidéotron as the remedy that would preserve a fourth national carrier. When then-Industry Minister François-Philippe Champagne approved the licence transfer on March 31, 2023, he expected Freedom to bring prices down significantly and to mirror Vidéotron’s record in Quebec, as Yahoo Finance Canada reported at the time. Pierre Karl Péladeau promised that Freedom and Vidéotron would become the fourth national carrier Canadians had been waiting for.

Three and a half years later, the fourth carrier proposes to swallow the third.

The arithmetic is stark. According to a compilation of third-quarter results by the advocacy group Build Canada, Rogers holds 32 per cent of the national carriers’ mobile subscribers, Bell 28, Telus 28 and Quebecor 12. Combine the last two and the company that was brought in to discipline the Big Three would become the biggest of three, with 40 per cent. Build Canada’s chart, drawing on federal price comparisons, puts Canada’s average monthly price for a comparable plan at $74, behind only Japan in the countries it compared, and far above the United Kingdom, Italy or France.

Everyone talks about value. Nobody talks about the bill.

Péladeau told The Canadian Press that the transaction is not based on raising revenue, and “certainly not based on increasing prices.” He framed the merger as a defence against foreign players taking market share. Telus’s new chief executive, Victor Dodig, said the offer “doesn’t nearly reflect the value of Telus,” and called regulation the largest hurdle.

Telus’s board went further in its formal statement, filed with American securities regulators. It objected that Telus shareholders would own most of the economic value but hold a minority of the votes. It called the combined company’s projected debt load too high. It cited spectrum policy, the conditions attached to recent wireless consolidation and the amended Competition Act as sources of serious approval risk, and complained that Quebecor had not disclosed what remedies it would accept.

Read the statement from the customer’s side of the counter and something is missing. Customers appear only in passing, as people who would face uncertainty during a long review. Prices, service and billing practices do not appear at all. One side is arguing about premiums and the other about governance and leverage. The people who pay the bills are not a party to the conversation.

That is not a scandal. It is simply how a takeover battle is conducted. But it is precisely why the CRTC’s consumer code matters, and why the advance-billing question is not petty.

Why the two stories are one story

Advance billing survives for one reason: there is no competitive pressure to end it. The Canadian Telecommunications Association calls it a long-standing industry practice, and that is accurate. Every major carrier does it, so switching carriers does not let you escape it. In the CRTC proceeding, no industry filing I have read, including Quebecor’s, proposed ending it.

In a market of four, there is at least a theoretical chance that the smallest player, hungry for customers, breaks ranks and offers something the others will not, such as billing only for what you have used. That is what a maverick is for. In a market of three, with the former maverick now the largest company in the country, that chance shrinks towards nothing. When competition cannot fix a practice, regulation is the only remedy left.

There is also a personal footnote. Koodo is a Telus brand. If the deal proceeds, the carrier I moved my partner to, in order to escape Fido’s billing, would end up owned by the company that was supposed to keep Fido honest. I took my complaint to the CRTC as a former Fido household. I may arrive at the hearing as a future Quebecor one.

What the code can do that a merger review cannot

The CRTC will not decide whether Quebecor buys Telus. That belongs to the Competition Bureau and to the federal minister who controls spectrum licences, and it will take months, perhaps years. The CRTC’s public hearing on harmonizing the consumer protection codes opens in Gatineau on November 30, 2026, and its rules will apply to whoever ends up owning the networks.

That is the point I wish the radio segment had reached. Mergers change the name at the top of the bill. The consumer code decides what the bill is allowed to say. If the industry consolidates from four national carriers to three, the protections written into that code become the only leverage most customers have left.

So I will accept the label. I am retired, I have time on my hands, and I am spending it on something that affects every Canadian with a phone. If that makes me a kvetch, I am in good company: roughly 140 interventions, a national consumer advocacy group, and more than 23,000 complaints a year that customers took far enough to file with the CCTS. A small contingent, perhaps. But the bill comes to all of us, and it comes a month early.
