Every prior instalment of this series has been building, in one way or another, toward this one. Part I traced how a Federal Reserve chairman’s silence about interest rates moves the term premium, and how the term premium moves mortgage pricing more directly than it moves almost anything else in a household budget. Part II traced a year of denial about affordability generally. Part III and Part IV traced two specific, concrete costs — tariffs and groceries — that show up weekly or with each renovation. This instalment turns to the line item that dwarfs both: housing, the single largest expense in most American budgets, and the one where the abstractions of monetary policy translate most directly and most painfully into a specific number on a mortgage statement or a lease renewal.

Where rates actually sit, and why “coming down” keeps not happening

The 30-year fixed mortgage rate averaged 6.65 percent as of August 20, 2026, according to Freddie Mac’s weekly survey — down slightly from the previous week’s 6.67 percent, but essentially unchanged from a year earlier, when the same measure stood at 6.58 percent. Multiple forecasters, including Wells Fargo and the Mortgage Bankers Association, expect rates to hold in a similar range through 2027 rather than fall meaningfully, with Wells Fargo’s economics group specifically noting that recent geopolitical events — the Iran conflict traced elsewhere in this series — have added fresh upward pressure on the same yields that determine mortgage pricing.

This is worth pausing on, because it is a direct, real-time confirmation of the mechanism described in Part I. Mortgage rates track the 10-year Treasury yield far more closely than they track the Fed’s own overnight rate, and the “generational reset” in bond yields — driven by fiscal deficits, AI-related borrowing competing with Treasuries for investor demand, and geopolitical risk — has kept mortgage rates elevated even as the headline Fed funds rate has moved only modestly. Buyers waiting for rates to fall back toward the 2010s-era norm have now been waiting through an entire presidential term with no meaningful relief, and Wells Fargo’s own forecast suggests that wait may extend through 2027 as well.

What that means in a monthly payment

The abstraction becomes concrete quickly. Financial analysts calculating the “rent versus buy” math for 2026 have pointed out that a mortgage priced around 6.4 percent moves a typical principal-and-interest payment to roughly $2,400 a month — an increase of nearly fifty percent compared with the payments many buyers locked in during the pandemic years, when rates below 3 percent, and in some cases closer to 2.5 percent, were widely available. That is not a rounding error in a monthly budget. It is, as one analysis put it plainly, the difference between having room to save, invest, handle an emergency, or simply live without constant financial stress, and not having that room at all.

The scale of the national picture makes the squeeze clearer still. With median household income sitting a little above $85,000 and the median home price around $391,000, the standard affordability benchmark — that housing costs should not exceed 30 percent of income, a figure some more conservative financial planners prefer to see closer to 25 percent — is difficult to hit for a meaningful share of buyers at current rates. The National Association of Home Builders estimates that roughly sixty-five percent of American households cannot currently afford the median-priced home at prevailing rates and prices, a figure that captures, in a single number, just how far the current mortgage environment has drifted from anything resembling broad-based affordability.

Homeowners versus renters: two different kinds of exposure

It is worth being precise about who is actually exposed to this squeeze, because “housing costs are up” describes two meaningfully different situations depending on which side of a lease a household sits on. Existing homeowners with fixed-rate mortgages locked in during the low-rate years are, for the moment, largely insulated — their payment does not move regardless of what the Fed or the bond market does, a genuine structural advantage this series should not understate. The exposure for that group is deferred rather than eliminated: it arrives whenever they eventually need to sell, refinance, or move, at which point the current rate environment becomes their rate environment too, whatever it happens to be at that moment. This deferred exposure is not a small population, either — millions of American homeowners took out or refinanced mortgages during the historically low rate years of 2020 through 2022, and each one of them faces the same reckoning eventually, whether through a life event that forces a move or, in more and more cases, an adjustable-rate feature or a balloon provision that forces the question regardless of personal timing.

Renters and prospective first-time buyers face the exposure immediately and directly. Realtor.com’s rental data for the fifty largest US metro areas found renters currently saving an average of $920 a month compared with the cost of buying an equivalent home — a gap so wide in some markets, like Austin, that buying costs more than double what renting does for a comparable property. That gap is, in one sense, a rational market signal steering people toward the more affordable option. But it is also a signal that homeownership — historically the primary vehicle through which American households build generational wealth — is being priced further out of reach for exactly the buyers who most need that vehicle. Realtor.com’s own research found that buyers who purchase a first home by age thirty accumulate roughly 22.5 percent higher net worth by midlife than those who wait until their forties, which means the current rate environment is not merely inconvenient for younger, first-time buyers — it may be quietly reshaping the long-term wealth trajectory of an entire cohort.

Renters, meanwhile, are not simply waiting out the storm in a stable position of their own. Rents are forecast to rise roughly 2 to 3 percent nationally by the end of 2026, broadly in line with overall inflation, as demand for apartments increases while supply falls — partly a function of would-be buyers who cannot afford current mortgage rates staying in the rental market longer than they otherwise would, adding demand pressure to a market already tightening on its own terms. Many renters already spend more than 30 percent of income on housing, a threshold considered a meaningful financial strain, and unlike a homeowner with a fixed-rate mortgage, a renter has no way to lock in that cost against future increases.

The renting-versus-buying gap offers renters an opportunity, on paper, to bank the difference toward an eventual down payment — the theory being that a household saving nearly a thousand dollars a month compared with an equivalent mortgage payment could accumulate meaningful savings within a few years. In practice, that theory assumes the savings are actually being saved rather than absorbed by the grocery and tariff-driven cost increases traced in the previous two instalments of this series, which for many households is not a safe assumption. A gap between renting and buying costs is only a path toward ownership if a household has enough slack elsewhere in its budget to bank the difference — and the evidence assembled so far in this series suggests that slack has been shrinking, not growing, for exactly the households most likely to be renting in the first place.

A market that isn’t behaving the way it used to

One of the more disorienting features of the current housing market, worth noting because it complicates the tidy narrative that rate cuts alone would fix the affordability problem, is that the usual relationship between Fed policy, inflation, and mortgage pricing has become less predictable. Mortgage applications fell nearly 11 percent in a single week last spring as rates moved higher alongside Treasury yields — a move driven not by traditional inflation data but by the same geopolitical uncertainty and capital-market dynamics traced throughout this series. Analysts have noted a growing disconnect between housing fundamentals, which have not materially changed, and pricing, which continues to be buffeted by forces well outside the housing market itself: AI-driven capital competition for Treasury demand, an unresolved war affecting energy costs, and a fiscal picture that keeps pushing yields upward regardless of what the underlying economy is actually doing.

This is the housing-market expression of exactly the dynamic Kevin Warsh’s Jackson Hole speech was meant to address, and exactly the reason this series began with his dilemma rather than treating it as a purely technocratic concern. A chairman who says too little, for too long, about how he reads these cross-currents does not remove uncertainty from the mortgage market — he leaves lenders and buyers to price in the uncertainty themselves, typically in the direction of caution, which in a mortgage market means higher rates than the underlying fundamentals alone would justify.

There is a further complication specific to home prices themselves, separate from mortgage rates. The S&P Cotality Case-Shiller Home Price Index shows home values have appreciated roughly 17 percent since the start of 2022 — despite, not because of, the sharp rise in mortgage rates over the same period, a combination that runs against the textbook expectation that higher borrowing costs should cool price growth. Forecasters expect that appreciation to slow rather than reverse: Realtor.com projects existing home prices rising a further 2.2 percent in 2026, and Zillow projects a more modest 1.2 percent, with some individual markets potentially seeing outright declines. Slower appreciation is a genuine, if modest, improvement for buyers. It does not undo three years of accumulated price growth stacked on top of a doubled mortgage rate — the two-part cost increase that defines the current affordability crisis is a home that costs meaningfully more, financed at a rate that costs meaningfully more, and slower future appreciation changes only the first half of that equation.
