At about ten in the morning on March 1, 2026, the second day of the war with Iran, a drone shaped like an arrowhead dropped out of a clear sky over Port Shuaiba in Kuwait. Its propeller was turned by the kind of engine you would find in a dirt bike or a lawnmower, and it made a high, rising whine that Ukrainians compare to a Vespa revving in the street. It went through the roof of an operations centre where Army Reserve soldiers from the 103rd Expeditionary Sustainment Command were coordinating supplies, and it exploded.

Six Americans died, among them Captain Cody Khork, Sergeant First Class Noah Tietjens, Sergeant First Class Nicole Amor, Sergeant Declan Coady and Chief Warrant Officer 3 Robert Marzan. More than two dozen were wounded, several critically. It remains the deadliest single attack on American troops in the war.

The weapon was a Shahed, and on November 15, 2026, Simon Shuster reported for The Atlantic that the Pentagon had been told how to stop it. According to more than a dozen current and former American and Ukrainian officers, Army commanders in Europe spent the months before the war urging their colleagues in the Middle East to field a cheap Ukrainian interceptor that had all but neutralised the Shahed over Kyiv. They wrote reports. They sent them up the chain. The Pentagon ignored them. Several of the officers who raised the alarm have since been fired or pushed into early retirement.

This series has spent thirteen instalments on what Pete Hegseth has done. This one concerns something he declined to do, and what it cost.

A thermos with rotors

Russia bought its first Shaheds from Iran in 2022 and soon began building its own, faster and nastier. More than 6,000 were launched at Ukraine in the first ten months of 2024, often in swarms of several hundred a night. Each costs Russia roughly $35,000. A Patriot interceptor costs several million dollars a shot. No country can win that exchange for long, and Ukraine, which received its Patriots in small batches, could not afford to try.

So Ukrainian engineers built something cheaper. By the summer of 2025, several companies, among them a Kyiv firm called Skyfall, were producing interceptor drones with names like Octopus, Sting and Bullet. Shuster describes them as looking like a large thermos with rotors attached to the base. Each costs a few thousand dollars and can be launched from an empty field or the back of a pickup.

The other half of the system is almost embarrassingly simple. To find the Shaheds, Ukraine built a network of acoustic sensors that listen for that lawnmower whine. Some are nothing more than smartphones fixed to tall poles. Having spent my working life in telecommunications, I confess to a professional delight that one of the most effective air-defence networks on earth is built partly from handsets on sticks. When a microphone hears a Shahed, a crew sends up an interceptor to ram it. By official Ukrainian estimates, the system brought down Shaheds at a rate of better than 90 per cent.

The reports from the bowling alley

None of this was secret. The Security Assistance Group Ukraine, the American command that coordinates support for Kyiv, works out of a converted recreation centre at the Army base in Wiesbaden, Germany, with desks standing on old bowling lanes. Its officers toured Skyfall’s factory in the autumn of 2025 and began training on the interceptors. They asked the Ukrainians to write up what they had learned.

The result, dated December 2025, carried the title “Operational Experience in the Employment of UAV Interceptors in Countering Shahed-Type Aerial Threats.” Its cover showed an interceptor beside a Shahed with a bull’s-eye painted on its belly. It circulated inside the American military and among allies roughly two months before the war began.

By then General Chris Donahue, who led the Army in Europe, had already deployed the interceptors at American bases in Romania and Poland. He and his staff pressed their counterparts in the Middle East, where the Shahed threat seemed to them most obvious, to do the same. In early 2026 the Wiesbaden team wrote a second report, about twelve pages long, explaining how European Command had fielded the interceptors and how other commands could copy it. To get it the widest possible audience, the Army cleared it for public release. It was published on February 23, 2026. The war began five days later.

“This should all have been figured out beforehand,” one of the officers told Shuster. “How are we going to defend against Shaheds?”

“Why do we need it?”

The officers never received an explanation for why their advice was ignored. They suspected two reasons, and both will be familiar to readers of this series: arrogance and politics.

The politics began in the Oval Office on February 28, 2025, when Trump told Volodymyr Zelensky that Ukraine had no cards to play. Inside the administration, Ukraine was to be treated as a supplicant rather than an ally. “Hegseth did not want to hear the word Ukraine,” a senior Ukrainian official told Shuster. An American officer in Europe put it more vividly: raise Ukraine at the Pentagon, and “if you were lucky, you were met with a sneer.” Lloyd Austin had received weekly briefings from the Wiesbaden command on the war. Once Hegseth arrived, those briefings stopped, along with nearly all American military aid to Ukraine.

A deal for the United States to buy Ukraine’s most advanced equipment was negotiated in the autumn of 2025 and then shelved. One Ukrainian negotiator summarised Washington’s message as: “Know your place. We have the greatest military in the world. We don’t need anything from you.” The officers who tried to get the interceptors to the Gulf heard much the same thing from their own side. “The technology is so effective. It’s so cheap,” one told Shuster. But the officials in Washington, “because of arrogance, they said, Why do we need it?”

On March 1 they found out. In its response to the war, by some estimates, Iran launched more than 2,000 Shaheds at American bases, airports, logistics hubs and civilian targets from Saudi Arabia to the United Arab Emirates. The United States and its allies burned through most of their Patriots trying to stop them. At Port Shuaiba, the defences failed.

The soldiers who survived have been saying so for months. Major Stephen Ramsbottom, who had shrapnel removed from his head, told the Associated Press in October that the base should have been better defended. “This type of fight has been going on for five years in Ukraine, and our excuse is we didn’t expect it to happen to us,” he said. “That’s preposterous in my mind.” The AP’s account, based on interviews with 13 soldiers, describes a site that officers had warned was vulnerable, a radar that some of them doubted could see low-flying drones, and a final all-clear issued about 20 minutes before the drone struck. Lieutenant Colonel Tim Halbur remembers waking in the wreckage of his office with his uniform covered in blood and the bones of his right elbow falling away.

The audience of one, again

Part X of this series argued that Hegseth performs for a single reviewer. The Shahed story is that argument with a body count.

On March 5, 2026, the sixth day of the war, Zelensky offered to send Ukrainian equipment and experts to help the United States counter Iran’s drones. Trump told Reuters the same day that he would accept “any assistance from any country.” Ukrainian teams went to Saudi Arabia, Qatar and the United Arab Emirates. Then, on March 13, Trump told Brian Kilmeade on Fox News Radio: “No, we don’t need their help in drone defense. We know more about drones than anybody. We have the best drones in the world, actually.”

That was the review, and the Pentagon read it. “It’s the politics,” one of the Wiesbaden officers told Shuster. “If I’m Hegseth, I don’t know where Trump sits with Ukraine day to day. So I don’t necessarily want to go all in.” A secretary of defence whose first question is what the President thinks this week will not get ahead of the President on anything, least of all on a country the President has been taught to resent. The troops in the Gulf were left to wait for a decision that depended on a radio interview.

General Donahue did not wait. After Kuwait he gathered a few hundred of the interceptors deployed under his command in Europe and sent them to American bases in the Gulf. He also signed a series of confidential requests to Kyiv, including to Zelensky’s office, asking for more. A Ukrainian counterpart asked him whether he was afraid of what signing them might cost him. “He said: I don’t care. I just want to help some American soldiers.”

The best case for the Pentagon

The Pentagon’s answer deserves a fair hearing. Its spokesman, Sean Parnell, called it “disgusting” to suggest the military had been “somehow unprepared,” and said that Central Command had built “the most extensive air defense umbrella in the world over the Middle East.” Most of the Atlantic’s sources are anonymous, as officers under a secretary who polygraphs his staff to find leakers tend to be.

There is also a serious technical objection, and the Atlantic’s own reporting supplies it. In early March, Army Secretary Dan Driscoll, the one senior official willing to stick his neck out, called Eric Schmidt, the former Google chief executive whose drone company had built an interceptor called the Merops with Skyfall. Within days they had flown 13,000 of them to the Middle East on chartered aircraft, hoping to help reopen the Strait of Hormuz. According to two people familiar with the outcome, the Merops shot down exactly one Shahed. Gulf host countries were slow to approve them. More important, without Ukraine’s network of acoustic sensors, the interceptors could not find their targets. “We screwed it up,” a senior Ukrainian official said.

That is the strongest argument for the Pentagon: the interceptor is not a silver bullet, and a weapon shipped in a panic will fail. It is also the strongest argument against it. The Merops failed because it arrived in March without the sensors, the training and the host-nation approvals that the Wiesbaden reports, written in December and February, had set out in detail. Washington’s sceptics asked why the Army needed the system at all. The answer was in the second report, which went public five days before the war: too late to field in time, and months after the first report should have been heeded.

The purge of the people who were right

What happened next is the part that belongs to Hegseth most clearly. Those who saw the threat did not get a promotion. They got a farewell ceremony.

Donahue relinquished his command in early July 2026, a year after his own boss, General Christopher Cavoli, among the first American commanders to take Ukraine’s battlefield lessons seriously, had left NATO’s top command. General Curtis Buzzard, who led the Wiesbaden command and made more than 30 trips to Ukraine, announced his retirement in the summer. Driscoll, who with Donahue had founded the Army’s first experimental drone unit, the 600-soldier Unmanned Assault Battalion, resigned at the start of September after 18 months of clashes with Hegseth. In August, General Christopher LaNeve, the Hegseth ally now running the Army, had announced that the battalion’s drone mission would end under a “back to basics” approach. As Military Times reported on September 3, a bipartisan group of lawmakers demanded to know how the Army intended to keep learning from Ukraine without it.

When Shuster visited Wiesbaden in September, the officers seemed to be in mourning. At the entrance to the base hang portraits of seven commanders. Donahue’s had been replaced with a black square.

The next drone

The story is not over, because the drones have changed again. In the spring of 2026 Russia began flying a jet-powered Shahed, fast enough to outrun the first generation of Ukrainian interceptors. Ukraine’s engineers took a few months to answer it. In late September Zelensky announced that Ukraine had deployed an interceptor that could bring down the jet version.

Iran has already used jet-powered Shaheds against American forces, which have relied on Patriots, fighter jets and other expensive hardware to shoot them down. The United States has no deal to buy the new Ukrainian interceptor. By the latest estimates, it has spent nearly three-quarters of its Patriot stockpile since February 28, and rebuilding it will take years. Eight months after Port Shuaiba, according to Shuster, the American military has still not fielded the kind of sensor-and-interceptor network that Ukraine uses every night.

The officers in Wiesbaden fear the Pentagon is about to make the same mistake twice. They have reason to. The command that wrote the reports has been gutted, the battalion built to learn from Ukraine has been sent back to the infantry, and the secretary who ignored the first warning is still in his office, by the grace of the same audience of one.

In October, the AP found the families of the six soldiers killed at Port Shuaiba still waiting for the Army to brief them on its investigation. Congress has had no clear account of the strike either. That gap is already an entry in The Reckoning Ledger, the companion to this series, under the heading of a full accounting of American casualties. The Atlantic’s reporting adds a question the ledger will now carry: not only how the six died, but who was told how to prevent it, and when.

Hegseth’s motto is “maximum lethality.” It turns out that lethality is not only a matter of what you can do to the enemy. It is also a matter of what you are prepared to learn from your friends. In Kyiv, people no longer flinch at the sound of a Vespa in the sky. American soldiers in the Gulf, Shuster writes, still keep close to their shelters, listening for the whine of a lawnmower engine.
