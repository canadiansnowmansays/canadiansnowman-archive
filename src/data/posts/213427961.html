Part XIII of this series, published July 27, 2026, laid out the mechanical case for a Tesla-SpaceX merger: a stock-swap structure that would resolve the governance and compensation problem created when a Delaware court voided Musk’s earlier Tesla pay package, achieved through SpaceX’s Class B share structure, which concentrates roughly 82 percent of voting power in ways a court would find much harder to second-guess than a public company’s shareholder-approved milestones. That instalment treated the merger as a plausible resolution to a legal problem. This one examines a related but distinct claim now circulating in investor commentary: that the specific milestones written into Musk’s compensation packages at both companies were never intended to be reachable at all, and that their unreachability is itself the tell.

The milestones in question

The claim, as it has been laid out by at least one investor commentator tracking both companies closely, concerns compensation structures at Tesla and SpaceX that were restructured between November 2025 and February 2026 — a roughly nine-month-old arrangement as of this writing. The Tesla-side milestone, on this account, requires progress toward putting a million people on Mars; the SpaceX-side milestone requires reaching some enormous threshold of orbital AI compute, specified in the hundred-terabyte range at a scale that would require the kind of orbital data-centre buildout this series will examine in the next instalment, on Starbase Louisiana’s actual purpose. Neither milestone, the argument runs, is remotely achievable on any timeline relevant to a compensation package, which are typically designed to reward performance over a period of years, not centuries. A million people on Mars, at any technology roadmap currently public, is a multi-generational undertaking. A hundred-terabyte orbital compute threshold requires launch infrastructure — the very infrastructure Starbase Louisiana was announced to provide — that does not yet exist and, on its own most optimistic public timeline, will not exist in meaningfully useful form before 2029 at the earliest.

Why deliberately impossible milestones would make sense

Here is where the argument, whatever one thinks of the specific numbers, becomes genuinely interesting rather than merely speculative. If Musk already controls a large enough share of Tesla’s voting power to dominate ordinary shareholder decisions, an ordinary reader might ask why an additional compensation package, particularly one with unreachable milestones, would be worth the legal and reputational cost of pursuing at all. The answer offered by this theory is that voting-rights leverage behaves differently in a merger than it does in an ordinary company. When two companies combine via a stock swap, an individual’s percentage ownership and voting power in the combined entity is not simply preserved from the larger of the two pre-merger stakes — it is diluted according to the relative size and terms of the deal, and a shareholder who currently dominates one company’s board could, in principle, end up with meaningfully less relative control over the merged entity than they had before, depending on exactly how the exchange ratio is set. A compensation package with milestones that are functionally impossible to satisfy through ordinary performance would, on this reading, exist specifically to preserve or restore that voting-rights position through the mechanics of the merger itself, rather than through any milestone actually being hit — the milestone’s role is structural, not motivational.

The honest caveats

I want to be unusually careful here, because this is the most speculative claim this series has examined in nineteen instalments, and the honest version of it should be labelled as such rather than smuggled in as established fact. Nobody outside the companies involved, and quite possibly nobody inside them either, has confirmed that the compensation restructuring was designed with a merger specifically in mind rather than as an ordinary, if unusually ambitious, incentive package. Executive compensation packages at fast-growing technology companies routinely include milestones that look implausible at the time they are written and are sometimes hit anyway — Tesla’s own history includes compensation targets that seemed absurd when set and were later achieved faster than sceptics expected, which is precisely why this theory is harder to dismiss out of hand than it might otherwise be, and also exactly why it should not be treated as confirmed. A theory that would explain the facts is not the same thing as a theory that has been shown to be the correct explanation of the facts, and readers of this series deserve that distinction stated plainly rather than blurred for narrative convenience.

The valuation gap the theory rests on

The commentator advancing this argument also ties it to a specific, checkable claim: that the merger has likely been delayed not by any legal or strategic obstacle but simply because the relative share prices of Tesla and SpaceX are not currently where either company would want them to be to execute an exchange ratio both sides find acceptable. On this account, the expectation following SpaceX’s public listing was that its shares would hold above roughly $200 while Tesla’s would trade well above $400, a combination that would have made a stock-swap merger considerably easier to structure and sell to shareholders on both sides. Instead, as this series documented in Part XIV, SpaceX shares fell below their IPO price as recently as August 19, 2026, and Tesla’s own valuation has been buffeted by exactly the kind of robotaxi-timeline uncertainty examined in Part XVI. If the theory is right, the delay in any formal merger announcement is not evidence against the underlying plan — it is evidence that the plan’s authors are simply waiting for a more favourable market moment to execute it, which is a less dramatic but considerably more mundane explanation than most merger speculation tends to offer.

The Delaware problem, revisited

It is worth returning briefly to why any of this machinery is necessary in the first place, because Part XIII’s original point bears repeating here. Musk’s earlier, enormous Tesla compensation package was voided by a Delaware court, which found problems with how it had been approved and disclosed to shareholders — a public company, incorporated in a jurisdiction with an unusually active and sceptical Court of Chancery, subject to shareholder derivative suits from any investor with standing and a grievance. SpaceX, by contrast, remains a private company, and its Class B share structure concentrates voting control in a way that makes an equivalent legal challenge dramatically harder to bring, because the ordinary shareholder-derivative machinery that produced the Delaware ruling against Tesla has much less purchase against a privately held company with a small, controlled shareholder base. A merger that folds Tesla’s public, court-scrutinised structure into SpaceX’s private, closely-held one would not merely resolve a compensation dispute — it would relocate Musk’s entire economic and voting interest in electric vehicles, autonomous driving, and now robotics into a corporate form considerably more insulated from the kind of judicial and shareholder oversight that has, however imperfectly, already once forced a correction. That is the structural stake underneath what might otherwise read as a dry corporate-finance story about exchange ratios and share prices.

What oversight bodies would actually need to ask

If this theory is even partially correct, the relevant question is not really whether Musk deserves the compensation in question — reasonable people can and do disagree about executive pay at companies of this scale — but whether regulators and remaining public shareholders have the information they would need to evaluate a merger structured this way before it happens rather than after. A stock-swap merger between a public company and a private one, where the private company’s own governance is not subject to the same disclosure regime, makes it considerably harder for an outside shareholder or a financial journalist to independently verify that the exchange ratio being proposed is fair, rather than one quietly calibrated to preserve a specific individual’s control regardless of the underlying economics. That is not an allegation that anyone has done this. It is an observation that the structure, if it proceeds the way this theory describes, would make such a determination unusually difficult to make from the outside — which is, not coincidentally, exactly the condition under which this entire nineteen-part series has argued Musk’s accumulated portfolio of power tends to operate best.

Why this belongs in the ledger regardless

Whether or not the specific mechanics of this theory prove correct, it belongs in this series for the same reason Part XIII did: because it describes, accurately, the underlying structural incentive that governs how Musk’s compensation, voting control, and corporate structure interact across two companies that this series has spent nineteen instalments arguing should be understood as a single integrated system rather than two separate businesses that happen to share a chief executive. A compensation package with genuinely unreachable milestones, whatever its designers’ actual intent, has the practical effect of entrenching control regardless of ordinary performance — and a system that entrenches one individual’s control over decisions this consequential, spanning transportation, aerospace, and now orbital computing infrastructure, deserves scrutiny on that structural basis alone, independent of whether the merger theory specifically turns out to be right. The milestones may never be hit. That may, on this reading, have been the entire point.

A closing note from north of the border

This series began, in its first instalment, by tracing Musk’s path from a South African teenager who absorbed his first-principles engineering instincts from Douglas Adams to a Canadian passport holder — this country’s citizenship the first he ever held that was not South African — and from there to building what I described then as the most concentrated portfolio of strategic power seen in the modern era. It is worth remembering, nineteen parts later, that this is not merely an American story, however much of its regulatory drama plays out in Delaware courtrooms and Nevada permitting offices. Starlink already provides connectivity Canadian governments, businesses, and remote communities increasingly depend on; a merger that further insulates the individual controlling that infrastructure from the ordinary mechanisms of public-company accountability is a matter of interest well beyond Tesla’s shareholder register. A country that helped make Musk who he became has, at minimum, standing to keep watching how he structures what he has built — and this series intends to keep doing exactly that, milestone by unreachable milestone.
