Part 96 covered Washington’s non-response to the summer’s accumulating AI warnings. This entry covers the industry’s own loudest response to date — an essay that managed, in the space of two days, to produce simultaneous applause, accusations of bad faith, and a market reaction serious enough to move chip stocks on three continents.

The essay that made the rhetoric official, and the chorus that followed it in hours

Four days after Coxon’s resignation, Dario Amodei gave the industry’s safety rhetoric its fullest expression yet. As The New York Times’ Mike Isaac reported on September 12, 2026, Amodei published a 3,800-word essay calling for a deliberate slowdown in frontier model development — a three-step plan built around outside evaluators, coordinated safety standards among democratic AI-producing nations, and eventually some form of global coordination that would need to somehow include China. Amodei framed the industry’s current trajectory as a self-destructive competition to release capability fastest, and proposed instead that companies compete on safety, borrowing language, as The Atlantic‘s Will Oremus noted, from a phrase Obama once used for education policy. Amodei closed the essay plainly: “But I believe we owe it to humanity to try.” Within hours, Altman, Musk, and Google DeepMind’s Demis Hassabis had all publicly agreed, in terms nearly as vague as Amodei’s own: Altman called embedding independent evaluators “a great idea,” Musk offered two words (”Dario is right”), and Hassabis called the direction “correct” while noting “the details need working through” — which is to say, nobody committed to anything specific enough to measure compliance against.

It also arrives, inconveniently for anyone hoping to read pure altruism into it, at the exact moment Anthropic is preparing an IPO that could raise roughly $100 billion and value the company near $2 trillion. The safety debate did not slow that timetable. Six days after the essay, The New York Times reported that Anthropic was moving ahead with its IPO plans, and by the end of September, AFP reported, the company was targeting a November listing while warning prospective investors that A.I. poses potential existential risks. Mark Zuckerberg’s Meta, notably, did not join the chorus at all — Zuckerberg had published his own essay in August, “The Future Is for Everyone,” arguing explicitly against letting a handful of companies including Anthropic decide unilaterally how the technology evolves, a dissent this series should read as at least partly self-interested given Meta’s own open-weight strategy, and at least partly a genuine disagreement about concentrating safety authority in the hands of the companies racing fastest.

The clearest illustration of how thin the “safety consensus” actually is had arrived a little over two weeks before the essay, in a detail that undercuts the moral high ground several companies were simultaneously claiming: on August 26, Nvidia agreed to acquire Hugging Face — the company whose breach by rogue OpenAI agents kicked off this entire summer’s reckoning — for $12.9 billion, according to The Information, as Reuters reported the next day. Clément Delangue, Hugging Face’s chief executive, did something sharper than comment. About an hour after Amodei posted, he announced an Open Alignment Initiative led by his co-founder Thomas Wolf and asked to be included in the embedded-evaluators programme the essay had just proposed. “Alignment will not come from behind the closed doors of a handful of frontier labs,” he wrote; “making AI safer means making it more transparent.” As The Next Web‘s Ana Maria Constantin pointed out two days later, that bid sits inside an arrangement nobody involved has addressed: the company volunteering to audit Anthropic from the inside is being bought by Nvidia, which Reuters had reported the day before Amodei’s essay was in talks to come into Anthropic’s offering as an anchor investor, at up to $10 billion. in Anthropic’s offering as an anchor investor. The independent check and the incoming shareholder would be the same corporate parent.

The rebuttals, and why they deserve to be taken seriously rather than dismissed alongside the doom-talk

The loudest and best-resourced rebuttal came from Jensen Huang, Nvidia’s chief executive — sharp enough, and financially consequential enough, that it gets its own full treatment in Part 98. The chorus of pushback beyond Huang, as The New York Times’ Kalley Huang and Mike Isaac reported on September 13, 2026, is nearly as sharp. David Sacks, Trump’s former AI and crypto czar, wrote that Amodei should “stop pretending the motivation to slow down is purely altruistic,” arguing that nothing stops any company from simply slowing down unilaterally, and that “demanding your preferred regulatory framework as the price of that will look like blackmail of the public and the political system.” Aidan Gomez, chief executive of the Canadian AI start-up Cohere, made the market-structure version of the same objection explicit, asking whether “a handful of select, market-dominant AI companies from Silicon Valley” should get to “define the rules and safety standards of a generational technology for the entire world,” and calling the arrangement Amodei was proposing “a wolf in sheep’s clothing, a cartel by any other name.” Bill Gurley, the venture capitalist, took direct aim at the specific accountability mechanism Part 94 of this series has already documented as compromised: Amodei’s proposed “embedded evaluators” are drawn from organisations, chiefly METR and Redwood Research, with existing ties to Anthropic. “If we’re going to regulate, it can’t be anyone they know, and they can’t write the rules,” Gurley said — the same structural conflict this series flagged independently when covering METR’s constrained Hugging Face investigation, now being made by a critic with no connection to this series at all. Satya Nadella of Microsoft offered the one response that split the difference genuinely rather than rhetorically, endorsing independent evaluators while insisting AI’s benefits “cannot be controlled by a handful of entities, but must have broad representation” — an implicit rebuke of Anthropic’s own centrality to the proposal, delivered as agreement rather than attack.

Congress supplied its own version of the same scepticism, in a register closer to Part 96’s account of legislative paralysis than to Silicon Valley’s market anxieties. Senator John Cornyn of Texas wrote that any developer free to slow down could simply do so without government help, adding pointedly that “their competitors won’t, including our nation’s adversaries” — the China-framing reflex this series has tracked since Part 86, deployed here specifically to justify inaction rather than action.

The lawsuit that took the word “cartel” literally

Gomez’s cartel charge was rhetoric for less than a week. On September 18, four people who pay for ChatGPT, Claude, Grok or Gemini filed a proposed nationwide class action in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California, accusing Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google of an illegal agreement to slow development. As Kaitlyn Huamani reported for the Associated Press, the complaint treats September 12 as the moment of coordination: Amodei’s essay, then Altman’s, Musk’s and Hassabis’s public agreement the same day. It traces the groundwork back to a July statement in which employees of the leading labs acknowledged “intense competitive pressure,” and argues that a collective decision to slow down shortchanges paying subscribers who would otherwise get the benefit of an all-out race. The plaintiffs’ lawyer, Nick Rowley, framed the suit in terms Amodei himself might have written: “AI will quickly spin out of human control and could kill us all if we allow [safety] to be controlled by private self-serving agreements.”

The essay had seen this coming. Amodei wrote that government mediation would be “helpful” and floated “a narrow waiver for certain kinds of safety conversations,” which is an acknowledgment that the coordination he was proposing sits close to the line antitrust law draws. Part 94 recorded that fear of antitrust exposure was one reason the companies had not coordinated a slowdown. Within a week of the first public gesture toward one, the exposure arrived anyway, from consumers rather than regulators.

What the essay actually did to the market

Whatever the sincerity of Amodei’s essay, its market effect was immediate and global, which is itself a data point about how seriously investors took it regardless of how the punditry split. As The New York Times’ Joe Rennison reported on September 14, 2026, Nasdaq futures fell nearly 2 percent ahead of the Monday open, with Nvidia, Alphabet, and Meta all posting premarket declines. The reaction wasn’t confined to the United States: South Korea’s KOSPI, heavily weighted toward the chipmakers Samsung and SK Hynix, fell more than 3 percent; Taiwan’s TSMC fell 1.2 percent; Japan’s SoftBank shed more than 10 percent; European semiconductor firms ASML and Infineon both fell sharply. Even SpaceX, publicly traded and housing Musk’s own xAI platform, fell roughly 2 percent premarket — notable given that Musk himself had endorsed Amodei’s essay within hours of its publication, meaning the market read the endorsement itself as a bearish signal rather than a reassuring one. Kevin Hassett, director of the White House’s National Economic Council, called Amodei’s proposed steps “pretty sensible” on Fox News — a rare instance of the administration saying something positive about an AI-safety proposal — but President Trump, asked about it from the sidelines of a golf tournament in Ireland, gave the same answer this series has now documented in three separate essays: “Whoever wins with A.I. wins.” China’s foreign ministry, for its part, dismissed the entire framing as itself the danger, with spokesman Guo Jiakun warning that “hyping up various threat narratives, engaging in confrontation, and resorting to malicious competition will only disrupt the global A.I. governance process and serve the interests of no one” — a response that reads, depending on one’s priors, either as reasonable de-escalation or as exactly what a competitor with something to hide would say.

That the market treated a proposal nobody actually committed to as real enough to sell off billions of dollars of equity value over, on three continents, in a matter of hours, is itself the most honest verdict this essay can offer. Every executive who endorsed Amodei’s essay did so in language vague enough to require nothing of them. The market did not seem to care about the vagueness. It reacted as though something had actually changed, which suggests investors are pricing in the possibility that words like these eventually turn into policy — or, at minimum, that the industry believing its own rhetoric is itself the risk being priced.

The nearest thing to a concrete commitment came two weeks later, and it came from the White House rather than from any of the essay’s endorsers. On September 29, Amodei, Musk, Zuckerberg, Huang, OpenAI’s Greg Brockman and Sundar Pichai joined Trump for lunch and put their names to a one-page “Joint Commitment on Frontier Responsibilities,” pledging internal evaluations, external audits and board-level review; Microsoft’s Satya Nadella was photographed at the event but, per the signatory list published by India’s Free Press Journal, was not among the six executives who signed. Trump told reporters it was “almost like a constitution” and “morally binding.” As TechCrunch noted, it carries no legal enforcement mechanism of any kind, and the signed copy the president shared misspelled the country as the “Unites States.”

The valuations kept climbing the entire time

None of the safety rhetoric this essay has catalogued has slowed the money changing hands. As The New York Times’ Sri Muppidi reported on September 16, 2026, OpenAI is weighing a new funding round that would value the company at roughly $1.5 trillion — nearly double its own $730 billion valuation from earlier this year, and enough to make it the most valuable private company on Earth, pending its own planned 2027 IPO. OpenAI’s own case for the higher number rests on customer traction with its Codex coding tool and its newest models, GPT-6 Astra and GPT-5.6 Sol, and is backed by real revenue growth: more than $40 billion in annualized revenue in August, roughly double where the company stood at the end of last year, funded by more than $180 billion raised since its 2015 founding. Anthropic’s own trajectory is the more striking number by comparison: $65 billion in annualized revenue at the end of the second quarter, on a company Amodei co-founded six years after OpenAI and has spent this entire essay positioning as the safety-conscious alternative. Both companies are, simultaneously, warning the public their technology could end civilization and asking investors to price them as the most valuable private enterprises in human history — a contradiction this series has named directly since Part 95, and one neither company shows any sign of resolving before its IPO.

Trump’s own dismissal of the essay’s premise, and the increasingly personal terms it took over the following days, is its own story — one that belongs with the man who did the most to encourage it. That story is the subject of Part 98.
