Part 15 of this series made an argument I was rather pleased with at the time: that the Trump family’s profiteering was beating the watchdogs not through concealment but through volume. Nobody needed to hide anything, I wrote, because there was simply too much of it, arriving too fast, for outrage to keep its footing. The grift was conducted in plain sight, on the theory that a thing done openly enough stops looking like a thing at all.

It turns out that theory has an exception, and his name is Jared Kushner.

On November 5, 2026 — two days after American voters handed both chambers of Congress to the Democrats — Kushner’s private-equity firm, Affinity Partners, quietly amended a routine filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission. The amendment disclosed a $32 million stake in a Connecticut defence contractor that the firm had owned for months and had simply left off the original form. As Ariel Sabar reported for The Atlantic on November 18, 2026, the filing described the omission without explanation, and neither Kushner nor Affinity answered repeated requests for comment.

Fifteen parts of this series have documented a family that monetises the presidency the way other families hold garage sales: loudly, on the front lawn, with prices written on masking tape. Part 16 is about the first item they seem to have tucked behind the shed.

What the Filing Says

The company is called Arxis. It is a roll-up of more than thirty aerospace and defence firms, assembled under one roof and taken public in April 2026. Affinity bought 689,411 shares — after Trump’s re-election, and after Kushner had returned to public service as an unpaid envoy negotiating over Gaza, Iran and Ukraine.

What Arxis makes is not incidental to what Kushner negotiates. Its components go into F-35 fighters, Arleigh Burke-class destroyers, Virginia-class submarines and missile-defence systems — hardware deployed in precisely the theatres where Kushner sits at the table. Of its $1.6 billion in revenue last year, roughly 47 percent came from defence and space spending. The company lost money in 2023 and 2024 and turned profitable in 2025, the year Trump returned to office. Its shares have risen about 60 percent since the IPO.

Arxis has never been shy about where its fortunes come from. Its own prospectus told investors that “ongoing global conflicts” and the prospect of future ones had accelerated defence investment. Its chief executive, Kevin Perhamus, described the firm on an earnings call as broadly tied to every key government spending priority there is — air defence, radar, missile systems, electronic warfare. That is not a scandal; it is a sales pitch. Defence contractors are supposed to say these things. The trouble begins when the man buying the shares is also the man whose diplomacy helps decide how long the conflicts last.

The mechanics of the omission are dull, which is usually where the interesting part hides. Under SEC rules, an investment manager holding more than $100 million in certain public securities must report those holdings on a Form 13F within 45 days of each quarter’s end. Affinity filed its second-quarter form in mid-August 2026. It listed one holding: $565 million in a building-supplies company called QXO. Arxis, already in the portfolio, was nowhere to be seen. It stayed that way for nearly three months, through the whole of the autumn campaign, and appeared only once the votes were counted.

The Indignation Period

The timing would matter less if Kushner had spent the autumn quietly. He did not.

On October 1, 2026, a CNN investigation reported that Affinity was the largest shareholder in Phoenix Financial, a Tel Aviv insurance and asset-management group that had put more than $450 million into nine companies supplying Israel’s military, from Elbit Systems to the makers of armoured bulldozers. Affinity had bought its first slice of Phoenix in 2024 for $128.5 million. In July 2026 it sold a quarter of the stake for more than $340 million — better than five times its money — and still held 7.4 percent, worth over $1 billion. Kushner himself had called Phoenix Affinity’s best investment.

Kushner’s response was swift, voluminous and, for a man who usually prefers to let others do the talking, unusually personal. He called the story deeply misleading. Phoenix, he explained on X, was not a defence company but a regulated financial institution — Israel’s equivalent of Vanguard or Fidelity — and he had no say over its investment choices. Misleading stories, he added, would not distract him from the work of ending wars.

Hold that sentence up against the calendar. Throughout October, while he was posting rebuttals about indirect exposure to Israeli defence firms through a pension manager, Affinity directly owned $32 million of an American defence contractor whose revenue rises with the very conflicts he was mediating — and had left it off the federal form that would have told anyone so. The indignation was real. It was also, by the Atlantic’s account, conducted from inside an undisclosed position.

The Vanguard Defence, and Its Limits

Fairness requires a pause here, because Kushner’s defenders have a point, and the essay is stronger for conceding it.

CNN itself found no indication that Kushner’s diplomacy directly affected the Phoenix investments. Owning a stake in a diversified Israeli financial group is not the same as owning a missile factory. And owning defence stock while negotiating peace is not, on its own, illegal; plenty of officials’ retirement accounts hold Lockheed Martin. Several readers of the Atlantic piece made exactly this objection, and it deserves a straight answer rather than a sneer.

The straight answer is that the defence stake is the least of it. What matters is the pattern the late filing completes. Kushner holds no formal government post, which — as CNN noted — means he files no financial disclosure at all. He is a volunteer, and volunteers are invisible to the ethics system. The 13F was one of the very few windows the law left open onto what his firm owns. When the single compulsory disclosure arrives late, incomplete, and only once an election is safely over, the question is no longer whether owning Arxis is a conflict. The question is why someone decided it was better that nobody knew.

There is a second, larger answer, and it concerns not what Affinity buys but where its money comes from.

Where the Money Comes From

Kushner founded Affinity in 2021, months after leaving his first-term White House job as Middle East envoy, with no substantive private-equity experience. He went on to raise as much as $6 billion, most of it from the sovereign-wealth funds of the same Gulf states whose leaders he had dealt with in office — and with whom he now deals again, as a negotiator. Saudi Arabia’s Public Investment Fund was the anchor.

In September 2026, Dexter Filkins reported in The New Yorker that while Kushner was engaged in talks with Iran earlier in the year, he had also been quietly raising another $5 billion for Affinity in the Middle East. Kushner published his own rebuttal, insisting no additional capital had in fact been raised. Even taken at his word, the episode describes a man whose fundraising calendar and negotiating calendar keep landing on the same cities.

Then, on October 22, 2026, Vanity Fair published an excerpt from Matthew Cole’s book Princes and Thieves. Cole alleges that during Trump’s first term, the rulers of the UAE and Saudi Arabia placed roughly $2 billion into two bank accounts to which Kushner had access — one at First Abu Dhabi Bank, framed as a foreign-policy fund, and a second said by a source to be for his personal benefit — and that Dutch intelligence, monitoring a compromised phone, watched him log in. These are allegations, and they should be read as such. Kushner called them completely insane. His lawyer, Chad Mizelle, said flatly that there are no accounts and never were, demanded that Vanity Fair take the excerpt down, and threatened the publisher.

Nobody outside a congressional hearing room or a courtroom is going to settle that dispute, and this series will not pretend to. But it is worth noticing the shape of the thing. The defence stake was hidden for three months. The Gulf money behind Affinity is hidden in plain sight, disclosed in outline and never in detail. And the most serious allegation of all concerns money that, if it existed, was never disclosed by anyone. Each layer is less visible than the one above it.

The Rest of the Portfolio

Arxis, the Atlantic notes, appears to be Affinity’s first direct bet on the defence industry — a sharp turn for a fund whose holdings had run to UAE online classifieds, a Brazilian fast-food chain and a stake in the $55 billion buyout of Electronic Arts. It does not look like the last. On November 12, 2026, a week after the amended filing, the Australian firm Q-CTRL announced a $290 million funding round with Affinity among its significant new backers, as The Quantum Insider reported. Q-CTRL builds quantum navigation that works where GPS is jammed or spoofed — a problem endemic to the wars Kushner mediates — and counts a DARPA contract and the CIA-founded venture fund In-Q-Tel among its credentials.

And then there is the coast of Albania.

Affinity’s €1.4 billion ($1.6 billion) project would put a resort on the uninhabited island of Sazan and roughly 10,000 hotel rooms and villas along the coast beside the Vjosa-Narta protected landscape — a wetland of flamingos, Dalmatian pelicans and nesting sea turtles around one of the last intact river deltas in the Mediterranean. In February 2024, Albania’s parliament amended its protected-areas law to permit luxury development for “strategic investors.” Weeks later, Kushner announced his plans, which prompted an inquiry by the country’s anti-corruption prosecutors. When heavy machinery arrived at the site, local protests followed, and in early June 2026 thousands marched in Tirana under banners reading “Albania is not for sale,” as Al Jazeera reported. Prime Minister Edi Rama vowed to press on regardless. By October, The Guardian was reporting that Dutch members of the European Parliament had accused Albania of “sneaky” practices to fast-track the resort, amid protests that had turned increasingly violent.

The Balkans have seen this film before. In 2025, Kushner abandoned a planned Trump-branded hotel on the site of a former army headquarters in Belgrade after street protests in Serbia. It is a remarkable record for a peace envoy: two countries, two protest movements, both provoked not by his diplomacy but by his real estate.

Why the Timing Matters

Ask why a filing might be held back until after an election and the answer more or less writes itself. Democrats had already named Kushner among the Trump-world figures they intended to investigate if they won. On October 6, 2026, Rep. Robert Garcia, the top Democrat on the House Oversight Committee, wrote demanding information about his investments after the Phoenix revelations, as CNN reported. After the Vanity Fair excerpt, Rep. Jamie Raskin, the ranking Democrat on Judiciary, called on Kushner to step aside from the Middle East and Ukraine negotiations and produce a full list of his bank accounts. A $32 million defence stake surfacing in mid-October would have handed both men a fresh exhibit in the last weeks of a campaign that Democrats were running, quite explicitly, on corruption.

It did not surface. And it turned out not to matter. When the votes were counted on November 3, corruption topped the list of concerns among the voters who threw out the Republican majorities, as the Associated Press reported. The exhaustion this series described in Part 15 was real — but it was the exhaustion of the news cycle, not of the electorate. Voters who could not have named a single item on the watchdogs’ trackers had nonetheless absorbed the general shape of the thing, and they voted on the shape.

That changes Kushner’s position more than any filing could. As Axios observed back in July, the most promising targets for Democratic investigators are the people around the president, who, unlike him, can be compelled to testify under oath. A volunteer envoy enjoys none of the protections of office. He is not a cabinet secretary, cannot comfortably claim executive privilege over his private fund, and runs a business whose investors and holdings a congressional committee can ask about by name. The informality that kept him off the ethics forms is the same informality that leaves him exposed now.

Part 14 wagered that only a personal, visible failure could crack the businessman myth for the believers. Part 15 asked what single fact might break through the exhaustion. The answer, it turns out, was not a single fact at all. It was a cumulative one, delivered in a polling booth. And the first thing to happen afterwards was a man who had spent the autumn insisting he had nothing to hide, quietly filing the paperwork that showed what he had been hiding.

This series has always run on a casino metaphor: the house always wins, unless the marks stop playing. On November 3, enough of the marks stopped playing to change who holds the subpoena pen. What Congress means to do with it — and how much it can actually do with a president who still holds the veto and the pardon — is the subject of Part 17.
