The first two parts of this series covered a hedge fund that broke under its own leverage and a liability so large it doesn’t officially exist. This part covers something harder to point to directly, because it isn’t a single number. It’s a loop.

The Loop, Traced

The basic shape of circular financing in this industry is simple enough to state in one sentence and complicated enough to spend a career untangling: Nvidia invests in or extends credit to AI labs and cloud providers, those companies use the money to buy Nvidia chips, and Nvidia books the resulting sales as revenue that helps justify its own soaring valuation — the valuation that, in turn, gives it more capital to invest in the next round of customers. Wall Street's own circular-financing skepticism, reported by CNBC, flagged Nvidia specifically for arrangements along these lines — Jensen Huang has said publicly that the $500 billion Wall Street financing consortium covered in Part I was designed specifically to address that concern, insisting "the capital is not Nvidia revenue," even as he declined to say which AI labs or startups would ultimately receive the money.

None of this is illegal, and little of it is even unusual by the standards of capital-intensive industries — telecoms did versions of this in the 1990s, and so did railroads a century before that. What makes it worth a full part in this series is the scale and the concentration: a small number of companies are simultaneously one another’s largest customers, largest investors, and largest creditors, in an industry where the underlying product’s value is still, by JPMorgan’s own more cautious language, a matter of “durable” investment rather than proven return. When the customer, the investor, and the lender are increasingly the same handful of logos, the ordinary market signal of “would an independent buyer pay this price” gets harder to isolate from “would this specific interested party keep the wheel turning.”

The Depreciation Question, Which Sounds Boring and Isn’t

The second thread this part needs to pull is a more technical one, and it deserves plain-language treatment because the stakes are not technical at all. Hyperscalers currently depreciate AI hardware — the GPUs and specialized chips at the center of this entire buildout — over five to six years on their books. Independent analysis, including estimates attributed to the investor Michael Burry, argues the real, usable economic life of that hardware is closer to two to three years, given how fast each new generation of chips outperforms the last and how quickly customers migrate workloads to newer, faster, cheaper-to-run hardware.

The gap between those two numbers is not a rounding error. If a company spreads the cost of a chip over six years when it will realistically need replacing in three, its reported earnings in each of those early years look better than the company’s true economic position, because the real cost of staying competitive — buying the next generation of chips on a faster cycle than the books assume — isn’t being reflected yet. One CFA-analyst estimate cited in this series’ research put the resulting understatement at roughly $176 billion across 2026 through 2028, spread across the industry. That’s not a claim of fraud. Depreciation schedules are estimates, set by accountants making a judgment call about a genuinely uncertain future, and reasonable people can disagree about how long a GPU stays economically useful. But it is a claim that the industry’s own preferred estimate happens to be the one that makes current profitability look best — and that the gap between the assumption and the likely reality is being carried, silently, by every investor pricing these companies off their reported earnings.

Where the Two Threads Meet

Circular financing and generous depreciation assumptions aren’t the same problem, but they compound each other in a specific way worth naming directly. Circular financing makes revenue look more durable than it might otherwise be, because a meaningful share of it is generated by the same small circle of interested parties transacting with one another. Favorable depreciation assumptions make the cost side of the ledger look lighter than it might otherwise be, because the real replacement cycle is faster than the books assume. Put those together, and profitability, as currently reported across much of this industry, is flattered from both directions at once — not through anything as clean as fraud, but through a series of individually defensible choices that all happen to point the same way.

This is, again, precisely the accounting-standard problem from Part II, wearing a different outfit. Nobody is breaking a rule. The rules were built for companies operating at a different scale, with more arm’s-length relationships between customer and supplier, and with hardware that depreciated on a timeline closer to the one still written into the standard playbook. An industry this concentrated, moving this fast, is generating exactly the kind of edge case those rules were never stress-tested against.

The Bull Case, Stated Fairly

It would be dishonest to close this part without the strongest version of the counterargument, because it exists and it isn’t foolish. JPMorgan’s research, cited throughout this series, argues that even accounting for aggressive depreciation and heavy debt financing, hyperscaler operating cash flow is projected to exceed $900 billion by 2027 — a cushion large enough to absorb a meaningfully worse-than-expected depreciation reality without triggering the kind of crisis Part I documented at Situational Awareness. Circular financing, in this reading, isn’t evidence of fragility; it’s simply how a genuinely new, genuinely capital-intensive industry bootstraps itself before independent, arm’s-length demand catches up — the same accusation, more or less, that gets leveled at any young capital-intensive sector before it matures into one with more diversified customers. Whether AI’s buildout is different in kind from the industries that came before it, or just different in speed, is the actual question underneath this whole series, and it isn’t one three parts can answer.

Where This Leaves the Ledger

Three parts in, the throughline is consistent with everything The Asimov Gap has argued about governance: the frameworks meant to keep this industry honest — accounting standards, depreciation conventions, the ordinary skepticism that arm’s-length transactions are supposed to provide — were built for a smaller, slower version of the thing they’re now being asked to measure. Nobody has been caught doing anything the rules forbid. That’s exactly why the ledger needs keeping. The rules aren’t being broken. They may simply no longer be measuring what they were built to measure.
