I. Two Industries, One Discovery

Two industries independently discovered the same loophole this summer: nothing is watching what AI tells people about a product or a candidate until someone outside the system happens to notice. One industry is selling supplements. The other is running for office. Neither is breaking a law, because in both cases, no law exists yet that squarely applies.

That absence is worth sitting with before the specifics, because it’s easy to read what follows as two unrelated stories about bad actors — a shady supplement brand here, an aggressive campaign consultant there — when the more accurate reading is that both are ordinary market behavior finding the one gap in an otherwise heavily regulated system. Political advertising is regulated. Health claims on television are regulated. What both stories actually document is what happens when the same claims move to a channel — a chatbot’s conversational answer — that inherited none of the old rules.

II. The Candidate Who Learned to Edit the Algorithm

As Stuart A. Thompson and Tiffany Hsu reported for the New York Times, Dustin Lloyd, a Democratic primary candidate for Missouri’s state legislature, discovered that chatbots like ChatGPT and Gemini knew almost nothing substantive about him — until he rewrote his campaign website to feed them what he wanted them to know. Before the edits, asked about his small-business positions, the chatbot demurred: it didn’t have enough information. After he published a Q&A about himself and made a few other changes, the same chatbot recited his platform back to voters in close to his own campaign’s language, describing him as having the “strongest and most explicit small-business platform” in his primary.

Lloyd relied on a tool called CampSight, launched last month by Run For Something Action Fund, a progressive group that recruits young candidates to run for office. It audited his website, his Wikipedia entry, and his Ballotpedia page, and told him plainly that his presence across all three was lackluster — going so far as to recommend he post threads on Reddit specifically so chatbots would start pulling from that forum when summarizing his positions. “Everyone’s just grasping for some sort of tool to at least inspect these results and ideally influence them,” Jordan Haines, Run for Something’s chief technology officer, told the Times. That’s an honest description of a defensive posture, not an offensive one — Lloyd wasn’t trying to deceive anyone about his own positions. But the tool he used works by the same mechanism a bad-faith actor would use: publish something, wait, and the chatbot repeats it as settled fact.

III. The Twelve-Minute Window

The industry now built around this practice has a name — answer engine optimization, or AEO — and its own research firms tracking how fast it works. Caucus AI, one of these firms, ran an experiment publishing new content to Wikipedia and timing how long it took to surface in a chatbot’s answer: about twelve minutes. The company’s own blog post about the finding described what that means in practice — candidates can insert their own framing “quickly, unvetted and without anyone on the other side watching.”

Twelve minutes is fast enough to be useful for a campaign correcting the record. It’s also fast enough that nothing distinguishes an honest correction from a fabrication, because the system doing the ingesting has no mechanism for telling them apart — it simply treats fresh text as fresh information. Caucus AI is now tracking what chatbots say about every Senate, House, and gubernatorial candidate in the country, and has already found unpredictable amplification effects: when the firm asked three different chatbots about Mary Peltola, the Democratic Senate challenger in Alaska, all three cited her campaign slogan — “Fish. Family. Freedom.” — without prompting. “We’re exploring what causes that to get picked up,” Meg Schwenzfeier, Caucus AI’s founder and Kamala Harris’s former chief analytics officer, told the Times. “There’s still a lot we don’t understand, and these models themselves are black boxes.” That’s a research firm built specifically to monitor this phenomenon admitting it can’t fully explain how the phenomenon works — which is a notable thing to be true of a system already estimated to be informing at least 16 million voters, according to Caucus AI’s own count.

IV. The Optimization Gap Nobody’s Talking About

The starkest finding in the reporting isn’t about any single candidate — it’s about who’s already winning this game before most campaigns know it’s being played. Tim Chambers, who runs digital strategy at Dewey Square Group, a Washington public affairs firm whose clients include labor unions and government agencies, told the Times his firm has been auditing client websites specifically for how visible they are to chatbot scrapers. Research the firm published in February found that far-right websites and sites with “very low” factual integrity had already restructured themselves to be easily read by AI tools — well ahead of outlets Dewey classified as center-left or highly factual, which hadn’t made comparable changes.

That’s a genuinely uncomfortable finding for anyone who assumed technical competence and factual reliability travel together. They don’t have to. A site optimized to be legible to a scraper says nothing about whether what it’s scraping is true, and the sites that got there first, per Dewey’s research, were disproportionately the ones with the weakest track record for accuracy. Chambers’s clients are now thinking, in his words, about “needing to rebuild their websites for machines as much as for humans” — which means the reputable, fact-checked, slower-moving institutions are the ones now racing to catch up to a head start set by the sites least equipped to be trusted with it.

Beth Simone Noveck, a Northeastern University professor studying democracy and large language models, put the deeper problem in plain terms: “There is a certain degree of panic around it, because we understand it so little and it’s changing so fast. Even if there are people who claim to understand it, even if they master some of the techniques, the changing and unpredictable nature of LLMs just means it’s really difficult to control this process.” And the stakes aren’t hypothetical. Researchers at Demos, a British think tank, studied AI-generated answers ahead of Scotland’s May parliamentary election and found more than a third were flatly wrong — invented candidates who didn’t exist, fabricated nepotism accusations, concocted financial scandals, incumbents described as running for re-election when they weren’t. Nobody optimized those particular errors into existence. They were simply what a system with no fact-checking layer produced when asked a question it didn’t actually know the answer to.

V. The Doctor Who Isn’t Real

The wellness-supplement version of the same discovery has a body count attached, or close to one. The Times found hundreds of AI-generated “doctors,” healers, and wellness influencers running social media ads for supplement brands — hyperrealistic avatars posting testimonial-style videos (”This is what we look like on Day 1 of taking beetroot every day. This is Day 50”), some presented as Eastern medicine practitioners standing in front of fake diplomas on fake office walls. The ads are built to look familiar rather than exotic: American flags, idyllic suburban backgrounds, the visual grammar of an ordinary social post rather than an obvious advertisement.

Pamela Wundrow, a 71-year-old retired truck driver with an autoimmune condition involving joint pain, fatigue, and inflammation, saw one of these ads for a moringa supplement from a brand called Rosabella and ordered it, drawn in partly because she already forages for plants and trusts herbal remedies generally. Months into taking it daily, she learned some of her pills had been recalled — the FDA and CDC announced in February that Rosabella’s moringa product was making people sick with salmonella. Wundrow told the Times she didn’t experience salmonella symptoms directly, but that her existing health issues worsened while she was taking it, and that she has no way to know whether the two are connected: “I can’t say, and I don’t know how they could prove it either.” Ambrosia Brands, the company behind Rosabella, had accumulated hundreds of complaints with the Better Business Bureau over the preceding three years and did not respond to the Times’ requests for comment.

Some of the ads the Times reviewed made claims that would be straightforwardly illegal to broadcast on American television — one showed a fake practitioner describing a supplement supposedly given to “100 people with Stage 3 kidney disease” with results implied to outperform actual medication. Online, per the Times’ own framing, ads like this face little enforcement. Both TikTok and Meta told the Times they have policies against this kind of content — TikTok says it bans accounts making misleading health claims, and Meta says it labels AI-generated material — but the Times found several examples still live on both platforms at the time of reporting, which the companies then removed only after being shown the specific posts.

VI. The Factory Behind the Face

What makes this durable rather than an isolated scam is the production economics underneath it. The Times visited a Chinese advertising agency, whose staffer Vivi Yi described the operation candidly, that produces roughly 1,200 AI-generated videos a day with a staff of 40, at a cost of about ten dollars per video, distributing them across hundreds of accounts on TikTok Shop. Yi declined to show the Times any supplement-specific ads, citing contractual restrictions with the brands she works for, but acknowledged that health products need extra caution on the platform — an acknowledgment that the caution exists as brand-side risk management rather than because any external rule requires it.

That is the production capacity now sitting behind every fake avatar the Times found: not a single bad actor to be identified and shut down, but a repeatable, cheap, geographically distant manufacturing process for synthetic testimonials, running legally because, as the report states plainly, “there is no federal law limiting the use of synthetic people in advertising.” Shut down one avatar, one account, one brand, and the factory that produced it keeps running at ten dollars a video.

VII. The Shape of the Gap in Both Cases

Neither industry required a company to build anything malicious. Answer-engine optimization is, in Haines’s own description, just “grasping for some sort of tool to at least inspect these results and ideally influence them” — a defensive reaction to a system nobody designed to be checkable. The AI-avatar supplement operations exploit the same absence from the other direction, using a completely legal production method to distribute content a regulator would have blocked in any older medium. In both cases, the platforms hosting the content — Google, Meta, TikTok — have a policy that technically covers the harm, and an enforcement mechanism that only activates once a journalist, not a system, does the finding.

It’s worth being precise about what that means structurally. A policy that exists but is enforced only reactively isn’t really a policy in the sense that matters here — it’s a promise to respond to embarrassment. The Times could only get five accounts investigated, in an earlier related story about account bans, because it had the institutional standing to get someone at the company to look. An ordinary voter reading a fabricated Wikipedia-derived answer, or an ordinary 71-year-old reading a supplement ad, has no equivalent channel. The correction mechanism this system actually has is journalism, applied case by case, to whichever instance happens to become visible enough to be worth a reporter’s time.

VIII. TACO, at the Speed of an Ad Buy

Run both stories through the TACO framework this series has used since Part 1. Threaten doesn’t apply to either case; nothing here is coercive, just unpoliced. Accommodate is what happens after the fact in both stories — Meta and TikTok pulling the specific ads the Times flagged, Wikipedia editors and campaign researchers racing to out-publish disinformation candidate by candidate, Dewey Square auditing clients’ sites defensively rather than waiting for a standard to be set for them. Capitulate, a structural fix that closes the category rather than the instance — a synthetic-persona disclosure law, a verification layer between chatbot training data and election or health information — exists nowhere in either story. Obfuscate is the silence: Ambrosia Brands not responding to the Times’ requests for comment, and the near-total absence, in either piece, of any platform explaining why detection is reactive rather than built in.

The Caucus AI research adds an unusual fifth category worth naming separately, since it doesn’t fit neatly into any of the four: genuine bewilderment. Schwenzfeier’s “these models themselves are black boxes” isn’t a company obfuscating an answer it knows and won’t share — it’s a firm built specifically to study this phenomenon admitting it doesn’t yet have the answer. That’s a different, arguably more honest failure mode than anything else in this series, and also no more reassuring: a system informing millions of voters is being run by an industry that includes serious people who candidly don’t understand its behavior yet.

IX. The Gap, at the Speed of an Answer

This series has spent sixty-six parts arguing that the Asimov Gap opens wherever deployment outruns the law meant to govern it. These two stories show the gap operating at its fastest possible speed: twelve minutes for a Wikipedia edit to become a candidate’s official chatbot biography, a single ten-dollar video to become a stranger’s medical advice, and — per Dewey Square’s research — a head start already banked by the websites least equipped to be trusted with one. Neither system asks whether the information reaching a voter or a patient is true before repeating it with the same flat confidence it uses for anything else. The correction, when it comes, arrives only after a reporter, a recall, or an election has already happened — which means the only functioning check on either system right now is journalism, and journalism was never built to run at the speed the thing it’s checking already moves.
