Morris Chang, the MIT- and Stanford-trained engineer who founded TSMC in 1987 and turned Taiwan into the world’s indispensable chipmaker.

In the autumn of 2021, Taiwan’s then-President Tsai Ing-wen published an essay in Foreign Affairs making an argument that has since become the foundational assumption of Western thinking about Taiwan’s security. Taiwan’s dominance in semiconductor manufacturing, she wrote, was a “silicon shield” — a guarantee that American reliance on Taiwanese chips would protect the island against any “aggressive attempts by authoritarian regimes to disrupt global supply chains.”

It was a compelling argument. It was also, as of May 2026, an argument that has been empirically refuted — not by a think tank paper, not by a war game, but by forty days of actual events in the Persian Gulf, and then confirmed, six weeks later, in a summit in Beijing where the man who called Taiwan’s chip concentration “the single biggest threat to the world economy” sat across the table from Xi Jinping while his president weighed whether to sell Taiwan the weapons it needs to survive.

The Iran war has done something that five decades of strategic analysis could not: it has run the experiment. And the results are not what Taiwan’s defenders were hoping for.

The Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company was founded in 1987 by Morris Chang, an engineer who had been born in mainland China, educated at MIT and Stanford, and spent his career in the American semiconductor industry before the Taiwanese government recruited him to build something that had never existed before: a pure-play foundry, a company that manufactured chips designed by other companies, on contract, at the highest levels of precision that the technology could achieve.

Chang’s model was considered eccentric at the time. The received wisdom in the semiconductor industry was that companies which designed chips should also manufacture them. Chang’s insight was that design and manufacturing were becoming so technically complex that no single company could remain world-class at both simultaneously. Specialisation was the future.

He was right. By 2026, TSMC produces more than ninety percent of the world’s most advanced semiconductors — the chips running at three nanometres and below that power smartphones, artificial intelligence systems, military hardware, financial trading systems, medical devices, and most of the digital infrastructure on which the global economy now depends. Its customers include Apple, NVIDIA, AMD, Qualcomm, Google, and the defence establishments of multiple nations. Jensen Huang, the CEO of NVIDIA — TSMC’s largest customer for AI chips — was in Beijing this week as part of Trump’s delegation to the Xi summit. He was not there to discuss Taiwan’s democratic aspirations.

The knowledge embedded in TSMC’s manufacturing processes cannot be relocated overnight. It took thirty years to build. It lives in the people who work there, in the supply chains that feed those factories, in the institutional culture of an organisation that has made itself irreplaceable by being better at one thing than anyone else on earth.

Inside a TSMC fabrication plant—the “silver highway” where 3nm-and-below chips that power the global economy are made. The knowledge here cannot be relocated overnight.

This, Taiwan’s strategists argued, was the shield. Any power that seized or destroyed TSMC’s manufacturing capacity would detonate a global economic catastrophe. China could not simply attack Taiwan without also destroying the chip factories that supply its own technology sector. The United States could not allow TSMC to fall under Chinese control without surrendering the technological foundation of its military and economic dominance.

The logic was sound. The conclusion — that the silicon shield protected Taiwan — was wrong.

To understand why, you have to look at what just happened in the Strait of Hormuz.

Iran — a nation with a GDP smaller than many American states, a military that the United States could theoretically destroy in a matter of weeks, and an economy already crippled by decades of sanctions — closed the Strait of Hormuz in March 2026. By doing so, it cut off roughly one-fifth of the world’s oil supply. Within days, the average price of gas in the United States had risen by nearly forty percent. Stock markets fell. Recession fears spread. A Pew Research Center poll conducted at the end of March found that gas prices were the biggest concern among Americans about the war with Iran — above the risk of large numbers of American military casualties.

Forty days later, the United States agreed to a ceasefire. Iran had not given up significant ground. The superpower blinked.

The lesson is not about military power. Iran knew it could not defeat the United States militarily. The lesson is about economic pain thresholds. Iran found the threshold, held it, and watched the calculation change in Washington. The supply chain chokepoint — the strait, the oil — was not Iran’s vulnerability. It was Iran’s weapon.

As Simon Shuster wrote in The Atlantic the day the ceasefire was announced: the Iran war has flipped the silicon shield argument on its head. An authoritarian regime far weaker than China used global supply chains as leverage and forced the United States to back away from its threats. The supply chain chokepoint was not a deterrent. It was the mechanism of coercion.

This is the sentence that should be read in Taipei, and in Washington, and in every foreign ministry that has been quietly relying on the silicon shield theory to avoid having to think harder about Taiwan.

The Taiwan comparison writes itself, and it is devastating.

Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz and cut one-fifth of global oil supply. Gas prices rose forty percent. Trump blinked in forty days.

A Chinese blockade of Taiwan would cut more than one-third of global microchip production. The economic damage, as Chris Miller wrote in his 2022 book Chip War, “would be measured in the trillions.” Losing thirty-seven percent of global computing power annually could well be more costly than the COVID pandemic and its economically disastrous lockdowns. It would take at least half a decade to rebuild the lost chipmaking capacity.

Iran’s leverage over the global economy was significant. Taiwan’s leverage is categorically larger. The pain threshold it could impose on the American economy — and on every economy on earth — dwarfs what Iran achieved in the Gulf.

But here is the inversion that Tsai Ing-wen’s silicon shield argument missed: Taiwan’s chip industry does not protect Taiwan the way a shield protects a soldier. It protects Taiwan the way a hostage protects a kidnapper. The value of the hostage is precisely what makes the threat credible. The more indispensable TSMC becomes to the global economy, the more devastating a Chinese blockade becomes — and the more powerful the economic argument for Washington to find a negotiated exit rather than a military one.

Taiwan’s semiconductor ecosystem—1.6 fabs every 10 km. The map that makes the “silicon shield” argument look so convincing… until it doesn’t.

The silicon shield is not a deterrent. It is the mechanism of coercion.

Mark Cancian, a retired colonel of the United States Marine Corps, gamed out this scenario for the Center for Strategic and International Studies. He and his colleagues ran twenty-six war games, each beginning with a Chinese blockade of Taiwan. The findings were sobering. The United States Navy would risk catastrophe in any attempt to break through. “It would be a major battle,” Cancian said. “You’re potentially losing hundreds of ships.”

The report’s conclusion was precise and unsparing: if China escalated to using military force, the United States had to accept a Taiwanese capitulation on China’s terms or become directly involved in the conflict. The Iran war has done that speculating for them. A president who blinked at forty days of forty-percent gas prices is now being asked to contemplate hundreds of ships, trillions of dollars in economic damage, and a global computing shutdown that would make the pandemic recession look like a mild correction. The war game dilemma is no longer hypothetical. It has a case study. It has a timeline. It has a pain threshold, empirically established.

China has all of this data. China has been watching every day of the Iran war with the attention of a student who knows the exam is coming.

There are those in Washington and Taipei who argue that the response to all of this is diversification — reducing the world’s dependence on Taiwanese chips by building capacity elsewhere. The argument is real. The timeline is the problem.

On the day the Iran ceasefire was announced, Elon Musk announced a partnership with Intel to design and produce advanced microchips at a Tesla facility in Texas. The new factory, when it eventually produces chips, will reduce American dependence on Taiwan. It will take years to begin production. It provides no protection now. Musk, too, was in Beijing this week, sitting at Xi Jinping’s table. The man building the factory that is supposed to reduce American dependence on Taiwanese chips was dining with the leader who intends to make that dependence irrelevant by other means.

NVIDIA’s Jensen Huang and Tesla’s Elon Musk—two Americans whose companies depend on Taiwanese chips—both in Beijing during the critical summit. Diversification is coming… eventually.

TSMC has been building facilities in Arizona, Japan, and Germany under sustained American political pressure. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick has called for Taiwan to move forty percent of its chip production to the United States. Those fabs, when complete, will produce some advanced chips outside Taiwan. Even so, approximately eighty-five percent of the world’s AI semiconductors will still be produced in Taiwan by 2030.

The silicon shield is being slowly dismantled. But slowly is not the same as gone. For the foreseeable future — for the window in which the Taiwan question is most likely to become acute — Taiwan remains the world’s indispensable chip producer. And indispensability, the Iran war has just demonstrated, is not protection.

It is leverage. And leverage, in a conflict between an authoritarian regime and a democracy with a pain threshold, tends to work in one direction.

Scott Bessent, Donald Trump’s Treasury Secretary, described the concentration of global advanced chip production in Taiwan as “the single biggest threat to the world economy.” He was not threatening Taiwan. He was describing a vulnerability. On May 14, 2026, Bessent was in the Great Hall of the People in Beijing, sitting beside the president he advises, across the table from the man who intends to resolve that vulnerability on his own terms. What the Iran war has revealed is that the vulnerability belongs not to the party holding the leverage, but to the party that cannot afford to let the leverage be used.

Taiwan holds the leverage. The leverage will be used against Taiwan.

Ilha Formosa. Beautiful Island. The beauty now runs through silicon wafers etched at three nanometres, and the whole world depends on it, and Iran just showed Beijing exactly how that dependence can be turned into a weapon.
