Every company tells two stories about its money: one to investors, who want to hear that the spending will pay off, and one to the tax authorities, who reward spending that might not. Usually the two stories are compatible. On September 30, 2026, Kashmir Hill, Jesse Drucker, Eli Tan and Mike Isaac reported in The New York Times that at Meta they have come apart so completely that the same buildings are two different things depending on who is asking.

To shareholders, Mark Zuckerberg’s A.I. build-out is a triumph. “Our investments in A.I. are accelerating every major part of our core business,” he has told them. “Every sign that we’re seeing in our own work and across the industry gives us confidence in this investment.” To the Internal Revenue Service, according to four people with knowledge of the company’s operations, those same data centres are “pilot models”: a giant experiment, technically uncertain, that could fail.

The data centre is at once a certain success and an uncertain experiment, depending on which filing you open. Physicists have a cat for this.

How the credit works

The tax break in question is the Research and Experimentation Tax Credit, created in 1981, in the first year of the Reagan administration, when Japan looked set to eat America’s technological lunch. Companies could already deduct research spending from taxable income. The credit went further, a dollar-for-dollar reduction in tax on top of the deduction, and so it came with a harder test. A company must show, in a four-part test, that it was running a genuine experiment to resolve a technical uncertainty, not simply rolling out a product. Early beneficiaries, a government report noted, developed electronic banking equipment and cancer drugs.

Thousands of companies claim it, overwhelmingly for the salaries of the researchers and engineers doing the inventing. Supplies qualify only when they are consumed in the experiment itself. The I.R.S. has repeatedly rejected claims for “proven and commercially available equipment and technology.”

In the summer of 2024, as Meta broke ground on tens of thousands of acres of data centres, a Meta employee overseeing the build-out had what the Times calls a “creative idea.” The single biggest cost of an A.I. data centre is its chips, mostly Nvidia’s, at thousands of dollars apiece. Why not classify the chips as experimental supplies?

The difficulty is obvious. Meta really is experimenting with server-rack layouts and with ways of networking thousands of chips for training. But the chips themselves are the most proven hardware in the industry. They have powered a decade of A.I. progress and made Nvidia the most valuable company in the world. Some in Meta’s finance department questioned whether the idea would survive an audit. The company’s outside lawyers pointed to a 2021 case in which a federal judge denied the credit to an Indiana shipbuilder for building new kinds of vessels: making a new product is not enough; you must identify the specific components that were technically uncertain and show you ran experiments to resolve them.

One lawyer Meta consulted, Alex Sadler of Morgan Lewis, a former Justice Department tax attorney, put the question at a Virginia tax conference in September without naming any client. Pilot models are an “area of controversy,” he said, and the I.R.S. “doesn’t like” it when companies relabel commercial production as research. Then he asked it outright: “What if we have a $10 billion data center that does cool stuff that hasn’t really been done? Is all the cost a research expenditure?”

After a few months, Meta decided that it was. It began tagging chips bound for A.I. data centres differently, for tax purposes, from chips bound for ordinary ones.

The numbers

The credit cut Meta’s taxes by $700 million in 2023, the year before the strategy began. In 2024 it saved $2 billion; in 2025, $3.9 billion. Meta is now the largest beneficiary of the credit among publicly traded companies. Congress’s Joint Committee on Taxation projected the credit would cost the Treasury $32.1 billion in 2025, making it the second-most expensive federal corporate tax break after the reduced rate on offshore profits. Meta alone accounts for more than a tenth of that.

Now for the other story, the one Meta tells investors in the small print. Because its accountants judge the I.R.S. likely to challenge the manoeuvre, Meta must report “unrecognized tax benefits”: the gap between what it paid and what it might owe if it loses. Since the data-centre strategy began, that reserve has grown 45 per cent, from $12.9 billion to $18.74 billion. The first contributing factor listed in its annual filing is “uncertainties with our research tax credits.”

Read the two filings side by side and the structure is exact. In the investor presentation the data centres carry no doubt at all. In the tax return they are full of it. And in the footnote that reconciles the two, Meta’s own advisers put a price on the chance that the tax return is wrong.

“Meta is claiming billions of dollars in tax benefits that its own accountants are telling investors are at risk of being overturned by the I.R.S.,” said Lisa De Simone, a former EY tax adviser who now teaches accounting at the University of Texas. Andre Shevchuck of the advisory firm BPM, a specialist in the credit, called the characterisation “kind of wild and out there,” and offered the one version he could imagine working: “If you had a data center that you’re building out to cure cancer.”

Meta’s spokesman, Andy Stone, said the company had invested $200 billion in research and development over five years, $57 billion of it in 2025, and uses “the tax incentives Congress established decades ago to encourage this type of domestic investment.” Unrecognised tax benefits, he said, are “simply a mandated accounting measure of uncertainty.” Meta declined to explain what made the data centres experimental.

A pattern, not an accident

This is not Meta’s first adventurous reading of the same credit. In 2013, it claimed that $4.1 billion of stock options exercised by Mark Zuckerberg counted as a research expense, on the ground that he had helped invent products such as the News Feed. When the Times reported, the I.R.S. was still trying to claw back the resulting $355 million. Separately, in one of the largest Tax Court disputes in American history, the agency is seeking nearly $16 billion in taxes and penalties on profits it says Meta shifted to the Cayman Islands.

Nor is Meta short of other help. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025 gave the whole industry an accelerated write-off for research and development spending. Part XVII of this ledger reported that Meta’s El Paso data centre came with an 80 per cent local tax break for thirty-five years. And Part XII met Marco Sanchez of the El Paso Youth Alliance, who organised against that deal. The same building, then, has been subsidised by the city and county through property-tax abatement and by the federal government through the research credit, and the company that owns it is worth well over a trillion dollars.

As of the Times’s report, Meta was the only big technology company to flag the research credit as a risk to investors. Apple, Amazon, Alphabet and Microsoft each report research credits of more than $1 billion a year, and none has disclosed whether any of it covers A.I. data centres. That may mean they are not doing it. It may mean they have decided the risk is not worth mentioning. The Times reports one further detail that points toward the second possibility: Meta’s auditor, EY, which signed off on the plan and advised on it, “has since pitched other companies on using the research credit to buy computer chips for A.I. training.”

What the credit was for

James Shannon, the former Massachusetts congressman who sponsored the credit in 1981, told the Times it was meant to support “people power, knowledge, information,” not “making things.” Told what it now pays for, he said: “This has gone way, way beyond what anybody could have imagined.”

The deeper problem is the one economists at the University of Southern California identified in a study published in 2025: when companies “simply reclassify existing spending as R&D,” they collect the tax break “without meaningfully financing innovation.” Meta was going to build these data centres whether or not the credit existed; Zuckerberg has said so, in effect, every quarter, with every confident word to investors. A subsidy for something a company would do anyway is not an incentive. It is a transfer.

That brings this ledger back to the question Part IV asked in August: what does A.I. owe the public? The industry’s usual answer is jobs, tax revenue and the benefits of the technology itself. Part VIII examined the claim that the A.I. boom would be a fiscal tailwind, lifting federal revenue as it lifted growth. Here, in the most direct terms possible, is the flow running the other way: billions of dollars a year leaving the federal Treasury to discount the Nvidia chips of a company worth well over a trillion dollars, on the theory that those chips are an experiment.

Heads I win, tails we settle

The Times offers one sentence that explains why companies take bets like this: “Even if the I.R.S. balks, companies can settle disputes and still wind up ahead.” A tax position that might lose is still worth taking if the likely worst case is a negotiated repayment of part of it, years later. Meta has set aside $18.74 billion against that day. In the meantime, the cash is working inside the build-out rather than sitting in the Treasury.

Part XXIII of this ledger borrowed Bethany McLean’s phrase: A.I. has become too big to stop. This is a small, precise example of what that looks like in practice. The build-out is so large that its tax treatment alone can move a line in the federal budget, and so politically favoured that a company can call its core business an experiment and wait to see whether anyone objects. An I.R.S. that has shed a large share of its workforce since 2025 is not an obvious objector.

Congress, or part of it, had started asking questions just before the Times story ran. In letters to Amazon, Meta, Alphabet and Microsoft, announced by Senator Elissa Slotkin’s office on September 29, 2026, Slotkin, Elizabeth Warren, Jeff Merkley, Bernie Sanders, Richard Blumenthal and Tina Smith demanded a full accounting of the A.I. and data-centre deductions each company had claimed under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, with answers due by October 11. “Rather than regulate AI to protect American families or address affordability, the Trump administration has prioritized handing out colossal tax breaks,” the senators wrote. InvestmentNews reported the figure behind the letters: Meta’s federal income-tax payments fell from $9.6 billion in 2024 to $2.8 billion in 2025, on comparable profits, and the senators noted that much of Meta’s $72 billion in 2025 capital spending, most of it on data centres and A.I., may have qualified for immediate write-off under the law’s permanent 100 per cent bonus depreciation. The research credit sits on top of that.

The questions became a formal accusation two weeks after the Times story. On October 14, 2026, seven Democratic senators on the Finance Committee, among them Ron Wyden, Elizabeth Warren and Sheldon Whitehouse, wrote to Zuckerberg accusing Meta of a “violation of the spirit and, potentially, the letter of the tax law,” as Kashmir Hill reported for The New York Times. They asked about the $6.6 billion in research credits Meta has claimed over the last three years, wanted the accounting behind it, and asked how much had been claimed for “microchips, computing equipment and other materials necessary to build or operate A.I. data centers.” They also asked about the $355 million break on Zuckerberg’s compensation a decade ago. The tax experts’ example of a project that genuinely qualifies, Hill noted, is the kind of thing Part XXI described: Google’s mini data centre on a satellite, which might fail. Meta’s spokesman, Andy Stone, answered that “unlike traditional data centers, A.I.-optimized data centers are inherently new and require extensive testing and experimentation that cannot be simulated in order to get the design and build right.” That is the experiment argument, stated by the company itself. It is the opposite of what Meta tells its shareholders every quarter.

There is a final irony, and it is the one Schrödinger would have enjoyed. If the data centres really are an experiment whose outcome is uncertain, then the investors who have bid Meta’s value so high should be told so more plainly. If they are the sure thing Zuckerberg describes, the credit should not apply. So far, Meta has found a way to have it both ways. The box will be opened when the I.R.S. finally audits it, or when the market reaches its own verdict on the build-out, whichever comes first.
