Every serious essayist eventually has to sit down across the table from someone who knows more than they do about the subject, and answer for what they've written. That is what this essay is. Simon Sebag Montefiore is a Cambridge-trained historian, author of Jerusalem: The Biography and a shelf of other prize-winning books; his new work, The Cauldron: The Making of the Modern Middle East, was published on August 27, 2026, to the kind of reception most writers only dream about. On September 16, 2026, he sat for an hour-long interview with David Frum, staff writer at The Atlantic and host of The David Frum Show, titled "The Atrocities That Shaped the Modern Middle East." I am not a historian. I am a retired telecom executive who spent this year writing thirteen parts of a series called "Israel/Palestine: The Original Sin and Its Compound Interest," and two addenda since. This essay doesn't pretend to out-argue Montefiore on his own terrain — it takes his strongest points seriously, on their own terms, and asks what survives the encounter.

I want to start with agreement, not disagreement, because a debate essay that opens with combat reads as a strawman-hunt, and this one isn’t meant to be. There is real overlap between what Montefiore describes and what this series has already argued, and it’s worth naming before getting to the parts where we part ways.

Where the accounts already agree

The single clearest point of agreement is the arbitrariness of the colonial map. Montefiore's account of Iraq's creation is almost gleeful in its detail: a country stitched together by the British out of three Ottoman vilayets — Basra, Baghdad, and Mosul — with Mosul kept in the new state specifically because that was where the oil was, over Turkish objections and against the interests of the Kurdish population who lived there and who, as Montefiore notes, "were betrayed by everybody." He tells a nearly identical story about Syria and Lebanon, where the French mandate authorities briefly considered carving Greater Syria into five or six separate states along sectarian lines — two Sunni statelets, a Druze one, an Alawite one on the coast, a Christian Maronite one — before settling for two. This is precisely the argument Part IV and Part V of this series make about the Balfour Declaration and the Mandate for Palestine: that the borders and political structures of the modern Middle East were drawn by distant men serving distant interests, with only incidental regard for the people who would have to live inside the lines. Montefiore does not treat Palestine as an exception to this pattern. He treats it as an instance of it.

The second point of agreement is the pattern of great powers making promises they had no intention of honouring, or no capacity to honour, once the moment of leverage passed. Montefiore’s aside about Woodrow Wilson is a gift to this series’ American-veto material: Wilson personally backed the idea of an American mandate to govern and protect a new Armenian state, went home, and failed to secure it through Congress or even the League of Nations he himself had championed. That failure directly enabled the Armenian nation to be reduced to a diaspora rather than a state — a hollow guarantee is not meaningfully different from no guarantee, just slower to reveal itself as one. Part XII of this series has already made the case, at greater length, that the United States has run a recurring version of that same pattern with Israel and Palestine for seventy-five years — the institutional mechanism isn’t identical (Wilson failed to midwife a state that didn’t yet exist; the American veto record concerns a state that already exists blocking recognition of one that doesn’t), but the underlying move, a hollow guarantee doing the work a kept one should have done, rhymes across both cases: rhetorical commitment to a two-state solution, forty-five vetoes protecting the other side, and nothing in between.

The third point, smaller but genuinely useful, is Montefiore’s account of the Farhud — the 1941 Baghdad pogrom in which a self-consciously Nazi-influenced militia went through the city marking Jewish homes before the killing started, and women were raped and their bodies mutilated. Baghdad’s Jewish community was, as Montefiore put it, a third of the city’s population at the time, and one of the oldest Jewish communities on earth. It’s worth being precise about the mechanism rather than compressing it: the Farhud itself killed on the order of a few hundred people and did not immediately empty the city. What actually ended the community was the legal campaign that followed almost a decade later — the 1950–51 denaturalization laws, the seizure of Jewish property, and the mass airlifts (Operations Ezra and Nehemiah) to the new state of Israel. The pogrom opened the door; the statutes closed it behind them. If Part VI of this series treats 1948 primarily through the lens of Palestinian displacement — which is, on its own terms, the correct centre of gravity for a Nakba narrative — this two-stage history is a fair prompt to widen the frame. Two population displacements were underway across the same years, in opposite directions, for different but not entirely unrelated reasons. Acknowledging the Mizrahi exodus doesn’t dilute the moral weight of the Nakba. It closes an obvious gap that a hostile reader would otherwise use to dismiss the whole account as one-sided.

That’s the shared terrain. What follows is where the two accounts genuinely diverge — not stylistically, but on questions of fact and framing that matter to how the whole conflict gets narrated.

Point One: Is Israel’s founding sui generis, or one case of a regional pattern?

Montefiore’s most direct challenge to a series titled “The Original Sin” is his refusal to treat 1948 as uniquely anything. He places Israel alongside Turkey and Pakistan as three roughly contemporaneous examples of a single phenomenon: new ethno-national states carved out of collapsing multiethnic empires through war, population transfer, and the expulsion of a rival indigenous or near-indigenous population. The Greek-Turkish population exchange following the 1922 Greco-Turkish War and the burning of Smyrna is his closest parallel — “a new concept of a new nation created with ethnic and religious characteristics... a brutal ethnic war... in which many of them are expelled.” He’s explicit that no one treats Turkish nationalism as illegitimate on that basis, and that Turkey’s Kurdish population remains unreconciled to this day in a way that, on his account, should trouble critics of Israel just as much as Israel’s Palestinian population does, if the underlying objection is really about ethno-national state formation rather than about Israel specifically.

This is a genuinely strong argument, and it deserves a real answer rather than a dismissal. The honest response is that the comparison holds at the level of mechanism and breaks at the level of origin. Turkey’s founding violence was carried out by an indigenous population — Anatolian Turks who had lived in that territory for centuries — against another population, ethnic Greeks, who had also lived there for millennia but who had an external patron state, mainland Greece, willing and able to absorb them afterward. Whatever one thinks of the ethics of that war, it was, structurally, two long-resident Anatolian populations fighting each other for control of the same territory, with one side eventually retreating to an existing homeland. Zionism’s founding claim was different in kind, not just in scale: it was a movement organized, funded, and largely staffed from outside the territory it sought to control, explicitly modelled — Herzl himself used the word — on European colonial expansion, whose stated founding document, the Jewish Colonial Trust, was named for exactly what it was understood to be at the time. That is a meaningfully different starting condition than two neighbouring populations fighting a war of succession over shared ground. The Pakistan comparison is closer in some ways — Partition was also, in part, externally negotiated by a departing colonial power — but even there, both post-Partition populations, Hindu and Muslim, were drawn from the subcontinent itself; nobody was importing a diaspora from Europe to populate either resulting state.

So: Montefiore is right that Israel’s founding was not unique in its violence, its population displacement, or its use of war to consolidate a new ethno-national state. He’s on much shakier ground arguing it was not unique in its origin. The “original sin” framing survives this challenge, but has to be more precise about what it’s claiming — not that Israel was uniquely brutal in the manner of its founding, but that its founding began from a starting point (external colonization of an already-inhabited territory) that Turkey’s and Pakistan’s did not.

This part narrows what the series’ title claims going forward: not uniquely violent or uniquely sinful in manner, but a different starting point. A hostile reader is entitled to hold the title to that narrower claim from here on.

That’s a first move, though, not a finished argument. Three objections a historian of Montefiore’s kind would put on the table next: there was already a Jewish population in Ottoman Palestine before political Zionism existed as an organized movement, the Old Yishuv, present for centuries and imported by no one. A substantial share of Israel’s Jewish population today is Mizrahi and Sephardic, descended from communities already resident across the Middle East and North Africa rather than from the European organizers of the Zionist institutions (see Point Four). And unlike Algeria, there was no metropole extracting value from the colony — no French treasury the Yishuv was enriching. None of that erases the institutional fact taken up directly in Point Three, but it does mean “colonial origin” carries real complications rather than being a closed case. A note on standing: the sentences above about Herzl’s own language and the Jewish Colonial Trust’s name are documentary — his words, the institution’s own choice of name. That this makes the origin “structurally distinct” from Turkey’s is my reading of that evidence, not a settled historical finding, and a fair reader should weigh it as exactly that: an argument, not a verdict.

Point Two: Does the word “genocide” belong anywhere near Gaza?

This is the sharpest and most personally pointed claim in the interview, and it deserves to be engaged honestly rather than either waved away or fully conceded. Both Frum and Montefiore argue that applying “genocide” to the war in Gaza degrades a word that should be reserved for events of the scale and singular intent they document elsewhere in the book: roughly 1.1 to 2 million Armenians killed between 1895 and 1918, 300,000 Maronite Christians, hundreds of thousands of Pontic Greeks, hundreds of thousands of Assyrian Christians, the Yazidi genocide of 2014, and Saddam Hussein’s chemical-weapons campaign against the Kurds. Frum’s framing is unambiguous: he calls the widespread use of “genocide” for Gaza “something strange” and, at the close of the interview, floats the suggestion that devaluing the word serves a political purpose — indicting Israel while absolving whoever benefits from the world forgetting the Armenians, the Assyrians, and the Kurds.

There’s a real distinction buried in this argument worth taking seriously on its own merits: intent versus outcome. The legal definition of genocide under the 1948 Convention turns centrally on specific intent to destroy a group “as such,” not merely on high casualty counts or civilian suffering, however severe. That is a genuinely higher bar than “large number of civilians killed in a war,” and Montefiore is not wrong that plenty of public discourse uses the word loosely, in ways that would fail that legal test for almost any modern war.

But this argument, as deployed in the interview, also does something it shouldn’t: it treats “has anyone formally proven specific intent in a court” as the only question worth asking, while the actual record includes evidence that cuts the other way. This series’ Part XI already documents the relevant facts rather than the rhetorical framing: the International Court of Justice’s January 2024 order found it plausible that at least some of the rights South Africa asserted under the Genocide Convention were at risk, and ordered provisional measures on that basis — narrower than “the Court found genocide plausible,” the shorthand this paragraph should not fall into, but still a real threshold finding from the actual body with jurisdiction to make it, not a hashtag. Statements from senior Israeli officials explicitly invoking the language of total siege and collective punishment, cited extensively by international law scholars, are part of the intent question that “was there wartime civilian death” alone does not settle. None of this constitutes a completed legal finding of genocide; the case remains open. But it also means the word’s use here isn’t simply innumerate hyperbole standing in contrast to Montefiore’s documented atrocities — it’s a live, contested legal question being adjudicated by exactly the kind of body whose findings this series otherwise treats as authoritative.

The honest position, and the one this series should hold going forward — in its own back catalogue first, not only the wider discourse — is neither Frum’s “the word plainly doesn’t belong here” nor an uncritical “it’s settled.” It’s: the ICJ found sufficient risk to Convention-protected rights to warrant provisional measures, not a finding of genocide itself, the underlying case remains open, and a series invoking the term should say so precisely rather than using it as an unqualified noun. That’s a real correction worth making — aimed at this series’ own usage first, not because Montefiore and Frum won the argument.

One gap remains, on substance rather than semantics. This section has answered the lexicon and the Court’s finding. It has not answered the wartime half of what Frum and Montefiore are actually arguing — that October 7 itself, Hamas’s charter and its tactics, and the underlying difficulty of urban warfare against a combatant force embedded in civilian infrastructure all belong inside any honest discussion of intent, not just Israeli conduct considered in isolation. If Part XI already made that case, it isn’t being relitigated here. If it didn’t, that’s a debt this series still owes, and a reader of this interview is entitled to notice its absence rather than assume it’s been quietly settled.

Point Three: Settler colonialism, or nation-building-through-war?

Montefiore’s Turkey/Pakistan framing implicitly offers a rival vocabulary to “settler colonialism” — nation-building through population exchange and war — without ever directly confronting the settler-colonial literature this series and others draw on. This is worth naming as a gap in the interview rather than a rebuttal within it: Montefiore never actually engages the specific institutional evidence for the settler-colonial framing, so there’s nothing here to steelman beyond the general comparative point already addressed above. The Jewish National Fund’s land-purchase and land-tenure practices, which functioned to exclude Arab tenant labour from land once it changed hands; the Histadrut’s “Hebrew Labour” campaigns explicitly organized to displace Arab workers from the pre-state economy; the demographic planning documents produced by the Jewish Agency in the 1930s and 1940s — these are institutional facts about how the Yishuv actually operated, and they fit the settler-colonial model (an external population using organized capital to displace and replace an indigenous economy and demographic base) considerably better than they fit “two indigenous populations fighting a war,” regardless of what the eventual war of 1948 came to resemble tactically. The two framings aren’t actually mutually exclusive — a settler-colonial project can and did culminate in a war that looked, in its final mechanics, like the population-exchange wars Montefiore describes elsewhere. And Montefiore’s frame is a genuine rival vocabulary, not a strawman to wave off; it simply never engages the JNF, the Histadrut, or the Jewish Agency’s demographic planning directly. So the fair description isn’t that this point goes to this series because the interview “lost” it; it’s narrower: the specific institutional evidence relied on here was never actually contested in this exchange, and stands, for now, inside this particular argument. The counter Montefiore would likely raise, if he’d taken the bait: JNF land purchases were legal transactions under Ottoman and later Mandate law, and “Hebrew Labour” can be read as ordinary nation-building economic policy — most new states privilege their own citizens’ labour market — rather than evidence of a uniquely colonial device. That’s a real counter, not a dodge, and this series hasn’t yet answered it; it simply isn’t the counter this particular interview raised.

Point Four: The Mizrahi exodus and the Farhud

Already addressed above in “where the accounts agree” — but it’s worth being explicit that this is the one point where Montefiore’s account should change what this series says going forward, not just how it’s argued. A future revision or addendum to Part VI should note that the same decade that produced the Nakba also produced the effective end of millennia-old Jewish communities across Baghdad, Cairo, and elsewhere, driven out by violence, expropriation, and state-sanctioned expulsion rather than by choice. This is not a “both sides” equivalence claim — the scale, the mechanism, and the ongoing consequences of the two displacements are not identical, and treating them as morally interchangeable would be its own kind of evasion. It’s simply a fact this series should state plainly rather than omit, because a series that claims to be “an honest accounting” loses credibility exactly at the points where it’s selectively silent.

It’s also worth stating this alongside Point One as one account, not two separate concessions reached for depending on which argument is in front of the reader at the time. Zionism was, per Point One, a movement substantially organized and financed from outside the territory, on an explicitly European colonial model. And, within the same decade, the state that project produced also became the receiving country for Jewish populations expelled from Baghdad, Cairo, and elsewhere in the Arab world. Both are true at once. They describe one founding — a European-organized national project that also became a refuge, sometimes the only available one, for a different and non-European Jewish population displaced by different events — rather than two competing origin stories to be selected between paragraph by paragraph.

A note on the messenger

One caveat is worth stating plainly, because a debate essay that doesn’t distinguish its sources is doing something less than honest work. Montefiore’s book and his on-air arguments aren’t identical to Frum’s framing of the conversation, and the two deserve to be weighed separately — Frum wrote much of the interview’s sharpest editorializing (the Yazidi opening, the closing argument about deliberate word-devaluation) and isn’t a neutral moderator. As The New Republic’s Rachel Kahn reported on October 8, 2025, hacked emails showed Frum, while under contract to The Atlantic in 2014, ghostwrote a draft speech for Israel’s then-UN ambassador Ron Prosor defending Israel’s Gaza conduct that year, at the same time he was writing a sympathetic Atlantic profile of Prosor — which The New Republic called an “egregious ethical breach.” The Atlantic’s response, through spokesperson Anna Bross, maintained Frum wasn’t a staff employee in 2014 and had advised “a friend, pro bono.” That dispute is unresolved, but it’s relevant to weighing how much interpretive work is Frum’s versus Montefiore’s actual scholarship. None of this touches Montefiore’s credibility — his arguments here are engaged on their own merits. It does mean this interview reads as an advocacy-inflected conversation, not a neutral scholarly exchange, and the series should stay precise about which claims belong to which speaker.

What survives, and what bends

Running the account: the “original sin” framing survives the sui generis challenge, but only in a narrower form than the title implies on its own — external colonization of an inhabited territory, not unique founding brutality — and that narrower claim itself has open objections (the Old Yishuv, the substantial Mizrahi share of Israel’s Jewish population, the missing metropole) still to be argued through rather than assumed settled. The genocide question doesn’t resolve in either direction: the ICJ’s finding is narrower than the popular shorthand (”risk to Convention rights was plausible enough for provisional measures,” not “genocide was found plausible”), the case remains open, and this series owes that precision in its own back catalogue, not just going forward — and it still owes an answer to the wartime half of the argument, October 7 and the urban-warfare problem included, that this essay has flagged rather than settled. The settler-colonial framing stands, for now, inside this specific exchange — genuinely uncontested here, not proven against the purchase-and-labour counter a more adversarial historian would raise. And the Mizrahi exodus is a real gap that should be closed, on the series’ own terms, stated in the same breath as the colonial-origin claim rather than beside it as a separate concession.

That’s not a draw, and it’s not a rout. It’s what an honest accounting is supposed to produce: a series that’s harder to attack after the argument than before it, because the places where it was vulnerable have been named rather than defended past the point they could bear.
