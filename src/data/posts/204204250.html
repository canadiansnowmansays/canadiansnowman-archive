Deep into that darkness peering, long I stood there wondering, fearing. — Edgar Allan Poe, “The Raven”

By now you know the ritual. I count. The number means something. I pretend to be surprised, and you pretend to believe me, and Dior pretends I am not there at all, which is the only honest performance in the room.

But this one is different, and I want to be upfront about why.

The others came to me. William the Conqueror arrived at 1,066 with an army and a memorable arrow. The Magna Carta turned up at 1,215 with a damp meadow and a reluctant king. Marco Polo appeared at 1,271 with twenty-four years of dust on his boots and a deathbed line I still can’t shake. Each number knocked on the door, and I answered, and we had our chat about conquest or law or curiosity, and the husky sighed, and the essay wrote itself.

This number did not knock. This number I had to go looking for, because 1313 — I will say it plainly — does not want to be written about. It sits there on the calendar of my output like a guest no one invited, and when I went to find out what happened in the year 1313, history handed me back a shrug. A Holy Roman Emperor died, Henry VII, of malaria or possibly poison, in Italy, which in the fourteenth century was the cause of death for approximately everyone. Dante was somewhere in the middle of the Divine Comedy, busy arranging his enemies into circles of Hell, which is the most relatable thing a writer has ever done. The Scots were being Scottish at the English. It was, by the standards of the years I have been visiting, an off night.

Except that an empty year is never empty. It only looks that way — the way a room looks empty in the half-second before you notice what is in the corner. 1313 is not insignificant. 1313 is the calm. And something is already moving, east of the count, out past the edge of what anyone alive in that year could possibly know: in the dry grass of the steppe, in a flea, in the warm dark of a rat’s fur on a ship that has not yet been built. Thirty-four years up the road from 1313, the rat reaches a harbour in Sicily and a third of the continent dies. The people of 1313 cannot feel it coming. I, sitting here with the whole map of the count spread out in front of me, can. That is the difference between us, and it is not an advantage.

And yet I find I cannot treat 1313 as just another number. Because it is not just another number. It is the number, doubled. It is thirteen, twice, sitting in a row, daring you to walk under it.

So this essay is not about the year. It is about the fear.

Let us begin with the proper name, because there is one, and it is magnificent: triskaidekaphobia. The fear of the number thirteen. From the Greek treiskaideka, thirteen, and phobos, fear, and the wonderful fact that someone, somewhere, decided this dread was common enough and specific enough to deserve eighteen letters of clinical Greek all to itself.

It is everywhere, once you look. Buildings skip the thirteenth floor — the elevator goes 11, 12, 14, as though the architects could fool the void by simply declining to label it. My own building did not get the memo. It not only kept its thirteenth floor; it made it the good one. Thirteen is where you ride up to reach the rooftop terrace — the open sky, the long view over the city, the best square footage in the place. While every other tower in the country was pretending twelve is followed by fourteen, the people who built mine looked at the cursed number and decided it should be where the residents go to stand in the sun. I have made my peace with thirteen, is what I am telling you. I take the elevator to it on purpose. Remember that I told you this. Airlines, for their part, skip the thirteenth row. The Italians, contrarians to the end, do not fear thirteen at all; they fear seventeen, because the Roman numeral XVII can be rearranged into VIXI, Latin for “I have lived,” which on a tombstone means “I am now dead,” which is the kind of thing that happens when you let a dead language linger. The afflicted have a word for the specific dread of Friday the thirteenth, too, and I am not going to attempt to spell it, because it has sixteen syllables and I have a husky to feed.

I want to be clear that I am reporting all this from a position of perfect calm. I am a rational man. A retired businessman. I have a spreadsheet. I do not believe a number can hurt me. I am writing essay 1313 with the same steady hand I brought to 1,066 and 1,215, and the fact that I have now mentioned my steadiness three times is not, I would argue, evidence of anything.

The thing about phobias is that they are reason’s embarrassing relatives. They show up to the family dinner of the rational mind and say something appalling and refuse to leave.

Consider the catalogue, because the catalogue is genuinely a comfort — there is nothing like other people’s fears to make your own feel reasonable. There is arachibutyrophobia, the fear of peanut butter sticking to the roof of your mouth, which is less a phobia than a Tuesday. There is hippopotomonstrosesquippedaliophobia, the fear of long words, which is either the cruellest coinage in the language or its finest practical joke, because the sufferer cannot say what is wrong with them; the doctor who diagnoses it must, I assume, write it down and slide the paper across the desk in silence. There is nomophobia, the fear of being without your mobile phone, which began as a joke and has quietly become the actual operating condition of the entire species. There is, I am told, a fear of the number 666, hexakosioihexekontahexaphobia, for those who find a single doubled thirteen insufficiently ambitious.

And it goes further than that, into territory so specific you begin to suspect the human mind will attach dread to literally anything you leave lying around. There is genuphobia, the fear of knees — not of injuring them, simply of their existence, the bare fact of a knee. There is turophobia, the fear of cheese, which in a country that put poutine on the national menu strikes me as a genuine disability. There is pogonophobia, the fear of beards, which I take a little personally, and which would not survive five minutes in this household. Somewhere out there is a person who cannot enter a room I am in.

I find this entire menagerie strangely reassuring, the way the back pages of an old medical encyclopedia are reassuring. Every one of these is a human being who woke up one day and discovered that the alarm system had been wired to the wrong door — that the same machinery built to keep our ancestors away from the cliff edge and the cave bear had, in their case, been quietly connected to a wheel of brie. The fear is real. The brie is not the problem. The fear simply needed somewhere to go.

Because here is the thing the silly ones are politely distracting us from. Some alarms are not misfires. Underneath the cheese and the knees, there is an older catalogue, and it is not funny at all.

The fear of snakes. The fear of spiders. The fear of heights — of the edge, of the long drop, of the small treacherous voice that speaks at the railing. The fear of deep water, of the thing below the surface that you cannot see and that can see you. The fear of being closed in, buried, unable to move. And the oldest one, the one we are all born with and most of us never fully shed: the fear of the dark. Not of the dark itself — the dark is only an absence — but of what the dark is permitted to contain. The child who needs the closet door shut is not afraid of the closet. He is afraid of what uses the dark as a doorway.

These are not embarrassing relatives. These are the founding members. Every absurd phobia in the encyclopedia is a descendant of these — the same ancient circuitry, the same jolt of the animal saying not safe, not safe, get away — only misfired, pointed at a vegetable instead of a viper. The machinery is sound. It is forty thousand years old and it kept your ancestors alive long enough to produce, eventually, you. It is only the aim that has wandered.

And there is one more, which I have saved for last because it does not behave like the others, and behaving differently is precisely what frightens me about it. The fear of the rat. The recoil — instant, bodily, older than reason — from the small grey shape at the edge of the granary, in the wall, in the ship’s hold. For most of human history this looked like exactly the kind of misfire I have been cataloguing. A rat is small. A rat does not bite the way a snake bites. The medieval mind had no germ theory, no notion of a flea, no idea that the thing to fear was not the rat at all but the passenger riding in its fur, the bite you would never feel, the swelling that came three days later. The peasant who flinched from the rat could not have told you why. He had no evidence. He looked, to his neighbours, as superstitious as a man who fears the number thirteen.

And he was right. That is the part I cannot get past. Of every fear in the whole encyclopedia, the one that looked most like superstition was the one telling the plainest truth — six hundred years ahead of the science that would finally explain it. The alarm was not misfiring. The alarm was simply early. It had read something the conscious mind would not be able to confirm for half a millennium, and it said get away from the rat, and the people who listened lived, and the people who reasoned it away did not.

So beware the proximity of any rat. I mean it more than I expected to when I started the sentence.

And then there is the one I keep circling back to, the one that does not have a silly name because there is nothing silly about it: the fear that is not attached to anything at all. The free-floating kind. The dread that does not require a snake or a height or a thirteenth floor, that simply arrives, sits down at the foot of the bed in the small hours, and waits.

I am familiar with that one. I suspect you are too. I suspect it is the parent of all of them, silly and primal alike — that the cheese and the knees and the snakes and the dark are all just the mind’s attempts to give the nameless thing a name, a shape, a floor number, a costume, anything at all to make it small enough to skip in the elevator. We would rather be afraid of a number than of nothing. A number, at least, you can see coming.

Here is what I have actually learned, sitting with this number that did not want to be written.

The fear is never about the thing. It is never really about thirteen, or the floor, or the Friday, or the peanut butter. The number is innocent. 1313 is just 1312 plus one, the same as every number is just the last one plus one, the same as today is just yesterday plus one, the same as I am just the man I was at essay 1,066 plus two hundred and forty-seven days of typing. The dread we hang on the number is dread we were already carrying. We just needed somewhere to set it down.

And that, I think, is the actual reason this essay fought me. Not because 1313 is unlucky. Because the counting itself — the thing I have been doing this whole time, the thing I have dressed up as William and the barons and Marco Polo, the thing I have made charming for you across four of these now — the counting has a direction. It only goes one way. Every milestone I have celebrated is also a milestone closer to the last one. Every round number I have pretended to be surprised by is the calendar of my output and, if I am honest, the calendar of something else.

I have been counting up. But a man of seventy-four is, by every measure that matters, counting down. The two counts are the same count. I just never said it out loud until the number with two thirteens in it made me.

And there is worse, now that I have let myself look at the map. I have been telling you, for four of these essays, that I do not choose the numbers — that they arrive, that I am merely the man at the desk when they land. I have made this charming. I should not have. Because if the numbers arrive on their own, I do not get to choose which one is coming, and I have seen which one is coming. It is not 1,492. Before Columbus, the count passes through 1347 — thirty-four numbers up the road, a little over a month at my pace. You cannot skip a floor that has already booked you. The rat is already in the fur. The ship is already at sea.

And now I must confess something, because if I do not put it down here it will sit in my chest all night, and I have enough sitting in my chest tonight.

Three of the thirteens arrived without me.

I did not arrange the year — 1313 came up in the count the way 1,066 and 1,215 and 1,271 came up, which is to say on its own, at its own pace, while I was busy with something else. I did not arrange the essay number; that is simply what happens when a man writes every day for long enough. And I did not arrange the date. I checked the calendar the way you check a phone number you have already dialled, hoping to have misread it, and I had not: at my present pace, essay one thousand three hundred and thirteen, about the year thirteen thirteen, falls on the thirteenth of July. Not a Friday. The universe declined to be that obvious, and I find I respect it for the restraint. An ordinary Monday.

Three thirteens, handed to me, unasked.

Here is the confession. I looked at those three, sitting there in a row like something left on a doorstep, and I found I could not leave them alone. I have scheduled this essay to publish at thirteen minutes past the thirteenth hour — one-thirteen in the afternoon — because three was not enough and I wanted a fourth, and because if you are the sort of person who notices that 13:13 is the same number written twice, that makes five.

Nobody asked me to do that. The count had done its work; it owed me nothing further. I was handed a coincidence, and rather than simply report it to you, the way I have reported every other coincidence in this series, I reached into it with my own hand and added to it.

At 1,215 I told you I had briefly considered accelerating the count so that the Magna Carta essay would land on the anniversary, and that I had run the numbers and reconsidered, because that way lies madness and I am a rational man with a spreadsheet. I want you to remember that I reconsidered. I want it noted that once, at least, I had the sense to leave a number where I found it.

I did not leave this one where I found it.

I told myself it was a joke — a small private flourish, the kind of thing a man does for his own amusement at seventy-four when the alternative is thinking about other matters. But sitting here now, in the dark, with the essay finished and the minute set, it does not feel like a joke. It feels like an appointment I made rather than one I was given, which is worse, because it means that when the clock reaches 1:13 tomorrow I will have nobody to blame for my being in this chair. The other three were the count’s doing. The fourth one is mine. I went to some trouble to be present at a particular minute, and I no longer entirely remember why I wanted to be.

It is late as I finish this — past midnight, into the small hours, the hours when a clock striking thirteen would mean something had gone badly wrong with the count of things. You will not read it until the afternoon, at the thirteenth hour, at thirteen minutes past, because that one is my doing and I have already confessed to it. But I am writing it now, in the dark, twelve hours ahead of you, and I want you to understand the arrangement: when the essay reaches you at 1:13, I will already have been sitting here for half a night, waiting for it to go.

The house is quiet. The boarders are asleep, Ugyen is asleep, and I am at the desk with the second monitor turned off because its glow felt, tonight, like company I had not invited.

I reached down a moment ago to scratch Dior behind the ears — the old reliable gesture, the one that closes a hundred of these essays, the husky at my feet, unimpressed, eternal.

She was already looking at the doorway.

She had been looking at it, I realized, for some time. Ears up. Very still. Not at me, not at the door itself, but past it — at the dark hallway beyond, the way a dog looks at a thing a man cannot see and was not meant to. The closet door. The doorway the dark uses.

And then, all at once, the catalogue came for me — not the silly ones, the founding members, in order, the way I had laid them out a thousand words ago as though I were not writing my own instructions.

The dark first. The hallway past the doorway is very dark, and I am suddenly certain it is not empty, and the certainty is not a thought, it is a temperature, it is the back of my neck. Then the height — though I am sitting, though there is no edge, the floor has gone soft and far away, that long-drop lurch with nowhere to drop. Then the closed-in feeling, the walls of the study a half-inch nearer than they were, the air thinner, the door behind me that I would have to pass to leave. Then the thing below the surface, except there is no water, only the dark, and something in it has noticed the noticing. My own pulse in my ears like something approaching at a distance and not stopping.

I told myself it was nothing. A draft. A settling house. The aim wanders, I told myself. The machinery misfires. There is no viper. There is only the brie. I am a man who rides the elevator up to the thirteenth floor on purpose, I told myself. I made my peace with this number. I told you I did.

But the machinery is forty thousand years old, and tonight it is not misfiring. Tonight, for the first time, every wire is aimed at exactly the thing it was built for, all of them at once, the full primal catalogue lighting up in a body that spent its whole life laughing at the cheese — and the alarm is not wrong. That is the part I cannot talk myself out of. I keep thinking about the peasant who flinched from the rat with no evidence and no germ theory and no name for what he feared, and was right, six hundred years early. I keep thinking that the oldest alarm is the one that reads what the conscious mind cannot yet confirm. The alarm has never in forty thousand years been this loud in me, and the alarm is not wrong, and somewhere down the count a ship is already at sea, and Dior is still looking at the doorway, and I am a rational man — I am, I am a rational man, I have a spreadsheet, I am calm, I would like that on the record, I am perfectly calm — and the peace I made with thirteen does not appear to have been mutual.

I find that I do not want to turn around to see what she is looking at.

I find that I cannot.

And under all of it, steady, getting louder, not stopping — the pulse in my ears, the one I told you about, the sound that has never once in forty thousand years been wrong about what is coming. It is in the room with me now. I have my hand on my chest. It is not my heart. It never was. And it is getting louder, and it is coming from the hallway, and Dior has not moved, and neither, God help me, have I.

In a few hours this will publish itself, at the minute I chose, whether or not I am at the desk to watch it go. That is how scheduling works. I set it, and it will happen, and my presence is no longer required for the thing to occur. The count brought me three thirteens and I added a fourth, and now the fourth one is out of my hands as well.

I have never before written a sentence that frightened me while I was writing it.

I will see you at essay 1,492. Perhaps much sooner.

If the count gets there. If I do.
