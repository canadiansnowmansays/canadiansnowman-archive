This ledger has followed the political cost of the data-centre build-out from the bottom up. Part IX watched a Missouri councilman recalled over a tax break. Part XI read the Republican Senate committee’s own memo calling data centres a “sleeper issue for the entire election cycle” and conceding the industry’s “toxic brand.” Part XII followed the teenagers organising against the campuses in their towns. Part XVII watched Josh Shapiro go from standing beside the pledges to calling developers “predatory.” Each was a local story. On September 29, 2026, the election-dispatch team at The New York Times — Patricia Mazzei, John Yoon and Benjamin Oreskes — reported that the sleeper issue had woken up inside the president’s own party, five weeks before the midterm elections of November 3.

The meeting and the fissure

The occasion was President Trump’s meeting on Tuesday, September 29, with leaders of the A.I. industry, part of a White House push to accelerate data-centre expansion. At that lunch, as Asimov Gap Part 135 recorded, he dismissed the backlash as a Democratic hoax and predicted the buildings would soon be “very popular.” The meeting put Republicans running in competitive House and Senate races in what the Times called “the uneasy position of having to distance themselves from one of the president’s priorities.” More than half a dozen Republican candidates, the report said, had aligned themselves with the voter backlash against data centres and with widespread fears about A.I.’s pace — some calling for construction moratoriums, others demanding protections against higher electricity bills.

That is a genuine fissure, and a telling one. The Republican Party’s national leadership, from the president down, treats data centres as the infrastructure of national greatness and the race with China. Its candidates in swing districts treat them as a liability to be managed. The memo Part XI quoted predicted exactly this; it recommended a “Ratepayer Protection Pledge” and a script about losing the A.I. race to China. With five weeks to go, some candidates were skipping the script and going straight to the pledge.

The roster had been growing since the summer. Fox News counted it on September 3, 2026, under a headline about MAGA beginning to crack. Mike Rogers, the Republican Senate nominee in Michigan, wanted a one-year moratorium on new data-centre construction: “Michigan needs stronger guardrails to protect community control, prevent utility price hikes, protect our water, and stop pay-to-play schemes.” Vivek Ramaswamy, running for governor of Ohio, promised a first-day executive order halting approvals of new projects. Stacy Garrity, the Republican nominee for governor of Pennsylvania and Josh Shapiro’s opponent, called for a temporary pause on new uncontracted large developments. Tom Tiffany in Wisconsin opposed new data centres unless strict conditions were met. In Ohio, Senator Jon Husted introduced a bill to keep ordinary ratepayers from bearing the energy costs, after the National Republican Senatorial Committee warned that the issue could cost him his seat. None of these people was a Democrat, and none was in a safe race.

The polling explains the stampede. A Fox News poll published on September 22, 2026 found 71 per cent of voters opposed to building A.I. data centres in their area, 46 per cent strongly. The same poll found 52 per cent in favour of building them in remote locations, which is the whole of the problem in one pair of numbers: Americans will accept the build-out as long as it happens somewhere else. Quinnipiac University, surveying from September 24 to 27, found 72 per cent of Americans opposed to an A.I. data centre in their community and 21 per cent in favour.

The New York swing seat

The clearest illustration is a debate on Monday, September 28, in New York’s Hudson Valley swing district between the Republican incumbent Mike Lawler and his Democratic challenger, Cait Conley — one of the most expensive House races in the country, with about $16 million already spent on advertising, according to AdImpact.

What is striking is that each candidate tried to paint the other as the tech industry’s creature. Lawler went after Conley’s work, after she left the Biden administration, for a company building anti-drone systems and another that used A.I. to scrape public sources for law-enforcement analysis, and her refusal to discuss it, citing non-disclosure agreements. “You left the Biden administration to get a private sector job with big A.I. and big tech to the tune of hundreds of thousands of dollars,” he said, “only to move up here two months after you got the contract.” Conley, a combat veteran, cast Lawler as a Trump loyalist and noted that Congressional Quarterly found he voted with the president’s position 94 per cent of the time in 2025.

On data centres themselves, they split cleanly. Conley, who has said data centres are needed, applauded Governor Kathy Hochul’s one-year pause on construction of the largest facilities, imposed in July — the same moratorium this ledger first recorded in Part IV — and said she backed such moratoriums. Lawler said the decision should be left to municipalities, and pitched reopening the Indian Point nuclear plant, closed in 2021, and permitting natural-gas extraction to bring energy prices down.

Conley made the issue local. CBS New York‘s account of the debate quotes her charge: “On Mike Lawler’s watch, there have been five data centers built in Rockland County. Five, with three more on the way.” Lawler’s answer was revealing in its own way: “The data centers that were built were built before I was in Congress, number one. Number two, they have nothing to do with AI.” A Republican incumbent was defending himself by saying the data centres in his district were neither his doing nor A.I.’s.

The record behind the exchange is less tidy than either candidate’s lines. New York Focus reported on September 16, 2026 that Lawler had attacked Conley on data centres while avoiding positions of his own on the local projects. He had criticised Hochul’s moratorium on Fox Business (”what she is saying is, ‘Don’t come here, go do your business elsewhere’”), and a Lawler advertisement had clipped Conley saying “We need data centers” at an April forum while omitting the rest of the sentence, in which she said communities should not “unknowingly agree to terms where these data centers are literally profiting off the backs of our communities.” Conley, for her part, had come out against the Databank expansion in Rockland County. Both candidates back the Ratepayer Protection Act; Lawler is a co-sponsor. The outlet also noted that Lawler’s campaign had taken donations from JPMorgan Chase and Morgan Stanley, both of which operate data centres in his district.

“Big A.I.” is now an insult

The detail worth underlining is not the positions but the vocabulary. “Big A.I. and big tech” is now something a Republican incumbent throws at a Democrat as an accusation, and “beholden to technology interests” is something each side accused the other of being. In 2024, association with the A.I. industry was a credential in a suburban district. It is now opposition research.

That is the same force Asimov Gap Part 132 documented from the money side. The A.I. industry’s super PACs spent $55.7 million in the primaries, and of 95 ads the Times reviewed, none mentioned data centres and only seven mentioned A.I. The companies are hiding their product from voters for the same reason Lawler is using it against his opponent: the public does not like it. Part 126 recorded the Times poll finding 61 per cent of likely voters opposed to data-centre construction. Politicians can read a poll.

The cost-transmission channel, completed

This ledger has catalogued the ways the build-out reaches ordinary people: through property taxes (Part IX), Treasury yields and mortgage rates (Part X), rents (Part XV), and health-insurance premiums (Part XXII). The political channel is the one that runs the other way. It is how the costs, having reached the household, come back to the people who approved the build-out — through the ballot.

Bethany McLean argued in Part XXIII that Americans’ dislike of the A.I. companies is the one thing that could break the “too big to stop” loop, and that a sufficiently shocking disaster or a regulatory crackdown could end the lavish funding suddenly. The midterms were the first test of that thesis at scale, and the answer came on November 3. Democrats took both houses of Congress. Voters in at least 10 states passed more than 30 local measures restricting data-centre construction and recalled at least three mayors who had backed projects, as Matteo Wong reported for The Atlantic. Distancing did not save the Republicans who tried it: in Ohio, Sherrod Brown, who put up billboards casting his opponent as the “face of data centers,” beat Jon Husted; Amy Acton beat Vivek Ramaswamy for governor, as Signal Ohio reported, promised first-day halt and all; and in Michigan, Abdul El-Sayed beat Mike Rogers. In the Hudson Valley, Lawler conceded to Conley at about 11 p.m. on election night, WAMC reported. Wong is careful to say that inflation, the Iran war and the president’s unpopularity probably mattered more than A.I. But the swing seats were not decided in a way the build-out can read as a licence.

The ledger

The entry is now simple. The sleeper issue the Senate Republicans’ memo warned about in August woke up, and then it voted. The president met the industry to accelerate construction; his candidates in close races promised to slow it down, and several of them lost anyway; and in one of the most expensive seats in the country, the incumbent who called his challenger an employee of Big A.I. lost to her. When the tech industry’s political committees are hiding the product and the tech industry’s political opponents are naming it, the industry has lost the argument in public, whatever it won in the primaries.
