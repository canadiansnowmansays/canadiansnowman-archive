By late April, this diary had reached Part LXVIII. By mid-May it was at Part LXXIII. By the end of July it had reached Part XC. The ten instalments since then have taken two months.

The war did not slow down. The coverage did. In the spring there was more news every morning than one essay could hold, and I wrote more than once a day to keep up. This week’s material came from live blogs, wire briefs, a think-tank tracker and one Sunday opinion column. The war is still killing sailors in the Strait of Hormuz, emptying hospitals in Yemen and pushing up the price of diesel in California. It has simply stopped being news in the way it was news in March.

That is the condition this hundredth instalment is written in. The number is not the story. What happened to the coverage is.

The Delivery Date

On Friday, October 2, the Pentagon announced that the USS Theodore Roosevelt carrier strike group and the USS Makin Island amphibious group were deploying to the Middle East. As Eleanor Watson and Frank Andrews reported for CBS News, that will put more than 20,000 sailors and Marines afloat in the region by late October, on top of the tens of thousands of American troops already ashore.

Readers of Part XCIX will remember the Wall Street Journal’s reporting that the President has told aides he expects to resume bombing Iran after the midterm elections on November 3. Now there is a deployment schedule to go with that expectation. The ships arrive in late October. The polls close in early November. Nobody has to say the two dates are connected. They sit beside each other on the calendar.

The same day, the senior national-security team spent hours at Camp David on Iran and the Saudi–Houthi war: Vice-President Vance, Secretary of State Rubio, Defence Secretary Hegseth, the envoy Steve Witkoff, CIA Director John Ratcliffe and General Dan Caine, chairman of the Joint Chiefs. No readout followed.

That night, at a rally in Mobile, Alabama,, the President offered this: “We could leave tomorrow, and it would take them years to rebuild.” Iran, he said, had “played their cards too tough.” The man who could leave tomorrow had spent the morning sending more ships.

The Third Front

Two days later, the war acquired another front. On Sunday, October 4, Rashad al-Alimi, who leads Yemen’s internationally recognised and Saudi-backed government, announced a military campaign to reclaim everything lost to the Houthis over the past twelve years. It would continue, he said, “until the country is liberated.” Within hours, the Saudi coalition’s spokesman, Major General Turki al-Maliki, pledged “full operational support” until the Houthis are “eliminated.”

As Vivian Nereim, Eric Schmitt and Saeed Al-Batati reported for the New York Times, this is the second time the Yemeni government has tried this with Saudi help. The first, in 2015, became a grinding war that contributed to hundreds of thousands of deaths from violence, hunger and disease, and it did not defeat the Houthis. Dan Shapiro, formerly a senior Pentagon official for the Middle East, called it “an open question” whether the Saudis and their Yemeni allies would do any better this time.

Readers of Part XCVIII will remember the President ordering airstrikes on the Houthis and cancelling them after a call from Mohammed bin Salman, with the bombs already loaded. Last month he rebuffed Saudi requests for American help. According to one current and one former U.S. official, though, more than 200 American intelligence and military analysts are working in command centres inside the kingdom, helping to analyse and select targets. The United States is also refuelling Saudi warplanes in the air. Central Command declined to comment. Washington said no to the war in public and is helping to run it from inside the kingdom.

The fighting is already moving faster than the announcements. On Sunday the Houthis took Al-Turbah, a government-held town on the main road between Taiz and Aden, leaving Taiz, one of Yemen’s largest cities, close to encircled. “It is the first time I have seen my neighbours’ children still awake at 3 a.m. because of the bombardment,” one resident told the Times. On Saturday, Times-verified video showed a fire at an Aramco refinery in Riyadh after a claimed Houthi missile and drone attack. The Saudi spokesman called the claim “misleading.” The World Health Organization counts more than 800 people killed in Yemen since early August, and the World Food Programme more than 150,000 displaced.

The Camp David meeting on Friday was about Iran and this war together. As of Sunday, they are harder to tell apart.

The Order of Steps

The diplomacy has not collapsed. It has stalled, which in this war is harder to report.

Washington’s reply to Tehran’s seven-day plan reached Iran through Qatari mediators on September 29 and 30. On October 1, Abbas Araghchi briefed President Pezeshkian on it at a cabinet meeting. As Middle East Monitor reported, drawing on Reuters, an Iranian official described the gap in one phrase. The dispute was over “the sequence of the steps, rather than the elements of the seven-day plan.” Axios, by the same account, found “no tangible progress” in Monday’s talks.

Put plainly, both governments agree on what a deal contains. The blockade lifts, the strait reopens and nuclear talks begin. They cannot agree on who moves first. Iran wants the blockade lifted before it opens Hormuz. Washington wants nuclear commitments before it lifts anything. A U.S. official told Al Jazeera there would be no agreement unless the nuclear programme was addressed.

This is the oldest problem in negotiation, and it is usually solved by people who trust each other a little. Neither side trusts the other at all. The June memorandum signed in Islamabad collapsed over exactly this question, who controlled the strait and in what order, and nothing since has given either government a reason to go first.

The President, for his part, called the plan “unacceptable.” He said of Iran, according to EA WorldView: “They are dying – the money isn’t coming in.” He described his blockade as “the greatest blockade in military history.” On September 28 he had posted “I offered them NOTHING!” in response to reports of possible sanctions relief. A U.S. official told CBS that he would in fact consider relief and the release of frozen funds in return for concrete nuclear progress. Those are the Threaten and the Obfuscate on the same weekend, as usual.

Tehran’s reply came from the secretary of its Supreme National Security Council: “You can win every day in your own imagination. But reality is shaped by Iran.” It is a good line. It lasted about a day.

On Saturday, October 3, the same official, Mohsen Rezaei, told a meeting of President Pezeshkian and senior ministers that Iran was in one of the most difficult periods it had ever faced. State media did not quote him directly, but IRNA reported that he “described the circumstances as among the most difficult periods the country has faced.” As Erika Solomon, Sanam Mahoozi and Zane Irwin reported for the New York Times, it was a rare admission. On Sunday the oil minister, Mohsen Paknejad, resigned. In July he had promised that Iran would keep exporting oil despite American sanctions. Nurses and teachers have posted online that they are quitting because their salaries no longer cover their lives. Retired civil servants have protested over unpaid pensions.

Both governments, the Times reported, are signalling that they can outlast the other’s tolerance for economic pain. Tehran’s analysts hope the pain reaches American voters before November 3. Washington is betting it reaches Iranian streets first. So the economic war runs on the same election calendar as the military one.

The Toll Booth Becomes Law

While the mediators carried messages, Iran’s parliament wrote one of its own. On October 2 it advanced legislation asserting Iranian management of the Strait of Hormuz, including fees on passing vessels and restrictions on Israeli ships and those of countries it deems hostile.

Since early summer this diary has followed the toll-booth thesis it borrowed from Parsi, Azizi and Landler. The argument was that Iran’s real war aim had become the power to charge for passage through the strait, not to win. In August, the reported Oman arrangement would have split “service fees” between the two countries. Now the Majlis is putting the toll booth into statute. A statute is harder to trade away at a negotiating table than a bargaining position, and that may be the point.

Saudi Arabia’s foreign minister told the General Assembly last week that conditions in the strait must return to what they were before February 28, “without imposing any fees or taxes.” Iran’s parliament has now answered him.

The Strait, in Numbers Nobody Agrees On

The administration says the strait is open. Peter Eavis’s September 29 report for the New York Times showed how hard that is to measure. Kpler, the tanker tracker, puts September flows through Hormuz at about 10 million barrels a day. TankerTrackers.com says 7.4 million. Before the war the figure was about 16 million. Admiral Brad Cooper of Central Command said a billion barrels had gone out “in the last couple months,” which would mean prewar levels. His spokesman later explained that “a couple” meant “a few,” and declined to give a daily average. Unnamed intelligence analysts and congressional Democrats told the Times they suspected the estimates had been inflated under White House pressure. The Pentagon denies it.

The secretary-general of the International Maritime Organization, Arsenio Dominguez, offered a simpler test. The strait “is not open to navigation, because it’s not safe.” His agency counts 85 ships attacked since the war began and 24 mariners dead.

This week added to the count. A tanker was struck near the strait on Friday afternoon, October 2. A second vessel was hit on Saturday about four nautical miles off Oman, and Fars, which is linked to the Revolutionary Guards, reported two tankers “exploded,” as Iran International relayed. Earlier in the week, UK Maritime Trade Operations reported strikes on an LNG carrier and a crude tanker. In one of the week’s stranger footnotes, Iraq sent two million barrels through Hormuz, its first large-scale tanker run in decades. The strait is open to some ships, on some days, at some risk. That is not what the President means by open.

The March of Folly

The most useful thing written about the war this week came from Nicholas Kristof’s column in the New York Times on October 3. He borrows Barbara Tuchman’s phrase “march of folly” and argues that the war “should be taught for decades as a model of how not to conduct either war or foreign policy.”

He also kept the receipts. Nine days into the war, in March, the President promised “military success, the likes of which people haven’t seen,” with lower oil prices to follow. Peter Navarro wrote an essay in March titled “Iran War Will Lower Energy Prices.” In April, Hegseth announced “a historic and overwhelming victory” that had left Iran “combat-ineffective for years to come.”

Against that, Kristof sets the bill. The war has cost more than $43 billion by a lowball Pentagon estimate. Higher prices have cost the typical American family about $1,000, by Mark Zandi’s estimate. Diesel has reached $9.99 a gallon at some California pumps, the highest price those pumps can display. The column also quotes Ali Vaez of the International Crisis Group, whose Foreign Affairs essay supplies this instalment’s title. “The unsettling reality is that the conflict is nowhere near over,” Vaez writes. He calls it “The New Forever War.”

Kristof’s most important point is not about the past. Vali Nasr of Johns Hopkins told him that Iran’s leaders “have come to the conclusion that the only way they survive is to shoot their way out.” Esfandyar Batmanghelidj warned that Iran “did not wage a scorched earth campaign — but it may yet do so if the current situation persists for too long.” The administration treats Iran’s economic pain as leverage. The people who study Iran treat it as a fuse.

Then there is the nuclear question, which the war was supposed to settle. Ali Khamenei had issued a fatwa against nuclear weapons, and the war’s opening strike killed him. His son has not reissued it. Nasr says there is now “a serious conversation in Iran” about building a bomb. A measure before the Iranian parliament proposes withdrawing from the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty, and 32 public figures have called for it. The President told the United Nations that “Iran will never have a nuclear weapon.” Kristof’s reply is the bleakest sentence in the column: the war “may have actually increased the likelihood” that it will.

It would be dishonest to leave Tehran out of the folly. Kristof does not. The Revolutionary Guards commander boasted in August of bringing the United States “to its knees,” and Araghchi has said Iran is ready for “a doomsday war.” Two governments are each sure the other is losing. That describes the Wright thesis this diary borrowed in May, in which both sides believe they have won. It does not describe a negotiation.

The Cost Ledger

Brent crude settled at $102.25 on Friday, October 2, essentially flat for the week, according to EnergyNow. That was after a Thursday surge driven by China’s suspension of fuel exports, and a Friday reversal when the G7 agreed to release 100 million barrels of crude and diesel over four months. As Rebecca F. Elliott reported for the New York Times on October 1, Gulf exports are at their highest since the war began and prices have barely moved. The U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve has fallen to its lowest level since 1982. Shipping a barrel from the Gulf to East Asia now costs about $34, against $3 in January. The President has mused about banning diesel exports.

The bond market has started to notice. Eshe Nelson reported for the Times on October 1 that the yield on the 10-year Treasury had reached 5.34 per cent, the highest since 2002.

In Iran, the rial has fallen to about 2.7 million to the dollar, and for the first time the euro is above three million rials. Pezeshkian has announced a “new posture” to manage the economy, without saying what it means. The air blockade adds another cost: sanctions experts warn that cutting most civilian flights in and out of Iran could choke imports of medicine.
