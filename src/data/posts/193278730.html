I should say, before proceeding, that I was born in London, Ontario — which is either a conflict of interest or a qualification, and I have never been entirely sure which. London is a city that has produced Ryan Gosling, who went to Hollywood; Karen Baldwin, who in 1982 won Miss Universe, which is a considerably larger jurisdiction than the Hub of the Southwest; and Guy Lombardo, whose arrangement of Auld Lang Syne has been heard by more human beings on more New Year’s Eves than any other piece of music in recorded history. It has also produced me, which I mention not as a comparable achievement but as evidence that London’s export record is varied and not always explicable.

I grew up there. It is a fine city. I say this without irony, which will become relevant shortly.

London is a city of four hundred thousand people on the Thames River in southwestern Ontario. It has named its river after the Thames River in England. It has named itself after the city those people built beside that river. It calls itself the Forest City, the Ambitious City, and the Hub of the Southwest. It has a university, two hospitals, a hockey arena, and a Broadway touring series at Canada Life Place that brings productions in from New York because London, in its ambition, requires New York to confirm what good theatre looks like.

The sign listed London. It listed the Grand Theatre. It listed Fanshawe Pioneer Village. It did not list Elsinore. Gerald noted this. He drove home.

Gerald Fitch discovered the sign on a Tuesday in March, driving back from a document pickup in Kitchener on the 401 westbound.

It was a Tourism Ontario directional sign of the standard green variety, positioned at the appropriate regulatory distance from the relevant exit, listing regional attractions for the traveler who had not yet committed to a destination and might be persuaded. The sign listed London. It listed the Grand Theatre. It listed Budweiser Gardens. It listed Fanshawe Pioneer Village, which is a reconstruction of a nineteenth-century settlement that Gerald has visited twice and found instructive.

It did not list Elsinore.

Gerald noted this. He drove home. He did not mention it to anyone for four days, because he was processing it at his own pace, and because he wanted to be certain he had read the sign correctly before raising what might prove to be a significant matter. On the fifth day he was certain. He mentioned it to no one because no one had asked.

The matter came to light the following week, when Wayne received a call from a South Korean tourism operator whose clients had missed the Elsinore exit and ended up briefly in Woodstock before correcting course. The clients had been following highway signage in good faith. The highway signage had directed them toward London. They had consulted their phones, which knew where Elsinore was even if Tourism Ontario did not, and had arrived only forty minutes behind schedule, which Wayne absorbed professionally. The ferret was fine.

He mentioned the sign situation to Alistair in the Festival Theatre parking lot on a Thursday afternoon.

Alistair’s response was seven minutes long. It covered the history of the Festival, the international profile of Elsinore, the obligation of provincial tourism infrastructure to accurately represent the cultural assets of southwestern Ontario, and the specific irony of a directional sign that pointed travelers toward a city which, in the current theatrical season, was staging Shrek the Musical, while neglecting to mention a city staging Waiting for Godot, Death of a Salesman, and Othello.

He had apparently already researched London’s season. He had been waiting for an occasion to raise it.

“They are doing Shrek,” he said. This was not, in his delivery, a question.

Wayne confirmed that this was what was reported.

“While we are doing Waiting for Godot.”

Wayne confirmed the scheduling.

Alistair stood in the parking lot for a moment in the particular silence of a man assembling a statement. Then he delivered it. It was four minutes on theatrical ambition and civic identity. It was the finest four minutes of his week and possibly of the parking lot’s history.

Wayne listened. He had a ferry booking spreadsheet to finish. He waited until Alistair was done. “I’ll put it on the Tourism and Culture Committee agenda,” he said, and went back to his office.

The Tourism and Culture Committee met the following Tuesday at the Municipal Hall. Gerald Fitch had prepared a summary document. He read it to the committee in the same measured voice he uses for all summary documents, which is to say the voice of a man who considers it his job to convey information accurately and completely and who has never felt that editorial emphasis serves this purpose.

He noted that the Tourism Ontario 401 westbound sign at the relevant exit listed London and its associated attractions. He noted that Elsinore was not listed. He noted that the sign had presumably been installed or last updated at a date he had not yet been able to confirm but was pursuing. He noted, as supplementary context, the current theatrical seasons of both municipalities.

Elsinore: The Tempest, Waiting for Godot, Death of a Salesman, The Importance of Being Earnest, Something Rotten!, Othello, A Midsummer Night’s Dream, The Hobbit, and four additional productions through November first.

London: the Grand Theatre’s current season, which Gerald acknowledged was a legitimate professional operation; the London Community Players at the Palace Theatre, presenting Urinetown: The Musical, a satirical work in which citizens are required to pay for access to public washrooms, and Mamma Mia; Musical Theatre Productions, presenting Shrek the Musical; and Broadway in London at Canada Life Place, presenting touring productions from New York.

He paused briefly at Shrek the Musical.

He continued.

The Swan Warden, who attends all public meetings in Elsinore in an advisory capacity, wrote one thing in her notebook. Nobody saw what it was.

Mayor Doug thanked Gerald for the thorough summary. He said Elsinore’s cultural assets spoke for themselves, which Alistair noted did not resolve the question of the sign. Mayor Doug said he would reach out personally to his counterpart in London and to the appropriate Tourism Ontario office and see what could be done. He wore his good coat. He was confident that with some dialogue the matter could be addressed to everyone’s satisfaction.

The committee voted to authorize the Mayor’s outreach. The motion passed. Gerald filed the minutes.

Jim heard about the sign from Maggie, who heard about it from a family in the waiting room.

They were from London — a couple with a beagle named Gerald, which Maggie chose not to mention to the actual Gerald, feeling it would complicate the annual cat negotiation — who had driven to the Perth County Small Animal Clinic because someone at a dinner party had recommended it specifically. They had driven past Elsinore on the way and noticed the Festival posters and asked Maggie what was on this season.

She told them what she knew, which was most of it, because Jim had been walking past the Festival Theatre every Tuesday and Thursday with the Swan Warden’s neighbour’s labrador and had been watching the season posters go up since February with the attention of a man who notices things and stores them for later.

The London couple were surprised Elsinore wasn’t on the highway sign. They said London had plenty of signs. They couldn’t speak to whether Elsinore deserved one. The beagle’s ear infection was responding well to treatment.

Jim heard the story at Sunday dinner.

“They’ll fix the sign in 2028,” he said. He passed the potatoes. He had looked it up. The next Tourism Ontario signage audit cycle was 2028. He had assessed the odds of an expedited review and found them unencouraging. He said nothing further on the matter.

Maggie said that seemed about right.

The boys ate their dinner.

Mayor Doug drove to London on a Wednesday in April in his very good coat. He met with London’s Mayor at the civic offices on Dufferin Avenue. The meeting was warm and productive. London’s Mayor was welcoming and collegial and expressed genuine appreciation for Elsinore’s cultural contribution to southwestern Ontario. He said the Festival was a remarkable institution. He had been meaning to get out for a production. Perhaps this summer.

The sign would be reviewed in the next Tourism Ontario signage audit cycle.

Mayor Doug drove home. He reported to council that London had been very welcoming, and that the sign matter was in hand, and that London had a lovely new arena, and that he was optimistic about the ongoing relationship between the two municipalities. His remarks were warm and correct.

Gerald filed the report.

The Swan Warden noted, in the subsequent committee session, that she had submitted a written inquiry to Tourism Ontario regarding the audit timeline. The response had thanked her for her interest.

She did not appear surprised by this. She had sent written inquiries before.

Waiting for Godot opened on a Thursday in June to a full house and reviews that described it as essential and devastating, which is what people say about Beckett when they mean they were not expecting to be as affected as they were.

In the third row sat eight South Korean tourists who had come back.

They had done the Literary River Tour in April. Wayne had given them a Festival brochure at the paddle boat dock, having quietly included a Gold Level Literary River Tour and Festival bundle in the spring package without formally consulting Alistair, on the grounds that what Alistair didn’t know wouldn’t require a parking lot conversation. The bundle had sold twelve units in the first two weeks.

The South Korean tourists had bought tickets to Godot on the strength of the brochure.

They sat in the third row and watched two men wait for someone who did not come, and at the end they applauded at length, and one of them took a photograph of the stage and sent it to someone, and within a week it had been shared significantly more than the paddle boat photograph.

Wayne noted the numbers with professional satisfaction.

Alistair received the review coverage with the complex emotions of a man who had programmed Beckett in a town that had just been omitted from a highway sign and found the universe making a point he had already made more eloquently, he felt, in the parking lot.

The Perth County farmer drove past the Festival box office on his way to the feed supply the morning after opening night. The Waiting for Godot poster was still in the window. He glanced at it.

He was asked, at the feed store, what he thought about the London sign situation.

“London’s alright,” he said.

He paid for his feed. He drove home.

This was considered, in Elsinore, a measured and complete assessment of the matter.

A Note for the Record

London, Ontario, is a city of four hundred thousand people that named its river the Thames and named itself after London and imports Broadway from New York to confirm what theatre looks like.

Elsinore, Ontario, is a city of thirty-three thousand people that named itself after Hamlet’s castle and stages Beckett and Shakespeare and produces one paddle boat excursion per summer that has now been shared internationally more times than anyone on the Tourism and Culture Committee has formally acknowledged.

Both cities have named their rivers after rivers in England. Both contain people who take their particular version of civilization very seriously. In Perth County, this has always been considered either admirable or the basis of all human comedy, and there has never been agreement on which.

The sign on the 401 still says what it says. The 2028 audit is, at this writing, twenty-one months away.

To the south, the question of which cities appear on which signs has acquired a different quality. Federal buildings are being assessed for renaming. Public institutions are being evaluated for rebranding. The signs say what they have been told to say, by whoever is currently doing the telling. In southwestern Ontario, the sign on the 401 says London. It says the Grand Theatre. It says Fanshawe Pioneer Village. It does not say Elsinore. This is either an oversight or a bureaucratic decision, and the 2028 audit will determine which. Nobody has suggested renaming anything for anyone. The question has not arisen. It is considered, in Perth County, a separate category of problem.

The Avon runs through Elsinore in the dark, past the Festival, past the paddle boat dock, past the bank the swans use in April, out toward Perth County where a man is already up and moving toward the barn.

London is alright.

Godot, as of press time, has not arrived.

Both of these things, in their way, are fine.
