I. Four Names, One Stage

On Wednesday, President Trump stood at Dover Air Force Base and watched four flag-draped transfer cases carried off a C-17. As Katie Rogers reported for the New York Times, before he went, he told reporters the soldiers had died in support of his war, that each of them had “said very strongly, ‘We cannot let Iran have a nuclear weapon.’” He did not explain how he knew this. Their families, when they spoke at all, did not talk about the war. They talked about their children.

Sgt. Michael Emmanuel Swinton, 30, was a jokester, his mother said. Pvt. Isabella Gonzales, 19 — the youngest American killed in this war, who finished high school in Carrollton, Texas, just over a year ago and joined the Army the same summer — was, in her uncle’s plain word, “a soldier.” First Lt. Tyler James Feehan, 25, of Hawaii, believed in the discipline and character military service builds in a person; his family is raising money for a scholarship in his name. Sgt. Angel S. Rampersad, 28, was a “gentle giant.” Four names. Eighteen dead now since February 28, roughly 550 wounded, at least a dozen flown to Germany for treatment serious enough to need it — a fact the Pentagon didn’t disclose until flight-tracker websites and reporters forced the question.

That afternoon, Trump flew one grieving family — Lieutenant Feehan’s parents and uncle — to his political rally in Georgia on Air Force One. He put them in the audience. He swayed to Lee Greenwood. And from the stage, in front of a cheering crowd, he told them about their own son: “This was a person who was going all the way. He would have been a general very quickly.” Earlier that day he’d claimed a poll showed Americans weren’t against the war. He didn’t name it. The actual number, from a Washington Post-Ipsos poll released the week before, is 29 percent support.

I don’t think there’s a cleaner image anywhere in this entire series for what this war has become to the man running it: a dignified transfer in the morning, a rally prop in the afternoon, and a casualty count treated the way a golf handicap gets treated — something to be managed, favorably compared, spun.

II. The War With No Name

It’s not only the families who aren’t being told the truth of it. Neither is Congress, and neither, largely, is the country. Nancy Youssef and Jonathan Lemire’s reporting for The Atlantic lays it out starkly: in the ten days since the U.S. resumed strikes, no senior administration official has addressed the campaign publicly. No classified briefings have reached Capitol Hill. The Pentagon hasn’t held a press conference on the war’s strategy since May.

When two soldiers died in Jordan, the Pentagon’s first statement said they’d died supporting Operation Inherent Resolve — the wrong war, the old one, against ISIS. Corrected later to “supporting overseas operations.” Still, at no point, the name of this specific war — Operation Epic Fury — was spoken. Hegseth, testifying before the Senate to request $70 billion in emergency funding on top of a $1.5 trillion regular budget, never said the word “Iran” in his opening remarks. He called it “an operation.”

The number, when it finally surfaces, is worse than most people realize: 18 American dead is more than were killed in combat in Afghanistan in 2016, 2017, or 2018 individually, and closing in on the total from the entire twelve-year campaign against ISIS. As PBS News reported from the Senate Appropriations Committee hearing captured Senator Patty Murray’s assessment of the funding request, which doubles as an assessment of the whole war: “The problem is not a lack of money, but poor planning.” Senator Susan Collins opened the same hearing by accusing Hegseth of purging women and officers of color from promotion lists — more than 40 senior officers removed, over half of them Black or female, per Times reporting she cited on the floor. Anti-war protesters were physically removed from the hearing room within thirty seconds of Hegseth’s opening statement.

The numbers themselves aren't even stable. On Wednesday, the Pentagon's own casualty website listed 18 Americans killed in the war. By Thursday, it read 14 — four names quietly removed, all from last weekend's cluster of deaths in Jordan and Iraq. Three military officials, speaking anonymously, said the change came because the administration had decided those four deaths technically occurred after an earlier ceasefire Trump had declared in April, and so didn't count toward the war total. The Pentagon's acting press secretary, Joel Valdez, offered a different explanation entirely — "temporary data disruptions" — and when the Times pressed for details, accused the paper of writing "a non-story" out of "lack of knowledge." Retired Lt. Gen. Ben Hodges, who commanded U.S. forces in Europe, put it plainly: "The administration seems to have a real hard time being honest about what is going on — not just about casualties, but about the whole war in general." By Friday the number was back up, and Trump himself, oddly, kept citing 18 even as his own department's website said otherwise — comparing it to other wars and saying even one death "is too many, but it's 18." The Pentagon couldn't agree with itself on how many of its own soldiers had died, in the same week it was asking Congress for $70 billion to keep going.

This is what a war being fought without a name, a strategy, or an accounting looks like from the inside of the government waging it.

III. Pickaxe Mountain and the Options Running Out

Into that vacuum steps Pickaxe Mountain — Kuh-e Kolang Gaz La, a facility buried more than 300 feet under a mountain a mile from Natanz, under construction since 2020, meant to house the centrifuge production Iran lost when an earlier facility was sabotaged. Anushka Patil’s explainer for the Times lays out what’s known: IAEA director Rafael Grossi has been asking Iran what it’s for since at least last year. “They are telling us,” he said, “’It’s none of your business.’”

Trump has now twice threatened to bomb it — “pretty soon, and very heavily,” he told reporters this week, in an Oval Office meeting with Lebanon’s president, adding, “there’s not a thing they can do about it.” What he doesn’t say publicly, and what experts have told the Times plainly, is that America’s most powerful bunker-busters — the ones that disabled centrifuges at Fordow in June 2025 — likely can’t reach a target buried this deep. The honest assessment is that Pickaxe Mountain “would be more suitable for ground forces to attack or sabotage.” Which means the threat, taken at face value, is a threat of invasion dressed as an airstrike.

Trump has escalated the rhetoric to match. The Times’ live coverage of the standoff reported that this week he posted that for every Iranian attack on a ship in the strait, the U.S. will “bomb and destroy ONE BRIDGE OR POWER PLANT, including those located next to, or in, the Capital City of Tehran.” Iran’s foreign minister Araghchi answered in kind: “Our defense doctrine is clear: eye for an eye... Those who contribute to such aggression, whatever the kind of support, will also be considered as legitimate targets.” Iran’s joint command went further still, warning that if Washington follows through, Iran will prevent “even a single drop of oil” from leaving the region at all — collective punishment of neutral Gulf states, not just retaliation against the U.S. Both governments are now threatening capitals and civilians explicitly, on the record, in writing.

IV. Two Straits Now

The war that started over one chokepoint now has two. As Jenny Gross, Rebecca F. Elliott, and Lisa Friedman reported for the Times, this week Iran’s Houthi allies in Yemen made good on their blockade threat and claimed strikes on two Saudi tankers, the Encelia and the Layla, in the Red Sea — Saudi Arabia confirmed an attack on the Encelia, crew safe, fire extinguished. Rubio, dismissively, from Manila: the Houthis had been “snookered” by Iran into a war that wasn’t theirs, and “should stay out of it.” Whether or not that’s true, the practical effect is the same either way — Saudi Arabia’s main workaround for the closed Strait of Hormuz, its Red Sea exports via Yanbu, is now also under threat, and roughly 4.5 million barrels a day, more than half of it bound for India and China, hangs on whether the Houthis mean it.

Brent crude closed the week within striking distance of $100 a barrel — up roughly a third since the war began, up $13 in a single week. Gasoline at $4.09 a gallon, diesel above $5.20, both climbing daily. Two of the world’s largest shipping firms, CMA CGM and Maersk, have already announced emergency fuel surcharges on ordinary cargo containers — the war’s cost now visibly passing through to consumer goods that have nothing to do with oil. The Japanese yen hit its weakest level against the dollar in roughly forty years, as global capital fled to safe havens. This is no longer a regional energy story. It’s become a measurable drag on economies that never asked to be part of this at all.

V. The Ceasefire Iraq Carried, and Iran Refused

There was, briefly, a real diplomatic opening this week, and it closed almost as fast as it appeared. Iraq’s prime minister, Ali al-Zaidi, flew to Tehran carrying a U.S. ceasefire proposal — the same trip that followed his White House meeting with Trump — and met with President Pezeshkian, Ghalibaf, and Araghchi directly. Iran rejected it. Araghchi’s explanation, delivered on state television, wasn’t about the message or the messenger: “The problem is not passing messages. The problem is America’s outlook, which is illogical, greedy and controlling.” Iranian officials told the Times the offer was rejected specifically because it left the Strait of Hormuz’s control unresolved — the one issue Tehran has said, for weeks now, it will not negotiate away as a temporary measure.

Two Iranian officials also described, for the first time on record, what their government is actually preparing for if Trump makes good on his threat to bomb Tehran directly: a full regional expansion, including strikes on Tel Aviv and a request to the Houthis to fully close Bab al-Mandab. This is no longer a hypothetical either side is only gesturing at. It’s a stated contingency plan.

VI. The Losers’ Ledger

Nobody who actually started this war is the one paying its most immediate bills.

In Karachi, Elian Peltier’s reporting for the Times found mango exporter Ghulam Mustafa, who rushed his fruit to the Iran border the moment last month’s ceasefire held, and watched the trucks turn back when it collapsed. His mangoes are rotting; traders are selling them at half price just to recoup something. Pakistan’s mango association has cut its export target by 30 percent this year. A Karachi trader, watching his own government’s mediation efforts fail to produce anything: “The leaders are showing off.” Another exporter, more plainly still: “The solution is in the hands of Mr. Trump. If he wants, he can solve all these problems.” A man selling fruit in Pakistan has more honest clarity about where the decision actually sits than most of Washington seems willing to admit in public.

In Kuwait, which draws 90 percent of its drinking water from desalination, Zane Irwin’s reporting for the Times documents plants struck twice in a week — fires, damaged generators, forced rationing. Oman relies on the same technology for 86 percent of its water, Saudi Arabia for 70. Iran, whose own water scarcity crisis predates this war, has accused U.S. strikes of cutting off access for 30 villages on one island alone. The U.N.’s spokesman said what should not need saying: “Vital infrastructure on which civilians rely for survival, including hospitals and water supplies, must be spared.”

In the Gulf of Oman, the investigation by Pranav Baskar, Pragati K.B., and Eric Schmitt reconstructed the death of a shadow-fleet tanker called the Settebello, drifting, engines off, on the morning of June 10 when American missiles tore into its engine room. Three men died: Suresh Patnala, the chief engineer; Shivanand Chaurasia, a fitter; and Aditya Sharma, 23, a trainee cadet on his first voyage, earning $200 a month, dreaming of one day making captain. His father had begged him daily to come home. Naval law experts who reviewed the strike footage question why the U.S. disabled a vessel that wasn’t underway; the Indian seamen’s union is calling it a war crime and demanding compensation nobody has offered. “He was doing his job there,” Sharma’s father said. “They don’t have weapons. They are all civilians.”

And in Baghdad, Vivian Salama’s profile for The Atlantic captures Iraq’s new prime minister, Ali al-Zaidi, 41, a businessman with no prior political career, putting his country’s own history to the war plainly. Asked what Iraq learned from its own eight-year war with Iran in the 1980s, he answered without hesitating, mid-drag on a vape: “Even the winner loses.” Iraq is now losing an estimated $50 billion in revenue from the closed strait alone — a bystander’s tab for a war it isn’t fighting.

VII. “The Sooner We Acknowledge... The Better”

And then, this week, someone who actually built part of this war’s plans said the quiet part in print. Andrew Exum, writing in The Atlantic — who personally oversaw contingency planning at the Pentagon for the 2025 strikes on Iran’s nuclear sites — argued that the United States has lost this war, and that the sooner Washington admits it, the sooner it can start managing the damage.

His argument is worth sitting with in full, because it doesn’t come from a dove. Hegseth, pressed by the Senate on what the war actually accomplished, claimed “a historic military victory.” Exum’s answer is that nations don’t fight wars for military objectives, they fight them for political ones — and by that measure, nothing has been secured. Iran’s supreme leader is dead, replaced by a leadership more extreme and less cautious than the one it succeeded. The strait that was open before the war is now closed. Iran’s allies have opened a second one. And the deterrent Washington spent decades building — the credible threat that kept Iran from closing Hormuz or racing for a bomb — is gone, on Exum’s account, precisely because Washington used it. He cites Thomas Schelling’s old insight: a deterrent’s power lies in the threat of its use. Once you’ve actually done the thing you were threatening, the enemy no longer fears what happens next, because they’ve already survived it.

Most damning is his read on the nuclear claim itself. Trump has said repeatedly that last year’s strikes “obliterated” Iran’s nuclear program. Exum, who ran the actual planning, says otherwise: the strikes did exactly what they were designed to do, which was degrade the program temporarily, not destroy it. Admitting that the war has failed to reestablish deterrence would mean admitting the original claim of obliteration was never true to begin with. That may be the real reason nobody in this administration can say the word “lost” out loud — not because the facts are unclear, but because saying it collapses a claim the president has been repeating as settled fact for over a year.

VIII. Why He Won’t Say So

I want to be careful here, because what follows is my own reading, not a fact I can source the way I’ve sourced everything above it — but I don’t think it can be left out, because I think it explains more of this war’s strangeness than any strategic analysis has managed to.

Trump has never, in forty years of public life, accepted a defeat as a defeat. Not the 2020 election, which he still disputes. Not the 2016 popular vote, which he lost by three million ballots and explained away with uncorroborated claims of mass fraud. Not the Mueller report, which declined to exonerate him on obstruction and which he nonetheless declared “complete and total exoneration.” Not his business bankruptcies, narrated always as bad luck or bad partners rather than bad judgment. Not the civil fraud verdict, not the Carroll verdicts, not the 2018 shutdown he ended without the wall funding he demanded, before routing around the loss with an emergency declaration instead of simply calling it what it was.

It’s hard, watching this war, not to wonder whether something similar is happening here, and whether one comparison in particular is doing more work than any other. Trump has spent years bristling at being called “crooked,” at every scandal since his return to politics getting reflexively filed under some echo of Watergate. He hates the Nixon comparison on the corruption side of the ledger. But Nixon’s actual legacy carries a second half most people forget when they reach for the shorthand: Nixon is also the president remembered for withdrawing from a war America couldn’t win, dressed up as “peace with honor,” understood by history ever since as a managed retreat from an unwinnable position. If Trump has spent this long resisting being cast as the next Nixon on corruption, it isn’t hard to imagine he’d resist even harder being cast as the next Nixon on retreat — because walking away from Iran now, on any terms, on any pretext, risks completing a parallel he’s spent a public lifetime running from rather than distinguishing himself from it.

I don’t know that this is true. I can’t know it — nobody outside his own head can. But it would explain a great deal that pure strategy doesn’t: why every serious voice in this series, from Sanger to Tannehill to Exum himself, keeps arriving at the same practical advice — cut losses, find an off-ramp, admit the deterrent is gone and build a new one — and why none of it seems to be landing. A man who has never once let a loss be recorded as a loss is not going to be the one who breaks that pattern over a war he can still, for now, describe as a “skirmish” from a rally stage.

X. What’s Actually Being Buried

Four soldiers came home this week in flag-draped cases. A former Pentagon planner wrote that the war that killed them has already been lost. The president who ordered it called it a skirmish, borrowed a grieving family for an afternoon rally, and quoted a poll that doesn’t exist to back a claim the real polling directly contradicts.

Somewhere underneath all of that is a simple, unglamorous truth nobody in power seems able to say plainly: this war has not achieved what it set out to achieve, it is costing more than anyone budgeted for, in dollars and in names, and the countries paying the heaviest incidental price — Iraq, Kuwait, Pakistan, the families of sailors who were never soldiers — never got a vote in whether it started. Wars like this don’t usually end because someone finally wins. They end when the people running them run out of ways to avoid saying so out loud. Judging by this week, that reckoning is still being postponed, one rally, one Truth Social post, one dignified transfer at a time.
