Ask most people how the American government pays for science and they will describe something like a cheque. Congress votes the money, the cheque goes to a university, and somewhere a scientist in a white coat cashes it.

The real system is a relay race with five handoffs. Congress passes the money to the White House budget office. The budget office passes it to the agencies. The agencies pass it through panels of outside scientists, who decide who deserves it. The winners’ universities take delivery and keep the lights on. And at the end of the line, a graduate student or a postdoctoral researcher finally gets to run the experiment.

For most of the eighty years since Vannevar Bush wrote Science, the Endless Frontier, every one of those handoffs was a formality. The baton moved because everyone expected it to move. Under the second Trump administration, each handoff has become a checkpoint, and at each one the money can be slowed, redirected or stopped.

This essay walks the baton down the track. The rest of the series depends on knowing where the handoffs are.

Handoff One: Congress

Federal science has no single budget. It is scattered across four of the twelve annual spending bills, each written by a different subcommittee with its own priorities. The Congressional Research Service lays out the map. The National Institutes of Health sit in the Labor, Health and Human Services and Education bill, alongside student loans and unemployment insurance. The National Science Foundation and NASA share the Commerce, Justice and Science bill with the FBI and the Census Bureau. The Energy Department’s Office of Science sits in the Energy and Water bill, next to dams and nuclear weapons. Pentagon research is a line in the defence bill called RDT&E: research, development, test and evaluation.

Added together, federal research and development came to about $202 billion in fiscal 2024 and about $192 billion in fiscal 2025, by the Research Service’s count. For fiscal 2026, the White House asked for $181 billion, with far deeper cuts to the basic-science agencies. Congress said no. Its four science bills were signed between 23 January and 3 February 2026, and they kept NIH, the NSF, NASA science and the Energy Department’s Office of Science within a few per cent of the year before.

Congress also did something less noticed. It wrote fences into the bills. One provision forbids NIH from changing how it pays universities’ overhead costs, a fight we will come to. Another caps how much NIH can pay up front on multi-year grants, a trick that had let the agency spend its full budget on fewer projects. Bloomberg Law reported in January that appropriators had rejected the administration’s planned overhaul of NIH funding.

So the first handoff held. The money was voted. That is where most people’s understanding of the system ends, and where the administration’s real leverage begins.

Handoff Two: The Valve

Between Congress and the agencies sits an office most Americans have never heard of: the Office of Management and Budget, run since 2025 by Russell Vought. By law, before an agency can spend a dollar, the budget office must “apportion” it, dividing each year’s appropriation into portions it can draw on over time. For decades, apportionment was clerical. It was the plumbing. If Congress is the reservoir and the agencies are the taps, the budget office holds the main valve in between, and for most of eighty years nobody paid much attention to who had the wrench.

Under Vought, it became a valve.

In March 2025, the budget office took down the public website where apportionments are posted, so that nobody outside the government could see what was being released and when. A federal court ordered it restored that July, according to a separate report from the Congressional Research Service. In August 2025, the Government Accountability Office ruled that NIH had broken the Impoundment Control Act. Between February and June that year, the agency committed almost $8 billion less than in the same months a year earlier while terminating more than 1,800 grants, as K&L Gates summarised the decision.

The ruling did not end the practice; it changed its form. For fiscal 2026, the budget office rewrote its own rules so that the automatic monthly releases agencies normally receive would cover only essentials such as salaries. Everything else waited for specific approval. In March 2026, it missed a 30-day deadline for releasing NIH’s money, R&D World reported. NSF’s research money came with a footnote requiring a detailed spending plan before it could flow. NASA’s came with ten named science missions, including the Habitable Worlds Observatory and the Chandra X-ray Observatory, held back until the agency filed a plan of its own.

The effect shows at year’s end. The NSF closed fiscal 2026 having made 6,218 awards worth $6.4 billion, against 8,647 the year before and 10,937 the year before that, AIP FYI reported. More than $1 billion that Congress appropriated was still unspent, much of it reportedly set aside for a White House science-office initiative that Congress had not specifically funded.

None of this requires cutting a budget. It only requires turning a valve.

Handoff Three: The Jury

When the money does reach an agency, the next question is who gets it. Bush’s answer in 1945 was that scientists, not officials, should decide. The machinery built on that answer is enormous.

NIH’s Center for Scientific Review handles somewhere between 80,000 and 90,000 grant applications a year, using about 19,000 outside scientists in roughly 1,200 to 1,300 review meetings, according to the centre’s own strategic plan. The NSF received 40,454 proposals in fiscal 2024 and funded about one in four, using about 32,000 reviewers who wrote nearly 188,000 reviews, its annual financial report shows. Reviewers are paid little or nothing. They do it because their own grants depend on the system being fair.

The process is slow and deliberately tedious. At NIH, several reviewers read each application and score it from 1, exceptional, to 9, poor; roughly half are judged not competitive enough even to be discussed. The panel argues over the rest, now often on a video call, and a combined score goes back to the institute. It is the least glamorous part of American science and arguably the most important, because it is what stops a senator’s favourite university from winning a grant simply for being a senator’s favourite university.

For decades, the jury’s verdict mostly stuck. About half of NIH’s institutes used a “payline”: applications scoring above a set cut-off were funded, more or less automatically. In November 2025, NIH abolished paylines and replaced them with what it called a unified funding strategy, in which review scores are weighed alongside agency priorities, as FASEB, the federation of biology societies, explained to its members. The Research Service reports that NIH’s success rate for research grants fell from 19 per cent in fiscal 2024 to 13 per cent in fiscal 2025.

Above the agencies, the rules changed too. An executive order signed on 7 August 2025, published in the Federal Register, declared that peer review recommendations “remain advisory,” that political appointees must review funding decisions, and that they should not routinely defer to the scientists. The proposed rule of May 2026, described in the Introduction, would go further still.

The jury still sits. It just no longer decides.

Handoff Four: The University

The winning grant goes to the university, not to the scientist. That is where the cheque picture breaks down completely.

American universities spent $117.7 billion on research and development in fiscal 2024, and $64.7 billion of it, 55 per cent, came from the federal government, according to the National Center for Science and Engineering Statistics. The health department, almost entirely NIH, supplied more than half of that federal money. The Pentagon came second, then the NSF, the Energy Department and NASA.

Each grant carries two kinds of money. Direct costs pay for the project: salaries, equipment, reagents, mice. Indirect costs, formally “facilities and administrative” costs, pay for what no single project can be billed for: the building, the power for the freezers, the safety officers, the compliance staff who make sure human subjects are protected and radioactive waste is handled properly. In fiscal 2024, $9.3 billion of the $33 billion NIH awarded went to those indirect costs, a third Congressional Research Service report found.

On 7 February 2025, NIH announced it would cap indirect costs at 15 per cent, effective immediately. For many universities that meant losing half or more of what they were being paid to run their laboratories. A federal judge blocked the cap within days and made the block permanent in April 2025. The First Circuit upheld that ruling in January 2026, and the government did not take it to the Supreme Court, as Ropes & Gray reported. The NSF, the Energy Department and the Pentagon tried similar caps. All three were struck down too, according to the litigation tracker kept by the Association of Public and Land-grant Universities.

The universities won every round. They also spent more than a year not knowing whether they would be paid to keep the buildings open, and planning accordingly: freezing hires, cutting graduate admissions, delaying equipment. A system can be legally intact and practically damaged at the same time.

Handoff Five: The Bench

At the end of the relay are the people who actually do the work, and many of them are not who you would expect.

A large share are students. About a quarter of full-time doctoral students in science, engineering and health in the United States rely mainly on federal money for their support, according to the NCSES survey of graduate students and postdoctoral researchers, and the real share touched by federal grants is higher. The same survey counted 69,877 postdoctoral researchers, the scientists in the precarious years between a doctorate and a permanent job. When a grant is cancelled, the first casualty is usually not a professor. It is often a graduate student in her twenties whose stipend came out of it.

The rest of the bench sits in places most people never see. There are 41 federally funded research and development centres, run on the government’s behalf by universities, non-profits and companies. Together they spent $31.8 billion on research in fiscal 2025, most of it from the Energy Department, NCSES reported. They include the Energy Department’s 17 national laboratories, from Argonne outside Chicago to Los Alamos in New Mexico. Sixteen of the seventeen are run by contractors, so their scientists are not federal employees.

That detail matters. When the Energy Department’s top science official, Darío Gil, told The New York Times in October that the department’s science mission had not suffered, he pointed to exactly this: the laboratories’ staff were outside the reach of the federal job cuts. He was right about the staff. But the grants that pay for their work still pass through the same valve, the same jury and the same budget office as everyone else’s.

The Relay Only Works If Everyone Runs

Laid end to end, the picture is clearer than any single statistic.

Congress protected the budget. The courts struck down the overhead caps. Almost every legal fight the research community has picked since January 2025, it has won. And still the NSF made 43 per cent fewer awards in fiscal 2026 than two years earlier, NIH funded thousands fewer projects while spending its whole budget, and more than a billion dollars that Congress appropriated sat unspent at the end of the year.

That is because the system Bush designed was never mainly about money. It was about trust. A professor can only hire a postdoctoral researcher for three years if she trusts that year two’s money will arrive. A university can only build a laboratory if it trusts that the overhead it was promised will be paid. A young scientist can only commit a decade to Alzheimer’s disease if she trusts that the field will still be funded when her work starts to pay off.

Every handoff in the relay is a place where that trust can be lost without a single vote being taken. The rest of this series follows what the relay produced over eighty years, and what happens when the baton starts to drop.
