Anthony Scaramucci’s essay “The Long Trade”, which prompted this series, ends on a specific, quotable claim, and it is worth returning to his exact words now that this series has spent fifteen parts building the evidence to test them against. Churchill, he writes, “made the best long-term call in modern history,” going long on America before America went long on itself, and “the least we can do is hold the position.” The dividend from that position, in his telling, “lands in the accounts of people who never read a word he wrote” — his own grandfather, a coal miner, and father, a crane operator, standing in for every ordinary American whose comfortable life was quietly underwritten by a bet Churchill made on their behalf eighty years ago. It is a genuinely moving image, and I do not think it is wrong so much as it is told from only one side of a ledger this series has now read in full.

Start with what this series has actually established the “dividend” cost, rather than only what it paid out. It cost, on the Churchill side of the family’s own account, a father destroyed by a single miscalculated political bluff in 1886, watched at close range by a son who spent the rest of his life proving the family name could survive catastrophe; it cost a wife who managed six decades of chronic financial anxiety her husband never fully shared, and who ended her own days selling his paintings to keep her household solvent; it cost at least one daughter her life, by her own hand, and another her peace, across three difficult marriages and a public struggle with alcohol; it cost a grandson his best political prospects the moment he cast a principled vote against his own government, delivered by a prime minister who professed to revere the very ancestor whose grandson he was expelling. None of this appears anywhere in Scaramucci’s essay, and there is no reason it should — his subject was the alliance, not the family’s private cost of producing the man who helped forge it. But an inheritance audit, unlike a tribute, has an obligation to put both entries on the same page, and this series has tried to do exactly that across fifteen parts.

Then there is the empire’s own ledger, which this series confronted directly in Part VI and cannot now simply set aside for the sake of a tidy closing argument. The same man whose December 1940 letter to Roosevelt this series has called, following Scaramucci, “the greatest pitch letter in history” was also the same man who told his own Secretary of State for India, by that Secretary’s own account, that he hated Indians and considered them a beastly people — and whose War Cabinet’s initial response to a famine that killed as many as three million Bengalis was slower and less urgent than the scale of the dying demanded, whatever the genuine wartime constraints that also shaped that response. Scaramucci’s essay invokes “the English-speaking peoples” as a unified moral community whose bond “does not rise and fall with the quarrels of the moment.” Several hundred million of the actual English-speaking peoples under Churchill’s own direct authority in 1943 experienced that bond rather differently, and any accounting of what Churchill’s career actually produced has to hold both the Lend-Lease letter and the Bengal famine in the same hand, because he wrote one and presided over the other within roughly three years of each other.

And then there is the dividend’s actual geography, which this series has spent considerable effort restoring to its proper shape. Scaramucci addresses “the English-speaking peoples” and imagines Churchill speaking to Congress “one more time” — and in doing so, faithfully reproduces the exact narrowing this series traced in Part VIII, back to Churchill’s own Fulton speech itself. Churchill did not coin the special relationship as a bilateral arrangement between London and Washington. He coined it, in his own words, as a model to be extended across the whole Commonwealth, and he named its actual working template explicitly: the Permanent Joint Board on Defence between Canada and the United States, the institution my own great-uncle Hugh Keenleyside helped build from its founding in August 1940, after his own mission to Washington that May had carried the first genuine warning of just how close Britain had come to losing its fleet entirely. Canada was not absent from the special relationship’s founding moment. It was, by Churchill’s own account, the actual prototype for it. The fact that eighty years of subsequent usage narrowed “special relationship” down to an exclusively Anglo-American phrase is not a natural drift of language. It is a specific, traceable omission, repeated so consistently across eight decades that a financier writing a heartfelt essay in July 2026 reproduces it without any apparent awareness that the omission has a history, or that his own essay’s silence on Canada is not incidental but structural — built into the phrase from the exact month Churchill first spoke it.

This is, I think, the actual argument this whole series has been making, essay by essay, since the introduction: that an inheritance and a legend are not the same object, even when they share a name. A legend compresses. It keeps the letter and drops the famine, keeps the fleet saved and drops the fleet nearly surrendered, keeps the special relationship and drops the third country that built half its actual machinery. An inheritance, properly audited, keeps all of it — the Lend-Lease letter and the Bengal famine, Clementine’s ledger and Diana’s overdose, Nicholas Soames’s ministerial competence and his own recorded conduct toward women colleagues, a sculptor turning her grandfather’s most famous speech into art and a distant cousin turning the family’s remaining prestige into a charge sheet. None of these entries cancel any other out. They simply sit, together, on the same page, which is where an honest account of any consequential family eventually has to put them.

It is worth tracing, one final time, the specific thread this series picked up in Part II and has never quite let go of since: the temperament Leonard Jerome brought to Wall Street — catastrophic risk, absorbed and survived, converted into a recovery story large enough to make the original catastrophe worth telling — and what became of that temperament as it passed down five generations into progressively smaller arenas. It built an empire’s wartime survival when Winston held it, on a stage genuinely large enough to make his own recoveries mean something to the entire world. It ruined a Chancellor of the Exchequer within a hundred and forty-two days when Randolph the elder held it, with no war available to absorb the loss. It produced three failed elections and a genuine wartime injury when Randolph the younger held it, and a lifetime spent trying to out-write his own father’s shadow rather than out-govern it. It shrank to a single marginal parliamentary seat and, eventually, a sale of the family’s own papers to the nation when the third Winston held it. And it arrived, finally, at Nicholas Soames’s quiet, competent, uncelebrated ministerial career — a Churchill descendant who broke the pattern entirely by declining to chase the dramatic risk his family had specialized in for five generations, and who was expelled from his party anyway, not for any catastrophic miscalculation of his own, but for the plain, undramatic act of voting his conscience once too often. The temperament did not disappear. It simply ran out of stages sized to make it legible as a family gift rather than an ordinary human life, lived under an unusually heavy name.

I want to return, finally, to the question I raised in Part I about my own standing to write any of this, because fifteen parts later it deserves a fuller answer than I gave it then. I am not English, not American, and I have no institutional stake in whether the special relationship is remembered as triumph or as myth. What I do have, by pure accident of family history, is a great-uncle who happened to be standing in the room — or more precisely, on a train from Ottawa to Washington — at almost exactly the moment this whole edifice was actually being built, carrying a warning that both Britain and America needed and that neither one, on its own, was positioned to deliver. Hugh Keenleyside built the rest of his career inside the unglamorous machinery that keeps an alliance functioning between its dramatic founding crises — joint defence boards, technical assistance administrations, ambassadorial postings nobody writes speeches about. Churchill wrote the letters and gave the speeches that get quoted eighty years later. Keenleyside, and the country he represented, kept the actual channels open that let those letters and speeches land somewhere useful. This series does not exist to argue that Canada deserves equal billing with Britain and America in the history of the twentieth century’s central alliance. It exists to note that the billing Canada currently receives — essentially none, in Scaramucci’s essay and in almost every other popular account of the special relationship — does not match the documentary record this series has actually walked through, part by part, with dates attached.

So: was it worth what it cost? I do not think that is a question this series, or any single essay, actually gets to answer, and I want to resist the temptation to supply a tidy verdict simply because fifteen parts of buildup seem to demand one. The dividend Scaramucci describes was real. Coal miners’ grandsons and crane operators’ sons really did inherit a more secure, more prosperous world than they might otherwise have had, underwritten in part by decisions Churchill made under genuine and enormous pressure. The famine was also real. The daughter who did not survive the weight of the name was also real. The distant cousin facing criminal charges today, trading on a title four centuries old, is also real. An inheritance this large, running through this many hands across this many generations, was never going to resolve into a single number at the bottom of the ledger — credit or debit, triumph or tragedy. It was always going to look exactly like what this series has actually found: an enormous, genuinely consequential family whose members did remarkable, admirable, catastrophic, and ordinary things in roughly the proportions any family produces, magnified only by the scale of the stage history happened to hand them.

It is worth noting, one last time before this closes, who actually kept that stage functional across the century and a half this series has covered, because the answer is not the men whose names the stage was built to advertise. Clementine managed six decades of finance and morale that made Winston’s own confidence affordable. Mary built the family’s actual institutional memory, wrote the biographies that keep the record honest enough for a series like this one to even attempt an audit, and stood at the literal handover ceremony ending the empire her own father spent a career defending. Even Jennie, dismissed for a century as a society beauty who married well, ran a wartime hospital ship and edited a literary quarterly on her own initiative, decades before anyone thought to ask what she had actually done rather than whom she had married. This series’ title refers to an inheritance, and inheritances, properly audited, include the unpaid labour that made the named heir’s achievements possible in the first place — labour this family’s own women performed for five generations, largely off the books, while the ledger’s headline entries kept accruing to men.

What I can say with more confidence is this: the version of Churchill’s story that gets invoked every time the special relationship feels strained — Scaramucci’s version, more or less, repeated with minor variations by countless commentators before him and countless more who will follow — is not false. It is simply an edited account, compressed for the purposes of a specific argument about the present moment, in exactly the way every generation of this family has edited its own story for its own purposes, going all the way back to a Brooklyn stock speculator whose grandson learned, from him, that a good recovery narrative is worth considerably more than an accurate account of the risk that made the recovery necessary. This series has tried, across sixteen parts, to write the version with the risk left in. Whether that makes for a better bedtime story than Scaramucci’s is a matter of taste. Whether it makes for a more honest inheritance is not, I think, seriously in question — and an honest inheritance, ledger fully read, dividend and cost both entered, is the only kind worth passing down to whoever eventually decides to argue about this family next.
