November 9, 1989. The night history was supposed to end

There’s a photograph from November 9, 1989. East and West Berliners are dancing on the Wall, hammers in hand, tears streaming down faces, champagne bottles passed between strangers who hours before couldn’t touch without risking death.

The Cold War was ending. Democracy had won. Francis Fukuyama would soon declare “the end of history”—not literally, but the idea that liberal democracy had emerged as humanity’s final form of government, that the great ideological struggle was over, that the future belonged to freedom.

Thirty-five years later, we’re watching that project collapse.

Not everywhere at once. Not in every nation simultaneously. But the trajectory is unmistakable: Democratic backsliding in Hungary, Poland, Turkey, India, Brazil, the Philippines. Authoritarian consolidation in Russia, China, Saudi Arabia, Egypt. Democratic breakdowns where they barely existed. Democratic erosion where they seemed secure.

And at the center of this collapse: America.

Not because America is the only democracy that matters—that’s the dangerous American exceptionalism that blinds us to our vulnerabilities. But because American power, American example, American choices shape global possibilities in ways no other nation’s do.

When America demonstrates that democracy can govern effectively, address challenges, maintain rule of law, provide prosperity—it strengthens democracy everywhere. When America demonstrates the opposite, it accelerates democratic collapse globally.

We’re in the collapse phase. And the counterfactual isn’t just about American domestic policy or international alliances. It’s about whether the democratic project survives this century—or whether we’re witnessing the beginning of a new authoritarian age.

Let me explain why this choice mattered so profoundly, informed by my family’s history at the creation of the postwar democratic order.

The Democratic Wager: Can Freedom Govern?

Democracy makes an extraordinary claim: Free people, governing themselves through elections, rule of law, and constitutional constraints, can produce better outcomes than authoritarian systems where power is concentrated in single leaders or ruling parties.

For most of human history, this seemed absurd. Every successful empire, every powerful kingdom, every stable government was authoritarian. Democracy was associated with mob rule, chaos, weakness—a luxury only small, homogeneous societies could afford.

The American experiment changed that calculation. Then the postwar democratic expansion: Western Europe, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan. By the 1990s, democracy seemed ascendant—not just morally superior, but practically superior. Democratic nations were wealthier, more innovative, more stable, more peaceful with each other.

But the wager was always fragile. Democracy requires:

Institutional integrity: Courts, legislatures, bureaucracies functioning independently rather than serving executive power.

Rule of law: Even the powerful face consequences for violations.

Free press: Information flows freely, power can be scrutinized.

Electoral legitimacy: Losers accept results, winners transfer power peacefully.

Constitutional constraints: Limits on power exist and are enforced.

Shared reality: Citizens operate from common facts, not tribal mythologies.

Economic opportunity: Enough broadly shared prosperity that people believe the system works.

When these elements erode, democracy collapses—not dramatically, but through incremental decay until authoritarianism emerges from democratic institutions themselves.

What we’re watching in America:

Every element eroding simultaneously. Institutions captured. Rule of law abandoned for the powerful. Press attacked as “enemy of the people.” Electoral legitimacy contested. Constitutional constraints ignored. Shared reality shattered. Economic opportunity concentrated upward.

The democratic wager is failing in its most powerful exemplar. And the world is watching.

The Authoritarian Alternative: Efficiency or Oppression?

The authoritarian sales pitch writes itself when democracy provides the footage.

Authoritarians make a competing claim: Democracy is messy, slow, weak. Strong leaders making decisions without democratic constraints can act decisively, implement policy efficiently, maintain stability, produce prosperity.

For decades, liberal democrats dismissed this—pointing to Soviet collapse, to dictatorships’ economic failures, to oppression’s moral bankruptcy.

But the Chinese model challenged those assumptions. China achieved extraordinary economic growth under authoritarian governance. Lifted hundreds of millions from poverty. Built infrastructure at speeds democracies couldn’t match. Demonstrated that authoritarianism could produce prosperity.

This gave autocrats everywhere a counter-narrative: Democracy isn’t superior—it’s luxury wealthy nations can afford while developing. Strong leaders get things done. Democratic debate is paralysis.

The American collapse validates this narrative.

When the world’s most powerful democracy:

  • Elects leaders who attempt coups when they lose

  • Abandons rule of law for powerful elites

  • Treats constitutional constraints as obstacles to ignore

  • Descends into tribal warfare destroying shared reality

  • Produces such economic inequality that broad prosperity disappears

The message to wavering democracies is clear: Democracy doesn’t work. Even in America, democracy produces chaos, corruption, capture by authoritarians who exploit democratic processes to destroy democratic governance.

The counterfactual would challenge this narrative:

Harris’s election would demonstrate that:

  • Democratic institutions can resist authoritarian capture

  • Rule of law can survive stress testing

  • Free elections can remove failed leaders

  • Constitutional systems can self-correct

  • Democracy remains viable even under pressure

Not proving democracy is perfect—proving it’s resilient enough to survive challenges authoritarianism exploits as evidence of democratic failure.

The Domino Theory Redux: Democratic Backsliding as Contagion

The original domino theory—that communist expansion threatened to spread country by country—was oversimplified. But the underlying insight was valid: Political systems influence each other. Success or failure in one nation affects calculations in others.

We’re watching democratic dominoes fall:

Hungary: Orban systematically dismantled democratic constraints while maintaining electoral facade. The lesson: You can destroy democracy while claiming to preserve it.

Poland: Law and Justice party captured courts, attacked press freedom, weaponized nationalism. The lesson: Democratic majorities can be used to eliminate democratic protections.

Turkey: Erdogan transformed parliamentary democracy into elected authoritarianism. The lesson: Emergency powers never expire when authoritarians control them.

India: Modi’s BJP combines electoral success with systematic erosion of secular democracy, press freedom, minority rights. The lesson: Democratic forms can mask authoritarian content.

Brazil: Bolsonaro attempted Trump’s playbook—contest elections, undermine institutions, cultivate loyal movements—but failed because Brazilian institutions held. The lesson works both ways: Democratic resistance can succeed.

Philippines: Duterte demonstrated that populist authoritarianism could win democratic elections then govern through violence and intimidation. The lesson: Voters sometimes choose authoritarians knowingly.

Each case is unique. But the pattern is global: Democracy is under siege, and authoritarian models are winning more often than losing.

American influence on this cascade:

When America elected Trump, every authoritarian felt vindicated. When Trump attempted a coup, every would-be autocrat took notes. When Trump faced no accountability, every authoritarian leader learned that coups can be attempted without terminal consequences.

Now Trump’s return teaches: Authoritarians eventually win. Attempt a coup, face prosecution, wait long enough, regain power with immunity. The lesson is devastating for global democratic health.

The counterfactual would teach different lessons:

Democracies can remove authoritarians through elections. Coup attempts face accountability. Institutional resistance works. Democratic norms can be restored. The fever can break.

Brazil demonstrated this in 2022—Bolsonaro attempted Trump’s playbook and lost. Harris’s election would have been the most powerful demonstration that democratic resistance succeeds.

Instead, Trump’s return proves that authoritarian patience outlasts democratic resistance.
