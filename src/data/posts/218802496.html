Stephen Gordon October 2026

On the morning of Saturday, October 3, 2026, this series left Alice in low Earth orbit with an instruction to keep her coat on. On the morning of Sunday, October 4, The New York Times explained why she would need it.

The lunar south pole, where the United States and a Chinese-Russian partnership both intend to build their bases, swings between roughly 54°C in sunlight and about −203°C in shadow. The lunar night there lasts about two Earth weeks. Solar panels are of no use in the dark, batteries big enough to carry a base through a fortnight of it are not practical, and so both camps have reached the same conclusion. The first permanent address on the Moon will need its own nuclear reactor, and whoever switches theirs on first may get to decide where everyone else is allowed to park.

Two pieces published together by Selam Gebrekidan, who covers the Chinese space programme for The New York Times from Hong Kong, set out the state of play: “Superpowers Race to Put Nuclear Reactors on the Moon” and “Who Owns the Moon?”, both dated October 4, 2026. They are worth reading in full.

For twelve instalments the question has been whether Starship can get there. Gebrekidan’s reporting asks what happens if it does.

Plugging in first

According to the Times, NASA asked contractors in late August 2026 to prepare to build a reactor that could survive the trip, run near the south pole without anyone tending it, and be ready for launch by December 2030. The agency’s design, Lunar Reactor 1, would produce about 20 kilowatts of electricity, roughly what sixteen American homes use, and run for five years without intervention.

On the other side, Moscow has assigned its state agencies a reactor called Selena, due by 2036 to power the Chinese-led lunar station. Gebrekidan reports that Selena would deliver up to 10 kilowatts, operate on its own for a decade, and borrow features from a reactor Russia designed for the Arctic. Nuclear power appears to be the one core task Beijing has handed to its partner, and the logic is plain enough. Russia flew more than thirty reactors in orbit during the Cold War. The United States flew one, in 1965, shut it down after an unrelated failure, and has since spent more than US$20 billion on space nuclear programmes without deploying another.

The reason for NASA’s haste is stated without much varnish. The agency fears that if China and Russia get a reactor running first, they could declare a safety perimeter around it, a keep-out zone that would in practice decide where American astronauts and rovers could and could not go. The race, in other words, is not to the Moon. It is to the best real estate on the Moon, and a reactor is the fence post.

Readers who have followed this series will notice something about the numbers, because the numbers have a habit in this story. When NASA’s then-acting administrator Sean Duffy announced the fast-track in August 2025, the directive called for a 100-kilowatt reactor, as Fox News reported at the time. NASA’s own Fission Surface Power page still describes a 40-kilowatt-class system. The specification NASA actually put to contractors this summer is for 20. The launch date has held. The reactor has shrunk to a fifth of its advertised size. That is not necessarily a scandal; a small reactor that works beats a big one that doesn’t. But it is the same pattern Part XII found with the Starship ship catch, which keeps arriving on schedule by the simple method of moving the schedule.

The Canadian precedent

Gebrekidan’s reactor piece devotes a singl

At 11:53 GMT on January 24, 1978, a Sovie paragraph to an event that, for Canadian readers, deserves rather more.et ocean-surveillance satellite called Kosmos 954 came down over the Northwest Territories. It carried a reactor fuelled with roughly fifty kilograms of highly enriched uranium. The system meant to boost the spent core into a safe disposal orbit had failed. Moscow’s first position was that the reactor had been designed to burn up completely on re-entry. It had not. Debris fell along a path of some 600 kilometres, from Great Slave Lake near Fort Resolution to Baker Lake, across land that was and is home to Dené communities.

What followed was Operation Morning Light, a joint Canadian-American search that swept about 124,000 square kilometres on foot and by air between January and October 1978. Searchers recovered a dozen large fragments, ten of them radioactive, at least one hot enough to kill someone who stayed close to it for a few hours. The recovered material, by most accounts, amounted to only a sliver of the fuel. The Times puts it at a tenth of one per cent; other estimates run to about one per cent. Either way, nearly all of it is still out there.

Then came the bill. Canada invoked the 1972 Convention on International Liability for Damage Caused by Space Objects and invoiced the Soviet Union for C$6,041,174.70. The seventy cents is a nice touch, and very Canadian. On April 2, 1981, after more than three years of diplomacy, Moscow agreed to pay C$3 million to close the file, and the settlement was signed by Canada’s ambassador, Geoffrey Pearson.

There is a footnote Stephen Leacock would have enjoyed. Four days after the crash, on January 28, 1978, Saturday Night Live ran a sketch in which radioactive Soviet debris had produced giant mutant lobsters marching on the eastern seaboard. That was the American response to a nuclear accident on Canadian soil: a joke about seafood, with New York as the victim.

I raise Kosmos 954 because it is the only real-world test of what happens when a space reactor goes wrong, and because it established two principles that matter for the Moon. The first is that the launching state is absolutely liable for damage it causes on Earth, no proof of negligence required. That is the one part of the treaty system that has actually been tested and actually worked, even if only at fifty cents on the dollar.

The second principle is the one nobody talks about. Under the same 1972 convention, liability for damage anywhere other than the surface of the Earth is not absolute. It depends on proving fault. On the Moon, in other words, the polluter is liable only if someone can show the pollution was its own fault, and both NASA and the Russian agencies, according to the Times, plan to deal with spent reactors by leaving them where they stand. On Earth, decommissioning a reactor takes years. On the Moon the plan is to walk away.

What could go wrong

Gebrekidan is careful not to overstate the danger, and I will try to follow her lead. Both the American and Russian designs would launch unirradiated, with fresh fuel that has not yet been run, and nuclear engineers say unirradiated uranium poses little radioactive threat even if it falls back to Earth. That is a real improvement on 1978, when Kosmos 954 came down after months of operation with a hot core.

But the risks that remain have a familiar ring for readers of this series. Rockets fail on launch. Most launch pads sit beside water, and water can slow neutrons enough to push a reactor towards criticality, which is why NASA’s specification demands a design that cannot go critical in the sea. No one has ever installed a reactor in low gravity. On the Moon there will be no containment building, so the shielding will be thinner and the no-go zones wider, and with weak gravity, debris from an accident would travel a long way.

The MIT nuclear engineer R. Scott Kemp gave Gebrekidan the line of the week: “The definition of an accident is things don’t go according to plan.” Twelve instalments of this series could serve as his footnotes. The question is what we choose to put on top of rockets while they are still learning.

There is also the fuel. NASA wants to use high-assay low-enriched uranium, the safer option for proliferation, which cannot readily be turned into a weapon. The trouble is that the United States has little of it, Russia is the biggest producer, and Washington has banned Russian uranium imports since 2024. Meanwhile at least one prospective bidder, Space Nuclear Power Corporation, is designing around highly enriched uranium, the very fuel that Representative Bill Foster, a physicist, spent years persuading Washington to abandon. A tight deadline and a fuel shortage make for an awkward pair. The Russian-Chinese partnership has not said what fuel Selena will use. Russia’s older space reactors, including the one that came down on Great Slave Lake, used the highly enriched kind.

Who owns the Moon?

Gebrekidan’s second piece opens with Robert Heinlein’s 1950 novella The Man Who Sold the Moon, in which a tycoon reaches the Moon first and then sells title to its surface. She notes that Elon Musk is a fan of Heinlein’s work, and that Musk now talks of trillion-dollar lunar markets, including data centres and a catapult for flinging satellites deeper into space. Jeff Bezos, for his part, wants to move heavy industry off the planet and leave Earth as a sort of garden.

Part XII met the data centres already, in SpaceX’s $26-trillion orbital-compute forecast. Moving the server to the Moon does not make the arithmetic easier. It does make the legal question far more interesting.

The legal framework is the Outer Space Treaty of 1967, which more than a hundred countries signed, and which says that no nation may claim outer space or the Moon by occupation or use. It is, at bottom, an arms-control treaty from the age of Ralph Kramden, written when the only people who could reach the Moon were two governments. It says nothing clear about what a company may do there.

There was an attempt to fill the gap. The Moon Agreement, concluded in 1979, declared lunar resources the common heritage of humankind and called for international rules on their exploitation. According to the Times, only seventeen countries signed on. The United States, the Soviet Union and China all stayed out, for the obvious reason that none of them wished to share either its technology or its future winnings.

So the vacuum has been filled by national laws and by clubs. A 2015 American statute allows companies to mine in space. The Artemis Accords, a set of non-binding principles drafted by Washington, now have 76 signatories, Canada among them, and they say that extracting resources does not violate the 1967 treaty. China has its own agreement with at least a dozen countries for its international lunar research station. The working argument, borrowed by some scholars from the law of the sea, is that you may own what you dig up even if you may not own the ground you dug it from.

Bleddyn Bowen, who teaches astropolitics at Durham University, summed up the arrangement for Gebrekidan as a grey area everybody is happy with. It is happy for the moment because nothing of great value has yet been extracted. A reactor changes that, because a reactor brings a safety zone, and a safety zone looks a great deal like a border. Space lawyers quoted by the Times say a perimeter that excludes others would collide with the first article of the 1967 treaty, which guarantees free access for all. A United Nations working group is supposed to produce principles in 2027. I would not hold my breath. As Bowen observes, if only two countries are doing anything on the Moon, they can more or less settle the rules between themselves.

Where Alice comes in

Which brings us back to the rocket.

Neither of Gebrekidan’s pieces mentions Starship by name, and nothing I have read says how NASA intends to get Lunar Reactor 1 down to the surface. But the Times points out that no one has a proven lander capable of lowering heavy and potentially radioactive cargo onto the lunar surface, and the only heavy lander the United States is paying for at scale is the one this series has been watching blow up in the Gulf of Mexico. Blue Origin is building the second. Either way, the reactor timetable now rides on the lander timetable, and the lander timetable, as Part XII set out, rides on something like fifteen tanker flights in orbit within days of one another and a propellant transfer SpaceX has yet to demonstrate even once.

Line the dates up. A crewed lunar landing targeted for 2028, in Artemis IV. NASA’s first nuclear-propelled spacecraft bound for Mars in December 2028, something that has never been tried. A reactor ready for launch in December 2030. A Pentagon programme for reactors of its own, in orbit and on the Moon. Each of these depends on the ones before it working on time, and Gebrekidan reports that few experts expect any of the deadlines to be met. On the evidence of the past three years, neither do I.

And yet the deadlines matter even if they slip, because the rules will be set by whoever arrives first, and both sides know it. That is the real hazard in the reporting. It is not that a reactor will fall on Fort Resolution again; the engineering has improved since 1978, and a cold core is a much smaller danger than a hot one. It is that the race, by its nature, rewards the side willing to cut the most corners. Julien de Troullioud de Lanversin, a nuclear scientist in Hong Kong, made the point to the Times: in a race, nobody wants to stop and solve the hard problem that no one has solved, which in this case is what to do with a spent reactor on a world with no one to clean it up.

Part XII ended with a mission-control voice saying “Actually, stand by”, as caution gave way to ambition with the reasons left for later. Put that culture on the Moon with a reactor and the reasons may never arrive.

Coda: the Kramden doctrine

Ralph Kramden never did send Alice to the Moon. The threat was the whole of the joke. Week after week he would raise the fist, and week after week Alice would look at him with the calm of someone who knew exactly how far his rocket could fly. She was the realist in the household, and she was always right.

We are now in an odd position. The rocket is real, the Moon is reachable, and the superpowers are no longer bluffing. What remains a bluff is the governance. A treaty written for two governments in 1967 is being asked to referee two superpowers, a handful of billionaires, a pair of nuclear reactors and whatever data centre comes next, and the one test case we have, from the barrens of the Northwest Territories, ended with half the bill paid and most of the fuel never found.

Katy Huff, who heads nuclear engineering at the University of Wisconsin-Madison and once ran the U.S. Department of Energy’s nuclear office, told the Times that the best things anyone has achieved in space came from countries working together rather than against each other. It is the most sensible thing in either piece, and the least likely to be heeded.

Alice is still in orbit. When she does get to the south pole, she will find two fences going up, and nobody quite sure who is allowed to hold the keys. She should keep her coat on. She may also want a Geiger counter.
