I. Screwed With a Kiss

The most revealing detail in Chrystia Freeland’s new essay in The Atlantic (September 29, 2026) is a small one. During the NAFTA renegotiation, while Canadian officials waited in the White House for a bilateral meeting, their hosts handed out Hershey’s Kisses in wrappers signed by Donald Trump. Gary Cohn, then Trump’s senior economic adviser, cheerfully explained what the chocolates meant. Canada was going to get screwed either way; the only open question was whether it would get screwed with a kiss.

It is a very Washington sort of hospitality. It is also, eight years later, a fair summary of the choice every American ally has faced since January 2025. Most of them chose the kiss.

The essay is adapted from Freeland’s memoir, Unreliable Boyfriend: An Insider’s View of Dealing With a Chaotic Superpower, Plutocrats, and Other Complicated People, which Simon & Schuster publishes on October 13, 2026. The title comes from her appearance on Bill Maher’s programme in February 2026, when she said the United States was not a very reliable boyfriend at the moment. Its argument can be stated in one line, and she states it in the subtitle: the only thing that works with this president is strength.

She closes by naming Mark Carney as the leader now proving that point to the rest of America’s friends. That endorsement is the reason the essay belongs in this series rather than in The Great Canadian Trade War, where her earlier New York Times piece already did its work. Freeland has written a manual. The question for The Sovereign Country is whether Carney is following it, and the honest answer is: now he is, but not for the first year, and not on the one page she never had to write.

II. Three Rules From 2018

Freeland’s playbook has three parts, and it is worth setting them out plainly before measuring anyone against them.

First, find the areas of genuine agreement. Freeland makes a concession that most Canadian commentary never has: Trump was partly right about the old NAFTA. Low Mexican auto wages really did undercut American workers in the states that elected him in 2016. When the Americans demanded a fixed quota of US-made parts in every compliant car, Canada countered with a labour standard instead, requiring a share of each vehicle to be built by workers earning at least $16 an hour. It gave Trump something to announce and gave Canada a floor under its own auto industry. It is, she argues with some justification, one of the best features of the agreement that replaced NAFTA.

Second, the doughnut. The president sits in the middle, but the country around him is large, and it has senators, governors, farm lobbies and chambers of commerce who all care about their own exports. Freeland courted Paul Ryan, whose Wisconsin sold nearly $7 billion a year to Canada, and John McCain, who told her some of the tariff ideas were insane. The point was never to go around Trump. It was to make sure that when the pain arrived, Americans with influence would be the ones complaining about it.

Third, and above all: do not capitulate. When Washington imposed Section 232 tariffs on Canadian steel and aluminium on June 1, 2018, under the absurd claim that Canadian metal threatened American national security, Canada answered dollar for dollar. The retaliation took effect on July 1, 2018, covered $16.6 billion in American goods, and was the largest trade retaliation Canada had imposed since the Second World War.

The targeting was the clever part. Ketchup and jam from Pennsylvania and Ohio for the Rust Belt. Bourbon and playing cards for Kentucky, home of Senate Majority Leader Mitch McConnell. Cheese and yoghurt for Paul Ryan’s Wisconsin. Canada did not try to hurt America in aggregate; it tried to hurt specific Americans who could pick up a phone. Within four weeks a farm-state business coalition had launched a campaign called “Tariffs Hurt the Heartland.” By Freeland’s account, coordinated retaliation cost Iowa’s farmers nearly $1.5 billion in lost exports, roughly $17,000 a farm.

Her summary of the whole approach, which she used against domestic critics at the time and repeats now, fits in six words: “Capitulation is not a negotiating strategy.”

III. The Year Carney Tried the Other Thing

Here is where the manual and the man part company, at least for a while.

On August 22, 2025, Carney announced that Canada would drop most of its counter-tariffs on American goods, effective September 1, 2025, keeping them only on steel, aluminium and autos. The logic was defensible. Most Canada-US trade was already moving tariff-free under CUSMA’s rules of origin, and Carney wanted the talks to proceed without a standing grievance on the table. It was framed as a gesture of good faith from a country confident enough not to need the theatre.

Not everyone read it that way. Unifor’s Lana Payne said walking back counter-tariffs isn’t an olive branch, and Pierre Poilievre complained that the prime minister kept making concessions without any gains. Alberta’s Danielle Smith, whose province’s energy exports had escaped the worst of the tariffs, supported the move. That split is worth remembering, because it returns in Section V.

By Freeland’s rules, September 2025 was a year-long experiment in the kiss. And the experiment produced a result. On August 21 and 22, 2026, the talks collapsed. Carney told Canadians the Americans had asked too much and offered too little, and noted that Canada had been willing to drop even its remaining counter-tariffs on steel, aluminium and autos if Washington lowered its own. Washington did not. Instead it imposed 50 per cent tariffs on roughly $20 billion of Canadian goods under Section 338 of the 1930 Tariff Act, and then threatened more.

A year of restraint bought Canada no better terms than 2018’s defiance had. If anything it bought worse ones, because the White House had spent that year learning that Ottawa would absorb a blow without returning it.

IV. Back to the Manual

Then Carney opened Freeland’s book.

On September 8, 2026, Canada imposed counter-tariffs on $27.6 billion of American goods, with rates from 15 to 50 per cent, covering steel, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper, and electronics. Ottawa described the measures in exactly the phrase Freeland used in 2018: dollar for dollar. Individual product rates were set to mirror the American rate on the same goods. Finance Minister François-Philippe Champagne paired them with a $7.5 billion support package for affected businesses and workers.

Two details make the parallel sharper than it first looks.

The first is the finance minister. In 2018, Freeland writes, at least one cabinet colleague wanted to push retaliation beyond the one-month grace period and keep talking. She does not name him, but the finance minister in 2018 was Bill Morneau. In 2026, the finance minister was the one announcing the tariffs. Whatever argument happened inside Carney’s cabinet, it ended with the Treasury on the side of retaliation, which is not where it stood last time.

The second is the word Carney chose. He said Canada was acting reluctantly, because the measures would raise costs and reduce choice for Canadians. Freeland said in 2018 that Canada responded more in sorrow than in anger. It is the same register: we did not start this, we would rather not be doing it, and we are doing it anyway. That tone is part of the playbook. Retaliation delivered with visible reluctance gives the other side’s moderates something to work with. Retaliation delivered with glee gives the president a grievance to post about.

So on the third rule, Carney has arrived. He simply took the scenic route.

V. The Lever She Never Had to Pull

There is a page missing from Freeland’s manual, and it is the page that matters most for Carney.

Freeland supplies the arithmetic herself. The $16.6 billion Canada targeted in 2018 equalled about 0.06 per cent of American GDP and about 0.74 per cent of Canada’s. Trade is roughly 65 per cent of the Canadian economy and roughly 25 per cent of the American one. Retaliation in kind is always going to hurt Canada more than the United States, by an order of magnitude. The 2018 playbook worked anyway because it did not rely on aggregate pain. It relied on concentrated pain in politically sensitive places.

Canada has one category of export where the pain would not be concentrated in Canada at all: energy and the raw materials American industry cannot easily replace. Heavy crude for Midwestern and Gulf Coast refineries built to process it. Potash for American farms. Uranium for American reactors. Hydroelectricity for New England and New York. As Part III of this series argued, Carney’s refusal to weaponise any of these is the clearest single read on his actual strategy.

Freeland never had to decide whether to pull that lever, because in 2018 Trump backed off before it came to that. Carney faces a harder version of the question, and the reason he keeps declining is not only economic. The provinces that produce most of that oil and potash are Alberta and Saskatchewan, and their governments are the least enthusiastic about confrontation with Washington of any in the country. Smith supported the 2025 climbdown; an export tax on Alberta crude would be a fight with her before it was a fight with Trump. A sovereign country that splits along provincial lines over how to defend its sovereignty has handed its opponent something more valuable than any tariff.

So Carney’s version of strength has a ceiling that Freeland’s did not. He will match tariffs dollar for dollar. He will not reach for the one weapon that would hurt the United States more than Canada, because reaching for it would hurt Canada’s unity more than either.

Whether that ceiling is prudence or a tell depends on whether Washington believes it is permanent. The White House has spent a year studying Ottawa. It is fair to assume it has noticed.

VI. The Witness and Her Interests

Freeland is an unusually well-placed witness, and she is also a witness with a book to sell. Both things are true, and neither cancels the other.

Her credentials are real. She led Canada’s side in the NAFTA renegotiation, survived two years of Trump calling her a “nasty woman” who hated America, and came home with a deal. She broke with Justin Trudeau in December 2024 over how to respond to Trump’s second term, and her resignation helped end his premiership. She then lost the Liberal leadership to Carney in March 2025, served in his cabinet, left it later that year, and stepped down from Parliament in January 2026.

That history makes her endorsement of Carney more interesting, not less. It is generous of a defeated leadership rival to hold up the winner as the model for the democratic world. It is also diplomatic in what it leaves out. Nowhere in the essay does she mention September 2025, when Carney dropped most of Canada’s counter-tariffs and bet on the approach her own playbook warns against. A reader who did not know the history would assume Carney had followed her rules from the beginning. He did not. He followed them after the alternative failed, which is a different and more useful lesson.

Her own record also carries a caveat she underplays. Strength got Canada the CUSMA deal, agreed on September 30, 2018. But Washington did not lift the steel and aluminium tariffs until May 17, 2019, more than seven months after the agreement it was supposedly the price of. And in February 2025, Trump reimposed them anyway. The 2018 victory was real, but it was, to borrow Carney’s phrase from August, written in pencil. Standing firm got Canada a signature. It did not get Canada a guarantee, because no one can get that from this president.

None of that makes her wrong. It makes her playbook a method for getting a better deal, not a method for getting a durable one. For durability, Canada needs something the playbook does not address: other customers. That is the rest of Carney’s project, from the European “alliance for the future” to the Gripen review to the potash talks, and it is where Carney has gone beyond anything Freeland was in a position to attempt.

VII. What Sovereignty Costs

Freeland’s essay borrows Pierre Trudeau’s line about sleeping next to an elephant: however friendly the animal, you never sleep altogether easily. It is the right image for a piece about 2018. It is slightly the wrong one for 2026, because the elephant is no longer merely large. It has started rolling over on purpose.

Measured against her manual, Carney’s record reads like this. On common ground, he has been more willing than she was to offer the Americans things they want, sometimes too willing. On the doughnut, he is working the same territory she did, and the midterm elections on November 3, 2026 will show whether the Americans in the ring are ready to complain on Canada’s behalf the way Iowa’s farmers did in 2018. On capitulation, he spent a year testing whether restraint would be rewarded, learned that it would not, and returned to dollar-for-dollar retaliation on September 8, 2026. And on energy, the lever that would hurt Washington most, he has written his own page, and it says no.

That last choice is the one worth watching. It is where the title of this series earns its keep. Carney’s line to reporters, that it means we’re a sovereign country, was never only about standing up to the United States. It was also about deciding for ourselves what we will and will not do to defend ourselves, and at what cost to whom. Freeland’s manual says strength is the only thing that works. Carney’s amendment is that strength includes knowing which weapon would split your own house, and leaving it on the rack.

Cohn was right in 2018 that Canada would get screwed either way. What he missed, and what Freeland’s essay is really about, is that the kiss is optional. Canada declined it once, accepted it for a year, and has now declined it again. The Atlantic’s comment section, like the country, seems broadly to approve. The White House will test whether the second refusal holds better than the first.
