Howard Lutnick made his promise under oath. At his confirmation hearing in January 2025, the incoming commerce secretary was asked about China’s progress in artificial intelligence. “We need to stop helping them,” he said. “I’m going to be very strong on that.” Companies like Nvidia, he told the senators, had to stop letting China use “our tools to compete with us,” as The New York Times recalled on July 14, 2025.

In June 2025 he repeated the point to a House subcommittee. Chinese companies, he said, “are trying to copy our technology and in the race for A.I. supremacy, they are behind us, but they are working with a central government out to get us.”

Eighteen months later, according to Ana Swanson, Tripp Mickle, Maggie Haberman and Jonathan Swan in The New York Times on November 12, 2026, there is broad agreement among senior officials that the United States should tighten controls on Nvidia’s chips. Then there is Lutnick. Critics inside the administration and in Congress describe him as one of the biggest obstacles to tighter rules, and accuse his department of becoming too close to Nvidia and its co-founder.

This matters more than any one cabinet feud, because of where Lutnick sits. The Commerce Department enforces export controls. It writes the rules for what American technology may be sold abroad, issues the licences, keeps the entity list of blacklisted companies, and investigates smuggling. Of everyone in the government, the commerce secretary is best placed to decide on his own whether China gets access to American chips. This part follows the man. Part V goes inside his department.

The bond trader at the gate

Lutnick came to Commerce from Wall Street. A former bond trader with no government experience, he ran Cantor Fitzgerald for decades and has known Trump since their days in New York. He led the presidential transition in late 2024 with an approach his internal critics considered heavy-handed, and it earned him few friends. He pushed hard to become Treasury secretary instead of Scott Bessent, at times irritating Trump in the process.

He has also shown himself willing to do whatever he thinks the president wants. That has included fighting to put Trump’s name on the Kennedy Center and, when his own standing was shaky, donating $25 million to the future Trump presidential library.

“Out of his hands”

The first test came in the spring of 2025, when the administration moved to ban Nvidia’s H20 chip in China. Lutnick told Nvidia the matter was out of his hands, and that the company’s only recourse was to appeal to the president.

That is accurate as far as it goes. But as Tripp Mickle reported in The New York Times on July 17, 2025, it was Lutnick who then invited Jensen Huang to Mar-a-Lago to make that appeal, at a $1-million-a-head donors’ dinner on April 4. The ban went ahead on April 15. But the commerce secretary had personally opened the door through which Huang would walk again, and keep walking.

In July 2025 the ban was lifted, as Part II described. Lutnick explained on CNBC that the decision was tied to rare-earth negotiations with Beijing, and that the idea was to sell China Nvidia’s fourth-best chip so that “they get addicted to the American technology stack.” Six months had taken him from “we need to stop helping them” to a strategy of helping them on purpose, in the hope they would get hooked.

The rule that backfired

The episode that seems to have shaped Lutnick most came in September 2025, and it went the other way.

That month, the Commerce Department issued a sweeping rule that added tens of thousands of Chinese companies to the entity list, which bars American firms from exporting to listed companies without a government licence. It was the most aggressive use of the department’s powers in years. China responded within days by cutting off rare-earth minerals to the United States, a supply that American factories making cars, tools and other products could not replace quickly.

Panicked calls from chief executives followed, and the administration quickly agreed to suspend the rule as part of a trade pact with China. Lutnick’s staff were then asked to write him a short summary of what the entity list is.

People familiar with the episode say he was chastened by the blowback. Since then the department has essentially stopped issuing new technology controls against China, concerned that any measure might upset a fragile truce. It has not added a single company to the entity list in more than a year, the longest stretch without an update in 18 years. Megaspeed, the company at the centre of Part I, is not on it, although other agencies have proposed adding it.

The cut, the licences, the lawyers

In December 2025 Trump cleared Nvidia to sell its H200 chip to China, with the government taking 25 percent of the revenue. U.S. law prohibits charging for export licences. Commerce Department lawyers were deployed to find a way around that ban and make the president’s idea work.

The department was also required to review companies for national security risks before the H200 sales went ahead, as Mack Liederman reported in The New York Times on November 12, 2026. According to the Times, it has approved licences for Nvidia to sell chips to Chinese firms that the paper found had links to sanctioned entities or military contractors, and it has refrained from adding major Chinese Nvidia customers to the entity list.

The department’s defenders point to its enforcement record. A Commerce spokeswoman said it had charged defendants in several major criminal investigations involving billions of dollars of chip smuggling, and that it imposed a licence requirement on the H20, unlike the Biden administration. It says it has been involved in at least six cases involving smuggling of Nvidia chips. None of those charges have been brought against Nvidia itself.

October 2026: “I don’t understand the rush”

The clearest case of Lutnick slowing things down came this autumn, a few weeks before the midterm elections.

The biggest loophole in American chip controls is not smuggling. It is remote access. The most advanced chips cannot be exported to China, but Chinese companies can rent their computing power from data centres in Southeast Asia, which is exactly the business Megaspeed is in. The Biden administration issued rules to restrict the practice in January 2025, and the Trump administration rolled them back shortly after taking office. Officials have argued ever since about what should replace them.

By October 2026 a replacement was ready. The State and Treasury Departments supported it. Elon Musk and Mark Zuckerberg backed it. The president had signed off. Officials had just persuaded Oracle to drop a $7 billion deal to lease advanced chips to Tencent, and Oracle had warned them that its competitors were still providing America’s best computing power to Chinese firms.

Then Jeff Kessler, Lutnick’s top deputy for export controls, weighed in to say the secretary had substantial policy problems with the idea. Lutnick told colleagues he didn’t understand the rush. The regulation would affect other countries as well as Nvidia, he said, and he warned the team of Susie Wiles, then the White House chief of staff, that new action could set off a wider trade war with China and that he needed more discussions with the president. The department says it is actively considering new rules on remote access, along with other agencies.

What Commerce put forward instead was a narrower version, as Ana Swanson, Aaron Krolik and Paul Mozur reported in The New York Times on November 15, 2026. It would cover just seven countries, Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Brazil, South Africa and Norway, where others in the administration wanted global coverage. It would apply only to future chip shipments, leaving data centres already full of American hardware untouched. Companies would have to tell the government when Chinese customers trained A.I. models on their machines, but would not automatically need a licence, and the rules would kick in only at higher levels of computing power. Chris McGuire, a former Biden official now at the Council on Foreign Relations, predicted the obvious workaround: “If you’re only restricting remote access to data centers in certain countries, then China will just rent from the data centers outside those countries.”

He had also been warned. Lutnick was among the handful of Cabinet officials who received the classified intelligence memo on Alice Huang and her relationship with the Nvidia founder, Kang and McCabe reported in The New York Times; by their account it reached him in May 2026, the month of the Beijing trip.

The confrontation

Lutnick’s colleagues have noticed. Bessent, Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Trade Representative Jamieson Greer have all pushed for tighter restrictions on Nvidia’s sales to China. The president has declined to choose. “Different viewpoints,” he said of the Lutnick–Bessent split on November 16. “One wants to be friendly with China, and one wants to have a blowup with China. Both are acceptable, but they’re different views.” Lutnick, he added, was “doing a very good job,” as The New York Times reported. Even after the scandal broke, Lutnick’s department was still expected to name Nvidia an A.I. “national champion,” with faster export approvals and access to federal financing, according to The Wall Street Journal, as The New York Times reported.

After the revelations about Huang, the tension came into the open. At a White House meeting in the first week of November 2026, according to the Times, Bessent confronted Lutnick over his handling of Megaspeed and demanded to know why the United States had not already banned chip sales to the company. Lutnick replied that new measures could be in place soon.

A Commerce spokeswoman denied that the exchange happened. On CNBC on November 13, Bessent declined to discuss White House conversations, but he left no doubt about where he thought the blame lay. As Ana Swanson reported in The New York Times that day, he said the Treasury, Defense and State Departments and the trade representative had all supported putting Megaspeed on the entity list for months, but that Commerce “has the pen.” “You will have to ask them about their internal processes,” he said. “Treasury and my deputy has been pushing for this for months at the deputies level.”

According to people briefed on the discussions, when the cabinet deputies took up the listing, Kessler deferred, saying the decision was Lutnick’s. The Commerce spokeswoman said decisions on the entity list are made across the administration. Bessent also turned the question back on Nvidia: did it know its client, he asked, and did it make a real effort to avoid selling to “obviously pass-through shell companies”? If not, he said, “anyone at the company there” should be held accountable.

November 16: the rule he didn’t write

The argument ended on November 16, 2026, the morning Trump left for Asia, and Lutnick lost it. The president had approved a data-centre rule in August, according to two senior officials, but Commerce never issued it. Now it did, and it was the broad version his rivals wanted, as Ana Swanson and Tony Romm reported in The New York Times. Overseas data centres will need a government licence before supplying “significant volumes of computing power” to firms headquartered in China, Russia, Iran and a few other high-risk countries, and must check their customers for links to them. Advanced chips sold to companies headquartered in Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand or Brazil will need a licence too. The rule takes effect in 90 days. Chip stocks fell that day: Nvidia more than 4 percent, Micron nearly 8.

The press release carried a line nobody could remember seeing before. The rule, Commerce said, had been “written by and at the direction of the Department of Treasury and the National Security Council.” A person close to Lutnick said the line was there to make clear who was responsible for what his camp views as a misguided policy, as The New York Times reported. Christopher Padilla, a Commerce official in the George W. Bush administration, said he had never seen the department publicly disown a rule it was publishing and would have to enforce. “Like open mutiny,” he told The New York Times. “That’s unprecedented.” Chris McGuire put it more bluntly: Commerce had been “dragged kicking and screaming over the finish line.” If enforced, he said, the rule “effectively kills the business of neoclouds like Megaspeed.”

If enforced: the job falls to Lutnick, its staunchest opponent. Trump, asked about the delay, defended him. “If you didn’t delay it, if you didn’t do something, you could have a blowup with China,” he said. Some China hawks now worry that the president will treat the rule as something to trade with Xi. H.R. McMaster, his former national security adviser, said Trump “is often impeded by his belief that he can get the deal nobody else can get.”

The case for the defence

Lutnick’s defenders make one argument above all: he was doing what the president wanted. One person who witnessed many discussions about chip policy said Trump was, more often than not, the person most sympathetic to Lutnick’s position. Another official said Trump wanted a good relationship with Xi Jinping. A third said the relationship between Lutnick and Huang was not always pleasant, including when Lutnick did things the Nvidia chief disliked.

All of that is probably true, and none of it changes the outcome. If the commerce secretary’s role is simply to anticipate what the president wants and make it happen, the safeguard that Congress built into the export control system, a department whose job is to say no, no longer exists. The Commerce spokeswoman called the suggestion that Trump’s friendship with Huang had chilled the department’s work “false and reckless.” But the record of the past year is the record of a department that stopped blacklisting, sat for three months on the one rule that would have touched Megaspeed’s business, and found legal workarounds for the president’s revenue share.

The man who promised

Lutnick will have to defend that record in person. On November 12, 2026, Senator Tim Scott, the Republican chairman of the Senate Banking Committee, asked him and Huang to testify on December 3, and warned that the committee could issue a subpoena. On November 23 Scott confirmed a public hearing that day under the title “Protecting America’s A.I. Advantage: Oversight of U.S. Export Controls.” The top Democrats on two congressional committees had written to Lutnick on November 12 to say they were investigating.

He promised the Senate in January 2025 that he would be very strong. The question now is whether the department he runs was ever allowed to be.
