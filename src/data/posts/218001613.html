The Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar is a walled garden, roughly the size of a few city blocks, enclosed on all sides by the backs of houses. In 1919 it had a handful of narrow entrances, most of them passable only on foot. On the afternoon of 13 April, the day of the Baisakhi festival, a crowd of somewhere between ten and twenty thousand people gathered there. Some had come to protest the arrest of two local nationalist leaders. Many were pilgrims and villagers in town for the holiday who may not have known that public meetings had been banned.

Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer arrived with about ninety soldiers, Gurkha and Baluchi and Sikh troops of the Indian Army, and two armoured cars that could not fit through the entrance. He positioned fifty riflemen at the main passage, the principal way out, and without warning ordered them to fire into the crowd. They fired for about ten minutes, some 1,650 rounds, directing their fire where the crowd was thickest and at the exits people were trying to reach. Some jumped into a well to escape. When the ammunition was nearly gone, Dyer marched his troops away. A curfew was in force. The wounded were left where they lay overnight.

The official inquiry counted 379 dead and about 1,200 wounded. The Indian National Congress, conducting its own investigation, put the dead at around a thousand. The bullet marks are still in the walls, outlined in white so visitors can find them.

Dyer told the inquiry that his purpose had been not merely to disperse the meeting but to produce a moral effect, as he put it, throughout the Punjab. He was not describing a loss of control. He was describing the fifth point of this series’ rubric.

How the Raj began: a company that kept the books

British rule in India did not start as a government. It started as a business. The East India Company, chartered in 1600 to trade in spices, won the Battle of Plassey in 1757 under Robert Clive, largely by bribing the commander of the Nawab of Bengal’s army to stand aside. In 1765 the Mughal emperor, defeated again, granted the Company the diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa: the right to collect their land revenue. A trading company had become the tax collector of one of the richest regions on earth, with its own army to enforce collection.

The Roman parallel is uncomfortably close. The publicani who bid for provincial tax contracts in the Republic were private companies too. And when Bengal suffered a catastrophic famine in 1769 and 1770, killing perhaps a third of its population by some contemporary estimates, the Company’s revenue collections held up remarkably well. The land tax was collected from the survivors.

Parliament eventually took notice. Warren Hastings, the first Governor-General, was impeached in 1788 and tried in Westminster Hall for seven years, with Edmund Burke leading the prosecution in speeches that deliberately recalled Cicero’s case against Verres. Hastings was acquitted in 1795. Like the Roman trial, it was a prosecution for excess, not for the system.

Annexation by paperwork

The Company expanded across the subcontinent over the following eight decades, by war where necessary and by paperwork where possible. Its favourite paper instrument would have been familiar to the Roman Senate. Under the Doctrine of Lapse, applied with particular energy by Lord Dalhousie as Governor-General from 1848 to 1856, a princely state whose ruler died without a natural heir passed to the Company, regardless of adoption, which Hindu law recognised. Satara went in 1848, Jhansi in 1853, Nagpur in 1854. The rich kingdom of Awadh, whose ruler had heirs, was annexed in 1856 on the grounds of misgovernment instead.

Rome took Pergamon by the will of a king. The Company took Jhansi by refusing to recognise one. The next year, the Rani of Jhansi, Lakshmibai, the widow whose adopted son had been denied the throne, became one of the leaders of the largest rising British rule in India ever faced.

The rebellion and the Crown

The rising that began at Meerut in May 1857, among the Company’s own Indian soldiers, spread across much of northern and central India and took more than a year to suppress. Both sides committed atrocities; the British reprisals were systematic. Captured rebels were hanged in rows along roads or tied to the mouths of cannon and blown apart, a punishment chosen because it destroyed the body and would be witnessed. When British forces retook Delhi in September 1857, the city was sacked and much of its population expelled. The last Mughal emperor, Bahadur Shah Zafar, eighty-two years old, was tried and exiled to Rangoon, and two of his sons and a grandson were shot by a British cavalry officer after they surrendered.

In 1858, Parliament transferred India from the Company to the Crown. Queen Victoria’s proclamation that November promised Indians equal treatment under the law and respect for their religions and customs. In 1877, at the Delhi Durbar, she was proclaimed Empress of India in a spectacle designed, in part, by John Lockwood Kipling, the father of the poet.

That Durbar took place in the middle of the Great Famine of 1876 to 1878, which killed somewhere between five and ten million people in southern and western India. Grain continued to be exported. Relief was administered on principles the Treasury of the 1840s would have recognised: Sir Richard Temple, sent to limit expenditure, cut the ration in relief camps to a pound of grain a day for a man doing heavy labour. Medical officers at the time warned that it was not enough to keep a working man alive, and in many camps it did not.

The mission, written down

What sets India apart from every other case in the British section is how explicitly the justifying language was put on paper, as policy, by the people making it.

In February 1835, Thomas Babington Macaulay, a member of the Governor-General’s council, wrote his Minute on Indian Education, which decided that government funds would support English-language education rather than Sanskrit or Arabic learning. He wrote that he had never found an Orientalist who could deny that a single shelf of a good European library was worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia. The goal, he said, was to create a class of people Indian in blood and colour but English in taste, opinions, morals and intellect, to act as interpreters between the rulers and the ruled. It is Tacitus’s Britons in their togas, written as a government programme, without Tacitus’s irony.

The verse the Introduction promised needs a correction of its own. Rudyard Kipling, born in Bombay in 1865, published “The White Man’s Burden” in February 1899, and it is the most famous statement of the civilising creed in English. But its subtitle was “The United States and the Philippine Islands.” Kipling wrote it to urge the Americans to take up empire in Asia, casting the colonised as new-caught, sullen peoples, “half-devil and half-child.” It was India’s creed exported, and it will reappear in this series’ American chapters. The poem did not invent the idea. It set to rhyme what Macaulay had set down as policy two generations earlier.

Indian thinkers answered in kind. Dadabhai Naoroji, elected to the British House of Commons for Finsbury Central in 1892, spent decades documenting what he called the “drain”: the transfer of Indian wealth to Britain through salaries, pensions, interest on debt India was made to carry, and the costs of wars India was made to fund. His book was titled Poverty and Un-British Rule in India. The title was the argument. The rule described itself one way and behaved another.

Amritsar, and what came after

The massacre did not stand alone. In the days around it, the Punjab was placed under martial law. Aircraft bombed and machine-gunned crowds at Gujranwala on 14 April. In Amritsar, on the street where an English missionary schoolteacher, Marcella Sherwood, had been attacked during rioting on 10 April, Dyer issued his “crawling order”: any Indian using the street had to crawl its length on hands and knees. Public floggings were administered.

The reaction in India was immediate. Rabindranath Tagore, who had won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1913, renounced his knighthood in a letter to the Viceroy. Gandhi, who had supported Britain during the First World War, turned decisively towards non-cooperation.

The reaction in Britain was divided, and that division is the most revealing part of the story. The Hunter Committee censured Dyer, and he was removed from his command. In the House of Commons debate on 8 July 1920, Winston Churchill, then Secretary of State for War and no one’s idea of an anti-imperialist, called the massacre a monstrous event without precedent in the modern history of the empire. But the House of Lords passed a motion in Dyer’s favour, and a fund launched by the Morning Post raised about £26,000 for him, a very large sum at the time, from people who believed he had saved India. He was, to a substantial part of the British public, a hero.

Scoring the Raj against the rubric

Overwhelming asymmetry. This is the case where the asymmetry needs explaining, because India was vastly larger than Britain in population and the British in India were always a small minority. The asymmetry was one of organisation, technology and division: British rule depended on Indian soldiers, Indian clerks and Indian princes, recruited and played against one another. At every point where it was tested, from Plassey to 1857 to Amritsar, the power gap in the room was real and uncontested. Satisfied.

Coercion as first resort. The Company’s rule began with a battle won by bribery and a tax grant extracted after defeat; expansion ran on war and annexation; Amritsar was an order to fire without warning. Satisfied.

Extraction is the point. Land revenue, the drain Naoroji documented, and grain exported through famines, while the stated purpose was the education and improvement of the governed. Satisfied.

Self-justifying rhetoric. Macaulay’s Minute, the Queen’s proclamation, Kipling’s burden. Of all the cases in this series, this is the one where the cover story was most openly published as the mission. Satisfied, in writing.

Resistance gets punished. Rebels blown from guns in 1857. Dyer’s “moral effect” in 1919. Satisfied.

Five for five.

A Canadian footnote that belongs in the text

Canada’s role in the Raj was small, but it is not zero, and the Introduction promised not to look away when the rubric points home. In May 1914, the Komagata Maru arrived in Vancouver carrying 376 passengers from the Punjab, nearly all of them Sikhs, all of them British subjects, testing Canadian immigration rules written specifically to keep Indians out. They were held aboard ship in the harbour for two months and then forced to sail back. When the ship reached Budge Budge, near Calcutta, in September 1914, British police tried to detain the passengers and opened fire; about twenty were killed. Canada’s Prime Minister apologised in the House of Commons in 2016. It is not a case of Canadian bullying on the scale of this series, but it is a reminder that the empire’s rules about who counted as a full subject were enforced in Vancouver as well as Amritsar.

What the Raj tells us

The Raj ended in 1947, in a partition that killed perhaps a million people and displaced some fifteen million more, carried out in haste by a Britain that could no longer afford to stay. Its most lasting lesson for this series is the one Dyer himself supplied. Every empire says its violence is regrettable and exceptional. Dyer said his was deliberate, and designed to be seen, and a large part of his own country agreed with him.
