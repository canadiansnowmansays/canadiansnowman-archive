by Stephen Gordon

In January 2025, Robert F. Kennedy Jr. sat before two Senate committees and asked to be trusted with the health of 340 million Americans. He had spent two decades as the country’s most prominent vaccine sceptic, and everyone in the room knew it. So he did what nominees with a record do. He made promises.

Some he made in open testimony. Others he made privately to Senator Bill Cassidy, the Louisiana Republican and physician whose vote on the Health, Education, Labor and Pensions committee was the one that mattered. Cassidy then took those private promises to the Senate floor, read them into the record, and voted yes. Kennedy was confirmed on February 13, 2025.

This series has covered, one installment at a time, almost every breach that followed. What it has never done is lay the promises side by side, in his words and Cassidy’s, against the dates on which each one gave way. That is what this installment does. It is not a new story. It is the old stories, filed in the order that makes the pattern impossible to miss.

There is a distinction worth holding onto from the start. A broken promise is not automatically a lie. People change their minds; evidence changes; circumstances change. A promise becomes deceit only if the person making it never meant to keep it. That is a claim about intent, and it has to be earned. The argument of this essay is that the ledger earns it — and that the most damning column is not what Kennedy did, but how quickly he did it.

The Ledger

Promise by Promise

The schedule. The words were Kennedy’s own, in answer to Senator Elizabeth Warren: as KFF Health News documented, he told the committee, “I support vaccines. I support the childhood schedule. I will do that.” Readers of “How Kennedy Tried to Erase the Vaccine Schedule and Called the Erasing a Rumor” (July 27) know what sixteen officials told Reuters he was doing behind closed doors in 2025: debating whether to replace the schedule altogether and whether to strip out MMR and polio. Readers of “The Director Who Wasn’t in the Room” (August 13) watched him stand beside the President as the schedule was cut to eleven diseases. There is no reading of “I support the childhood schedule” that survives both.

The committee. According to Cassidy’s own account of their agreement — quoted by Dr. Paul Offit in his open letter to the senator — Kennedy pledged to keep ACIP’s recommendations “without changes.” Three and a half months after confirmation, he announced by video on social media that Covid shots were no longer recommended for healthy children and pregnant women, without an ACIP vote. Two weeks after that, he fired all seventeen members of the committee and replaced them with a panel that included vaccine opponents. It is difficult to change a committee’s recommendations more thoroughly than by changing the committee.

The system. The pledge to work within existing vaccine approval and safety systems, rather than build parallel ones, was the promise meant to reassure the Senate that Kennedy would be a steward rather than a rival. The ACIP purge answered it within four months. The August 10, 2026 executive order answered it definitively: it does not reform the statutory advisory process, it goes around it.

The money. Here the record is starkest, because the question was specific and so was the answer. Cassidy asked whether Kennedy would commit not to impound, divert or otherwise reduce vaccination funding Congress had appropriated. Kennedy said yes. About six weeks after confirmation, the CDC clawed back roughly $11 billion in grants that state and local health departments were using for, among other things, vaccination work. In August 2025 he cancelled $500 million in mRNA vaccine research — the cancellation this series has treated since its first installment as a national security decision, not a budgetary one. And as this morning’s installment, “The Body Is Still There,” recorded, the New York Times has now reported that $362,000 was taken from the Vaccines for Children programme to pay for a no-bid study of a debunked autism hypothesis. “Divert” was the verb in the question. It is hard to find a better example of the thing.

The autism statement. This is the one promise Kennedy can claim to have kept, and the manner of keeping it tells you the most. Cassidy said he had been guaranteed that the CDC would not remove its statement that vaccines do not cause autism. In November 2025 the statement stayed. The page around it was rewritten to say the claim is not evidence-based and that studies have not ruled out a link. The letter of the promise survived. Its purpose — that the CDC would keep telling parents the truth — did not. A lawyer would call this compliance. Everyone else would call it a trick.

The testimony. Cassidy said Kennedy had agreed to appear before the HELP committee on a quarterly basis if requested. As NBC News reported, Kennedy appeared in May 2025; Cassidy’s request of September 18, 2025 then sat unscheduled for five months. This series documented the evasion in December 2025’s “Propaganda Pivot,” and the eventual appearances of April 2026 in “Seven Hearings, Zero Accountability” — where Kennedy, asked whether he would accept the vaccine decisions of the CDC director he had just nominated, replied that he would not make that kind of commitment. By that point, it is fair to say, he had stopped making them.

The relationship. Cassidy described the arrangement he had secured as unusually close and collaborative, with frequent meetings. When Cassidy publicly asked in June 2025 that the newly restocked ACIP postpone its first meeting until the new members had been properly vetted, the meeting went ahead on schedule. A promise of consultation is only as good as the first time the consulted party says no.

The Speed Is the Evidence

Now return to the right-hand column of the ledger, because it is the part of this story that has never been told in one place.

Six weeks. Three and a half months. Four months. Six months. Nine months.

A man who sincerely intends to keep a promise, and is later persuaded by new evidence to abandon it, leaves a particular kind of trail. There is a period of keeping it. There is a moment of doubt, usually public. There is new evidence, usually cited. There is an explanation, usually to the person who was promised. Kennedy’s record contains none of these stages. The first breach — the grant clawback — came before most of his senior appointees had been sworn in. The ACIP firing, the single most consequential act of his tenure, came barely four months in, announced in an op-ed rather than to the senator whose vote rested on the committee’s survival. No new body of evidence emerged between February and June 2025 to explain why a committee worth preserving in the winter was worth abolishing by the summer. The only thing that changed was that Kennedy had been confirmed.

And the Reuters reporting this series covered in July closes the remaining gap. According to sixteen officials, the plan to dismantle the schedule was being debated inside HHS in 2025 — the same year in which he told the Senate he supported it. People do not usually begin planning to demolish a thing within months of sincerely promising to protect it.

Set against his two decades of advocacy before he took office, the most economical explanation of the ledger is not that Kennedy changed his mind. It is that he never changed it at all — and that the promises were the price of the vote, paid in a currency he did not intend to honour.

The Defence

Kennedy’s position deserves to be stated fairly, because he states it often. He has told Congress he is complying with all of his agreements with Cassidy. He argues that the old ACIP was riddled with pharmaceutical conflicts of interest, and that replacing it was a way of restoring trust, not undermining vaccines. He points out that the autism statement remains on the CDC’s website, exactly as promised. And in April 2026 he told a House committee that he has never been anti-vaccine.

Each claim has an answer in the ledger. The conflict-of-interest argument might explain replacing some members; it does not explain replacing all seventeen, nor keeping recommendations “without changes” by first changing them. The autism statement remains in the way a load-bearing wall remains after the house around it has been rebuilt. And “never anti-vaccine” is a claim that has to be squared with an August 2026 order cutting the childhood schedule by a third, a September 17 speech to the anti-vaccine organization he founded — covered here in “Friends in All the Wrong Places” — and a CDC whose director, one month in, has said nothing in the vaccines’ defence.

The fairest thing that can be said for Kennedy is that he kept the letter of one promise out of seven. The fairest thing that can be said about him is that he chose which one.

Why It Isn’t a Crime — Yet

If this is tantamount to lying to Congress, the obvious question is why it isn’t treated as one. Lying to Congress is a federal offence. Perjury covers false testimony under oath; the separate false-statements statute reaches knowingly false statements in congressional proceedings more broadly. People have gone to prison for both.

The difficulty is that a promise is a statement about the future. Breaking it later does not, by itself, make it false when spoken. To prosecute, the government would have to prove that Kennedy did not intend to keep his word at the moment he gave it — which is to say, it would need documents: emails, messages or witnesses showing that in January 2025 he was already planning the opposite of what he was telling the Senate. Several of the commitments were also made privately to Cassidy rather than in sworn testimony, which makes them politically devastating and legally softer.

Two further facts matter. The first is that only the Justice Department can bring a charge, and this Justice Department — as a separate series on this site, “The Enemies List,” has documented at length — is not in the business of prosecuting the President’s allies. The second is that the clock is long. The ordinary limitation period for these offences is five years. Testimony given in January 2025 remains within reach until early 2030, which is to say, well into the next administration.

That is why the ledger matters beyond this week. Every internal email Senator Sanders has released, every official who spoke to Reuters, every document the Times obtains, is a piece of evidence about what Kennedy knew and planned in the months around his confirmation. The question of intent is not closed. It is merely waiting for a prosecutor who wants to ask it.

The Senator Who Took Him at His Word

Then there is Cassidy, whose arc is the ledger’s human column. He voted for Kennedy on the strength of private assurances. He watched the committee he had been promised would survive be abolished. He was reduced, at the close of Kennedy’s April 2026 testimony, to observing that things had been illuminating. On June 25, 2026, he told CBS’s Face the Nation that public health built on a foundation of lies will not be adequate public health. In mid-September, he told Semafor, in language not usually heard from a Senate committee chairman, just how he felt about guarantees that were broken.

And yet, as this series recorded on August 13, Cassidy in July did the same thing again: he pressed Dr. Erica Schwartz in public, came away unconvinced, spoke with her privately, and voted to confirm her on the strength of what she told him behind closed doors. Twice in eighteen months, a physician-senator has traded his vote for private assurances about vaccines. The first set of promises is in the ledger above. The second, one month into Schwartz’s tenure, has so far produced silence in the vaccines’ defence and an order to challenge Pennsylvania’s measles deaths.

Reading the Ledger Through TACO

Threaten: two decades of anti-vaccine advocacy, which made promises necessary in the first place. Accommodate: the promises themselves — specific, recorded, and aimed precisely at the senator who needed them. Capitulate: not Kennedy but the Senate, which accepted private assurances in lieu of enforceable ones, and then accepted them again. Obfuscate: the autism page, where a promise was kept in a way that defeated its purpose, and the continuing claim that every agreement is being honoured.

What Was Promised, and What Was Paid

Confirmation is meant to be a contract. The nominee gives the Senate his word; the Senate gives him the power. Kennedy received the power in full. The Senate received a ledger.

The measles cases now outnumber any year since the disease was declared eliminated. The committee he promised to keep does not exist in its old form, and its replacement has been bypassed anyway. The money he promised not to divert has been diverted. The statement he promised to preserve has been preserved in the way a specimen is preserved, which is to say, in a jar, without life.

One can call that a change of heart. One would have to explain why the heart changed so quickly, so completely, and in exactly the direction it had always pointed.
