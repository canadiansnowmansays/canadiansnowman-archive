⚖️ Serious — a two-sided one, my own country’s ledger, and the two sides do not balance no matter how I keep the books. There is a famous photograph in it and three sets of graves it left out of frame. No wink. The dog stays home for this one.

I count my essays, and when the number lands on my own country I write slowly and I keep my hands still, because it is easy to be an auditor of other people’s empires and much harder to open the book with your own name in it. Essay 1,867 was Confederation — the addition done by committee, in winter, three provinces choosing peace, order, and good government. I told you then that it was the most personal essay on the map save two. This is not one of the protected ones. But it is the year the country Confederation promised was finally finished — and the year it did the subtraction that the finishing required. 1885 is one calendar in which a nation was completed and a man was hanged to complete it, nine days apart, and I am going to lay both entries on the same page, because they were always on the same page.

The last spike

On November 7, 1885, at a nothing of a place in the British Columbia mountains called Craigellachie, the Canadian Pacific Railway drove its last spike, and the line ran unbroken from the Atlantic to the Pacific. There is a photograph — you have seen it — of a white-bearded financier named Donald Smith bent over the rail with a maul, a crowd of tired men in greatcoats packed close around him, and it is one of the founding images of the country.

Here is the first number that matters, and it is a small and perfect one. The spike was iron. A plain iron spike, identical to the hundreds of thousands already in the line. There had been loose talk of a gold or silver spike, the way the Americans had used one, but the CPR’s managers decided against ceremony metal — the thing that closed the continent would be exactly the same humble unit as every spike before it. I love this detail with my whole heart, because it is the one moment this series offers where the people in charge refused to inflate the figure. They drove the cheapest possible spike and let the meaning carry its own weight, and it did. The country was stitched together coast to coast with an iron nail worth a few cents, photographed like treasure, and worth, in what it joined, more than any gold spike ever could be.

British Columbia had agreed to join Confederation in 1871 only on the promise of a railway to bind it to the distant east. For fourteen years the promise was a line of credit the young country could barely service — scandal, near-bankruptcy, impossible mountains, a syndicate forever one bad season from collapse. At 1,867 I called Confederation addition done by committee. The railway was the nervous system that made the addition real — the physical sinew without which the sum on paper was just three colonies and a wish. If the telephone of 1,876 gave the country a voice, the CPR of 1885 gave it a spine. A nation is not finished when it is declared. It is finished when you can get from one end of it to the other.

What the spine carried first

And now the ledger turns, because the first great thing the finished railway did was not knit the country in love. It carried soldiers.

In the spring of that same year, 1885, the Métis of the South Saskatchewan — a people of mixed Indigenous and European descent, with their own language, their own farms, their own long memory of being promised recognition and handed surveys instead — rose in what we now call the North-West Resistance. Their leader was Louis Riel, who had already, fifteen years earlier, led the Red River Resistance that forced the creation of Manitoba, and who had been living in exile, and who came back to speak once more for a people the national ledger kept declining to enter. Alongside Cree and Assiniboine allies pressed by starvation and broken treaty promises, the Métis fought at Duck Lake, at Batoche, and lost.

They lost in large part because of the railway. The half-finished CPR — the very line that would be completed that November — was used to rush militia from central Canada to the prairies in days rather than the months it would once have taken. The instrument built to bind the country was used, before it was even finished, to crush the people standing in its path. I wrote at 1,857 that a captured rebel pointed to the telegraph line and called it the cord that strangled us, and I wrote at 1,868 that every tool is exactly as humane as the hand that picks it up. Here is my own country’s entry in that ledger. The spine of the nation carried its first troops west to put down the nation’s own.

Nine days

Louis Riel was tried for treason in Regina that summer — in a packed little courtroom, before a jury of six, under a judge appointed by the government prosecuting him. His lawyers wished to argue that he was insane. He refused to let them; he insisted he was sane and that his cause was just, and in doing so he chose, in effect, the gallows over the asylum, because he would not have his life’s argument recorded as madness. He was convicted. The jury recommended mercy. The government declined it.

On November 16, 1885 — nine days after the last spike closed the country — Louis Riel was hanged.

Set the two dates beside each other, because the calendar did, and the calendar is the most honest editor this series has. November 7: the country completed. November 16: the man who spoke for the people the country was completed across, dropped through a trapdoor in Regina. The Prime Minister, John A. Macdonald — the chief architect of the addition at 1,867, a man I do not get to keep as only a hero either — is remembered for the sentence he reportedly spoke of Riel: He shall hang though every dog in the country bark in his favour. They did bark. Quebec, which saw in Riel a French-speaking Catholic martyr executed by an English Protestant government, erupted, and the wound that opened in 1885 along that fault line has never fully closed in a hundred and forty years. The country was finished and fractured in the same fortnight, with the same hand.

The third ledger, and the tax on the builders

There is a third set of books for 1885, and it is the one the photograph crops out most completely.

The hardest and deadliest work of the railway — the blasting of the roadbed through the mountains of British Columbia — was done by labourers brought from China, some fifteen thousand of them, given the most dangerous tasks for the lowest pay. No one counted their dead with any care, which by now you will recognise as this series’ surest sign of whose dead were thought to matter: the estimates run to many hundreds, possibly over a thousand, lost to rockslides, explosions, cold, and disease. They were, quite literally, the men who built the spine, and they were enumerated the way 1,857 enumerated the Indian dead — as a range.

And then, in that very same year of 1885, with the last spike driven and the Chinese labour no longer needed, the Parliament of the finished country passed the Chinese Immigration Act, imposing a fifty-dollar head tax — a price, per human being, on Chinese people wishing to enter Canada. Read that as the Numbers Game episode it is. The country assigned a number to a person as a condition of counting them at all — the same arithmetic of partial personhood I traced through the three-fifths clause at 1865 and the estimated dead at 1857, now written into my own country’s statute book in the same year it celebrated its completion. We used their hands to build the nation and then put a tariff on their existence the moment the nation was built. The head tax would rise to five hundred dollars and culminate in outright exclusion. It is the plainest example in the whole series of a human being turned directly into a line item — bought cheap, taxed dear, struck from the welcome.

The honest sum

So here is 1885, added up honestly. A country completed with an iron spike that refused to lie about its own value. A people — the Métis — subtracted from the sum to make the arithmetic of ownership balance, their spokesman hanged nine days after the celebration. A workforce — the Chinese labourers — used to build the spine and then taxed for the crime of being in the country they had built. Three ledgers, one year. The triumph is real; I will not shrink it, because a nation stitched coast to coast against those odds is a genuine wonder. But the triumph was paid for, and it was paid for by people whose names the founding photograph does not record, and the thesis of this series is that there is no such thing as a figure without a cost, only a figure whose cost has been moved to a page you were not meant to read.

My country is a good one. I believe that, and I have lived nowhere else, and I would defend the addition of 1867 and the sinew of 1885 to anyone. But I was raised, as this whole series was raised, to open the whole book and not just the celebrated page — and the whole book of 1885 says that we completed the country by subtracting some of the people in it, and that the spike was iron and honest and the arithmetic around it was neither.

The closer

The count moves on, and it moves to the next thing the wires and the rails made possible — the moment electricity, which Edison had sold by the unseeable unit from a station a mile wide at 1,882, learned to leap a hundred miles from a waterfall and arrive with its power intact. The next landmark is essay 1,895, and it is half a Canadian story too, because the water that first proved it falls on our border and thunders in two countries at once. We have wired the city and wired the country. At 1895 we discover that distance has stopped counting for power the way it stopped counting for news at 1844 — and that the argument over how to deliver it was, at bottom, an argument about which number to count in.

See you at 1895, where the river does the work and the current wins the war.

The Numbers Game is an occasional series in which the essay count lands on a year that already means something — a conquest, a charter, a departure — and the author stops whatever he was doing to write about it. Some entries are satire. Some aren’t satire at all. A few are the most personal things he writes. This is one of the serious ones.
