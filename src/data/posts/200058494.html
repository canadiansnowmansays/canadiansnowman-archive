David Ignatius. The veteran columnist’s verdict on the Iran war — “a war of choice, and it’s a peace of necessity” — captured the gap between Trump’s ambitions and the outcome the world now sees.

Introduction: The Verdict the Administration Didn’t Want

David Ignatius has covered the Middle East since the year after the Iranian Revolution. He has watched American presidents stumble into the region and stumble out of it for nearly half a century. He is not a polemicist. He is not a partisan. He is the kind of foreign affairs columnist who measures his words the way surgeons measure incisions — carefully, deliberately, and with full awareness that carelessness has consequences.

So when Ignatius sat down with Jeffrey Goldberg on Washington Week at the end of May 2026, and reached for a phrase to summarize three months of American military action in Iran, the choice of words mattered. He didn’t say “strategic miscalculation.” He didn’t say “costly overreach.” He said: “This was a war of choice, and it’s a peace of necessity.”

Six words. Prosecution and verdict in the same sentence.

This essay is about what those six words mean — not just as a summary of the Iran war, but as a description of the world Trump has built around it. Because the consequences of that war of choice have now rippled far beyond the Strait of Hormuz. They have reached Romania’s apartment buildings. They have reached the nuclear steering rooms of Paris and Berlin. They have reached Riyadh’s defense ministries and Islamabad’s generals. They have reached every capital city where a leader sits quietly asking the question that used to be unthinkable: Can we still trust the Americans?

The prosecution, in this series, has been building since December 2025. The verdict is now in.

I. The Case for the Prosecution

The road to Operation Epic Fury was documented in this series in real time. The key counts bear brief restatement, because they provide the foundation on which the verdict rests.

Count One: The promise was explicit. Trump ran as the anti-war candidate. Vance wrote the op-ed. Gabbard sold the shirts. At his inauguration, Trump declared: “My proudest legacy will be that of a peacemaker and unifier.” The central organizing claim of the campaign was abandoned without apology.

Count Two: The warnings were ignored. This was not, as Ignatius confirmed to Goldberg, an intelligence failure. General Caine told the president directly and repeatedly that military power could not assure the outcomes Trump wanted. Trump, in Ignatius’s phrase, “blew through those warnings.” Venezuela was in his rearview mirror and he believed he could do anything.

Count Three: The diplomacy was sabotaged. Omani mediator Badr Al Busaidi went on CBS News the morning before the bombs fell and described a historic breakthrough — Iran’s agreement to never stockpile enriched uranium, full IAEA verification, irreversible degradation of existing materials. “I think we have cracked that problem,” Al Busaidi said. Within hours, Trump told reporters he was “not exactly happy” with negotiations. Within a day, Operation Epic Fury began. The same mediator, the same framework, the same result as June 2025 — diplomacy used as cover, not as a genuine alternative.

Count Four: The justification was self-contradicting. Trump’s public case rested on an imminent Iranian nuclear threat. His own DIA had assessed that Iran’s nuclear program was set back two years by the June 2025 strikes — the strikes Trump said had “obliterated” it. In private congressional briefings after Epic Fury, officials acknowledged that intelligence did not suggest Iran was preparing a preemptive strike. The public justification and the private reality were not the same thing.

Count Five: The constitutional framework was bypassed entirely. No congressional authorization. No legal justification offered publicly. A Truth Social video at 2 AM on a Saturday, and the bombs were falling — consistent with the pattern from drug boats to Venezuela to the June 2025 nuclear strikes, each operation larger than the last, each conducted without congressional consent.

The prosecution rests.

II. The Verdict

Three months after Operation Epic Fury began, here is where things stand.

The ceasefire brokered by Pakistan in April — itself a telling detail, that it was Pakistan and not the United States that had to play the mediating role — has been described by Trump himself as on "life support." Intermittent fighting has continued since May 4. The Strait of Hormuz remains effectively closed, with a U.S. naval blockade of Iranian shipping adding another layer of global economic disruption. Negotiators reached a tentative agreement in late May to extend the ceasefire by sixty days and begin a new round of nuclear talks, as PBS NewsHour reported on May 28 — while the Treasury Department simultaneously imposed new sanctions on the Iranian military's oil sales arm, a move that rather undercuts the spirit of negotiation. The Congressional Research Service assessment of the negotiations noted the significant distance remaining between the parties on core issues.

The terms Iran demanded in its ten-point peace proposal — including acceptance of its nuclear enrichment program, the lifting of all primary and secondary sanctions, and an end to all UN Security Council resolutions against it — are a considerable distance from what Trump promised three months ago. He promised unconditional defeat. He is now trying to exit a military morass.

Ignatius, characteristically precise, put the strategic outcome plainly: “It appears the hardliners are in greater control than they ever were.” The Ahmadinejad plan — the apparently serious proposal to install a hardline former president as leader of a post-war Iran — was scuttled. The Kurdish militia crossing the border idea was scuttled. The plan to help Iranian protesters “take it for themselves,” as Trump urged in March, produced thousands of deaths and an intimidated population that stayed indoors when the moment came. As Ignatius observed, it was the Americans who had encouraged people to stand up, and then the help didn’t come for a month — a month during which tens of thousands of Iranians who had taken Trump at his word were slaughtered.

“We have the capability to bomb anything,” one source involved in war planning told Ignatius. “But what can we do that will change Iranian decision-making?”

The answer, as of late May 2026, remains: not much.

This is the verdict. A war of choice produced a peace of necessity. The objective — regime change, a “decimated evil empire,” the Iranian people rising up to claim their country — has not been achieved and will not be achieved by the terms being negotiated. What has been achieved is a regional destabilization so thorough that its consequences are now reshaping security architectures that have been stable for eighty years.

III. The World That Watched

Here is where the story gets larger than Iran.

Ignatius posed the question directly to Goldberg: “If you’re in Taipei, Seoul, Riga, Warsaw, Tokyo — will the U.S. actually rescue us from the authoritarian states who surround us, or will we be on our own for the first time since the end of World War II?”

It is not a rhetorical question anymore. The world has been watching — and the world has been drawing conclusions.

Romania. On the night of May 28-29, 2026 — just days before this essay was written — a Russian drone struck an apartment building in Galați, Romania, wounding two people and setting the building ablaze. Romania is a NATO member. This was the twenty-eighth time Russian drones have breached Romanian airspace since Russia began attacking Ukrainian ports across the Danube. NATO’s response was a statement from Secretary-General Mark Rutte about defending “every inch of Allied territory,” two scrambled F-16s, and a summoned Russian ambassador.

Ignatius said it plainly on Washington Week: “I think there is a window in which, while Trump is here and the world is so destabilized, Russia might well make a move to demonstrate that the NATO umbrella is gone.” He said the answer to what the U.S. would do about Romania being struck was “probably not much, and the world will see that.”

The world is seeing it.

Damage from a Russian drone strike on an apartment building in Galați, Romania, May 2026. A NATO member territory hit — and the world watched to see what Article 5 really means in 2026.

Germany and France. In early March 2026 — the same week Operation Epic Fury entered its first full month — Macron and German Chancellor Friedrich Merz announced the creation of a high-level nuclear steering group. The joint declaration described “closer cooperation in the field of deterrence in response to the evolving threat landscape.” Germany will participate in French nuclear exercises. Denmark has signed a similar agreement with France. Poland is exploring comparable arrangements.

Macron and German leaders advancing nuclear cooperation, March 2026. Allies once unthinkable now discuss joint deterrence as questions mount over the reliability of the American umbrella.

The official framing was careful — the cooperation “will add to, not substitute for, NATO’s nuclear deterrence.” But Defense News was less diplomatic: the steering group was created “as questions hang here over the long-term reliability of the United States as a security guarantor.” Merz said publicly at the Munich Security Conference in February that he had held “confidential talks with the French president about European nuclear deterrence” because of “worries about U.S. security commitments to Europe.”

As Ignatius observed on Washington Week: “Who would have ever imagined that Germany would turn to France and in effect discuss a joint nuclear strategy? But that’s a consequence, I think, of people beginning to lose faith in that American nuclear umbrella, which is part of what’s kept the world safe.”

Saudi Arabia. In September 2025, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan signed a Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement, declaring that any aggression against one country would be treated as an attack on both. Middle East Eye reported the signing came "amid growing fears in the Gulf over the US's willingness to constrain Israeli belligerence." Pakistan's Defence Minister confirmed the agreement extended Pakistan's nuclear umbrella to Saudi territory. A Saudi analyst was more direct: "We can say it literally that there is a nuclear umbrella over Saudi Arabia." The Middle East Institute assessed the pact as modest in its immediate military implications but significant as a signal — Riyadh diversifying its security arrangements because it no longer trusts the American guarantee alone. The Georgetown Journal of International Affairs noted the direct implications for U.S. force posture in the Gulf. This is nuclear proliferation in slow motion: not the acquisition of new weapons, but the reconfiguration of who shelters under whose.

Saudi Arabia and Pakistan sign their Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement. Nuclear-armed Pakistan extending its umbrella over Riyadh — one more sign that key partners are hedging against uncertain American commitments.

IV. The Confidence Cycle Completes Itself

This series documented the confidence cycle in March 2026 — quick military victory breeds overconfidence that leads to catastrophic miscalculation. Venezuela was the proof of concept. Iran was the escalation. Cuba remained the contemplated finale.

The cycle has now completed its middle phase. Three months after a war it called a certain victory, the administration is negotiating an exit while hardliners consolidate power in Tehran, the Strait remains closed, and the global credibility of American commitments has reached its lowest point since at least the 1970s. The damage is not confined to Iran policy — it is systemic. Alliance credibility is not a policy lever adjustable in isolation. When Germany questions the American nuclear umbrella, it is not making a statement about Iran. It is making a statement about whether the United States, in 2026, can be trusted to fulfill its commitments when they become costly.

Ignatius offered a counter-argument in the conversation’s final minutes — his imagined Chinese intelligence analyst concluding that the United States is probably somewhat stronger than it appeared during the Trump-Xi summit, and China somewhat weaker. America, he said, “has a way of falling uphill.”

But falling uphill is not a foreign policy. It is a description of what happens after foreign policy fails. The question is not whether America will survive the Iran war. It will. The question is what the world looks like on the other side — and how many of the alliance structures that underpinned eight decades of relative stability will need to be rebuilt from scratch.

V. The Leacock Addendum: On Declaring Victory

There is a tradition in Canadian letters, traced most elegantly to Stephen Leacock, of treating the gap between what men claim and what they do as the richest possible source of material. Leacock called it the comedy of small pretensions. In Mariposa, the great men always declared victory on the morning after their schemes collapsed.

Trump’s Truth Social post from late May — announcing that Iran had agreed to “immediate opening of the Strait of Hormuz, a promise never to develop a nuclear weapon” and a series of other conditions that he would “claim add up to victory” — is, in this tradition, a Mariposa document. The Strait remains closed. The nuclear question is unresolved. A sixty-day ceasefire extension, brokered by Pakistan, is being sold as the fulfillment of a promise made in March that America would “decimate their whole evil empire.”

Ignatius’s assessment of the victory claim was precise: “There is widespread skepticism in the United States and around the world whether you can possibly call this a victory given all that the U.S. put into it.”

He is right. But skepticism, however widespread, does not undo the damage. The hardliners are entrenched. The allies have begun building alternative arrangements. The Romanian apartment building has a hole in its roof. And somewhere in Galați, two people with abrasions are wondering what Article 5 actually means when nobody comes.

Conclusion: What the Canadian Observer Notes

NATO’s Article 5. The world is watching whether the United States will still honor its commitments when they become costly — or whether the post-1945 order is entering its twilight.

From the 49th parallel, the view is clarifying.

The Iran war was not an accident of circumstance. It was the predictable terminus of a trajectory this series documented from its beginning: the DOW rename, the Nobel letter to Norway, the sabotaged Omani diplomacy, the bypassed Congress, the overridden intelligence community. When you rename the Department of Defense to the Department of War, tell Norway you are no longer bound by obligations of peace, ignore a diplomatic breakthrough that achieved your stated objectives, and launch a regime-change war at 2 AM on a Saturday — the war of choice is not a surprise. It is the destination.

The peace of necessity that follows is also not a surprise. Military power can destroy infrastructure and kill leaders. It cannot force political change. The United States learned this in Iraq. It learned it in Afghanistan. It has learned it again in Iran, in three months, at extraordinary cost to its credibility, its alliances, and the stability of the international order it built after 1945.

The world watched. Germany and France drew their conclusions. Saudi Arabia drew its conclusions. The leaders of Taipei, Seoul, Warsaw, and Tokyo drew their conclusions. They will continue making quiet arrangements — nuclear steering groups, mutual defence pacts, alternative security architectures — for as long as the credibility deficit persists.

Ignatius is right that America has a way of falling uphill. But the world, having watched, may not wait around to see how high it falls this time.
