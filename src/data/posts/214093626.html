Nine months before Uber and the British start-up Wayve put their first paying passengers in a driverless-capable car on London’s streets, Wayve’s chief executive, Alex Kendall, said something that reads differently now than it did at the time. Taking a reporter on a test ride through Soho earlier this year, he said he was looking forward to watching a rival attempt what his own company was attempting, precisely because London’s roads are so difficult, as he told AOL/PCMag’s chaotic-ride feature. As he put it: “I can’t wait to see another autonomy company come into London because I think it’s extremely challenging.” That rival has already arrived. By the time Wayve’s own commercial service launched, Waymo had been quietly trialling its own vehicles across 19 London boroughs since January — and, per Part I of this series, Baidu has separately said London is next on its own list.

What actually launched, and what already went sideways

On September 2, 2026, Bloomberg reported, Uber and Wayve began operating a small fleet — 15 Ford Mustang Mach-E vehicles fitted with Wayve’s sensor suite — through Uber’s regular app, with a human safety driver still present in every car. Riders can’t request a robotaxi directly; the option surfaces only after booking, when a Wayve vehicle happens to be nearby, and can be accepted or declined like any other match. Fares match a standard UberX or Uber Comfort ride, and — in a small but pointed detail — passengers aren’t prompted to tip at the end of the trip.

Within the service’s first days, a safety driver already intervened during a live ride, Technology.org reported. Wayve’s account of the moment, as reported afterward, was that the intervention was the driver’s own judgment call and that the company’s system would have handled the situation correctly regardless. That is not, on its own, a damning admission — safety drivers are there precisely to make judgment calls — but it lands at an awkward moment for the technology’s confidence narrative. Earlier this year, government officials and industry figures had floated the idea that fully driverless robotaxis, with no safety driver at all, could be operating in London before the end of 2026. That timeline now looks out of reach. The Department for Transport is instead working toward a regulatory framework for driverless operation sometime in 2027, without committing to a firmer date.

The regulator’s studied patience

Transport for London’s public posture has been consistently cautious rather than celebratory, as covered by WTOP/AP. Christina Calderato, the agency’s director of transport strategy and policy, described the approach as taking the time to get it right rather than holding up a decision under pressure. Heidi Alexander, Britain’s transport secretary, called the launch a major milestone for the city’s transport future — genuine enthusiasm, but paired, in the same official channel, with TfL’s own promise to closely monitor the rollout and take immediate action if passenger safety is compromised. Sarfraz Maredia, Uber’s global head of autonomous mobility, framed public acceptance as something that has to be earned twice over — proving the technology is both safe and reliable, not just one or the other.

Why London was never going to be an easy proving ground

The skepticism attached to this launch predates it by months, and it comes from an unusually credible source: the people whose entire profession is built on knowing London’s streets better than any sensor suite currently can. Steven McNamara, general secretary of the Licensed Taxi Drivers’ Association, dismissed the entire premise earlier this year, calling self-driving cabs a solution looking for a problem, per the Associated Press, and pointed to something American AV operators rarely have to contend with — London’s road network is a convoluted spiderweb tracing back to Roman and medieval routes, laid out centuries before anyone imagined a car, let alone a computer driving one, would need to navigate it. Unlike the grid layouts of Phoenix or San Francisco, where Waymo built its home-market advantage, London offers no such underlying logic to learn.

There’s a specific legal quirk underneath McNamara’s skepticism that’s easy to miss and genuinely important: jaywalking isn’t a criminal offense in Britain the way it is in the United States, which means pedestrians cross wherever and whenever they choose, with none of the behavioral predictability American AV systems have been trained against for a decade. London’s famous black-cab exam, the Knowledge — 150 years old, requiring aspiring drivers to memorize roughly 25,000 streets and 6,000 points of interest across a six-mile radius — exists precisely because the city’s layout resists the kind of shortcut memorization or pattern-matching that makes most other cities’ road networks learnable in a fraction of the time. This series will give the Knowledge and the labor question underneath it their own full treatment in a future essay; the relevant point here is narrower: London wasn’t picked as a test market because it was convenient. It was picked, according to Kendall himself, because succeeding there means a system has been forced to generalize rather than memorize — which is exactly the argument Wayve makes for why its approach should outlast a rival’s.

Two philosophies now driving the same streets

Wayve’s technical bet is genuinely different from Waymo’s, and the distinction matters more in London than it would almost anywhere else. Wayve runs what it calls an end-to-end AI driving model — a system trained to generalize from raw sensor input and react to unfamiliar situations the way a human driver improvises, rather than relying on the meticulously pre-mapped, sensor-calibrated approach Waymo has used to scale across Phoenix, Austin, and San Francisco. Kendall has drawn an explicit parallel between Wayve’s approach and the end-to-end method Tesla uses in its own self-driving system — a comparison worth flagging without chasing further, since anything Tesla-specific belongs to this Substack’s Musk Stack series rather than this one. What matters here is narrower: Waymo’s HD-mapped approach requires enormous upfront investment per city before a single paying passenger takes a ride; Wayve is betting that a system trained to handle London’s chaos on the fly can be redeployed elsewhere with far less city-by-city retraining. Waymo, for its part, is running its usual playbook regardless — gathering real-world driving data across London before it commits to a public launch of its own, as Euronews has documented, the same sequencing it used in every American market it now operates in at scale.

The licensing model, again

Wayve’s approach to actually getting cars on the road echoes something Part I already established about Pony.ai’s strategy in China: rather than building or owning a fleet itself, Wayve licenses its driving system to vehicle manufacturers and partners with Uber to actually operate the service. That structure is already expanding well past London. In March 2026, Reuters reported, Wayve, Uber, and Nissan announced a partnership to bring the same model — Wayve’s software, a manufacturer’s vehicle, Uber’s platform — to a robotaxi pilot in Tokyo by late 2026, part of a stated ambition to launch service in more than ten cities worldwide. Sarah Gates, Wayve’s director of public policy, has described the arrangement as a supply chain in which each partner — the AI provider, the vehicle manufacturer, the fleet operator, and Uber — has to separately prove its own piece of the safety case to regulators. It’s a slower, more legally fragmented path to scale than Baidu’s vertically integrated approach in China, but it’s also one that doesn’t require Wayve to raise capital at anything close to the rate a fully owned fleet would demand.

Where this leaves the race

London is now the only city on Earth where three of the world’s most significant AV efforts are converging inside the same few years — Wayve and Uber already operating with passengers, Waymo already gathering data with an eye toward its own launch, and Baidu, per its own public statements covered in Part I, treating London as a deliberate next step in its post-China expansion. Kendall got his wish faster than he probably expected. Whether London’s uniquely resistant streets end up proving his end-to-end approach right, or simply prove that every approach struggles equally against several centuries of unplanned road-building, is the question this series will keep watching as all three companies’ timelines start to overlap in the same city, on the same streets, in front of the same skeptical black-cab drivers who’ve already seen one disruptive American platform arrive and reorganize their industry once before.
