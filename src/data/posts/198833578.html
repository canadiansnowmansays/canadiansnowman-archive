This series has spent fifteen months documenting the gap between Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s performance as Health Secretary and the reality of his record. The memes and the mandate. The Twinkie suplex and the measles deaths. The congressional testimony last month in which he defended mathematically impossible drug discounts, could not remember his own quotes, and shouted that there are no cuts to a program being cut by up to a trillion dollars.

The gap has always been visible. This week it became dangerous.

Two deadly outbreaks are unfolding simultaneously. Ebola in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Uganda — a disease with a mortality rate of 25 to 50 percent, no approved vaccine for the current strain, now confirmed as the third-largest Ebola outbreak in history. And hantavirus, with a 38 percent mortality rate and no approved treatment, spreading among cruise ship passengers who dispersed to their home countries before the WHO confirmed the outbreak. As of this writing, more than 600 suspected Ebola cases and 139 deaths have been recorded. One American has tested positive. Six high-risk contacts are being medically evacuated to a specialist isolation ward in Germany.

This is not a foreign crisis conveniently contained elsewhere. This is a public health emergency of international concern — the WHO’s highest designation — unfolding in precisely the conditions that America spent a decade and billions of dollars learning to prevent. And it is unfolding on the watch of a Secretary of Health who last month was before Congress claiming historic wins for American health.

What the system was built to do

The 2014 Ebola outbreak infected more than 28,000 people and killed 11,308. The United States alone spent more than $5.4 billion containing it. That catastrophe produced rare bipartisan clarity: America needed the capacity to detect and manage these diseases wherever they emerged, before they crossed borders.

What followed was a sustained investment — approximately $1.3 billion annually through the State Department, USAID, and the CDC, plus hundreds of millions more through global health programs and biosecurity funding through the Department of Defense. The investment included specialized quarantine facilities, Ebola treatment centres across the country, trained response teams, early-warning systems, bilateral and multilateral partnerships, and CDC and USAID personnel embedded in the field in affected regions.

The investment worked. It is why most Americans never heard about the twelve Ebola and Marburg outbreaks that occurred during the Biden administration, each contained before they became public emergencies. When the system functioned, outbreaks of one to five cases were detected and suppressed before the rest of the world knew they had started.

That system has been dismantled.

The New York Times was blunt about the consequence in its mapping of the outbreak published yesterday: “The response has also been hampered by the near-absence of the United States, which used to fund disease surveillance and maintained emergency teams in the region.” That is not a Democratic talking point. That is the newspaper of record describing the vacuum left by fifteen months of deliberate demolition.

What Kennedy’s tenure produced

The RFK Files has documented this dismantling in detail across previous installments. The firing of nearly 20,000 HHS employees — the scientists, epidemiologists, and public health experts Kennedy described last month during congressional testimony as “painful but necessary” cuts. The destruction of USAID, which maintained the field presence and partnerships that produced early outbreak detection. The CDC’s diminished international role, gutted of the staff and funding that kept America informed of emerging threats before they became emergencies. The withdrawal from the WHO, severing the collaborative intelligence network that made rapid response possible.

The authors of yesterday’s Atlantic piece — How to Contain the Ebola Outbreak, by former National Security Council staff who managed twelve Ebola and Marburg outbreaks during the Biden administration — are unambiguous: the destruction of that system appears to have contributed to the delayed detection of these outbreaks.

The numbers support that conclusion. According to the CDC, Ebola had been circulating for at least six weeks in the DRC before public confirmation. This is the first time in history that an Ebola outbreak has reached such scale before detection.

First time in history. Six weeks of undetected circulation. The third-largest Ebola outbreak ever recorded.

Acting CDC Director Jay Bhattacharya has said that the agency was not alerted to the outbreak until May 14. The same Jay Bhattacharya who, two months ago, told CDC staff he had not been informed of the outbreak in a timely fashion — the same man who, in this series’ coverage of The Reckoning, represented the first flicker of institutional normalcy at an agency Kennedy had systematically destabilised. Even that flicker was not enough to catch what the old system would have detected at five cases.

The 2014 comparison

The echoes between the current Ebola outbreak and the 2014 outbreak are hard to ignore. That outbreak began in Guinea, likely in December 2013. When Guinea notified the WHO in March 2014, there were still only 49 cases and 29 deaths. The WHO didn’t declare a global emergency until August 2014, by which point there were nearly 2,000 confirmed cases. By the time the outbreak ended in June 2016, more than 28,000 people had been infected and 11,308 had died — and the U.S. alone had spent more than $5.4 billion to contain the spread.

The current outbreak has already exceeded the point at which 2014’s was still containable, and it is doing so with a strain — the Bundibugyo virus — for which many standard rapid field tests produce false negatives, and for which there is no approved vaccine or treatment. The outbreak is centred near a porous border where people regularly cross to Uganda, Rwanda, and South Sudan.

The testing failure is worth dwelling on. Equipment in Ituri Province — more than 1,000 miles from Kinshasa — only tests for the most common Ebola species. Early samples from the current outbreak came back negative. It was only when samples were sent the full 1,000 miles to the capital that the correct identification was made. This is not a system that failed under pressure. It is a system that was never built to catch this particular threat, in this particular place — and that the United States used to supplement with its own field capacity. That capacity no longer exists.

This is also not the first time Ituri Province has been the centre of a catastrophic Ebola outbreak. A previous outbreak in the same region, beginning in 2018, became the second deadliest Ebola crisis ever recorded and took two years to contain. The world learned hard lessons from that outbreak. America invested in not repeating them. Those investments have been dismantled.

The current outbreak has already surpassed the combined toll of the only two previous Bundibugyo outbreaks in history — Uganda in 2007 and DRC in 2012. As of May 19, over 500 suspected cases and 130 deaths have been recorded. One American citizen has been exposed to the virus in a healthcare setting in DRC and is being medically evacuated along with six high-risk contacts to a specialist isolation ward in Germany.

The hantavirus parallel

Running alongside Ebola is a hantavirus outbreak with a 38 percent mortality rate and no approved treatment, traced to a cruise ship. Nearly a month passed from when the first passenger became sick in April to when the WHO confirmed the outbreak. Two dozen passengers left the ship and traveled home in the intervening period.

A month. Two dozen dispersed passengers. No approved treatment.

The government’s guidance to those passengers has been, by multiple accounts, confusing. Some travelers who went home rather than to the University of Nebraska Medical Center quarantine unit left without clear monitoring instructions. This is not a failure of individual competence. It is the predictable consequence of gutting the institutional infrastructure that once made clear, coordinated guidance possible.

What Kennedy said last month

Secretary of Health and Human Services Robert F. Kennedy Jr. testifies before a Senate subcommittee on April 21, 2026.Graeme Sloan/Bloomberg—Getty Images

Last month, over four days of congressional testimony, Kennedy told Congress he had delivered “historic wins” for American health. He cited new dietary guidelines. He cited progress on food dyes. He described the firing of 20,000 HHS employees as “painful but necessary.”

He shouted that there are no cuts to Medicaid. He defended a drug pricing calculation that does not exist in mathematics. He said the measles outbreak has nothing to do with him.

As the third-largest Ebola outbreak in history was already escalating in the DRC during that same week, Kennedy’s four days of testimony concluded without a single reference to Ebola. Senator Cassidy offered his assessment of the four days: “Things were illuminating.”

The Secretary of Health did not, in seven hearings over four days, address Ebola.

The cost of the gap

This series has used the phrase “the gap between the meme and the mandate” to describe Kennedy’s tenure — the distance between the performance and the substance, between the Twinkie suplex and the measles deaths, between the birthday steak and the collapsing public health infrastructure.

That gap now has a body count.

Not Kennedy’s alone — the dismantling of USAID, the withdrawal from the WHO, the gutting of CDC international capacity are administration-wide decisions that go beyond one Secretary. But Kennedy is the Secretary of Health. The 20,000 fired employees were his employees. The institutional knowledge that Elizabeth Jacobs told Congress last month will take “a decade or a generation to rebuild” was destroyed on his watch, by his department, while he was making AI videos about Twinkies.

Each outbreak does not wait for the previous one to pass, and biological risks — whether naturally occurring, deliberate, or accidental — are growing. The authors of yesterday’s Atlantic piece conclude that Ebola and hantavirus are containable — but only if the will and the tools exist to contain them. The tools, as this series has documented since its first installment, have been systematically removed.

President Trump has said he is “concerned” about the Ebola outbreak. Concern, at this point, is insufficient. What is needed is the infrastructure Kennedy spent fifteen months dismantling — the early warning systems, the field personnel, the bilateral partnerships, the institutional knowledge of the scientists and epidemiologists who were fired and will not be easily replaced.

The Twinkie is still on supermarket shelves.

The Ebola virus does not stay where it starts.
