Part XXIII ended on a Friday morning, with Elon Musk announcing on X that the Cybertruck would be retired to make room for the Cybercab. By Friday afternoon he had moved on to a bigger idea.

Speaking with the investor Ron Baron at Baron Capital’s annual conference on November 6, 2026, Musk said the spectrum licences SpaceX has bought would let it “get very far” in its race to compete with AT&T, Verizon and T-Mobile. Then he went further. “At a certain point if we’re able to make much better use of the spectrum than, say, a legacy telco is,” he said, “we can just acquire that legacy telco and use their spectrum, you know, 10 times more effectively.”

William Gavin, reporting the remarks for MarketWatch, noted that SpaceX shares closed up 9 per cent that day, after steep losses on Thursday. Verizon and AT&T each fell about 1 per cent. T-Mobile rose 1 per cent, which is its own kind of signal.

This series has followed the Stack from rockets to cars to social media to artificial intelligence. Friday added a fifth layer: the phone in your pocket.

The pieces on the board

Musk’s remark was not a stray thought. It was the latest move in a campaign that has run, in plain sight, for more than a year.

The spectrum. In 2025, SpaceX agreed to buy wireless spectrum from EchoStar in a deal worth about $17 billion, giving it mid-band airwaves that the Federal Communications Commission has approved for use both from space and from the ground. On October 8, 2026, it added a second layer. Reuters reported that SpaceX had agreed to buy Grain Management’s entire nationwide portfolio of 800 MHz spectrum, low-band airwaves that Grain had itself bought from T-Mobile earlier in the year. Reuters later valued the deal at $8 billion. Low-band signals travel far and get through walls and trees, and most phones already sold in the United States can use them. The same week, the FCC approved SpaceX’s application for a second-generation constellation of 15,000 satellites designed for direct-to-phone service.

The towers. On October 22, Reuters, following a Bloomberg report, said SpaceX was in talks for access to at least 80,000 cell sites in the United States, roughly half of the country’s purpose-built towers. Crown Castle, which owns about 40,000 of them, said only that it was talking to “all parties.” TD Cowen estimated that a nationwide ground network would cost more than $80 billion and take many years to build.

The customers. On November 2, Reuters, citing The Washington Post, reported that SpaceX was negotiating to buy EchoStar’s Boost Mobile, or to use its network. Musk had met EchoStar’s chairman, Charlie Ergen, the month before. EchoStar had about 7.4 million wireless subscribers at mid-year. There is a catch: Boost leases its network from AT&T and T-Mobile, and those agreements may not transfer to a new owner without their consent.

The equipment. On Wednesday, November 4, SpaceX said it would work with Qualcomm to power the ground-based infrastructure for its network. Gavin, again in MarketWatch, called the deal the latest shot in SpaceX’s “burgeoning war” with the American carriers, and a sign that it is serious about becoming a major wireless operator quickly.

Spectrum, towers, customers and chips. That is most of a phone company. The missing piece is the network that ties them together, and on Friday Musk explained how he might get one.

Build or buy

Two months ago, SpaceX sounded less sure. In September, its chief financial officer, Bret Johnsen, told a Goldman Sachs conference that the company had not decided whether or how to build a ground network, and was open to building one or working with existing carriers. Michelle Donegan of Light Reading read his remarks as a retreat. Johnsen also pushed the launch of SpaceX’s phone service back from the end of 2027 to the first half of 2028, and called the carriers’ joint satellite venture a possible “cartel.”

The carriers were dismissive. T-Mobile’s finance chief said “the physics just don’t allow” satellite service to replace a phone network. Verizon’s chief executive said Starlink was “not a direct competitor.” When SpaceX bought the 800 MHz spectrum, a Verizon spokesman, Rich Young, put the industry’s case in one sentence: “This company can have tons of spectrum available, but if they do not have the network to use it, it doesn’t matter.”

Musk’s answer on Friday was that SpaceX does not have to build the network. It can buy one.

The squeeze

The analysts Gavin quotes think that is the point of the past month. Sam McHugh of BNP Paribas described an “unprecedented” amount of news putting pressure on the American carriers. The visible push from SpaceX, he wrote, “suggests to us there is a clear strategy to try to ramp pressure on one of the Big Three to fold” on a deal to use its network. He also suggested SpaceX might be trying to make an acquisition “more accretive” by pressuring telecom stocks.

The pattern supports him. Carrier shares fell about 6 per cent in after-hours trading on the 800 MHz news, and between 4 and 5 per cent on the tower report. Each announcement made the targets cheaper. Citi analysts have said that buying a carrier would let SpaceX launch a full mobile service years ahead of schedule. Gregory Williams of TD Cowen has called T-Mobile the “clear choice,” given its existing satellite partnership with SpaceX. That may explain why T-Mobile was the one carrier whose shares rose on Friday.

There is an obvious question about how SpaceX would pay. Part XIV of this series described a stock that had fallen by half from its post-IPO high while its owner’s promises kept growing. That stock is the currency of any takeover. On Thursday it fell sharply with the rest of the technology sector; on Friday it rose 9 per cent on the strength of a sentence. A company that can move its own share price by talking about an acquisition, while moving its targets’ prices down with every press release, is negotiating from an unusual position.

The moat is the rocket

The same MarketWatch report contained a reminder of why SpaceX can make threats its rivals cannot. AST SpaceMobile, which also wants to connect ordinary phones to satellites, announced on Friday that it would build its own 20-ton rocket, using engines from Firefly Aerospace. It will pay Firefly $800 million in cash and stock up front, plus $500 million for delivery and engineering support. By late 2028, it hopes to launch five satellites at a time. AST has depended on partners to reach orbit and was set back when Blue Origin had a major incident on the launch pad earlier this year. Firefly’s shares rose almost 37 per cent.

SpaceX does not have that problem. It launches its own satellites on its own rockets, and it launches most of everyone else’s too. A competitor that has to buy a rocket company to stay in the race is a measure of how far ahead SpaceX already is.

What the Stack would hold

This series has argued since its first part that the story is not any single Musk company but the way they fit together. Part XIII described how a SpaceX–Tesla merger could put the whole enterprise under SpaceX’s share structure, where Musk’s Class B stock holds most of the votes. Add a national phone carrier and the picture changes again. One person would control the rockets, the satellites, the broadband service, a mobile network, a social-media platform and an A.I. model. Analysts at William Blair already expect SpaceX to bundle Starlink broadband with Starlink Mobile, and some have suggested Grok could come with it.

None of that is unlawful, and some of it might be good for customers. American wireless prices are high, and the carriers’ own scepticism about satellites has served them well. The FCC’s chairman, Brendan Carr, welcomed the 800 MHz deal as “more investment, more opportunities for a range of providers.” But a takeover of AT&T, Verizon or T-Mobile would need the approval of the FCC and the Justice Department, and T-Mobile is controlled by Deutsche Telekom, which would have its own price. That review would be the first time regulators were asked to weigh the Stack as a whole rather than one company at a time.

A note from the other side of the counter

I spent eighteen years in the Office of the President at Rogers, handling the complaints that had climbed past every other level of the company. Nobody ever called that office about spectrum. They called about the bill, the dropped call, the contract they did not remember signing and the agent who had promised something nobody could find in the notes.

Reuters noted that SpaceX would need heavy investment in customer service, handset subsidies and promotions, all areas where the Big Three already have large operations. That is the part of a phone company that does not show up in a fireside chat. Spectrum can be bought in a weekend. Twenty years of customer relationships, and the people who answer when they go wrong, cannot. Musk may well use the airwaves ten times more effectively. The test of a carrier is what happens on the eleventh call.

The ledger

Part XXIII said that every promise in the Stack ends the same way: by being replaced with a bigger one. On a single Friday, the Cybertruck became the Cybercab, and a phone service due in 2028 became an offer to buy a phone company outright. The carriers say spectrum is useless without a network. Musk’s reply was that networks are for sale.

He may be bluffing. The analysts who think he is applying pressure, not preparing a bid, may be right. But the spectrum is bought, the tower talks are real, the chip deal is signed and the stock moves on cue. The Stack has a habit of turning its owner’s offhand remarks into plans. This one has a price tag.
