On Friday, November 20, 2026, in the White House briefing room, Kaitlan Collins of CNN put a plain question to the president of the United States. A few days earlier in Shenzhen, Donald Trump had greeted Vladimir Putin with the widest smile of the trip and called him “my friend.” Russia, Collins noted, is Iran’s biggest ally, and the United States is at war with Iran. Did Trump believe Putin was a friend to America?

“I can’t speak to America,” Trump said. “I’ve been dealing with Putin during the fake Russia, Russia, Russia hoax, and to be honest, he was very nice about it. It was a rigged hoax. It was a hoax that was made up by the Democrats. It probably helped me win the election—re-win the election.”

In October, this Substack asked, in an essay called “Donald, Whose Side Are You On?”, what had happened to the idea of America as the good guys. The question was meant rhetorically. It did not expect an answer from the podium. It got one anyway, five weeks later, in four sentences.

The pretence that ran for ten years

For as long as he has been in politics, Trump has kept up one pretence without a break: that everything he does, he does for the United States. He came down the escalator in 2015 promising to set his businesses aside, at great personal sacrifice, to rescue the country he loved. The slogans said it for him. America First. Make America Great Again. He hugged flags on stage, literally, more than once.

Jonathan Chait, writing in The Atlantic a few hours after the news conference, called Friday’s answer the moment Trump finally broke character. The tilt towards Moscow, his defenders have always said, was hard-headed calculation on America’s behalf. Last year, asked about it by a reporter, Trump insisted he was aligned with nobody but “the United States of America and for the good of the world.” This week, after the Shenzhen embrace, Vice President JD Vance posted the clip on X with the caption that the president “will talk to anyone, anywhere, to advocate for American interests.”

Then the president was asked directly whether the man he talks to on America’s behalf is good for America, and he said he couldn’t say.

Read the answer slowly. It has two halves, and the second half does all the work. Trump could not speak to Putin’s relationship with the country Trump leads. He could speak, fluently and at once, to Putin’s relationship with Trump. Putin had been nice to him. Putin had been nice about the investigation into whether Putin had helped him. And that investigation, Trump added, as though it rounded off the case for Putin, had probably helped him win.

What Putin was being nice about is a matter of record. Russian military intelligence hacked the Democratic National Committee and John Podesta in 2016 and released the stolen material to damage Hillary Clinton. Russia’s Internet Research Agency ran a social-media campaign for the same purpose. Robert Mueller’s report documented both, and a Republican-led Senate Intelligence Committee confirmed them in a bipartisan report in 2020. Any other president, as Chait drily put it, would have been cross with a foreign leader caught doing that. Trump was grateful that Putin had been gracious about being caught.

Helsinki, with the mask off

The answer was not new so much as unguarded. On PBS’s Washington Week that evening, Liz Landers, the NewsHour’s White House correspondent, reached back to Helsinki in July 2018. Standing beside Putin at the Presidential Palace, Trump was asked whether he believed his own intelligence agencies about Russian interference. He said Putin had denied it, and that he saw no reason why it would be Russia. A president of the United States took the word of the man accused over the findings of the CIA, the FBI and the NSA, on foreign soil, with the man accused at the next lectern.

The difference eight years makes is in the framing. In Helsinki, Trump at least pretended that he was weighing two accounts and acting in the national interest. On Friday he did not bother. He did not claim that Putin was a friend to America. He did not claim that Putin was not. He declined the question as beyond his competence, and answered a different one about himself.

Jeffrey Goldberg, hosting the programme, put the point in the fewest possible words. The president’s job, he said, “is literally to speak for America.”

That is not a figure of speech. The Constitution makes the president commander-in-chief and gives him the power to receive ambassadors and to make treaties with the Senate’s consent. In 1936 the Supreme Court called the president the “sole organ” of the federal government in foreign relations, a phrase Trump’s own lawyers have quoted happily whenever it suited them. Of the 340-odd million Americans, there is exactly one whose job description is to say what America’s relationship with Russia is. On Friday he said he couldn’t.

What the friend did that week

It is worth setting out what Putin had done in the days before Trump declined to judge him.

At the Shenzhen summit on November 19, Trump bounded into his one-on-one meeting with Putin asking reporters, “Who is he? He’s my friend.” Donald Tusk, the Polish prime minister, answered within hours: “God, protect us from such friends.” Susan B. Glasser, writing in The New Yorker, noted that Western intelligence agencies have assessed that Putin has no current interest in ending the war. Putin confirmed it himself after the summit: “We will not be negotiating,” he said, ruling out ceasefire talks with Ukraine. Asked about the comparisons to Yalta, he asked what had been so bad about Yalta, and explained that it had merely codified the realities on the ground.

Within a day of the meeting at which Trump said he had urged Putin to end the war by diplomacy, Russia launched huge airstrikes across Ukraine. At Friday’s news conference, a reporter put it to Trump that Kyiv had been bombed that day, with people killed, and asked how that squared with Putin wanting peace. Trump never really answered.

What he did, according to Politico, was call in from Air Force One to a meeting of European leaders in Berlin who were gathered to support Ukraine. Five European officials briefed on the call said Trump told them Putin was serious about peace and that they could save money as well as lives by ending the war. The White House denies the account. A day earlier he had boasted to Fox News about how much money the United States was making by selling Europe the weapons it sends to Kyiv. On Washington Week, Glasser pointed out a fact that many Americans may not know: the United States now contributes nothing directly to Ukraine.

So the money argument does not hold up. Trump has said for years that he opposes aid to Ukraine because he resents sending American dollars overseas. The American dollars have stopped. What Trump was asking the Europeans to stop was the spending of their own money, on their own continent’s security, against an invader who had just told the world he would not negotiate. That is not thrift. As Chait concluded, the more economical explanation is that Trump wants Russia to win because he admires its leader.

The friend’s other war

Collins built Iran into her question for a reason, and it is the part of the answer that should trouble Americans most.

On the same Washington Week panel, Nancy Youssef, The Atlantic’s Pentagon correspondent, set out what Russia does for Iran: missiles, explosives, intelligence, targeting and surveillance, and improved versions of the Iranian drones Russia has been firing at Ukraine. She called it the military backbone of Iran’s war. China, she said, is Iran’s economic lifeline. Phillips Payson O’Brien, writing in The Atlantic the same day, traced the help back to March, when American intelligence assessed that Russia was passing Iran targeting information on American installations. Americans have died at those installations. This Substack set out the record in The Shenzhen Opera’s closing part, “The Washington Edition,” and it does not need repeating in full here.

The point here is narrower. The question Collins asked was not abstract. It was whether a man whose government has helped another government kill American service members is a friend to America. That is about as easy as foreign-policy questions get. The answer that would have cost Trump nothing, and that any of his predecessors of either party would have given without thinking, was some version of “No, and I’ve told him so.” Trump could not bring himself to say it, because saying it would have meant choosing America over a man who had been nice to him.

Autocrat envy

William Burns, who ran the CIA for Joe Biden and served as ambassador to Moscow for George W. Bush, gave Glasser the line that will probably outlive the summit. Shenzhen, he said, was a meeting of “two autocrats and a third leader afflicted with autocrat envy.”

Friday explained the diagnosis. Envy is personal. It is about how the other man is treated, what he is allowed to do, and how he treats you. It has nothing to say about national interest, which is why the president could not speak to it. Asked about America, Trump went blank. Asked about Putin, he lit up. Xi Jinping had met him at the plane, he told the room, something Xi had never done for anyone. Putin had been nice about the hoax. A Chinese leader had called a disgraced American chief executive brilliant. Every measure he reached for was a measure of how he had been treated.

There is a consolation, of sorts, and Goldberg named it at the top of his programme. The commander-in-chief is still the commander-in-chief, even after a blue wave, but he is no longer the only voice. Democrats take both houses of Congress in January. Hakeem Jeffries and Chuck Schumer will have subpoenas, appropriations and the floor. They cannot speak for America abroad in the way a president can. They can, however, ask the question Collins asked, under oath, of the officials who carry out the policy, and they can decide what the Treasury pays for. Somebody in Washington will have to be able to speak to America. Since Friday, it is clear that it will not be the president.

The answer, at last

“America First,” Chait wrote, “has always meant Trump first.” For ten years that was an accusation, made by critics and denied by the man himself, his vice president and his party. On November 20, 2026, the president of the United States confirmed it in front of the White House press corps, unprompted by anything but a straight question, and moved on to talk about asphalt.

So, to answer the question this Substack asked in October: whose side is he on? He told us. He is on the side of whoever is nice to him. On Friday that was Vladimir Putin. America, he said, he couldn’t speak to.

A Canadian may be allowed one observation. North of the border we have spent two years learning that a president’s friendship can be withdrawn by social-media post and his enmity announced the same way. It has been instructive, if not pleasant. What Friday added was the other half of the lesson. The man who could not vouch for Putin’s friendship towards America had no trouble at all vouching for Putin’s friendship towards him. Stephen Leacock once wrote of a man who flung himself upon his horse and rode madly off in all directions. This one rides in exactly one direction, and it has never once been ours.
