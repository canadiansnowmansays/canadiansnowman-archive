Every legitimating mechanism examined so far in this series predates the twentieth century by centuries or millennia, resting on some combination of theology, bloodline, marriage, demonstrated military capability, or a sacred physical object believed to hold the nation’s spirit. The Gulf monarchies offer this series its newest case study, and in some respects its most nakedly transactional one: kingship whose durability rests less on any ancient theological claim and more on a straightforward and continuously renewed material bargain between ruling families and their populations, funded by hydrocarbon wealth on a scale no earlier monarchy examined anywhere in this series ever commanded or could have imagined.

Saudi Arabia’s ruling house traces its modern political authority to Abdulaziz Ibn Saud, who spent the early decades of the twentieth century unifying much of the Arabian Peninsula through a patient and often ruthless combination of military conquest, tribal alliance, and strategic marriage, completing the process with the kingdom’s formal founding under his own name in 1932. The family’s underlying claim to legitimate rule rested from the outset not primarily on descent or conquest alone but on an alliance dating back to 1744 between the Saud family and Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab, a religious reformer whose austere interpretation of Islamic doctrine, later known as Wahhabism, provided the Saud family’s political ambitions with a specifically religious justification and a network of committed local clerical support in a manner this series has not seen so explicitly paired since the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb’s own religious consolidation strategy examined in Part IX. This alliance survived a considerable eclipse during the nineteenth century, when Ottoman-backed rivals twice drove the Saud family from power entirely, before Abdulaziz’s own early twentieth-century campaign, beginning with his dramatic recapture of the family’s ancestral capital at Riyadh in 1902 with a small raiding party scaling the city’s walls under cover of darkness, restored and then dramatically expanded Saudi authority across the peninsula.

What transformed this regional religious-political alliance into one of the wealthiest states on earth, in relative terms, was the discovery of vast oil reserves beginning in 1938 by the American-operated Arabian American Oil Company, later renamed Saudi Aramco once the Saudi state progressively nationalized full ownership through the 1970s. The revenue this discovery eventually generated, particularly following the dramatic oil price increases of the 1970s, driven substantially by the coordinated production and pricing decisions of the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, of which Saudi Arabia was a founding member in 1960 and has remained the dominant voice ever since given its position as the group’s largest producer with by far the greatest spare production capacity of any member state, allowed the Saudi state to fund an entirely new and historically unprecedented kind of legitimating bargain with its own population: no income tax whatsoever on citizens, extensive government-subsidized housing, free education through the university level, comprehensive free healthcare, and government employment guaranteed for a substantial share of the citizen population, all funded by resource wealth extracted and sold on international markets rather than by taxing citizens directly at all, a structural inversion of precisely the taxation grievance that this series has already documented toppling King John’s authority in Part VI and contributing directly to Louis XVI’s own downfall in Part XIV.

Before turning to those smaller states individually, it is worth explaining briefly how the British protective relationship that preceded their independence actually functioned, since it shapes why the Gulf ended up with this particular constellation of separate small monarchies rather than a single larger state. Throughout the nineteenth and much of the twentieth century, Britain maintained a series of treaty relationships with individual ruling sheikhdoms along the Gulf coast, collectively known as the Trucial States, under arrangements that, much like the Indian princely states examined in Part X, left local rulers considerable internal autonomy in exchange for British control over external defense and foreign policy, a system maintained primarily to protect British trade and shipping routes to India rather than out of any deep interest in the internal governance of the sheikhdoms themselves. When Britain announced in 1968 that it would withdraw from its remaining commitments east of Suez by 1971, largely for its own budgetary reasons following a costly and prolonged post-war economic decline, the individual Trucial States faced the same essential choice India’s princely states had faced in Part X: unify into some larger federated structure or attempt independent survival alone. Most of the smaller sheikhdoms chose federation, and seven of them, under the patient diplomatic leadership of Sheikh Zayed bin Sultan Al Nahyan of Abu Dhabi, formally united in December 1971 to form the United Arab Emirates, a federal monarchy in which each constituent emirate retains its own hereditary ruler while the country as a whole is led by a president drawn, by long-standing convention rather than strict legal requirement, from the wealthier and more populous Abu Dhabi branch of the family. Qatar and Bahrain, each larger and more confident of independent viability than the seven uniting emirates, chose to become fully separate sovereign states instead.

The smaller Gulf states, Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain, the United Arab Emirates, and Oman, developed broadly similar arrangements following their own transitions to full independence from British protective treaty relationships through the 1960s and 1970s, each ruling family converting oil and, increasingly, natural gas revenue into an extensive welfare and patronage system tailored to its own particular circumstances. Kuwait presents this series with a genuinely distinct variant worth noting separately, because its ruling Al Sabah family adopted a somewhat more constitutionally constrained bargain than most of its neighbors from a considerably earlier date. Kuwait’s National Assembly, an elected parliament first established in 1963 and possessing genuine, if periodically suspended, legislative and budgetary oversight powers, has functioned as the most politically developed elected legislature anywhere in the Gulf region for six decades, giving Kuwaiti citizens a formal channel for airing grievances and challenging government policy that most of their neighboring monarchies never developed to nearly the same degree, even as the ruling Emir retains ultimate executive authority and the power to dissolve the Assembly outright, which Kuwaiti rulers have exercised on several occasions when parliamentary opposition grew sufficiently inconvenient. This slightly more participatory bargain has not made Kuwait meaningfully more vulnerable to unrest than its more purely autocratic neighbors; if anything, Kuwaiti citizens’ greater formal voice in government appears to have functioned as its own kind of pressure valve, allowing grievances to be aired and partially addressed through legitimate institutional channels rather than accumulating unaddressed until they demanded street-level expression the way this series has already documented happening so catastrophically in France and Russia in Parts XIV and XV.

Qatar’s case is perhaps the most extreme demonstration of what this model can achieve when a very small citizen population sits atop truly vast per-capita hydrocarbon reserves: with a citizen population numbering only in the low hundreds of thousands even as the country’s total resident population, overwhelmingly composed of foreign migrant workers with no path to citizenship or political voice, runs into the millions, Qatar’s ruling Al Thani family has been able to fund a scale of individual citizen benefit, from guaranteed government employment to extensive foreign scholarship funding, that few other governments on earth could hope to replicate, while simultaneously projecting Qatari influence far beyond what its physical size would ordinarily suggest through instruments like the Al Jazeera media network and a globally prominent sovereign wealth fund.

This transactional model has faced a genuine and instructive stress test in recent memory, and the outcome is worth examining directly because it demonstrates the model’s actual resilience under real pressure rather than merely its theoretical design. The Arab Spring uprisings that swept across the wider Middle East and North Africa beginning in late 2010 and through 2011 toppled or seriously destabilized long-standing republican and quasi-monarchical authoritarian governments in Tunisia, Egypt, Libya, and Yemen, and produced a prolonged and catastrophic civil war in Syria that continued for well over a decade afterward. Notably, and in sharp contrast to this regional pattern, every hereditary Gulf monarchy survived the same period of regional unrest largely intact, with the partial exception of Bahrain, whose ruling Al Khalifa family faced a genuinely serious domestic uprising in 2011 driven substantially by longstanding grievances among the country’s Shia Muslim majority population against a Sunni ruling family, a crisis the Bahraini government ultimately suppressed only with direct military assistance from Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates, sending an estimated one thousand Saudi troops and several hundred additional UAE police personnel across the causeway connecting Bahrain to the Saudi mainland under the banner of the regional Gulf Cooperation Council’s Peninsula Shield Force, a demonstration that the Gulf monarchies, whatever their individual differences, were prepared to treat a serious uprising against any one of their number as a threat to the entire regional model of hereditary rule, worth intervening militarily to contain before it could serve as an example to restless populations elsewhere in the Gulf.

It is worth noting one further internal feature of the Saudi case specifically, because it shows this legitimacy model facing a genuinely distinct kind of stress separate from external unrest entirely: the question of succession itself. Rather than the strict primogeniture this series traced through most European monarchies in earlier parts, Saudi Arabia has, since Abdulaziz Ibn Saud’s death in 1953, passed the throne through a system of agnatic seniority among his own numerous sons, each of the kingdom’s rulers since the founder’s death having been one of his direct sons, a pool that included over forty sons from multiple wives, before the line of succession finally moved, with the accession of Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman as heir apparent, toward the founder’s grandchildren for the first time. This sons-first succession model bought the family several decades of relatively predictable, if occasionally contentious, transitions between brothers, but it was always a temporary solution rather than a durable structural answer, since the founder’s sons were a finite and steadily aging population, and the eventual transition to a much larger and more numerous generation of grandsons, several of whom command their own networks of patronage and armed security forces within different branches of the sprawling royal family, has introduced a genuine and closely watched succession uncertainty this series has not needed to examine in quite the same form since the Mughal Empire’s own recurring wars of succession in Part IX.

Political scientists studying this period have attributed the Gulf monarchies’ comparative durability directly to their capacity to respond to emerging unrest with immediate and substantial new spending commitments, effectively buying continued quiescence in real time using the same oil wealth that had funded the underlying bargain for decades already. Saudi Arabia’s own government, watching the regional uprisings spread with evident alarm, announced well over a hundred billion dollars in new social spending commitments within weeks in early 2011, including new public sector jobs, a substantial minimum wage increase for government employees, additional unemployment benefits, and a large new fund earmarked specifically for affordable housing construction, alongside more modest political gestures such as expanding the electorate eligible to vote in limited municipal council elections. The speed and scale of this response was itself the message: rather than debate the merits of specific grievances raised by protesters elsewhere in the region, the Saudi state simply demonstrated, within days, that it retained more than enough accumulated financial reserve to outspend any domestic discontent before it had a chance to organize into anything resembling the sustained popular mobilizations that toppled governments elsewhere in the region that same year. This is a demonstration that when this particular legitimating model faces genuine popular pressure, its available response is neither theological reinterpretation, as this series traced through Japan’s imperial line in Part XVI, nor negotiated territorial buyout, as this series traced through India’s princely states in Part X, but simply writing a very large check quickly enough that the underlying grievance never has time to harden into an organized movement, an option available to remarkably few of the monarchies examined anywhere else in this entire series.

This transactional model carries its own distinct and genuinely novel vulnerability, one this series has not examined in quite this specific form before: legitimacy purchased through continuously distributed resource wealth depends entirely on that wealth continuing to flow at a sufficient volume and price, tying a ruling family’s political durability directly to global oil and gas markets and, increasingly and more consequentially over the coming decades, to the pace of the world’s broader transition away from fossil fuel consumption entirely, a long-term structural pressure no divine mandate, sacred bloodline, ancient bronze plaque, or golden stool has ever had to contend with in quite the same way. Saudi Arabia’s own government has responded to this looming pressure with an ambitious economic diversification program known as Vision 2030, launched in 2016 under the direction of Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, explicitly aimed at reducing the kingdom’s dependence on oil revenue through investment in tourism, entertainment, technology, and other non-hydrocarbon sectors, alongside a set of accompanying social liberalization measures, including lifting a longstanding ban on women driving in 2018 and permitting public entertainment venues such as cinemas that had been closed for decades, changes justified explicitly in official government messaging as necessary preparation for a post-oil economy that will require a more socially open society to attract the foreign investment, tourism, and skilled labor such a diversified economy will depend on. It amounts to a genuinely unprecedented attempt by a monarchy examined anywhere in this series to consciously and proactively re-engineer the very economic and, to a real if still limited degree, social foundation its own legitimacy has rested on for nearly a century, before market forces or global climate policy make that re-engineering unavoidable rather than optional.

Part XVIII turns to the monarchy that read exactly this kind of long-term structural pressure earliest and most successfully among every case this series has examined, surrendering its own political power gradually and voluntarily across centuries rather than waiting to be forced by revolution, invasion, or economic collapse, and in doing so became the model this series’ remaining chapters will keep measuring every other surviving monarchy against.
