By Stephen Gordon

There is a particular kind of defence that works better as an indictment. On the morning of October 6, 2026, the most careful fact-checker in American journalism stepped forward to rescue Donald Trump from his worst headline of the week, and in doing so produced the most precise clinical description this series has yet been handed.

To understand why, we need to start in Nebraska.

The Passage

On the evening of Monday, October 5, 2026, Trump spoke for close to ninety minutes at the Pinnacle Bank Expo Center on the Nebraska State Fairgrounds in Grand Island. He was there to campaign for Senator Pete Ricketts and Governor Jim Pillen, a month out from the midterms. Somewhere past the midpoint, talking about the Iran war and the price of gasoline, he said this, as transcribed by KJ Hiramoto for FOX 11 Los Angeles from the FOX News live feed:

“It’s a small price to pay for keeping the world safe, keeping our country safe. They can take out a city — let them take out Los Angeles, let them take out San Diego. This is a very small price to pay.”

The crowd applauded.

Within hours, Governor Gavin Newsom had called the president “deranged and dangerous.” Los Angeles Mayor Karen Bass said the remark could compromise her city’s safety. San Diego Mayor Todd Gloria said his city was not collateral damage. Even Jim Desmond, the San Diego County supervisor Trump has endorsed in California’s 48th district, said he disagreed “vehemently.” Laura Chung, writing for The New York Times on October 6, 2026, recorded the most honest sentence in the whole news cycle: it was not immediately clear what Mr. Trump meant.

That sentence is where The Baseline Problem lives.

The Defence

Daniel Dale has appeared in this series before. In Part XXII, it was Dale who took apart Trump’s invented account of rescuing people at Ground Zero. He is not a man inclined to give the president the benefit of the doubt for sport. So it is worth taking seriously that on the morning of October 6, as reported by Sean James for Mediaite, Dale publicly objected to the viral version of the clip.

His reconstruction runs in three steps. First, Trump claimed that high gas prices were temporary, even “artificial.” Second, he contrasted those prices with the damage an unchecked Iran could supposedly do by taking out an American city. Third, in what Dale called an “especially confusing style,” he circled back to an argument he has made many times: that the costs of the war are a small price compared with a city wiped off the map. On this reading, Trump was not inviting Tehran to bomb Los Angeles. He was trying to say the war is the price of making sure Tehrancannotbomb Los Angeles, and the sentence fell apart in his hands.

Dale added, fairly, that it is still inappropriate for a president to toss off the destruction of a city as a rhetorical prop. But his central point stands, and I think he is probably right about intent.

Here is the difficulty. Accept Dale’s reading in full, and look at what it describes.

A man who has made the same argument, by Dale’s own account, repeatedly. A familiar talking point, rehearsed across months of rallies, carrying no novelty and no complexity. And in the delivery of that worn-smooth argument, the speaker lost the conditional mood halfway through (”they can take out a city” became “let them take out Los Angeles”), dropped the step that made the argument an argument, and returned to his opening phrase with the word “very” bolted on, as though intensity could do the work grammar had abandoned.

The Nebraska Examiner, which had a reporter in the hall, caught a slightly longer tail, reported by Nebraska Examiner on October 5, 2026: after the cities, Trump added, “It’s essentially almost over with.”

The charitable reading is not a defence of the speaker’s mind. It is a description of it.

The Anatomy of Twenty Seconds

This series counts patterns rather than diagnosing them, and the Grand Island passage offers an unusually clean specimen, short enough to hold in the hand.

The anchor phrase. “A small price to pay for keeping the world safe” is a stock line, a piece of prefabricated language the speaker reaches for when the subject is cost. Prefabricated phrases are the last things to go in a speaker under strain, because they do not need to be built; they only need to be retrieved.

The departure. The speaker leaves the anchor to construct something new: a contrast between gas prices and nuclear catastrophe. This is the hard part, because it requires holding two ideas in relation while producing a sentence. “They can take out a city” is the first half of that contrast, and it is grammatically intact.

The slide. The second half never arrives. Instead, “can” becomes “let them,” a hypothetical becomes an imperative, and two named cities appear as illustrations of a sentence that no longer has a frame. Whether this is disinhibition (the filter that should catch “let them take out Los Angeles” before it leaves the mouth failing to engage) or simple derailment (the thread dropped, the words continuing on momentum), the effect on the listener is identical.

The return. Having lost the new construction, the speaker goes home to the anchor: “This is a very small price to pay.” This is perseveration in its textbook shape. Not repetition across days, which every politician does, but repetition within a single breath as a recovery mechanism: when the thought fails, the phrase that started it comes back, louder.

The applause. The crowd cheered, which is its own data point, and arguably a more troubling one than anything the speaker did. Jim Acosta observed that the applause began on the cities line. Whatever the president meant, the audience heard something it liked, and the speaker, who has calibrated his performance against crowd response for a decade, received confirmation rather than correction.

Twenty seconds. Four of the categories this series has been tracking since Part I, running in sequence.

Elsewhere in the Hall

The cities passage was not the only stumble in Grand Island, though the others are lighter and should be weighed accordingly.

The Daily Beast, a hostile outlet with an eye for a slip, reported on October 6, 2026 in The Daily Beast that Trump went off his teleprompter to insult a governor, producing the name “Jim Walz” before correcting himself to “Tim Walz.” He was standing a few feet from Jim Pillen, the governor he had come to endorse. The error fused the first name of the man beside him with the surname of a man he dislikes. In clinical terms, a blend: two lexical targets activated at once, neither suppressed.

The same report has him describing the giant screens outside the venue as “telerons or whatever the hell they call it,” and, at the end of the evening, missing the curtain that had been opened for his exit and wandering down the stage until he found another way off. I would not build anything on the curtain. Exits are awkward for everyone. But the word-finding failure and the name blend belong in the ledger, and they occurred in the same speech as the cities passage, which matters: these are not scattered incidents across weeks but a cluster inside ninety minutes.

The Controlled Condition

A rally is the hardest test of speech there is. It is long, loud, improvised and fuelled by feedback. Fairness requires asking how the same speaker performs under calmer conditions. As it happens, we were given those conditions four days earlier.

On October 1, 2026, Time published the full transcript of a 75-minute interview Trump gave to correspondent Eric Cortellessa and editor Sam Jacobs. An interview is close to the opposite of a rally: a quiet room, two questioners, time to finish a thought. Ian Prasad Philbrick’s analysis for Slate on October 2, 2026 walked through the transcript, and several moments in it fit the patterns this series tracks almost exactly.

The layered correction. Asked about a dinner with Anthropic’s Dario Amodei, Trump instead explained the dinner had been delayed by the Presidents Cup golf tournament, which he said he had chaired “in Chicago at Marion.” The tournament was held at Medinah. Trump then corrected himself, sort of: “Actually, that was yeah, Medinah, at Medinah, last year, Marion, this time of year.” As Philbrick notes, there was no Presidents Cup last year (it is held every other year), and it has never been played at Marion.

This is worth pausing on. In Part XXI, the confabulation was a number, “57,” offered with confidence. In Part XXII, it was a self-mythologising story about Ground Zero. Here it is something subtler: a correction that introduces new errors as it fixes old ones. The speaker notices the mistake, reaches for the right word, finds it, and then lets the wrong word back in alongside an invented timeline. The repair mechanism is working, but it no longer knows what it is repairing toward.

The derailed answer. Asked what he would do about Hamas not having disarmed, Trump said he would “probably sit in the Situation Room and make an appropriate decision,” then moved to the booming economy, then to whether the fighting was a war at all (”some people would say it’s a war, some people would say it’s not really a war”), then to a sequence in which “somebody drops a bomb, and nothing happens, and they drop a bomb,” ending with “you’ll find out what I’m doing.” Compare the structure with Grand Island: an anchor (the Situation Room, the economy), a departure, a slide, and a return to bluster. The same shape, in a quiet room, with no crowd to blame.

The dead presidents. Defending the length of the Iran war, Trump asked his interviewers why they hadn’t asked “Kennedy, Lyndon Johnson, how long have you been in various wars?” Both men are dead. One of the Time journalists replied that he would have if he’d had the opportunity.

Here, too, there is a charitable reading, and this series should offer it: Trump may have meant “why didn’t the press ask Kennedy and Johnson back then?” That is a coherent, if historically shaky, rhetorical point. But it is exactly the same kind of charitable reading Dale offered for Grand Island. A speaker who requires an interpreter to reconstruct the intended sentence twice in one week is generating a pattern, whatever his intent.

The displacement. In September, this series banked Tom Nichols’ observation that Trump lashes out hardest at those closest to him when frustrated. The Time interview extends the pattern from allies to institutions. Asked about the Federal Reserve raising rates, Trump said the central bank was acting not to fight inflation but to damage him politically. Asked about his approval ratings, he called them fake, the pollsters corrupt, and said he would beat anybody by twenty points. Asked about the war, he blamed the press for the public’s confusion. Every institution that delivers an unwelcome number is reclassified as an enemy acting against him personally. At one point he told the interviewers they had not asked him a single positive question, with an expletive in the middle.

The Same Claim, Twice

One more thread connects the two settings, and it may be the most telling.

In the Time interview, asked why a war promised to last weeks has run more than seven months, Trump said he had “met my timeline” but then “decided I wanted to go further.” Four days later in Grand Island, he told the crowd the war was “essentially almost over with.” On September 26, 2026, he had rejected Iran’s latest cease-fire proposal. In the same speech, he signed an executive order waiving federal taxes on red-dyed diesel, a measure that exists only because the war has driven fuel prices to levels his own voters can feel.

A war that has exceeded its timeline by choice, that is almost over, that he has just declined to end, and whose costs he is subsidising on stage. These claims cannot all be true at once. A politician who tells different audiences different things is ordinary. A speaker who holds incompatible positions in the same week without, apparently, registering the incompatibility is the thing this series exists to count.

What Dale Got Right

I want to end where I began, with the defence, because I think it is the most important development in this story.

For most of this series, the public argument about Trump’s cognition has been a contest between two camps: those who see decline and those who see a showman doing what he has always done. The second camp has relied heavily on intent.He’s joking. He’s riffing. He knows exactly what he’s doing. You’re taking him literally.Fox 11’s own report called the cities remark likely tongue in cheek.

Dale’s intervention quietly closes that escape route. He did not say Trump was joking. He said Trump was trying to make a real, familiar argument and could not get it out in order. That is a more serious claim than the one Newsom made, because “deranged” is an insult and “especially confusing style” is an observation. One is a political judgement. The other is the beginning of a baseline.

Lawrence O’Donnell’s question, the one this series was built to answer, was how we would know. Part of the answer, it turns out, is this: we will know when the people defending the speaker stop arguing about what he meant and start explaining what he wastryingto say. When the case for the defence is that the president’s sentences no longer reliably contain his thoughts, the defence and the prosecution have arrived at the same exhibit.

The neuropsychological letter Representative Jamie Raskin sent the White House, which this series first noted in Part XIX, has, as far as this series has been able to determine, still not received an answer.

The crowd in Grand Island applauded. That, I suppose, is a very small price to pay.
