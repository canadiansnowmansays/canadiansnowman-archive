There is a phrase buried in Reuters’ July 14, 2026 special report on Robert F. Kennedy Jr. that deserves to be framed and hung in the lobby of the Department of Health and Human Services: “shared clinical decision-making.” It sounds like something a hospital ethics committee would produce after a very long meeting. What it actually meant, according to sixteen current and former administration officials who spoke to Reuters on condition of anonymity, was Kennedy’s proposed replacement for the entire federal childhood immunization schedule — the sixty-year-old framework that tells pediatricians, insurers, and the Vaccines for Children program which shots a child needs and when.

Under “shared clinical decision-making,” there would be no schedule. There would be a conversation, between a doctor and a parent, about whether to bother.

This is not a caricature of the reporting. As CNN detailed, Kennedy and his advisers spent months debating not just the framing but the specifics, including whether to remove the measles-mumps-rubella and polio vaccines from the schedule entirely. A recommendation for COVID-19 shots in children had already been quietly dropped in May, announced via a social media post rather than a policy memo. The MMR and polio discussions reportedly continued into the fall before Kennedy walked them back — not, by any account, because he changed his mind about vaccines, but because of what officials described as fear of political blowback and direct pushback from the White House.

Two of the administration’s most senior FDA officials were, in the paper’s word, alarmed. Marty Makary, the FDA commissioner who resigned in May, and Dr. Tracy Beth Hoeg, the agency’s top drug regulator who was fired the same month, are both described as having pushed back on the substance of what Kennedy was trying to do before they were no longer in a position to push back on anything.

Ask HHS about any of this and you get a different story entirely. Spokeswoman Courtney Spencer told Reuters the claim that Kennedy sought to eliminate the entire immunization schedule was untrue. Pressed for specifics on what part of the reporting was wrong, she did not respond. This is the department’s answer to nearly everything now: dispute the framing, decline to specify the correction, move on.

This is also, notably, not what Kennedy told Congress he was doing. Testifying before a House committee on April 22, 2026 about the department’s 2027 budget request, Kennedy insisted repeatedly that he has “never been anti-vaccine” and that he bears no responsibility for the resurgence of measles cases nationwide — this from the man whose own department’s internal debates, per Reuters, ran to eliminating the schedule those vaccines sit on. Dr. Amesh Adalja of the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security told HuffPost at the time that Kennedy was “brazenly evading” his own record, noting that Kennedy wants to construct a statue to Andrew Wakefield, the researcher whose fraudulent study originated the vaccine-autism claim Kennedy has spent his HHS tenure trying to “elevate” through new federal research. The measles outbreak Kennedy told Congress wasn’t his doing has, as of July 24, 2026, produced 2,318 confirmed cases this year alone — a new annual record surpassing every year since the disease was declared eliminated in the United States in 2000, and more cases across the last two years combined than in the twenty-five years before them. A panel of international experts is scheduled to meet in November to decide whether the U.S. has lost its elimination status altogether. HHS spokeswoman Courtney Spencer, asked about the record, pointed reporters to $8.5 million in outbreak-response funding and reiterated that “MMR vaccination remains the best protection against measles” — a sentence that is true, and also not something the department has spent any money telling the public through a national ad campaign. “We have seen virtually no national messaging,” Dr. Jonathan Temte, a former chairman of the CDC’s vaccine advisory committee, said of the response. “I think that really tells us something about their priorities.”

The TACO Playbook, Applied to Children’s Vaccines

Readers of this series know the shape by now. Threaten: float the elimination of the entire schedule, debate stripping MMR and polio specifically, do it in rooms with sixteen people who will eventually talk to reporters. Accommodate: quietly drop the COVID recommendation for children, the least politically costly cut, and let that stand in as evidence of “reform.” Capitulate: shelve the MMR and polio removal once the West Wing signals it isn’t worth the midterm exposure. Obfuscate: have Spencer call the entire framing false, without saying which fact is wrong.

The pattern isn’t new to Kennedy — it is Kennedy, and has been since the Gavi funding freeze and the ACIP purge chronicled in earlier installments of this series. What’s new is the paper trail. Reuters didn’t have to speculate about intent; it had sixteen people in the room describing it in their own words.

One of those people has his own reason to know. Aaron Siri, the vaccine-injury lawyer who serves as Kennedy’s personal attorney, participated in the internal discussions about removing MMR and polio from the schedule, according to the administration officials Reuters spoke with. Siri told Reuters he wasn’t aware of any such plan, or of any requirement that HHS policy changes be run by him first — before adding, with the kind of candor that reads like an accidental admission, that he wished the department had followed that “purported directive,” since it would spare him the trouble of continuing to sue HHS over vaccine policy in the meantime. Four legal experts interviewed by Reuters noted that HHS policy in this area has a direct bearing on Siri’s own law practice. A man advising the health secretary on vaccine schedule design while suing the health department over vaccine policy is not, strictly, a conflict of interest requiring further comment. It is simply the org chart.

The Task Force Nobody Was Watching

While the immunization-schedule story broke, a second and quieter demolition was proceeding on a different floor of the department. On July 7, 2026, HHS confirmed it is preparing to announce eight new members of the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force — the panel whose recommendations determine which cancer screenings, colonoscopies, mammograms, and pap tests insurers and Medicaid must cover without a copay. It will be the task force’s first meeting in seventeen months, after the administration canceled four prior sessions, pushed out two of the panel’s vice chairs in May, and simply let other members’ terms lapse without replacement. A body designed to hold sixteen experts has been allowed to shrink by half.

Kennedy has described this task force, in terms he previously reserved for the CDC’s vaccine advisory committee, as “lackadaisical” and “negligent.” It is worth noticing how consistent the method is: identify a panel of independent experts whose recommendations constrain administration priorities, describe its ordinary functioning as failure, let it atrophy through attrition and cancellation, and eventually restock it with appointees more amenable to the person doing the restocking. It worked on ACIP. It is now being tried on the panel that decides whether your mammogram is free.

Former task force members told reporters the group will face a substantial backlog of recommendations when it finally reconvenes in late August — recommendations on which Americans have, in the meantime, simply had to guess.

The stakes here are not abstract. Task force recommendations graded “A” or “B” are what obligate state Medicaid expansion programs and most private insurers to cover a service without a copay or deductible — the mechanism behind free mammograms, free colonoscopies, free pap tests, and a range of other routine screenings that catch disease before it becomes expensive or fatal. A panel that hasn’t convened in seventeen months, missing half its normal membership, isn’t merely an administrative inconvenience. It is a pipeline of preventive care sitting idle while the paperwork required to restart it moves at whatever pace the secretary’s office finds politically convenient.

The Emails Sanders Already Had

None of this required new documents to become undeniable; it only required someone to release the old ones. In June, Senator Bernie Sanders published a cache of internal HHS and CDC emails showing the mechanics behind decisions this series has already chronicled. One email, from Kennedy’s then-chief of staff Matthew Buckham to then-CDC-Director Susan Monarez, called for a “political review” of major CDC decisions to ensure Kennedy’s office signed off before anything went into effect. Less than a week after that email, Kennedy fired Monarez — for, in Sanders’s characterization, failing to rubber-stamp his agenda. The emails also documented the cancellation of flu vaccine campaigns, new restrictions on vaccine access, researcher access to confidential data in service of the autism-vaccine hypothesis, and changes to COVID-19 vaccine recommendations made without CDC input.

Sanders called it a “dangerous misinformation campaign” being run out of the nation’s health department. Senator Bill Cassidy, the Republican whose vote confirmed Kennedy in the first place, has spent the months since extracting promises from Kennedy that he would not alter vaccine guidance — promises that, as this series has documented at length, have not been the most durable currency in this administration.

The Committee That Sells What It’s Voting On

If the task force is Kennedy hollowing out an inconvenient panel by attrition, the FDA’s peptide advisory committee shows the opposite technique: stack it instead, and let the numbers do the rest. On July 23 and 24, 2026, an FDA advisory panel met to decide whether specialized pharmacies should be allowed to resume producing seven peptide injections the agency banned three years ago over safety concerns. FDA scientists had prepared briefing documents advising against broader use, writing that the risks and benefits of the peptides under review remain poorly understood. Nineteen people sat on the committee weighing that advice. Six of them sell or dispense the very peptides on the agenda — and in the weeks before the hearing, Kennedy’s office had personally nominated all eight of the panel’s newest members.

The math worked exactly as designed. On the first day, the six peptide sellers joined a compounding pharmacist and a pharmacy-business executive to vote 8 to 6 in favor of adding BPC-157 — described in testimony as useful for everything from cardiac patients to endometriosis — to the list of substances pharmacies can dispense. The panel went on to approve KPV, MOTS-c, and TB-500 the same day; the last of those was, according to the FDA’s own records, originally developed as a performance drug for racehorses and greyhounds. Dr. Elizabeth Rebello of MD Anderson asked the obvious question out loud — had the FDA ever approved a drug delivered this way that hadn’t first been studied in humans? The agency’s own official answered: no.

This is not a subtle irregularity. FDA advisory panels exist to be neutral outside experts; peptide sellers voting on peptide access is closer to asking a car dealership to grade its own financing terms. Paul Knoepfler, a UC Davis biologist who studies FDA oversight, put it plainly: there is, in his words, “a fingerprint of R.F.K.” on the committee’s makeup. HHS’s defense — that all members passed the standard ethics review — does not address why six committee slots went to people with a direct commercial stake in the outcome in the first place. One panelist, Dr. Gabriel Alizaidy, is scientific director at Maximus, a telehealth company that sells growth-hormone peptides for roughly $200 a month and has already received an FDA warning letter over its compounded weight-loss claims. Another, Bobby Harshbarger, is a Tennessee state senator and pharmacist whose mother, Rep. Diana Harshbarger, wrote to Kennedy in November urging him to loosen restrictions on exactly these peptides.

Kennedy’s personal stake in the outcome predates the hearing by years. He has used and publicly promoted some of the peptides under review, and told a podcast host last year that the FDA should “just do the science” on items like peptides without telling doctors what they can or cannot prescribe. Advisory committee votes are nonbinding by design — the FDA commissioner typically weighs them alongside staff review — and the health secretary holds the authority to overrule the process entirely. As it turned out, he didn’t need to. The eight members he’d personally nominated delivered the outcome without him having to spend the political capital of an override.

The pattern held for four peptides in a row, then briefly broke. On Friday, the same committee voted 7 to 6 against adding a fifth, emideltide, a sleep-inducing compound whose evidence base consists of a handful of studies from the 1980s and early 1990s, none involving more than twenty people. Dr. Kevin Zacharoff, a pain specialist added to the panel specifically for this vote, said he couldn’t set aside the FDA’s own safety findings, calling it impossible, “as a clinician,” to ignore the agency’s own recommendation. It was a narrow margin and a single no, not a reversal of the week’s broader pattern — but it was evidence the panel isn’t a pure rubber stamp, which makes the four approvals that preceded it harder to wave away as an aberration rather than a method.

Dr. Peter Lurie, president of the Center for Science in the Public Interest, said what the week amounted to without much hedging: “an extraordinary abuse of the advisory committee process to secure ends that have no basis in science.” Even the testimony calling for caution rather than an outright ban ran into a wall the FDA had built for itself. Russell Wesdyk, an FDA pharmaceutical-quality official, told the committee the agency has no authority over the state pharmacy boards that actually oversee compounding pharmacies — meaning the “guardrails” witnesses kept requesting, like mandatory adverse-event reporting, aren’t something the FDA can promise regardless of how the vote goes. “I don’t have those regulatory authorities,” he said. Dr. Brian Lee, a USC liver transplant specialist, was one of the six no votes on BPC-157, noting that a placebo alone typically produces improvement in roughly 30 percent of patients — a bar he said the actual peptide data didn’t clear. “I cannot, in good conscience, vote yes,” he said.

The scientific case against doing so is not partisan. Dr. Eric Topol of Scripps Research summarized the actual evidentiary record in six words: “We’re missing safety, we’re missing efficacy.” FDA reviewers flagged that some peptides marketed as longevity tonics may also promote tumor growth, and that a small study of one peptide for ulcerative colitis — a serious, potentially life-threatening condition — produced results the agency’s own scientists found unconvincing. Dr. Pieter Cohen of Harvard Medical School warned that capitulating to the pressure would reposition the FDA “in a much weaker position” for every fight after this one, since, in his assessment, there is no scientific reason these particular products should be approved.

None of this means every voice in the room was self-interested or unserious. Scott Brunner of the Alliance for Pharmacy Compounding had proposed guardrails along similar lines before Wesdyk’s admission made clear how little the agency could actually enforce them — a reasonable position overtaken by the FDA’s own limits, not by the secretary’s preferences. Commercial interests showed up too, if more bluntly: Dr. Anant Vinjamoori, chief medical officer at the online medication provider Hims and Hers, told the committee the company would sell peptides and profit from doing so if the ban lifted, while conceding the evidence for one widely used peptide “isn’t” strong and asking the panel not to mistake thin evidence for no evidence at all. Even the researchers without a financial stake found themselves boxed in by the choice on the table. Dr. Pinchas Cohen of USC, who published the first research on one of the peptides under review, said he was “very conflicted” — he considers the compound likely safe and worth studying, but would rather see it developed as an approved drug through the normal pathway than semi-legalized through a compounding loophole built, in practice, around tens of thousands of people who are already using it regardless of what the FDA decides.

The pattern by now is familiar from ACIP and the Preventive Services Task Force alike: take a body built to insulate scientific judgment from personal and financial interest, restaff or reweight it until it reflects the secretary’s own conclusions, and let the vote supply the appearance of expert consensus for a decision that was never really in question.

What the Schedule Was For

The childhood immunization schedule is, on its face, a bureaucratic document. It is also the reason an American child born today has never seen a case of diphtheria. “Shared clinical decision-making” sounds, in a press release, like empowerment. Applied to a sixty-year-old public health framework by a secretary who has spent decades questioning whether vaccines work at all, it is something closer to an unwinding — paused this time, according to Reuters, mostly by other people’s politics rather than by anyone’s judgment.

There is a market forming around the assumption that the unwinding continues. With vaccination rates sliding below the threshold needed to stop outbreaks, biotech firms have begun developing the first-ever measles treatments — antivirals and antibody infusions aimed not at preventing the disease but at managing it once it arrives. One executive described the country’s roughly 7 million infants, 26 million immunocompromised people, and 11 million pregnant women as a “pretty big population” worth protecting commercially, at a projected cost of roughly $2,500 per infusion. Public health researchers are blunter about what the investment implies. “We realize that the vaccine isn’t going to get us out of this,” Michael Osterholm of the University of Minnesota said. The country is building a business model for a disease it used to consider solved.

The MMR shot is still on the schedule. The task force still hasn’t met. Both facts are being presented, this week, as evidence that everything is fine.
