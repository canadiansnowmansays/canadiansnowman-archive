President Trump signs the June 2, 2026 executive order requiring a 30-day government review of new AI models. The first major federal assertion of oversight over frontier AI.

On June 2, 2026, President Trump signed an executive order requiring technology companies to give the federal government a 30-day window to review new AI models before public release. The same day, Senator Kirsten Gillibrand introduced legislation requiring congressional approval before the Pentagon can deploy AI to control most autonomous weapons in combat. Senator Elissa Slotkin’s bill — which would codify bans on using AI for domestic surveillance, to command autonomous drones, or to launch nuclear weapons — was on track to be incorporated into the Senate’s version of the National Defense Authorization Act.

Three separate legislative actions in a single day. This does not happen in Washington without pressure. The pressure has been building for forty-two parts.

This series has been asking, since Part 1, when the laws governing AI would begin to arrive. The answer, as of June 2026, is: now. Imperfectly, incompletely, in fragments that do not yet add up to a comprehensive framework. But now.

The question this essay asks is what the first wave of laws tells us about the governance framework that is being built — who shaped it, what it covers, what it leaves out, and whether the laws that are coming are adequate to the gap they are supposed to close.

What the Executive Order Says and Doesn’t Say

The Trump executive order is the most consequential AI governance action the federal government has taken. That is a low bar. It is also a real one.

For the first time in American history, the federal government has asserted a formal right to review AI models before they reach the public. The 30-day window is not an approval process. It is a review process, with the government empowered to examine new models for national security implications before release.

The reality is more instructive than the framing. Daniel Remler of the Center for a New American Security described the order as effectively formalizing “what has already been happening between the U.S. government and the leading AI companies.” The major AI firms already had voluntary agreements to share models for safety testing. What the order codifies is a practice that was already occurring. The preamble celebrates that Trump “unleashed tremendous technological growth” by removing Biden-era constraints — and then reproduces those same constraints as its operative provisions. Dean Ball, a former Trump AI adviser, wrote that the policy “is considerably more intrusive” than Biden’s original order. David Sacks, who reportedly called the president to kill a stronger version of the order just two weeks earlier, declared the watered-down result a “game changer.” Both former advisers are correct that the order is meaningful. They disagree about whether that is good. The room where it happens produces confusion as reliably as it produces law.

The order’s internal contradiction is worth naming precisely. It tasks the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency — CISA — with implementation. CISA is the federal agency built to protect the nation against hackers. It is also one of the agencies the Trump administration has systematically gutted. The governance mechanism created by the order depends for its execution on an institution the order’s own administration has been dismantling. This is the Asimov Gap expressed as organisational design: the structure built to close the gap is itself subject to the forces that created it.

The framing throughout is national security, not consumer protection, democratic accountability, or the rights of workers whose jobs are being automated. Jen Easterly, the former director of CISA, writing in the New York Times on June 4, offered the most precise articulation of what the national security framing can and cannot do. The executive order is a necessary first step, she argued — the Mythos disclosure confirmed that AI models with offensive cybercapabilities are dual-use technologies in the same sense as pathogens, certain chemicals, and nuclear materials, and deserve analogous governance. “A powerful model released without meaningful access controls is not an American asset; it is a global one.” But a voluntary framework, she was careful to note, “cannot guarantee that government technical experts will be able to evaluate the most consequential capabilities of frontier models before those models are released.” The principle is right. The mechanism is insufficient. It is time, she concluded, for Congress to act — pointing to a bipartisan bill from Representatives Jay Obernolte of California and Lori Trahan of Massachusetts expected that week as a promising sign that the harder work was underway.

Easterly’s piece is notable for one detail she did not need to mention but did: CISA, the agency she formerly led and the agency tasked with implementing the executive order, has been systematically weakened by the same administration that signed it. She did not dwell on this. She did not need to.

What the Military Legislation Says

The Gillibrand and Slotkin bills are a different matter. They emerged from a different set of pressures, and they reflect a different set of concerns.

The Slotkin bill would codify, in law, exactly the protections that Anthropic was seeking when it refused to sign the Pentagon’s “any lawful use” standard and was designated a supply-chain risk to national security. Bans on using AI for domestic surveillance. Bans on commanding autonomous drones. Bans on launching nuclear weapons. These are the red lines Anthropic drew. They are the red lines the Pentagon rejected. They are now, if Slotkin’s bill passes, the law.

Senator Kirsten Gillibrand speaking during a Senate hearing. Her June 2026 bill aims to keep humans — not algorithms — in control of life-and-death military decisions.

The Gillibrand bill goes further. For most autonomous weapons deployments in combat — with exceptions for cybersecurity, missile interceptors, and base defenses — the Defense Department would be required to obtain congressional approval. Not the secretary’s approval. Congress.

“Lethal decisions require a conscience, not just an algorithm,” Gillibrand said, “and a machine shouldn’t make those types of decisions. AI can be a critical tool in decision making, but it really lacks the important elements of humanity. It doesn’t have the capacity for love, loss or grief, and those types of human impacts are necessary to make hard decisions about targeting.”

This is, word for word, the argument this series has been making since Part 1. It is also, as noted in Part 41, what Pope Leo XIV wrote in Magnifica Humanitas. And it is what Vice President Vance said at the Air Force Academy — that “decisions over life and death must be made by humans and not machines.” An argument that was, eighteen months ago, primarily made by AI safety researchers and Canadian journalists writing from the outside is now being made simultaneously by a Democratic senator, a Republican vice president, and the leader of 1.4 billion Catholics.

The moral consensus has arrived. The legal framework is beginning to follow.

There is at least some bipartisan support for the principle. Democratic staffers noted that Vance had “voiced broad support for the basic principles the measures are trying to codify.” Emil Michael, the Pentagon’s top technology officer, told a Senate hearing in May that the autonomous weapons policy — drafted in 2012 and updated three years ago — needed to be updated given advances in adversary capabilities and lessons from the war against Iran. Senator Joni Ernst noted the Pentagon had proposed spending $55 billion on its autonomous weapons development office. The pressure is coming from multiple directions simultaneously.

The legislation has not passed. The NDAA process is long and subject to amendment, conference committee, and presidential signature. The Gillibrand bill faces steeper odds as a standalone measure. But the bills exist. They have been introduced. The argument has moved from moral philosophy to committee markup.

The Sovereign Wealth Argument

Senator Bernie Sanders proposes the American AI Sovereign Wealth Fund Act — giving the public a 50% stake in major AI companies

On June 1, 2026, Senator Bernie Sanders published an op-ed in the New York Times introducing what he calls the American AI Sovereign Wealth Fund Act. The legislation would give the public a direct ownership stake in the largest AI companies — a 50 percent stock position, acquired through a one-time transfer rather than a cash payment, with the federal government holding voting shares and equal board representation at each company’s board.

Sanders’s argument begins with a fact that Sam Altman, the CEO of OpenAI, supplied him. AI models, Altman has said, were trained on our “collective experience, knowledge” and “learnings of humanity.” Sanders takes Altman at his word: if AI is built on collective human knowledge — our books, songs, artwork, journalism, code, scientific research, and conversations spanning generations — then the wealth it generates must benefit that collective. The CEO of the world’s most valuable AI company has, in Sanders’s formulation, made the senator’s case for him.

The argument is not original to Sanders, and he says so. What is striking is the company it keeps. OpenAI has proposed creating “a public wealth fund that provides every citizen — including those not invested in financial markets — with a stake in AI-driven economic growth.” Anthropic has proposed “national sovereign wealth funds with stakes in AI.” Elon Musk has written that “Universal HIGH INCOME via checks issued by the Federal government is the best way to deal with unemployment caused by AI.” Even President Trump has proposed establishing an American sovereign wealth fund by executive order.

The industry that spent $47.8 million lobbying Washington in 2025 is publicly conceding, across the political spectrum, that the public should own a piece of what AI produces. The precedents are not radical. Norway’s sovereign wealth fund — built from oil revenues, now worth more than $2 trillion — has for decades ensured that the extraction of a national resource benefits the nation rather than the extractors. Alaska’s Permanent Fund has paid annual dividends directly to residents for fifty years. American public pension funds already hold hundreds of billions in corporate stock. The mechanism Sanders proposes has been tried, in different forms, in different places, and it works.

Whether the industry will endorse the specific mechanism — a 50 percent equity stake with voting shares and board representation — is a different question, and the answer is almost certainly no. The distance between the industry’s public endorsement of the principle and its private resistance to the mechanism is the Asimov Gap applied to economic governance. The companies are in the room where the laws get written. The public is not. And the laws that emerge from that room tend to reflect the gap between what the industry will say in public and what it will accept in private.

What the Mathematicians Understood

Mathematicians worldwide sign the Leiden Declaration on Artificial Intelligence and Mathematics, June 2, 2026 — pushing back against AI’s colonization of mathematical research.

On June 2, 2026 — the same day as the executive order and the Gillibrand bill — sixteen mathematicians published the Leiden Declaration on Artificial Intelligence and Mathematics. It is worth examining alongside the legislation, because it represents a different kind of democratic response: a professional community asserting collective control over how its work is used.

The declaration addresses concerns that are, on the surface, specific to mathematics. AI companies are using mathematical research as training data without consent or compensation. They are prioritising problems that are amenable to AI methods rather than problems that are mathematically significant. They are generating plausible-seeming proofs that are wrong in ways that are difficult for mathematicians to detect — and journal editors are already overwhelmed by the flood. The OpenAI announcement that its model had disproved an 80-year-old Erdős conjecture arrived on the same day the company filed for its IPO. The timing was not coincidental.

But the declaration is not really about mathematics. It is about what happens when a commercial industry with concentrated resources and proprietary methods colonises a gift economy built on openness, transparency, and credit.

Michael Harris of Columbia put it directly: the purpose of the declaration is “to recover control of the narrative about the values and the goals of mathematics from the AI industry.” The industry wants to promote the belief that AI is transforming mathematics. The declaration says: let the mathematical community have the last word on what is, and what is not, exciting.

Rodrigo Ochigame of Leiden went further: the companies have repeatedly stated that they are using mathematical research as training data not because they want to advance mathematical knowledge, but because they hope it will improve the models’ reasoning abilities more generally. “Without their consent,” he said, “their published work is being used as strategic training data for the development of general-purpose AI. The resulting models are being commercialized for many purposes, including military applications, that raise grave ethical concerns. Most mathematicians never imagined, much less consented, that their work would be used for such purposes.”

This is the copyright argument from Part 33, stated by the people whose work is being taken. It is also something broader. The Leiden Declaration is the refusal of a professional community to let a commercial industry define the terms of its own colonisation. It is the assertion of democratic values — openness, transparency, credit, consent, independent verification — against commercial logic that has no use for any of them.

The declaration is being endorsed by the International Mathematical Union. It will be on the program at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Philadelphia in July. It is open for signing by individuals and organisations worldwide.

It will not stop OpenAI from using mathematical research as training data. It has no enforcement mechanism. It is, like the papal encyclical examined in Part 41, a moral argument issued by an authority with no legislative power.

And it may, like Rerum Novarum, matter more than it can enforce.

The Pattern

Three legislative actions and one professional declaration on a single day. The coincidence is not evidence of coordination. It is evidence of pressure reaching a threshold simultaneously across multiple systems.

The pattern, across all four responses, is the same. Communities that have been on the receiving end of AI’s advance — soldiers who will fight wars decided by algorithms, workers whose labour trained the models that replaced them, mathematicians whose research was taken without consent, citizens who will vote in elections shaped by ungoverned oracles — are beginning to assert the right to have a say in how the technology that affects them is governed.

The forms of assertion differ. Legislation. Executive orders. Academic declarations. Trade union platforms. Town council meetings. Papal encyclicals. What they share is the insistence that AI governance is not a technical question to be decided by the people building the technology. It is a democratic question to be decided by the people living with its consequences.

Asimov understood this. His Three Laws were not written by the robots. They were written by the humans — specifically, by a roboticist named Susan Calvin who understood, decades before anyone else in Asimov’s fictional universe, that the machines would shape human civilisation in ways that required rules written by humans, in advance, before the consequences arrived.

The Three Laws came first. The technology came second.

In the actual universe, the sequence has been inverted. The technology came first — at speed, at scale, without the laws. The laws are beginning to come now, three and a half years after ChatGPT launched, eighteen months after the governance crisis became undeniable, forty-two parts into a series that has been asking when they would arrive.

They are arriving imperfect, incomplete, and shaped in significant measure by the industry they are supposed to govern. The executive order reflects the concerns of the people who were in the room. The military legislation reflects the moral consensus that has been building for two years. The sovereign wealth proposal reflects the public’s claim on the value that AI is extracting from collective human knowledge. The Leiden Declaration reflects the refusal of one professional community to let its gift economy be consumed without resistance.

None of this is the Three Laws. None of it closes the Asimov Gap. But the laws are beginning to come.

The question the series will continue to ask is whether they are arriving in time.
