In late August 2026, during one of Karoline Leavitt’s last meetings with the president before she stepped down as press secretary on August 27, Donald Trump gave her a parting assignment. Move the reporters out of the James S. Brady Press Briefing Room. Get them out of the building. It would be, he told her, her “last legacy.”

That detail comes from Maggie Haberman and Jonathan Swan, writing in the New York Times on October 20, 2026, and it is the sort of phrase that makes a reader stop. Most departing officials hope to be remembered for something they built. Mr. Trump was offering Ms. Leavitt the chance to be remembered for something she emptied.

She did not do it. According to the Times, the president had put the same request to her several times over several months, and it never moved. But the idea did not leave with her. It has a new champion, a foam-board rendering, and a historical alibi that comes with its own chlorine.

The Pool Beneath the Floor

The alibi is Franklin Roosevelt. In 1933 an indoor swimming pool was installed in the West Terrace, between the West Wing and the Residence, so that a president paralysed by polio could exercise his legs in warm water. It was paid for not by the government but by public subscription, a campaign organised by the New York Daily News and funded by its readers. A newspaper, in other words, gave the president his pool.

Richard Nixon later had the pool floored over, and on top of it the White House built the press quarters that reporters have worked from ever since. The old basin was never removed. It is still down there beneath the seats, a tiled cavity full of cables, which may be the most honest piece of architecture in Washington: the free press, literally standing on top of a buried presidential indulgence.

Mr. Trump would like to dig it back up. He has told aides he would prefer to restore the Roosevelt pool. On September 27, 2026, he posted a pair of images on Truth Social: the briefing room as it is, captioned “Fake News,” and the same room reimagined as a gilded swimming pool, captioned “FDR Pool.”

A Canadian reader may be forgiven for noting that the White House already has a swimming pool. Gerald Ford had an outdoor one built on the South Grounds in 1975. The demand for a second pool is not a demand for a pool. It is a demand for an empty room where the reporters used to be, with water poured in so that nobody can put them back.

Forty-Nine Seats and a Hallway

It is worth being precise about what eviction would take away, because the administration’s defenders will say, as Reince Priebus said in January 2017, that moving briefings across the street to the Eisenhower Executive Office Building is a matter of logistics and room size. The briefing room has roughly 49 seats. Surely a bigger hall would serve everyone better.

But the value of the briefing room was never its size. It is its distance from the Oval Office, which is a few dozen steps. The press office sits a few feet from the podium. Reporters in the West Wing see who comes and goes. They can be summoned into the Oval Office in minutes when the president wants an audience, and they can shout a question at him on the way to Marine One when he does not. They notice which cabinet secretary walked in looking grim and which foreign envoy was hurried out a side door. Proximity is not a perk. It is the method.

That is precisely why it has always irritated the people around Mr. Trump who think of the press as an enemy formation. Stephen Bannon told the Times this month that he considered it a national security threat to have reporters surrounding the Oval Office, and that from his first days in 2017 he had a plan to move them next to Blair House, or at least to the Eisenhower building. His plan, he said, landed with a thud among the other senior advisers.

The phrase “national security threat” should sound familiar to readers of this series. In Part 17 we watched the Justice Department, on September 23, 2026, discover a national security rationale for the CNN, MS NOW and Politico ban five days after the president announced it without one. Mr. Bannon got there nine years earlier. The justification has always been the same; it has simply been waiting for a judge it might work on.

Why It Failed Four Times

The eviction idea has a longer record than most of Mr. Trump’s policies. Per the Times, it has come up at least four times:

  • The 2016 transition. Mr. Bannon proposed a townhouse near Lafayette Square, then the Eisenhower building as a fallback. Senior officials rejected it.

  • January 2017. Mr. Priebus publicly floated moving briefings to the Eisenhower building, then dropped it as too disruptive.

  • Summer 2017. With the West Wing torn up for renovation, someone wondered why the reporters should be let back in at all. Sean Spicer and Sarah Huckabee Sanders raised the logistical problems and it died.

  • Late 2024. During the second transition, most advisers again argued it was better to keep the media close.

The president himself explained the first retreat with unusual candour. “The press went crazy, so I said, ‘Let’s not move it,’” he told Fox & Friends in January 2017, as Newsweek reported at the time.

Read the four failures together and a pattern emerges. The idea was never stopped by principle. It was stopped by people: senior aides who calculated that Mr. Trump liked having cameras within shouting distance, that the inconvenience outweighed the satisfaction, and that if they followed his order and his coverage then got worse, he would blame them for the consequences of his own idea. That last observation, which the Times attributes to his more seasoned advisers in 2024, is the most revealing sentence in the whole saga. The press stayed in the building because the staff feared the president’s temper more than they feared the reporters.

Nearly every other brake in this story was built the same way. Remove the people, and the brake goes with them.

The Staff Has Changed

That is what is different now. Mr. Trump’s inner circle has shrunk. The aides who argued that keeping reporters close served him better are fewer. In their place, the Times reports, are advisers of growing influence who encourage him to punish journalists.

One of them is Bill Pulte, the director of the Federal Housing Finance Agency, a man with no formal role in White House operations and a remarkable amount of weekend access. Mr. Pulte reportedly produced the foam-board rendering of the briefing room restored as an in-ground pool. Senior officials told the Times he also brings foam boards to the golf course identifying potential targets for revenge prosecutions. Readers of The Enemies List will recognise the craft. It is apparently possible, in this administration, to make policy at a scale of roughly 20 by 30 inches.

Another is Boris Epshteyn, the president’s longtime lawyer, who pushed several of his lawsuits against news organisations and whose influence, per the Times, has grown in recent months.

And then there is the feed. In a weekend report around September 19 and 20, 2026, the Wall Street Journal described how Natalie Harp, the aide who controls much of what reaches the president’s desk, showed him a clip of MS NOW commentators attacking his administration shortly before he banned three outlets from the White House. As TNND summarised the Journal’s account, that clip brought his irritation with the network to a peak. NewsNation added that a Politico graphic was among the material believed to have angered him, which explains why a print outlet ended up swept into a ban otherwise aimed at television.

White House officials told the Times that Ms. Harp did not intend to prompt the ban, and that the president acted after months of his own anger. Both things can be true. The point is not her intent. The point is that the decision to expel 70-plus journalists from the seat of government turned, by the White House’s own account, on what one aide chose to put in front of one man on one afternoon. That is information control in its purest form: not the control of what the public sees, but of what the president sees, and therefore of whom he decides the public may hear from.

The Courts Say No; the Ratchet Says Wait

There is good news in this instalment, and it should be stated plainly. The rule of law has, so far, held.

Part 18 ended with Judge Timothy J. Kelly’s 14-day temporary restraining order of September 24, 2026, granted on due-process grounds. On October 13, 2026, Judge Kelly, a Trump appointee, went further. He issued a preliminary injunction keeping CNN, MS NOW and Politico inside the White House complex while their lawsuit proceeds. He found that the outlets were likely to succeed not only on their due-process claim but on both of their First Amendment claims, as TheWrap reported. In the ruling, he reportedly cited the president’s own remarks to Time magazine singling out CNN and MS NOW for their coverage. It is difficult to argue a ban is content-neutral when the man who imposed it has explained, on the record, which content he disliked. The administration is appealing.

But notice what a credentials case can and cannot do. Judge Kelly can order that a reporter’s hard pass be honoured. He cannot order that the room the pass opens continue to exist. An injunction protects access to the press quarters. It does not prevent the president from turning the press quarters into a swimming pool and inviting every outlet, equally and without viewpoint discrimination, to cover him from across the street.

That is the genius of the pool, if genius is the word. Revoking three outlets’ credentials is a First Amendment violation. Relocating all of them is a renovation.

And it is a renovation that would be very hard to reverse. The Times makes the point in a single sentence, and it deserves more than that. Readers of The Pendulum and the Ratchet will recognise the mechanism: some of what Mr. Trump does swings back when power changes hands, and some of it clicks into place and stays. A future president could restore three revoked press passes on her first afternoon in office. Who will tear out a working swimming pool, with its new marble and its new historical plaque about Franklin Roosevelt, in order to let reporters back within earshot? Every president since Nixon has found the press corps irritating. Not one of them would have built the room for it from scratch. They merely inherited it, and declined to evict it. Once the water is in, the default reverses.

No Backlash, No Brake

The last time a White House seriously considered this was 1993, when Hillary Clinton reportedly wanted the press quarters converted back into a pool, according to George Stephanopoulos’s memoir. When word leaked, the blowback killed it quickly.

The Times observes that Mr. Trump faces no comparable pressure. He has received relatively little backlash for his moves against the media, even from Democrats. In 2017 he admitted the press “went crazy” and he backed down. In 2026 the reaction has been far more muted, and the administration has learned to treat the press’s attempts to defend itself as an offence in their own right.

Consider what happened to the one act of collective resistance this story has produced. In Part 16 we followed the five networks of the television pool (ABC, CBS, CNN, NBC and Fox News) as they suspended pooled coverage from September 21, 2026, rather than normalise CNN’s exclusion. On Friday, October 9, 2026, the Justice Department sent all five networks letters demanding their communications about the pool. The next day a department spokeswoman, Emily Covington, said the networks had “proudly boycotted covering White House events” and that group boycotts among competitors can violate the Sherman Act, as the Associated Press reported.

It is worth sitting with the inversion, because it is the purest specimen of Ministry of Truth logic this series has yet recorded. The White House excludes a news organisation. The other news organisations decline to cover events from which their colleague is barred. The government then investigates the second group, not the first, on the theory that refusing to participate in the exclusion is the anticompetitive act. The press pool exists, of course, precisely because competing networks agreed decades ago to cooperate in covering the president. If that cooperation is a conspiracy when it says no, it was presumably a conspiracy all along when it said yes.

Add the grand-jury subpoenas served at the homes of Times reporters by FBI agents this summer, after their reporting on presidential security during the July 2026 NATO summit in Turkey and later withdrawn after a judge admonished federal lawyers. Add the Pentagon pledge that drove nearly every major outlet, Fox News included, out of its workspace in the building, which this series has tracked since Part 1. Add the closing of “Upper Press” to reporters without appointments on October 31, 2025. The eviction of the briefing room is not an isolated whim. It is the next room in a house that has been closing doors one at a time for a year.

The Room as Witness

Orwell’s Ministry of Truth did not need to ban newspapers. It simply rewrote them, and it worked in a building that the public could see from everywhere in London and enter from nowhere. The architecture was the argument: power that could watch, and could not be watched back.

The Brady Briefing Room is the opposite argument, made in plaster and folding seats. Its premise is that the people who report on the president should be close enough to be inconvenient. They should hear the shouting through the wall and see the visitor who wasn’t on the schedule. They should be summoned on a whim and stay to ask the question the whim didn’t anticipate. Since Nixon, the room has stood as a quiet admission by every occupant of the building that the presidency is watched as a condition of being held.

Steven Cheung, the White House communications director, told the Times that “all options remain on the table.” It is a curious phrase for a table that may soon be underwater.

Mr. Trump has called the room “Fake News.” He has drawn it full of gold-trimmed water. He has asked one press secretary to make its emptying her legacy, and he will presumably ask the next. What has stopped him before was never the Constitution, which a federal judge has now twice enforced on the narrower question of credentials. It was a handful of staff who judged the eviction more trouble than it was worth. Those staff are fewer each month.

A newspaper’s readers once paid for a president’s pool. It would be a fitting irony, and a very American one, if their successors were drained out of the building to make room for its return.
