There is a particular species of joy, thin and cold as a Seattle drizzle, that comes from watching a man get exactly what he asked for.

Folarin Balogun received a red card in the match against Bosnia and Herzegovina. This is, in the sport of soccer, meant to be the end of the discussion. A red card is not a suggestion. It is not a talking point. It is a small rectangle of plastic that says, in the universal language of consequence, you are done for the day, and also the next one. Article of the FIFA disciplinary code duly applied. Case closed. Next match.

Except that this is 2026, and nothing stays closed anymore, least of all a case that a sitting President of the United States has decided to open with his own hands. Donald Trump called FIFA President Gianni Infantino. He did not, by his own telling, instruct Infantino to do anything in particular — he merely wanted to “understand” the red card, this being a man who has famously never needed to understand a rule to feel qualified to overturn it. “I didn’t think it was a foul,” the President explained, with the quiet authority of a man who also didn’t think Article II had a limit on it. “I didn’t know what the hell a red card was,” he added, which may be the single most honest sentence uttered in the Oval Office all year.

By Sunday, FIFA’s Disciplinary Committee had found its way to Article 27 — a probationary clause previously deployed, tellingly, for Cristiano Ronaldo, because apparently the rules bend fastest around men who are used to bending them. The suspension was suspended. The red card, we were assured, remained red in spirit, if not in consequence — rather like a criminal conviction that leaves you free to run for President twice. Trump took his victory lap on Truth Social, thanking FIFA “for doing what was right, and reversing a great injustice.” One imagines the phrase felt familiar in his mouth. He has used it before, about other institutions, other rulings, other inconvenient facts.

The Belgians, who play a version of this sport that still assumes the red card means something, were “astonished.” UEFA said FIFA had “crossed a red line,” which is either an accidental pun or the driest institutional joke of the tournament. Sepp Blatter — Sepp Blatter, a man who left FIFA under an ethics cloud so thick it required its own weather system — popped up on social media to ask, with what I can only assume was a straight face, “Quo vadis, FIFA?” When Sepp Blatter is your integrity check, you have already lost the argument.

Belgium’s coach, Rudi Garcia, needed only one line to skewer the whole affair, joking through a translator that FIFA seemed to think July 5 was April Fools’ Day in Europe. Senator Tom Cotton, who cheerfully admitted he isn’t much of a soccer fan, praised the President for urging FIFA to “do the right thing” anyway — a category of endorsement that should worry anyone who has ever needed an institution to hold the line on a rule it clearly understood better than the men leaning on it. Balogun himself marked his reprieve by posting a photo of himself scored to Michael Jackson’s Bad, which, four goals against and one round earlier than hoped, reads less like triumph and more like foreshadowing nobody asked him to write.

And then there was the slide. Somewhere in FIFA’s pre-match briefing materials, a single slide went missing before it reached the Belgian delegation — the one explaining automatic suspensions for red cards. Nobody remembers deleting it. Nobody ever does. It is the great recurring miracle of this administration that documents simply misplace themselves, slides simply vanish, evidence simply arrives already digested into the shape someone needed it to be. Andrew Giuliani was reportedly on the phone too, because if there is one name that reliably attaches itself to a controversial reversal of established process, it is Giuliani, and apparently the son inherited the father’s gift for turning a legal document into modeling clay.

So: Balogun played. And Belgium won, 4–1 — not a stolen point, not a nervy penalty shootout, but a plain and thorough beating.

I will not pretend to be surprised, watching from north of the border as sixty-eight thousand people at Lumen Field got a very different outcome than the one they came for. Karma is not, whatever the greeting cards suggest, a system of punishment. It is simpler and colder than that — it is just consequence, delayed long enough that people convince themselves they’ve outrun it, and then arriving anyway, usually in a stadium, usually on television, usually at the worst possible moment for public relations. Trump got his player back on the pitch. Belgium led 2–1 at the half and did not so much hold on as pull away, finishing it 4–1. He did not get to dictate the score. That part of the game, mercifully, remains beyond even his reach — for now, and only because nobody has yet found the slide that controls it.

There is a lesson in here about institutions, the ones that are supposed to be the last ones standing when politics comes calling — courts, central banks, election boards, and yes, apparently, the disciplinary committee of an organization whose last president is currently banned from his own sport. The lesson is not a comforting one. It is that these institutions do not fail all at once, with a bang. They fail one phone call at a time, one missing slide at a time, one Truth Social victory lap at a time, until the day arrives — and it always arrives — when the thing that was fixed unfixes itself in front of everyone, and the only ones left standing to explain it are the men who insisted, right up until the final whistle, that they hadn’t really asked for anything at all.

Belgium moves on. Seattle goes quiet. And somewhere in the Oval Office tonight, one imagines a rather different kind of phone call being avoided altogether.
