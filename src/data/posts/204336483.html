The U.S. Supreme Court building at twilight — where procedural speed and substantive review have produced two very different records for the same administration.

The term ended yesterday. Sixteen months and change since Inauguration Day, and the docket finally closes on a presidency that has put more emergency applications in front of nine justices, faster, than any administration in memory. So let’s do what nobody in the cable-news ecosystem has time to do properly: lay out the entire scorecard, not just this week’s headlines, and see what it actually says.

I want to be upfront about the trap here. It is tempting to total up the column and declare a winner, the way you’d read a hockey standings page. The Court doesn’t work that way, and pretending it does flatters nobody. What the full record actually shows is more interesting than a win-loss tally — it shows two completely different Supreme Courts operating depending on which door a case walks through, and a pattern, visible only once you lay the whole eighteen months side by side, of early procedural wins quietly curdling into substantive losses once a case has time to mature.

Door One: the shadow docket, where Trump usually wins, but not always

Since January 20, 2025, the Brennan Center’s shadow docket tracker has counted roughly two dozen emergency applications tied to administration policy that have reached the Court’s so-called shadow docket — the fast lane, used for stays and injunctions pending full review, frequently decided with no oral argument and sometimes no written reasoning at all. By the Brennan Center’s count, the administration has won at least partially in the large majority of them. As Erwin Chemerinsky wrote for SCOTUSblog, a similar count for 2025 alone landed on 20 wins out of 24, with Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson dissenting in literally every one of those cases. Only a single one of the twenty-four was unanimous — a narrow ruling in Margolin v. National Association of Immigration Judges that sent a dispute over immigration judges’ speech rights back down for further fact-finding rather than granting the administration’s request outright. It was, by Chemerinsky’s count, likely the only shadow-docket case all year in which Justices Thomas and Alito voted against the government.

Emergency applications and shadow-docket orders — the fast lane where the administration has secured most of its early procedural wins.

This is the door through which Trump got Gwynne Wilcox and Cathy Harris removed from the NLRB and MSPB without cause back in May 2025, before the underlying legal question — can the President fire independent agency officials at will — had been briefed or argued on the merits at all. The Wilcox fight alone took the long way around: fired in January 2025, reinstated by a district court that called the removal “unlawful” and “void,” reversed by a D.C. Circuit panel, reinstated again when the full D.C. Circuit vacated that panel’s stay en banc, and finally stayed by the Supreme Court in May on the strength of a two-page order finding only that the government was “likely” to show the NLRB and MSPB wield “considerable executive power” — without the Court actually deciding whether that’s true. It’s the same door through which he revived asylum “metering” at the southern border and ended Temporary Protected Status for hundreds of thousands of Haitians and Syrians.

But the shadow docket is not the clean sweep it’s sometimes described as, and the National Guard fights prove it. In a three-page unsigned order on December 23, 2025, the Court left in place a Chicago judge’s block on deploying federalized National Guard troops — including units pulled in from Texas — to the city, finding flatly that the government had “failed to identify a source of authority that would allow the military to execute the laws in Illinois.” Three justices dissented, with Alito writing a 16-page opinion joined by Thomas arguing the majority had overstepped by addressing the underlying legal theory rather than the narrower stay question actually presented. It was, notably, the administration’s second loss before the Court in just four days. As NPR reported, the ruling didn’t formally bind deployments elsewhere, but it gutted the legal theory — that protests near ICE facilities amount to a “rebellion” justifying military activation — that the administration had been leaning on simultaneously in Oregon, where a federal judge had already found no lawful basis for deploying troops to Portland, and in other cities where similar fights were underway. Even on the shadow docket, in other words, there is at least one entire category — domestic military deployment over a governor’s objection — where Trump has not been getting what he asks for.

Door Two: the merits docket, where the story flips harder

President Trump responding to Supreme Court decisions on tariffs and other executive actions.

Here’s the thing the shadow-docket framing misses if you stop reading there: when cases actually get full briefing, full argument, and a final written opinion — the cases decided on the merits rather than waved through on an emergency footing — Trump’s record this term is considerably worse, and getting worse as the term winds down.

Tariffs (February 20, 2026): Learning Resources v. Trump, consolidated with Trump v. V.O.S. Selections. This was a 6-3 loss, with Chief Justice Roberts in the majority alongside Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Barrett, and Jackson, holding that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act simply does not give the President authority to impose blanket tariffs on every trading partner he names — the power to tax imports, the Court found, is squarely a congressional one that IEEPA’s vague reference to “regulating” importation doesn’t come close to delegating away. This is not a small case. The Tax Foundation estimates IEEPA tariffs had already raised something north of $160 billion before the ruling, with another $1.4 trillion projected through 2035 had they stood. Trump pivoted within hours, issuing an executive order invoking Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 to impose a 10% global tariff instead — a much smaller hammer, capped by statute at 15% and 150 days at a stretch before Congress has to weigh back in. The refund question for everything already collected was left to the Court of International Trade, where it’s still being sorted out months later. This was the first case all term to test the substance, not just the procedure, of a signature Trump policy. He lost it outright, with three of his own appointees — Gorsuch, Barrett, and Roberts himself — going further than the bare statutory holding to invoke the “major questions doctrine,” the same principle that’s been used against expansive agency power for a decade now, this time pointed back at the presidency that benefited from it in other contexts.

The Federal Reserve and the broader removal-power fight (2026): Trump v. Slaughter and Trump v. Cook, decided close together, tell you the Court is drawing a real distinction rather than handing the President a blank check. Slaughter — argued in December 2025 after the Court agreed to hear it alongside the unresolved Wilcox dispute — formally overturned the 90-year-old Humphrey’s Executor precedent, the 1935 case that let Congress insulate independent, multi-member regulatory bodies from at-will presidential removal. The Court held that agencies like the FTC, and by direct extension the NLRB and MSPB, now exercise enough “executive power” that the President can fire their leadership without cause, full stop. It is genuinely the largest single expansion of presidential authority to come out of this entire term, and it retroactively validates Wilcox’s firing as constitutional rather than merely stayed. But when the identical removal-power logic was pointed at the Federal Reserve specifically, in Cook’s case, the Court balked. Cook keeps her seat. The majority opinion, with concurrences from both Kavanaugh and Jackson and dissents from Thomas, Alito, and Barrett on the question of how far to go, carved the Fed out as a unique historical institution whose independence predates and sits outside the ordinary executive-agency framework — the same theoretical distinction Roberts had already drawn years earlier in Seila Law and reaffirmed in the Wilcox stay itself. Cook’s attorney, Norm Eisen, told CNN it was a win for the independence of the central bank and, by extension, the broader economy; it’s hard to argue with the practical stakes of that framing even if you think the doctrinal line the Court drew to get there is somewhat arbitrary.

E. Jean Carroll (June 29, 2026): Cert denied, no explanation, no dissents noted. The $5 million verdict finding Trump liable for sexual abuse and defamation, arising from her 2022 lawsuit under New York’s Adult Survivors Act, stands permanently. A second, larger appeal — over the separate $83.3 million defamation judgment from her original 2019 lawsuit, which went to trial second but was filed first — is still working its way toward the Court, with Trump’s lawyers indicating in a June court filing that they intend to petition within the month. Worth remembering: a New York jury never found that Trump raped Carroll, as she originally alleged; it found him liable for sexual abuse and battery under a different, broader standard, and for defamation when he called her claim a hoax. Both distinctions survive the Supreme Court’s silence this week exactly as they were.

Birthright citizenship — the whole arc, which is the cleanest illustration of the two-door problem there is. In June 2025, in Trump v. CASA, the Court handed Trump a procedural win on the shadow docket: by a 6-3 vote in an opinion by Justice Barrett, it didn’t touch the constitutional question of birthright citizenship at all, but it gutted the practice of nationwide, or “universal,” injunctions, which had been blocking his executive order everywhere at once based on rulings from just three district courts. The decision came after the Congressional Research Service noted that district courts had issued roughly 25 such universal injunctions in just the first hundred days of the second Trump term — a volume the administration’s lawyers argued was swamping the Court’s emergency docket and needed to stop. Conservative commentary treated CASA as a major win at the time, and on its own narrow terms it was. It bought the administration roughly a year of breathing room, during which plaintiffs scrambled to convert individual suits into certified class actions just to keep the order blocked nationwide by other means — which a Maryland district court did within months, certifying a class covering every affected baby born in the country.

Then, today, on the actual merits, in Trump v. Barbara, the same Court ruled 6-3 that the executive order itself is flatly unconstitutional. Roberts, writing for the majority, traced the citizenship clause back through the 1898 Wong Kim Ark precedent the administration had been trying to write around for a year and a half, and described the right at stake, in a phrase that’s already being quoted everywhere, as the right to have rights. Three of the Court’s own conservatives — Thomas, joined by Gorsuch, and separately Alito — dissented from that result, with Kavanaugh concurring only in the judgment and dissenting in part. Barrett — the same justice who wrote the CASA opinion limiting nationwide injunctions a year earlier — was the only one of the Court’s more conservative members willing to join Roberts’s opinion on the merits in full, alongside Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson. The order is dead now, permanently, not just procedurally, and as the Texas Tribune reported, Trump’s own reaction — calling the result “too bad for our country” and pledging support for legislation to override it — concedes as much.

The nine justices whose votes have split differently depending on whether a case arrives via emergency application or full merits briefing.

That sequence — shadow-docket win, then merits-docket loss on the identical underlying policy, a year apart, written in part by the same justice both times — is, I think, the single most useful data point in this entire essay. It tells you the procedural wins early in a case’s life do not predict the substantive outcome once a court actually has time to read the statute and the Constitution properly, and it should make everyone, on either side, more careful about declaring a shadow-docket order as the final word on anything.

Where Trump is actually still winning on the merits

To be fair to the other side of the ledger, because the evidence-based version of this essay has to include it: this was not exclusively a losing stretch, and the back half of June produced real wins alongside the birthright-citizenship loss.

  • TPS for Haiti and Syria (June 25, 2026): Courts can’t review DHS’s decision to end the designations at all, the Court held, treating the statute as committing that judgment entirely to the Secretary’s discretion. Administration win, fully briefed, no asterisk — and one that directly affects roughly 1.3 million people currently living in the U.S. under some form of Temporary Protected Status nationwide.

  • Transgender athletes (June 30, 2026): As NBC News reported, a 6-3 ruling authored by Kavanaugh upheld West Virginia’s and Idaho’s bans on transgender athletes in girls’ sports under Title IX and the Equal Protection Clause, with the majority writing that neither the Constitution nor federal law requires an overhaul of women’s and girls’ sports nationally. This wasn’t formally a Trump-party case — the named parties were the states and two transgender student-athletes — but the administration’s Justice Department backed the states throughout the litigation, and Trump claimed it immediately as a personal win on social media. It’s the latest in a string of losses for transgender rights at the Court, following last year’s ruling upholding state bans on gender-transition treatment for minors and a separate pair of decisions allowing the administration’s restrictions on transgender service in the military and on passport gender markers.

  • Campaign finance (June 30, 2026): The Court lifted the Watergate-era cap on coordinated spending between political parties and their candidates — a structural win for the kind of party-and-candidate fundraising machinery that tends to benefit a sitting president’s reelection apparatus and a national party more than an outside challenger.

  • Independent agencies, broadly (Slaughter): As above — genuinely the largest single expansion of presidential power to come out of this term, the Fed carve-out notwithstanding, and one that will likely reshape how every future president, not just this one, can reconstitute regulatory agencies on taking office.

What the scorecard actually says

The Constitution and gavel — central to the merits decisions on birthright citizenship, tariffs, and removal power.

If you tally the shadow docket alone, this reads like one of the most successful runs of executive litigation in modern history — 20-odd wins against a small handful of losses, almost all decided fast and almost all without much written reasoning to constrain the next request. If you tally only the fully argued, fully briefed merits cases from the back half of this term, the picture inverts: tariffs gone, the Fed protected, birthright citizenship dead, Carroll’s verdict permanent. The wins that remain on the merits side — TPS, trans athletes, campaign finance, the broader agency-removal power in Slaughter — are real, and substantial, but they cluster overwhelmingly in immigration enforcement and culture-war terrain rather than in the structural, money-and-power questions that define how much genuinely unilateral authority a president holds day to day.

The honest read, I think, is this: the emergency docket has functioned for a year and a half as a kind of advance on presidential power, granted on thin briefing and reversible in principle, and the merits docket is where the bill is finally starting to come due. Tariffs were the first big-ticket item to get the full treatment, and the administration lost. Birthright citizenship was the second, and the administration lost that one too — after winning the procedural skirmish that delayed the reckoning by exactly a year. Even within the National Guard fights, which never made it to a full merits ruling this term, the shadow docket itself produced a loss once a single circuit’s reasoning was specific and well-supported enough to be hard to wave away on an emergency basis.

Whether that pattern holds for whatever’s still moving through the pipeline is the actual question worth watching into the midterms. The second Carroll appeal over the $83.3 million judgment is coming. The Epstein files litigation now sitting on Judge Sullivan’s desk in Washington could eventually generate its own appellate record. The tariff refund fight at the Court of International Trade is unresolved and could produce billions of dollars in disputes over who — importers or their customers — is even entitled to get the money back. And the next round of independent-agency firings, now that Slaughter has settled the general removal-power question for everyone except the Fed, will test exactly how far that “Fed is special” carve-out extends to other institutions that might plausibly claim a similar pedigree.

The emergency docket bought Trump eighteen months of nearly uncontested wins, decided fast, on thin records, with Justice Jackson dissenting in nearly all of them and warning, in one notable opinion this term, that the Court appeared to be operating by a single consistent rule favoring the administration regardless of the underlying law. The merits docket, now that it’s finally had the time to catch up, is telling a noticeably different story — one with real wins still in it, but a string of the most structurally significant losses of his second term arriving in just the last four months. That’s not a verdict on where this ends. It’s a description of where it stands, on the day the term actually closed the book on it.
