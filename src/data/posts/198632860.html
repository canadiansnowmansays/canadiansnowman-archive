Starship on the pad at Starbase, South Texas. The most powerful rocket ever built, preparing for Flight 12 on the same day its parent company files the largest IPO in history

May 21, 2026

Ralph Kramden couldn’t make rent.

SpaceX has filed for the largest initial public offering in the history of capital markets — $75 billion at a valuation of $1.75 trillion — and later today, it will attempt to launch the most powerful rocket ever built from a brand-new launch pad in South Texas.

The same day. The S-1 and the countdown clock, running simultaneously. If you had pitched this as a screenplay, the studio would have sent it back with a note: too on the nose.

And yet.

The Machine

Starship V3 — also known as Flight 12, also known as the thing Elon Musk has been promising since last autumn — is not an incremental upgrade. According to SpaceX, nearly every component has been redesigned. The Raptor 3 engines produce roughly nine percent more thrust at sea level and seven percent more in vacuum, while weighing over a hundred kilograms less per unit. Multiply that by thirty-three engines on the Super Heavy booster and six on the upper stage, and the mass savings alone are measured in tons. The booster has traded four grid fins for three larger ones, repositioned to survive the thermal violence of hot-staging — the moment when the upper stage ignites its own engines before the two halves of the rocket have actually separated. The hot stage ring itself, previously a disposable interstage piece that fell into the Gulf of Mexico, has been permanently welded to the booster. It’s not coming back because it never leaves.

The propulsion plumbing is a clean-sheet redesign. New ignition system. Larger propellant tanks. Rerouted fluid and electrical lines that eliminate the engine shrouds and the cavernous aft close-out volume that previous versions required. The rear flap actuation system — the mechanism that controls the massive fins Starship uses to belly-flop through the atmosphere — has been simplified from two actuators per flap to a single actuator with three motors. Less weight. More redundancy. The engineering logic is elegant: fewer parts, more ways to survive.

The target payload capacity in reusable configuration is over 100 metric tons to low Earth orbit, with a design goal of 150. For context, the Space Shuttle could carry about 27.5 tons. The Saturn V that carried Armstrong to the moon could manage 140 — but it was expendable. You used it once and dropped it in the ocean. Starship is designed to land, refuel, and go again.

That’s the point. That has always been the point.

Starship’s heat shield tiles up close. One deliberately missing, others painted white — so the rocket can literally photograph and examine itself in flight

The Inspection

Flight 12 carries twenty-two Starlink mass simulators — dummy satellites matching the size and weight of the next-generation constellation hardware. That alone would be a milestone; previous flights carried eight or ten. But tucked among the twenty-two are two spacecraft with a different job. They are inspector satellites, and their assignment is to photograph Starship’s heat shield from the outside during flight.

SpaceX has deliberately removed a single heat shield tile and painted several others white to serve as imaging targets. They want to see what happens to the tiles around a gap. They want to measure the aerodynamic loads that adjacent tiles experience when one of their neighbours is missing. They want, in a phrase that sounds almost philosophical, to watch the rocket examine itself.

I find this detail irresistible. The entire Starship program has been, from its earliest explosive failures in Boca Chica, a process of iterative self-examination. Build it. Fly it. Watch what breaks. Fix it. Fly it again. The inspector satellites are simply the latest and most literal version of that methodology — and they arrive on the same day the company files its financial self-examination with the Securities and Exchange Commission.

The S-1

Let’s talk about the money, because the money is the rocket fuel that doesn’t show up in the engineering specifications.

SpaceX’s IPO filing — dropped publicly on May 20, the day before the launch window — reveals a company generating $18.67 billion in annual revenue. First quarter 2026 alone: $4.69 billion. The breakdown is instructive. Connectivity — overwhelmingly Starlink — accounts for $3.26 billion. Space operations, meaning launches, brings in $619 million. And then there is a line item that nobody outside the company fully anticipated: an AI segment generating $818 million in quarterly revenue.

That last number deserves a pause. SpaceX built a supercomputer cluster called Colossus for its AI subsidiary xAI. It is now leasing that compute capacity to outside customers. The rocket company has a sideline in artificial intelligence infrastructure. If you have been reading The Asimov Gap or The Great Energy Challenge, you already know that AI and energy and space are converging into a single industrial story. The S-1 confirms it in black and white.

The filing also reveals a first-quarter operating loss of $1.94 billion. SpaceX is not yet profitable on an operating basis. It is spending enormous sums building Starship, expanding Starlink, and scaling Colossus — all simultaneously. The IPO is designed to fund that ambition. BlackRock is reportedly negotiating to invest between five and ten billion dollars as an anchor investor, a sum that, adjusted for inflation, would have covered a meaningful fraction of the Apollo program.

The company will list on Nasdaq under the ticker SPCX. Musk retains control through a super-voting share structure — Class B shares carrying ten times the voting power of Class A. Gwynne Shotwell, the president who has actually run the company’s operations for most of its existence, is the fifth-largest holder of Class A shares. Antonio Gracias, whose Valor Equity Partners leases over $20 billion in equipment to xAI, holds 7.3 percent of the total.

The valuation — $1.75 trillion — would make SpaceX one of the ten most valuable companies on Earth on the day it begins trading. Possibly top five. For a company that has never turned an operating profit.

The market is not buying a balance sheet. It is buying a bet.

The Bet

Here is what the bet looks like from the launchpad:

NASA needs Starship to work. The Artemis program — the one we celebrated in Part I of this series when Artemis II launched on April Fool’s Day — depends on Starship as its Human Landing System. The actual crewed lunar landing, now designated Artemis IV, is targeted for 2028. Before that can happen, SpaceX must demonstrate orbital propellant transfer at scale: launching more than ten tanker missions to fill a single Starship with enough fuel to reach the lunar surface and return. V3 is the vehicle designed to make that cadence economically survivable. Without rapid, reliable reusability, the math doesn’t work. Ten expendable tanker flights would bankrupt even SpaceX.

And here is what the bet looks like from the prospectus:

Musk’s compensation package awards him 200 million additional shares if SpaceX reaches a $7.5 trillion valuation and helps establish a permanent Mars colony of one million people. Read that sentence again. It is in the filing. The largest IPO in history includes, as a contractual performance milestone, colonizing another planet.

Ralph Kramden threatened to send Alice to the moon because he couldn’t think of anywhere more absurd. Elon Musk has written Mars into his pay stub.

What We Don’t Know Yet

As I write this, the launch window opens in a few hours. The 124-metre stack — Super Heavy Booster 19 mated to Ship 39 — completed its wet dress rehearsal this morning at Starbase. The weather looks cooperable. The FAA license is confirmed. The countdown will begin at approximately 5:45 p.m. Eastern, when SpaceX starts its livestream.

What happens next is genuinely uncertain. V3 is a new vehicle. Nearly everything on it has been redesigned. The Raptor 3 engines have suffered setbacks during development — a booster explosion in November, an engine blowup in April. The launch pad itself is untested in an actual flight. SpaceX is not just testing a new rocket; it is testing a new rocket on new infrastructure with new engines using a new ignition system and a new staging ring and a new grid fin configuration and a new thermal protection layout with a deliberately missing tile.

That is either extraordinarily brave or extraordinarily reckless. In aerospace, the line between those two adjectives is drawn after the fact.

If it works — if Booster 19 separates cleanly, flies back to the Gulf, and splashes down under control; if Ship 39 deploys its twenty-two simulators, relights a Raptor in space, survives reentry with its intentionally damaged heat shield, and lands offshore — then the Artemis timeline becomes real, the IPO valuation becomes defensible, and the path to the moon runs through South Texas.

If it doesn’t work, SpaceX will study the wreckage, revise the design, and try again. They have done it eleven times before. The program’s most important engineering principle is not any particular technology. It is the willingness to fail in public, learn in public, and come back.

Ralph Kramden’s Grandchildren

There is something worth noticing about the arc from Part I of this series to today.

When Artemis II launched on April 1, the story was about NASA — a government agency, funded by taxpayers, accountable to Congress, carrying four astronauts on a trajectory that Apollo had pioneered fifty-three years earlier. The rocket was the Space Launch System, built by Boeing and Northrop Grumman and a constellation of legacy contractors. It was a public enterprise in the deepest sense.

Today’s story is about a private company filing an IPO on the day before it launches a test rocket. The crew is zero. The payload is dummy satellites. The shareholders-to-be include BlackRock, Valor Equity Partners, and Alphabet. The astronauts who will eventually ride this vehicle to the lunar surface will arrive there because investors in a Nasdaq-listed company decided the return on investment justified the risk.

This is not a complaint. It is an observation. The journey to the moon has always been shaped by the institutions that fund it. In 1969, it was Cold War competition and a presidential promise made in the shadow of Sputnik. In 2026, it is an S-1 filing and a super-voting share structure. The destination is the same. The propulsion — financial, political, institutional — is different.

Ralph Kramden was a bus driver. He worked for the Gotham Bus Company. He couldn’t afford a television set. His dream of sending Alice to the moon was a dream about escape — from the apartment, from the job, from the smallness of his life.

His grandchildren work in venture capital. They are not dreaming about the moon. They are pricing it.

Tonight, if the weather and the engineering and the countdown all cooperate, the most powerful rocket ever built will lift off from a patch of South Texas coastline. Two tiny satellites will photograph its belly in flight. A $1.75 trillion company will hold its breath.

To the moon, Alice.
