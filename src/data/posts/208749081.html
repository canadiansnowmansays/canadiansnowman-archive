Every account of the Silk Road eventually has to deal with the Taklamakan, and every account handles it the same way, because there was only one way to handle it: you went around. The desert occupies most of the Tarim Basin, a vast depression ringed by some of the highest mountains on earth — the Tian Shan to the north, the Kunlun and the Karakoram to the south, the Pamirs to the west — and it is genuinely, comprehensively lethal, a shifting sand sea with essentially no reliable water across its interior. Caravans took the northern rim or the southern rim, following the chains of oasis towns fed by meltwater streams running down off the mountains, and after several weeks of doing so, both routes converged again at the basin’s western edge, at Kashgar.

This is the entirety of Kashgar’s structural importance and it is more than sufficient. A traveller coming east out of Central Asia over the Pamirs — a crossing that was itself brutal, running through high passes at altitudes that killed pack animals and men with regularity — arrived at Kashgar, rested, resupplied, and chose a rim. A traveller coming west out of China, having spent weeks on one rim or the other, arrived at Kashgar and prepared to attempt the mountains. Neither direction offered an alternative. There were no bypasses, no competing junctions, no way to make the trip without passing through. For roughly two thousand years, Kashgar was the single unavoidable node where the Chinese world, the Central Asian world, the Persian and later Islamic world, and the Indian world all made contact, and it was — this is the part that gives the town its particular character — profoundly peripheral to every one of them.

That combination is unusual. Samarkand, discussed in Part III of this series, was a crossroads that periodically became an imperial capital, the centre of somebody’s world. Chang’an was the centre of the Chinese world by definition. Kashgar was never the centre of anything. It sat at the outer administrative limit of whichever empire currently claimed it, roughly four thousand kilometres from Chang’an and comparably distant from Baghdad, Delhi, or Samarkand, which meant that its rulers were always somebody’s frontier governor and its population was always managing a relationship with a capital too far away to administer them closely but not so far away as to leave them alone. The result was a town with an unusual degree of practical autonomy, a persistent tendency toward independent or semi-independent statehood whenever a distant empire weakened, and a population accustomed to the fact that its overlords changed.

They changed frequently. Han forces first established control over the Tarim Basin oasis states in the first century BCE and lost it repeatedly thereafter; the general Ban Chao’s campaigns at the end of the first century CE, which reasserted Han authority across the region with a small force and a great deal of diplomacy among the oasis kingdoms, are among the more celebrated episodes in Chinese frontier history. The Kushans exerted influence from the south. The Tang established a protectorate and made Kashgar one of the Four Garrisons of Anxi, the military anchor points of Chinese control in the Tarim Basin, until the An Lushan rebellion and the Tibetan advance discussed in Part VIII ended Tang power in the region entirely. Tibetan control followed, then Uyghur, then the Kara-Khanid Khanate, then the Kara-Khitai, then the Mongols, then the Chagatai Khanate and its successors, then a period of local rule under a religious dynasty, then Qing conquest in the eighteenth century, then a rebellion and a brief independent emirate in the nineteenth, then Qing reconquest, then the Republic, then two short-lived independence attempts in the 1930s and 1940s, then the People’s Republic. The list is tedious to read and was presumably more tedious to live through, but it makes the point: Kashgar changed hands more often than almost any comparably important settlement in Asia, and kept functioning throughout, because the geography that made it valuable was indifferent to who held it.

The most consequential of those transitions was religious. Kashgar and the western Tarim Basin were, for centuries, substantially Buddhist — the neighbouring oasis kingdom of Khotan, on the southern rim, was one of the most important Buddhist centres in Central Asia, a major transmission point for texts and monks moving toward China, and Kashgar functioned similarly. That changed under the Kara-Khanids, a Turkic dynasty whose ruler Satuq Bughra Khan converted to Islam in the mid-tenth century, making the Kara-Khanids the first major Turkic dynasty to adopt the religion. Kashgar became a Kara-Khanid capital and a centre of Islamic learning, and over the following centuries the Tarim Basin’s Buddhist establishment was displaced — Khotan fell to Kara-Khanid conquest in the early eleventh century after a long war explicitly framed in religious terms, and Buddhism in the region declined toward extinction. The transformation was substantially complete by the time the Mongols arrived, and it is the reason the region’s population today is predominantly Muslim rather than Buddhist, a fact that traces directly back to a tenth-century conversion in a frontier town.

The mechanics of that conversion are worth examining, because they differ instructively from the pattern of Arab conquest that brought Islam to Samarkand and Bukhara three centuries earlier. The Kara-Khanids were not conquered into Islam; they adopted it. Satuq Bughra Khan’s conversion, according to later and heavily legendary accounts, followed contact with Muslim merchants and missionaries operating in the Kara-Khanid territories, and was initially a private matter that became a political one when he used it as a basis for seizing power from a non-Muslim relative. Once the dynasty was Muslim, conversion spread through the Turkic population by the ordinary mechanisms of elite emulation and practical advantage rather than by compulsion — the same process by which the Uyghurs had earlier adopted Manichaeism, and roughly the reverse of what had happened in Transoxiana. The subsequent war against Buddhist Khotan, however, was fought explicitly as a religious campaign, ran for decades, and ended around 1006 with the destruction of one of the oldest continuously Buddhist polities in Central Asia. The distinction matters: Islam arrived in Kashgar by persuasion and left Khotan’s Buddhism by force, in the space of a single century, and both processes were carried by the same trade routes.

Kara-Khanid Kashgar produced two works that matter well beyond their local origin, both written in the 1070s, which is a remarkable output for a single decade in a single provincial city. Mahmud al-Kashgari — the name means, straightforwardly, Mahmud of Kashgar — compiled the Dīwān Lughāt al-Turk, a comprehensive dictionary and compendium of Turkic languages written in Arabic and intended to explain Turkic to an Arabic-reading audience in Baghdad. It includes a map, dialect comparisons, proverbs, poetry, and ethnographic observation, and it remains a foundational source for the historical study of Turkic languages, preserving vocabulary and verse that survive nowhere else. Yusuf Khass Hajib, also of the region, wrote the Kutadgu Bilig, a long didactic poem on statecraft and ethics in the “mirror for princes” tradition, and the earliest substantial surviving literary work in a Turkic language. That a frontier oasis town produced both the first great Turkic dictionary and the first great Turkic literary work within a few years of each other says something about what the crossroads position actually enabled: a place where Turkic, Arabic, Persian, and Chinese cultural worlds all touched was exactly the place where someone would think to write a Turkic dictionary for Arabic readers.

The practical mechanics of a caravan halt at Kashgar are worth reconstructing, because they explain why junction towns generated wealth rather than simply witnessing traffic pass through. A caravan arriving from the Pamirs had, typically, been travelling for weeks with animals in declining condition, cargo that needed repacking, and personnel who needed to sleep somewhere secure. Kashgar supplied all of it commercially: caravanserais offering walled courtyards where animals and goods could be kept safely overnight, animal markets where exhausted camels and horses could be sold and fresh ones purchased, moneychangers dealing in the several currencies that circulated along the route, provisioners, harness makers, and the guides without whom the next stage of the journey was substantially more dangerous. Every one of these was a business, and every one of them extracted a margin from traffic that had no alternative but to stop. Layered on top sat whatever customs duty the current administration imposed. A junction town, in other words, does not merely watch commerce; it taxes it privately at a dozen points before the state taxes it publicly at one, and Kashgar had two thousand years of practice.

The Tarim Basin also holds one of the more genuinely strange archaeological stories associated with any stretch of the Silk Road, and it predates the route entirely. Beginning in the early twentieth century and continuing with major finds since, excavations at sites across the basin have recovered hundreds of naturally mummified human remains, preserved by the extreme aridity and salinity of the desert soil to a degree that leaves hair, clothing, and facial features intact. The oldest date to roughly 2000 BCE — nearly two millennia before Zhang Qian — and many display physical features and textile traditions, including woven twills with a striking resemblance to Central European weaving techniques, that prompted decades of speculation about migration from the west. More recent genomic analysis has complicated that picture considerably, suggesting the earliest of these populations were a genetically isolated local group rather than recent arrivals from Europe, while nonetheless maintaining cultural and technological contacts across surprising distances. The debate remains active and has acquired political overtones that this series will not wade into. The point relevant here is simpler: the Tarim Basin was hosting long-distance cultural contact two thousand years before anyone in the Han court had heard of it, which is a useful reminder that Zhang Qian did not open a route so much as document one that had been operating, in some form, since the Bronze Age.

Marco Polo passed through Kashgar in the 1270s, which this series notes mostly for completeness — he is due his own installment shortly — and described it in his usual manner, remarking on the gardens, vineyards, and cotton production, and offering some unflattering commentary on the mercantile character of the inhabitants that reads, seven centuries later, as the standard complaint of a Venetian who found somebody else’s traders sharp. The observation about agriculture is the more useful one: Kashgar’s oasis was genuinely productive, watered by the Kizilsu and Tuman rivers running off the Pamirs, and the town’s ability to feed itself and provision caravans was as essential to its function as its position.

One further category of evidence from the Tarim Basin deserves mention, because it demonstrates the region’s connective function more directly than any trade ledger could. Manuscripts recovered from Turfan, Kucha, and other oasis sites in the early twentieth century turned out to be written in a previously unknown Indo-European language — two closely related dialects, in fact — now called Tocharian. Its discovery was genuinely disorienting for historical linguistics, because Tocharian belongs to the centum branch of Indo-European, which is otherwise a western European and Anatolian grouping, and its speakers had somehow ended up several thousand kilometres east of any related language, in the middle of a desert basin surrounded by Iranian, Turkic, and Sinitic speakers. The texts themselves are largely Buddhist translations, produced in oasis monasteries between roughly the fifth and eighth centuries, and Tocharian appears to have died out as the Turkic linguistic expansion absorbed the region. Alongside Tocharian, the same sites yielded material in Khotanese and Sogdian, both Middle Iranian; in Sanskrit and Gandhari Prakrit from India; in Chinese; in Tibetan; in Old Turkic; in Syriac, brought by Nestorian Christians; and in Middle Persian and Parthian carried by Manichaeans. That a handful of desert towns should have generated documents in a dozen languages from four language families says everything necessary about what the Tarim Basin was for.

The overland route’s long decline, which this series has begun tracing through the plague and Mongol fragmentation and will pursue further through the Ottomans and the maritime turn, hit Kashgar as hard as anywhere. A junction town’s entire value proposition depends on traffic, and when the traffic moved to ships, Kashgar became simply a remote oasis city in a very large desert basin rather than the pivot of Eurasian commerce. It never entirely stopped mattering — it remained a regional centre, and the nineteenth century brought a strange late flourishing of importance when Kashgar became a listening post in the Great Game between the British and Russian empires, both of which maintained consulates there for decades, staffed by men whose principal occupation was watching each other watch the passes.

The town has become, in recent years, a subject of contemporary political controversy well outside this series’ scope, centred on the demolition and reconstruction of large portions of its old city and on the broader situation of the Uyghur population in Xinjiang. That is a live political dispute with strongly contested accounts, and a historical series on the Silk Road is not the place to adjudicate it. What can be said within this series’ frame is narrower and, in its own way, sad: the physical fabric that made Kashgar recognizable as a two-thousand-year-old caravan town — the mud-brick lanes, the courtyard houses, the layered accretion of centuries of building on the same footprint — is substantially gone, and whatever one concludes about the reasons, the loss is real. The crossroads remains, since crossroads always do. The town that grew around it over two millennia largely does not.

What Kashgar contributes to this series is a corrective to the imperial framing that dominates most Silk Road narratives, this one included. Parts I, V, VIII, and others have told the story through capitals and dynasties, because capitals and dynasties generate the records historians work from. Kashgar was never a capital of anything that mattered to the wider world, produced comparatively few records of its own, and appears in most accounts as a waypoint mentioned in passing. It was also, for two thousand years, the single point that every overland traveller between China and the west had to pass through, without which none of the imperial stories happen at all. The places that make a network function are rarely the places that write its history, and the ones that write the history rarely notice.
