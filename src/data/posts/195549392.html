Hugh Keenleyside, C.C. — the quiet builder in the author's own family, who helped design the frameworks the world still uses

In the summer of 1945, with Europe in ruins and the architecture of the prewar world shattered beyond repair, a small group of countries set about building something new. The United Nations. The Bretton Woods financial system. The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade. NATO. The International Monetary Fund. The World Bank. The institutional framework that would govern the postwar world for the next eight decades — the rules-based international order, as diplomats would later call it, with the particular fondness that diplomats have for phrases that make complex things sound simple.

Canada was in the room. Not as a great power — Canada has never been a great power and has never pretended to be one, which is itself a form of power that great powers rarely understand. But as something arguably more useful: a country with enough credibility to mediate, enough capacity to contribute, and enough modesty to build institutions without insisting on dominating them.

This is the era of the quiet builders. It produced a generation of Canadian diplomats, politicians, and public servants whose influence on the shape of the modern world is vastly disproportionate to the size of the country they represented. Their names are not well known outside of Canada, and in some cases not well known inside it. But the institutions they built — the ones that are now under assault from the very country that was supposed to anchor them — bear a Canadian fingerprint that is worth tracing, particularly at this moment in history, when the rules-based order they constructed is being dismantled by a president who regards international institutions as obstacles rather than assets.

Among the quiet builders was Hugh Keenleyside — a name that appears in my own family’s history, a connection to the diplomatic tradition that gives this series its personal anchor alongside its historical one.

Keenleyside was a career diplomat and public servant who served Canada across multiple decades and multiple capacities. He was Canada’s ambassador to Mexico. He was deputy minister of mines and resources. He served as director-general of the United Nations Technical Assistance Administration — the forerunner of the United Nations Development Programme — where he helped design the frameworks for international development cooperation that remain, in modified form, the basis of how wealthy nations engage with poorer ones.

Hugh Keenleyside played a key role in the early years of the Colombo Plan, the ambitious Commonwealth-led initiative (strongly supported by Canada) that channeled technical assistance, training, and capital to newly independent countries in South and Southeast Asia

It was unglamorous work — the kind that produces no headlines and wins no prizes. But Keenleyside's Technical Assistance Administration pioneered something that had not existed before: a systematic framework for transferring knowledge and institutional capacity from developed countries to developing ones. Not charity. Not empire dressed as aid. A structured proposition that expertise could be shared across borders without strings that led back to colonial ministries. When the Colombo Plan brought Canadian engineers and educators to South and Southeast Asia in the 1950s, they were working within an architecture that Keenleyside had helped design — an architecture built on the radical premise that development was a right, not a favour.

He was not the most famous of the quiet builders. He was not the most powerful. But he was representative of a type that Canada produced in unusual quantities during the postwar period: the competent internationalist. The person who showed up at conferences with prepared positions and detailed knowledge, who drafted resolutions and built coalitions, who did the unglamorous committee work that turns principles into programs and programs into institutions. The kind of person who, in the Canadian tradition, makes the machinery work while louder people take the credit.

The most famous of the quiet builders was, of course, Lester Bowles Pearson — “Mike” to everyone who knew him, a nickname acquired during his pilot training in the First World War when his instructor decided that “Lester” was insufficiently dashing for an aviator.

Pearson’s career is a concentrated expression of everything this series has argued about the Canadian character. He was a professor who became a diplomat who became a politician who became a prime minister — each transition accomplished with the reluctant competence of a man who would rather have been doing something else but who recognized that the job needed doing and that he was, unfortunately, qualified.

As a diplomat, Pearson served as Canada’s ambassador to the United States and then as president of the United Nations General Assembly — the first Canadian to hold that position. He was present at the founding of NATO. He helped negotiate the terms of the Korean War armistice. He was, by the early 1950s, one of the most respected diplomatic figures in the Western world — a man whose country was small enough that he had no imperial agenda and whose judgment was sound enough that larger countries listened.

His defining moment came in 1956, during the Suez Crisis. Britain, France, and Israel had invaded Egypt after President Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal. The invasion threatened to shatter the Western alliance — the United States, under Eisenhower, was furious at its allies for acting without consultation. The Soviet Union was making threatening noises. The Middle East was on the brink of a wider war.

Pearson proposed a solution that was, in retrospect, so characteristically Canadian that it could have been designed by a committee in Ottawa expressly for the purpose of embodying the national character. Instead of choosing sides — instead of backing Britain (as imperial loyalty demanded) or condemning Britain (as American pressure demanded) — Pearson proposed the creation of a United Nations Emergency Force: a neutral, multinational peacekeeping force that would be deployed between the combatants, separate the armies, and create space for diplomacy.

It was not a military solution. It was not a grand strategic vision. It was an institutional innovation — the invention of a mechanism that had not previously existed, designed not to win a war but to prevent one from getting worse. It was, in other words, a Canadian answer to a global problem: build an institution, staff it with people from countries that have no stake in the outcome, and let the machinery of process do the work that the machinery of war cannot.

The UN Emergency Force was deployed. The crisis was defused. Pearson won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1957. And peacekeeping — the blue helmet, the neutral force, the Canadian soldier standing between warring parties — became Canada’s signature contribution to international affairs.

Peacekeeping was not merely a policy. It became an identity.

For decades, Canadians understood themselves through the blue helmet. It was the answer to the perennial Canadian question — what are we, if not British, not French, and not American? We are the peacekeepers. We are the honest brokers. We are the country that builds institutions and monitors ceasefires and stands in the space between combatants so that diplomats can do their work.

Canadian peacekeepers wearing the iconic blue beret. For decades, the blue helmet became Canada’s signature contribution to the postwar order — standing between combatants not to win wars, but to create space for diplomacy.

This identity was idealized, certainly. The reality of peacekeeping was often brutal, thankless, and morally ambiguous. Canadian peacekeepers in Cyprus maintained a frozen conflict for decades without resolving it. Canadian soldiers in Bosnia were forced to watch atrocities they were not mandated to prevent. In Rwanda, Lieutenant-General Roméo Dallaire commanded the UN mission during the 1994 genocide and was denied the reinforcements and the mandate that might have allowed intervention. He was left to witness, with a small and under-equipped force, the slaughter of 800,000 people. The psychological damage he suffered — the PTSD, the suicide attempts, the decades of anguish — became, in itself, a symbol of the gap between the peacekeeping ideal and the peacekeeping reality.

And in Somalia, members of the Canadian Airborne Regiment tortured and killed a Somali teenager named Shidane Arone in 1993 — an atrocity that shattered the myth of Canadian moral superiority and forced the country to confront the possibility that its soldiers were capable of the same cruelty as anyone else’s. The Airborne Regiment was disbanded. A public inquiry revealed systemic failures of leadership and accountability. The peacekeeping identity survived, but it was no longer innocent.

These failures do not negate the peacekeeping contribution. They complicate it. And the complication is important, because any honest account of Canada’s postwar role must include both the invention of peacekeeping and the moments when peacekeeping failed — sometimes because the mandate was inadequate, sometimes because the resources were insufficient, and sometimes because the Canadians who wore the blue helmet were not the paragons that the national myth required them to be.

The generation that built the multilateral system — Pearson at the UN, Keenleyside in development, their colleagues across a dozen agencies and treaty organizations — understood that this work was inseparable from managing the relationship that defined Canada's existence. You could not build international institutions without American participation, and you could not secure American participation without navigating American power on terms that preserved your own sovereignty. That balancing act — building outward while managing the elephant at home — was the central diplomatic challenge of postwar Canada, and the relationship it produced was intimate, asymmetric, and defined by a fundamental imbalance of power that both sides agreed, for most of the period, to manage politely.

Pierre Trudeau’s observation that living next to the United States was like sleeping with an elephant — “no matter how friendly and even-tempered the beast, one is affected by every twitch and grunt” — captured the relationship with an accuracy that has not diminished in the half-century since he said it. Canada and the United States were allies, partners, and neighbours. They built NORAD together — the joint aerospace defence command that has monitored North American airspace since 1958. They were founding members of NATO together. They traded freely across the longest undefended border in the world. American investment poured into Canada. Canadian resources — oil, lumber, minerals, water, hydroelectric power — flowed south.

The relationship was functional, mutually beneficial, and profoundly unequal. Canada was the junior partner, and being the junior partner required a particular set of skills — the ability to influence without commanding, to disagree without rupturing, to maintain sovereignty while acknowledging dependence. Canadian diplomats became masters of this art. They learned to read American intentions, to find the spaces where Canadian interests could be advanced without provoking American resistance, and to accept, with varying degrees of grace, that the elephant would twitch when it twitched and there was often nothing to be done about it.

The quiet builders understood this dynamic and navigated it with extraordinary skill. Pearson worked with the Americans when alignment was possible and worked around them when it was not. Keenleyside built international institutions that gave Canada a voice disproportionate to its size. The entire generation operated on the premise that Canada’s influence in the world depended on its institutional credibility — on being the country that showed up prepared, played by the rules, and could be trusted to act in good faith.

That premise held for decades. It produced a Canada that punched above its weight in international affairs, that was welcomed in rooms where its size alone would not have earned a seat, and that built a reputation for competent, principled, unglamorous diplomacy that was the envy of countries ten times its size.

The premise is now being tested — not by Canada’s failures but by the failure of the country that was supposed to anchor the system the quiet builders helped create. When the United States withdraws from international institutions, undermines the rules-based order, and treats its closest ally as a target rather than a partner, the entire architecture that Pearson and Keenleyside and their generation constructed begins to crack.

The quiet builders built for a world in which the United States would be a responsible anchor. They did not build for a world in which the anchor would become the storm.
