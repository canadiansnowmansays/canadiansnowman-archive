Majed Alessa, a 25-year-old engineer, was eating lunch in Terminal 4 of King Khalid International Airport on Saturday, October 10, and had just opened a book when he saw the blast through the windows. A Houthi cruise missile had hit the terminal next door. He walked out unhurt. “What happened to these innocent people,” he told Vivian Nereim of the New York Times, “they have nothing to do with anything.”

In Bihar, in eastern India, Aditya Raj’s sister has two children, aged four and seven, who keep asking where their father is. Sumit Kumar Singh died in an engine-room fire on the tanker El Gaia after an Iranian missile and drone hit it near the Strait of Hormuz in September. “We don’t have anything to do with Iran and America,” Raj told Jenny Gross of the New York Times, “and we are losing our family members and our loved ones.”

The same sentence came from Riyadh and from Bihar in the same week. Nobody said anything truer about the war.

Last week the President promised voters he would not attack Iran before November 3. This week he promised the Houthis he would deal with them afterwards. In between, the dead are pilgrims, restaurant workers, a flight attendant and sailors earning $200 a month, from Bangladesh, Jordan, Egypt, Syria, Palestine, India and, in far greater numbers, Yemen. None of them has a vote on November 3. The war is being run around it anyway.

“I’ll Be There After the Elections”

On Tuesday, October 13, the President turned to Truth Social again. “The Houthis are behaving very badly,” he wrote, according to Business Today. “They’ve been silent for a long time, but now they’ve emerged again, probably at the behest of Iran.” They were “hitting Saudi Arabia, and purposefully trying to drive up Oil Prices, probably prior to the U.S. Elections — NOT NICE.” Then the promise: “but I’ll be there after the Elections, and I will never forget!”

He offered no evidence that the Houthis are timing anything to the midterms. The post shows that he is. He is not saying he cannot act or will not act, only that he will act after the vote, and he has told the people he is threatening exactly when. Last week’s pledge covered Iran. This one covers Iran’s allies.

He also told the Houthis they had already been beaten. In the same round of posts he said he had thought they “learned their lesson a year ago when we bombed the HELL out of them.” Neil MacFarquhar’s account in the New York Times is less flattering: the United States “fought the Houthis to a standstill for four months in 2025.” On September 12 the President was telling reporters that the Houthis “called us and they don’t want to fight with us,” according to Kurdistan24. He was right about that. They wanted to fight Saudi Arabia.

The same day, speaking to reporters, he found someone else to do it. He “might” ask President Erdogan of Turkey to intervene on Saudi Arabia’s behalf. “President Erdogan, I think, would do as I ask,” he said, as reported by the Times of Israel. Five days earlier, Turkey’s foreign minister, Hakan Fidan, had said publicly that Turkey “will not send its troops to another country’s territory to launch an attack,” according to the Korea Times. Ankara has not said otherwise since.

Last week this diary asked whether the President was moving closer to helping the Saudis. This week the New York Times gave the answer. Ismaeel Naar and Pranav Baskar reported that although he condemned the airport attack, he “has so far rebuffed the kingdom’s requests for U.S. support in the fight.” On Thursday, October 15, MacFarquhar went further: “With President Trump denying Riyadh’s recent requests for help, Saudi Arabia invoked a mutual defense pact” with Pakistan and Turkey. Those countries are expected to help mainly with intelligence and communications.

So the Crown Prince has a promise from Washington for after November 3 and a pact with Islamabad and Ankara for now. Meanwhile, as of early October, more than 200 American analysts were helping choose Saudi targets from the kingdom’s command centres. Washington will not fight this war in public. It is helping to run it in private.

Terminal 3

The missile hit Terminal 3 just as passengers were lining up to board, many of them dressed in the plain white that pilgrims wear to Mecca. Part of the roof fell on the travellers below. A doctor at one of the city’s largest hospitals told Al Arabiya that his staff cleared 100 emergency beds and performed 22 operations in less than a day. Saudi authorities say at least 13 people were killed and more than 300 wounded. By Nereim’s count, at least 18 people have now been killed in five attacks on Riyadh’s airport since the Houthis declared Saudi airports a “theater of military operations.”

Nereim’s piece names the dead.

Mohammad Nasrullah, 37, was an Islamic studies teacher from Bangladesh and a father of three. He was leading a group of pilgrims to Mecca.

Buthaina Owaida, 56, was a Syrian mother of four who had moved to the kingdom. She was on the phone with her grandson when the missile hit. Her sister says she would sometimes “laugh so much until she cried.”

Mahmoud Barqawi, 33, worked in a restaurant at the airport. He was saving to build a house back home in Jordan and leaves two sons, aged four and one. He was buried in Amman on Monday. “He was an innocent man,” his mother, Lina Barqawi, said. “Why did they kill him?”

Azza Ramzi, 28, was an Egyptian flight attendant. She was buried in her village, Al-Atf, on the same day.

Talal Dani, 60, was a Palestinian retiree who had lived in Saudi Arabia all his life. He leaves his wife, four sons and two daughters.

About a third of Saudi Arabia’s population is foreign. The airport’s dead reflect that: people who came to work, to pray or to pass through. The American killed on Saturday has still not been publicly named.

The Houthis’ answer to “why” came from Mohammed al-Bukhaiti, a senior Houthi political official. Saudi Arabia, he said, bore responsibility for the civilian casualties “because it failed to evacuate its airports” after the Houthis had warned them. You were warned, so it is your fault. As Graeme Wood pointed out last week, it is also the logic the Houthis reject when Saudi jets hit a market in Taiz.

Then there is Noora al-Otaibi, a 35-year-old intensive-care doctor. On Thursday, October 8, she was sitting on a plane at the same airport when a projectile hit the Saudia jet next to hers. Three people died in that attack. She got off and helped others. The next day, having not seen her family in months, she came back for another flight, while announcements warned travellers to stay away from the windows. She reached Jeddah safely. “I am with my family and in my home,” she said, “but I feel numb.”

The day after her flight came Saturday’s attack.

“Fear” Is Not in the Dictionary

At the WPC Energy Congress in Riyadh, foreign diplomats outside the exhibition hall were being warned of a missile possibly headed for the capital. Inside, as Lisa Friedman reported for the New York Times, attendees were told to take their seats because the energy minister, Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman, was about to speak. “The word ‘fear’ in the Saudi dictionary does not exist,” he said on Monday, October 12. He called Riyadh “the capital of energy security.” The chief executives of Exxon, Shell, Chevron and BP had all cancelled. With Riyadh’s airport closed “until further notice,” delegates flew in through other cities and countries. MacFarquhar reports that the Prince struck a harder note too: “Our response must be equally serious.”

On Tuesday, both sides announced their targets in advance. The Saudi-led coalition’s spokesman, Major General Turki al-Maliki, told civilians to leave the area around al-Sabaha Central Station, an oil facility in Sanaa. The Houthi military spokesman, Yahya Saree, then named three targets he would hit if Yemen’s oil sites were attacked: Aramco’s headquarters, Aramco’s refinery in Riyadh, and King Khalid International Airport, which had already been hit. Saudi jets struck the Sanaa station and another oil facility at Hodeidah, where the Houthi-run health ministry said at least two people were killed. That night, according to Naar and Baskar, a strike set fire to an Aramco administrative building in Dhahran, on the Gulf coast. The energy ministry reported “a limited fire” and no injuries. Two people with knowledge of the damage told Jenny Gross and Lisa Friedman of the New York Times that the top three floors of the tower were damaged. No oil production or export facilities were hit. Missiles and drones aimed at Riyadh, Dammam and the south were intercepted, and debris fell on homes in Dhahran.

The Crown Prince’s cabinet promised the Houthis would “bear the consequences.” The congress went on as scheduled on Wednesday, with panels on the future of hydrogen.

The event that matters more to the Crown Prince is the Future Investment Initiative, his annual summit known as “Davos in the Desert,” which opens on Monday, October 26. Its organisers say it will go ahead with “comprehensive security measures.” Update, October 19: On the day this diary posted, the organisers postponed it to 2027, according to a notice sent to participants and seen by the New York Times. Neil Quilliam of Chatham House explained why the kingdom cannot cancel: doing so would hand the Houthis a “psychological” victory. But, he warned, “Hosting major events demonstrates resilience, but there are limits to what symbolism can achieve.”

The Mosquito and the Hammer

MacFarquhar’s analysis on Thursday explains why so many Houthi weapons get through. Saudi Arabia spends more than $82 billion a year on its military. Since 2020 it has bought at least 730 Patriot interceptors from Washington, along with THAAD batteries. Analysts call much of this the “glitter factor”: advanced fighter jets and interceptors built to stop ballistic missiles flying high and on a predictable path. The Houthis mostly fire cheap drones and low-flying cruise missiles that weave through Yemen’s mountains and appear on Saudi radar only once they are over Saudi territory. Using a Patriot that costs more than $3 million against a drone that costs $15,000 to $25,000 is, as analysts put it, like swatting a mosquito with a hammer. The world is running short of the hammers.

The kingdom’s defences were also built facing the wrong way. They are concentrated in the east, to protect the oil fields from Iran and Iraq, not along the long, mountainous southwestern border with Yemen. The Times’s map of this month’s airport attacks runs north from the border: Jazan and Najran on October 5, Abha on October 6, then Riyadh, then Dammam. On Thursday, Saudi authorities said they had intercepted a cruise missile over the Medina region. The Houthis denied aiming at it.

“It’s a hugely rattling moment for Riyadh, humiliating on a number of fronts,” Burcu Ozcelik of the Royal United Services Institute said. Jean-Loup Samaan of the National University of Singapore offered little hope that more bombing would help: “There is nothing really that the Saudis did not already try.” In 2015, as defence minister, Mohammed bin Salman said it would take weeks to defeat the Houthis. That war is now in its twelfth year.

The Israeli analyst Danny Citrinowicz described what is at stake for Washington. “Riyadh is a critical American partner,” he wrote, “and a Houthi victory, or a political settlement imposed on Saudi Arabia under military pressure and largely on Houthi terms, would represent an enormous strategic achievement for Iran.”

Taiz

The Saudi airstrikes have their own funerals, and they are mostly Yemeni.

On Saturday, October 17, as Ismaeel Naar reported for the New York Times, the Houthi-run health ministry said four airstrikes had killed nine civilians in Taiz. The Saudi government did not respond. The World Health Organization says at least 2,007 people were killed or wounded in Yemen between October 3 and October 11, double the recent daily average, and at least 1,282 have died since the fighting escalated in August. Taiz, Yemen’s third-largest city, is where the cost is highest. It sits between the government-held south and the Red Sea coast the Houthis took in September, and the Houthis now hold the mountains around it, watching its roads. Last week they called on government units inside to surrender and accused them of using residents as “human shields.” The W.H.O. says the city is being cut off and its hospitals are running out of medicine and fuel. More than 200,000 Yemenis have been displaced since August, over half of them now sheltering in Taiz. The World Food Programme says it can no longer reach about 600,000 people in and around the city.

The airports war runs both ways too. Late on Friday, two Houthi missiles landed inside Aden’s international airport, in the government’s own capital, while a passenger jet prepared to take off. The coalition says it destroyed two ballistic-missile launchpads in Saada and intercepted missiles and drones aimed at Riyadh and the Red Sea port of Yanbu. Riyadh’s airport was still closed on Saturday, and the kingdom has cancelled or postponed a string of tournaments and conferences.

On Friday, October 16, the United Nations Security Council condemned the Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia and called for full enforcement of a 2015 resolution requiring the Houthis to give up the territory and weapons they have seized. The Saudis welcomed it. The Houthis have ignored that resolution for eleven years, and nothing in Friday’s statement suggests they will start now. The women and children in Taiz did not choose this war any more than the pilgrims in Terminal 3 did.

The Strait, Both Ways

By Wednesday, October 14, maritime analysts counted at least 15 attacks on ships in a single week, about as many as in all of September. By Thursday at least 26 vessels had been struck in October alone, making this the most dangerous month for shipping since the war began. The Singapore-flagged container ship GFS Pride, 730 feet long, sank off Oman on Wednesday, a day after being hit. Iran says it struck a mine; analysts say there is no evidence of that. Its sister ship, the GFS Giselle, was hit by “unknown projectiles” the same day. The tanker Cape Dao sank on Tuesday, three weeks after it was struck; one sailor died in that attack. More than 100 ships have been hit since February.

“There’s no U.S. response, so Iran is trying to further assert its control over the Strait of Hormuz — and it’s succeeding,” Dimitris Maniatis of the maritime-risk firm Marisks told Gross and Friedman.

That is half true, and fairness requires the other half. As Peter Eavis reported for the New York Times on Thursday, the oil is still getting through. TankerTrackers.com counts more than 12 million barrels a day through the strait in October, slightly more than in September. Kpler counts nearly 10 million. Both are well below the 17 million a day that passed through before the war, but neither shows the strait closing. Pipelines around Hormuz carry about five million more. Saudi Arabia alone has exported 7.7 million barrels a day this month, nearly three million of it loaded on the Red Sea coast, within range of the Houthis. Captain Tim Hawkins of Central Command said 1.25 billion barrels have moved through the strait under American protection, 19 million of them on Monday. “Centcom remains vigilant and present,” he said.

Both things are true. Iran has not closed the strait, and the United States has not stopped Iran attacking ships in it. Shipping a barrel from the Gulf to East Asia cost about $3 before the war and now costs more than $30. Some operators have begun turning back before they reach the strait. Asked whether Iran will escalate further, Noam Raydan of the Washington Institute said, “I would expect that.”

The toll-booth thesis this diary has argued since the summer never claimed Iran would close the strait. It claimed Iran would charge for passage, in money, in risk and in lives. This month the cost went up.

The Ones Who Crew the Ships

Most of the people paying that cost in lives are from Asia.

At least 25 seafarers have been killed since February, almost all of them by Iran, and four are missing. Of seven grieving families Gross interviewed, only one has received compensation, about $150,000.

Sumit Kumar Singh earned $1,500 a month on the El Gaia, more than most people in Bihar earn in a year. He was his family’s only income, and his widow has enough money for three to six months.

Dalip Singh, 26, was killed on the Skylight on March 1, the war’s second day, a few hours after a video call with his parents in Rajasthan. His father died of a heart attack six weeks later. The family cannot claim compensation without a death certificate they have been unable to get.

The same reporting shows that not all the deaths were Iran’s doing. On June 10, the U.S. Navy attacked the tanker Settebello, which Central Command said had run the blockade and ignored warnings. Three sailors died. One was the chief engineer, Suresh Patnala, 44. His widow’s life-insurance claim was denied in a letter stating that he “was expired in Oman in US Strike on MT Settebello Tanker” and that the death therefore “falls outside the purview of policy terms and conditions.” The operator has stopped answering the families. Bhargavi Patnala and her husband used to take their sons to the Tirupati Balaji temple. She says she will not go back. “I’m not going to trust anymore these temples and gods.”

Another of the three was Aditya Sharma, 23, a trainee cadet on his first ship, earning $200 a month. He wanted to become a captain. His father, Rajesh, has been writing to Indian officials asking that the families be paid. No one has answered. “My son was our only child,” he said.

By the Pentagon’s own lowball count, the war cost about $2,700 a second through early September. Aditya Sharma’s monthly wage went by in less than a tenth of a second. Nobody on the Settebello had a vote on November 3 either.

Fairford, Reconsidered

A note on last week’s diary. Part CI reported that twelve B-1 bombers left RAF Fairford after officials cited a planned Iranian-backed drone attack. On Tuesday, October 13, MI5’s director general, Ken McCallum, said Iran is now going after “harder targets, like military sites,” with more than 50 arrests over Iran-linked plots in Britain in a year. But asked whether Iran could mount a sophisticated drone attack on British soil, he said what had been seen in practice “has not been anywhere near the level of capability,” as Adam Goldman reported for the New York Times. The five men arrested near Fairford were bailed after police found only petrol in their vans. Prime Minister Andy Burnham says the plot was “almost certainly” directed by the Revolutionary Guards. A botched arson plot is still a plot, but it is not obviously the threat the bombers were withdrawn from.

The Cost Ledger

The Pentagon has now given its most detailed estimate yet of what the war has cost the United States: $43.6 billion through September 3, broken down by Ashley Wu and Robert Jimison for the New York Times. Munitions account for $28.1 billion of it, more than 60 per cent. Experts say replacing them could take years. Air Force operations cost $6.6 billion, and replacing destroyed aircraft and radar $4.3 billion. The Times says the total is “almost certainly on the low side”: it does not include the damage to the Gulf bases, which the Pentagon still will not estimate for Congress, and it is now six weeks out of date.

Congress has never authorised any of it. The House and Senate have held 19 votes on war powers over Iran, and five times one chamber or the other has adopted a measure calling on the President to stop the war or seek authorisation. He says he does not need it. In June the White House asked for another $67 billion, and in July the House passed a party-line blueprint for $73 billion more. The Senate has not acted.

Brent crude traded at nearly $106 on Wednesday, against just under $70 on the eve of the war. The International Energy Agency says Gulf producers exported 13.9 million barrels a day in September, 75 per cent of their pre-war level. American drivers were paying $4.37 a gallon last week.

The Numbers

  • $43.6 billion: the Pentagon’s count of the war’s cost through September 3, which leaves out the damage to its bases.

  • 19: war-powers votes in Congress, none of which stopped the war.

  • At least 18: people killed in five attacks on Riyadh’s airport.

  • More than 300: wounded in Terminal 3 on October 10.

  • 2,007: Yemenis killed or wounded between October 3 and 11, by the W.H.O.’s count.

  • 600,000: people around Taiz the World Food Programme can no longer reach.

  • 26: ships struck around Hormuz in the first two weeks of October.

  • At least 25: seafarers killed since February, and four missing.

  • $200: a month, the pay of a cadet killed by an American strike on the Settebello.

  • $3 to $30: the cost of shipping a barrel of oil from the Gulf to East Asia, before and after.

  • Zero: American strikes on the Houthis since the President promised to “be there after the Elections.”

  • 15: days from Monday, October 19, to the midterm elections.
