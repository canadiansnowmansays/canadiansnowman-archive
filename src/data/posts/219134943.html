By Stephen Gordon

Every carnival game has a scoreboard, and the cruellest thing a carnival can do to a player is let him choose it.

Peter Navarro chose his. Of all the ways to measure whether a nation’s trade policy is working (wages, productivity, factory employment, the price of a child’s winter boots), Mr Navarro has spent twenty years insisting there is only one number that truly matters: the trade deficit. It is the number he put at the centre of his books, the number he carried into the first Trump White House in January 2017, the number he wrote into his Project 2025 chapter in April 2023, and the number he brought back with him in January 2025 as the President’s senior counsellor for trade and manufacturing. Shrink the deficit, he promised, and everything else follows.

On October 6, 2026, the Commerce Department posted the scoreboard.

As Ana Swanson reported for The New York Times on October 6, 2026, in “U.S. Trade Deficit Hits 17-Month High Despite Trump’s Tariffs,” the American trade deficit rose to $105.6 billion in August, up 13.7 per cent from July. It was the largest monthly figure since before the so-called Liberation Day tariffs of April 2025, and larger than any single month in the final year of the Biden administration, the administration Mr Trump and Mr Navarro spent a campaign describing as a catastrophe of foreign dependency. Imports hit a record $420.8 billion. Exports managed $315.2 billion.

The mole, it turns out, did not merely survive the mallet. It has been eating well.

The Man Who Picked the Metric

It is worth pausing to remember who Peter Navarro is, because the administration would very much prefer we judged the tariffs on their intentions rather than their author.

Mr Navarro holds a Harvard doctorate in economics, earned in 1986 with a dissertation on why corporations give to charity. He ran for office in San Diego five times and lost five times. He spent the mid-2000s reinventing himself as a China specialist, producing The Coming China Wars in 2006 and Death by China in 2011, the second of which became a documentary narrated by Martin Sheen. By the accounts of the China scholars Foreign Policy contacted in March 2017, most had either never heard of him or had only brushed past him.

His views on trade are not merely unconventional; they sit so far outside the professional consensus that the economists who disagree with him include Republicans, Democrats, libertarians and Nobel laureates in roughly equal measure. Greg Mankiw, who chaired George W. Bush’s Council of Economic Advisers, once said Mr Navarro’s reasoning on the trade deficit contained errors a first-year Harvard student would catch by the end of introductory economics. Tyler Cowen of George Mason University, who has called Mr Navarro a productive and versatile scholar, has also said that close to nobody in the profession shares his belief that a trade deficit is bad in and of itself.

None of this troubled the President. Mr Navarro once described his own job, with a candour that should have ended it, as supplying the analytics that confirm Mr Trump’s intuition, adding that the President’s intuition on trade is always right. That is not an economic adviser. That is a man hired to hold up a mirror.

And there is a small, delicious footnote. In 1984, as a doctoral student, Mr Navarro wrote a book called The Policy Game in which he warned that once protectionist wars begin, the likely result is a downward spiral that drags the entire world economy with it. Young Peter, it seems, would have had some sharp words for old Peter. Unfortunately, the only colleague old Peter ever seems to consult is Ron Vara.

Ron Vara and the Arithmetic of Wishes

For readers who missed it: in six of his books about China, Mr Navarro quoted a trade expert named Ron Vara, a hard-nosed former Harvard doctoral student with a gift for colourful, frequently bigoted aphorisms about the Chinese. In October 2019, the Chronicle of Higher Education went looking for Mr Vara and discovered that he did not exist. Ron Vara is an anagram of Navarro. Mr Navarro admitted he had invented the man, and his publisher agreed to add a disclaimer to future editions of Death by China.

Then, in December 2019, as Alan Rappeport reported for The New York Times in “Ron Vara, A.K.A. Peter Navarro, Highlights Case for More China Tariffs,” a memo arguing for keeping the China tariffs began circulating in Washington under the name of Ron Vara. Mr Navarro confirmed that he had written it. The imaginary expert had been brought out of retirement to lobby for the real policy.

I raise this not to mock (well, not only to mock) but because the Vara episode is the clearest window we have into how Mr Navarro does his numbers. When the evidence he needs does not exist, he supplies it himself.

Consider the revenue promise. In the spring of 2025, Mr Navarro claimed the tariffs would raise about $600 billion a year, some $6 trillion over a decade. The method, as The Economist and the Washington Post’s Fact Checker both noted at the time, was to take America’s roughly $3.3 trillion in annual goods imports and multiply it by a 20 per cent effective tariff, as though Americans would keep buying exactly the same goods in exactly the same volumes no matter what they cost, and as though no trading partner would retaliate and no importer would reroute. It is the kind of arithmetic that works beautifully on a cocktail napkin and nowhere else.

He had form. When the first Trump administration launched its trade war in 2018, Mr Navarro argued that no country would dare retaliate against the world’s largest and most lucrative market. Canada, China, Mexico and the European Union retaliated within weeks. In August 2019, he insisted the China tariffs were not hurting Americans; PolitiFact, citing a mountain of evidence to the contrary, rated the claim “Pants on Fire.”

Ron Vara, presumably, agreed with him every time.

The Headache Ledger

Readers of this series will know the next part by heart, because we have been tracking it since October 2025.

On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that the President had misused the International Emergency Economic Powers Act to impose his sweeping global tariffs. Within hours, he pivoted to Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, a 150-day balance-of-payments authority. When that clock began running down, he reached for Section 301. In July 2026, a new round of tariffs landed on more than 80 countries. According to the Times, another round aimed at more than 40 countries is now being prepared. In the meantime, a baseline tariff of 10 to 12.5 per cent sits on many products. The Penn Wharton Budget Model estimated in February that the Supreme Court ruling could expose the Treasury to as much as $175 billion in refunds, money the administration had collected under an authority the Court said it never had.

That is the mallet coming down, again and again, on a different hole each time.

And what has all this banging produced? Here the Times offers the most damning single number in the whole sorry business. Since Mr Trump returned to office in January 2025, the average monthly trade deficit has been $74.5 billion. In the final year of the Biden presidency, the supposedly ruinous baseline, it was $73.8 billion.

Read those two figures again. Twenty months of tariff letters, Truth Social threats, Liberation Day ceremonies with giant cardboard charts, statute-shopping, court losses, refund fights, retaliation from allies, and a decline in American goodwill that no tariff schedule can price, and the needle has moved by seventy cents on the hundred dollars. In the wrong direction.

What the tariffs did accomplish was volatility. Importers rushed in goods ahead of each new levy, then stopped, then rushed again. The monthly deficit has bucked like a rodeo horse, soaring before Liberation Day, collapsing after it, and now climbing back above anything Joe Biden ever posted. For an American business trying to plan a supply chain, the tariff regime has been less a policy than a weather system.

Christopher Rupkey, chief economist at FWDBONDS, told the Times there is simply “no good alternative” to foreign imports: American labour costs too much to make many goods at prices Americans will pay, and factories cannot be built fast enough to change that. Grace Zwemmer of Oxford Economics warned in a note on October 6 that the trade figures are on track to drag noticeably on third-quarter growth.

The administration’s explanation, naturally, is that the Supreme Court sabotaged the plan. This is the Obfuscate stage of our old TACO cycle (Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate), and it does not survive contact with the calendar. The deficit was jumping around long before February 20, 2026, and the three biggest drivers of August’s surge were petroleum, gold and artificial-intelligence chips. The United States imported more than $90 billion in semiconductors in the first eight months of 2026, almost double the same period of 2025, because American companies are racing to build data centres and the chips are made in Asia. No tariff written by anyone, Ron Vara included, was going to stop Silicon Valley from buying the chips it needs.

There is also the matter Mr Navarro has never wanted to discuss. Most economists believe a country’s trade deficit is driven chiefly by the gap between what it saves and what it spends. When a government runs enormous budget deficits, as Washington does, and cuts taxes, as Mr Trump did, and pays for a war in Iran, as it now is, the country spends more than it saves, and the difference arrives through the ports. You cannot tariff your way out of your own federal budget. Mr Navarro, to his credit, once understood this: in 2010 he co-wrote a book with Glenn Hubbard arguing that raising the American saving rate should be part of trade policy. That Peter Navarro, too, appears to have been retired.

Meanwhile, in Guangzhou

If the tariffs were really aimed at one country, it was China, the “Dragon” of Mr Navarro’s books and film. So how is the Dragon doing?

As Keith Bradsher reported for The New York Times on October 6, 2026, in “The Hidden Policies That Power China’s Export Boom,” China ran a record global trade surplus of $1.19 trillion in 2025 and is on course to match or beat it in 2026. Beijing refunded its exporters $318.2 billion in value-added tax last year, about 1.5 per cent of the entire Chinese economy. The renminbi is down more than 14 per cent in inflation-adjusted terms from early 2022, making Chinese goods cheaper everywhere. Europe’s trade deficit with China now exceeds $1.1 billion a day, and a delegation from Brussels arrived in Beijing this week to complain about it.

Here is the part that ought to sting. Export subsidies and a manipulated currency are precisely the charges Mr Navarro levelled against China in Death by China in 2011. On the diagnosis, he was not entirely wrong; plenty of mainstream economists share some of those concerns. But having correctly identified the levers, he spent two administrations pulling a different one. A tariff wall around the United States does nothing to China’s rebates, nothing to its exchange rate and nothing to its factories’ ability to sell to Brazil, Indonesia, Germany and, yes, Canada. The surplus simply flowed around the wall like water around a rock, and landed on America’s allies, who are now erecting barriers of their own.

Nor did America’s tariffs spare China’s own workers, which is perhaps the only thing that could be said to have gone according to plan. Mr Bradsher found day labourers on Haizhu Island in Guangzhou waiting hours for a single day’s sewing work at $45 to $60, rates that once reached $150. A sewing-machine distributor told him business is down by a third, perhaps by half. Their misery is real. It has not made a single American factory reopen.

The Layer Cake

So what would actually work? Curiously, the answer appeared in the same newspaper on October 5, 2026, in a story that never mentions Peter Navarro at all.

As Ivan Penn reported for The New York Times in “A New Crop of Solar Panels Is the U.S.’s Best Hope of Catching China,” American manufacturers say they are close to commercialising tandem solar panels, which stack two or three layers of material like a layer cake to squeeze out roughly 25 per cent more energy than conventional panels. Hanwha Qcells, a South Korean company that owns the old German firm Qcells, has spent billions on production lines in Cartersville, Georgia, and has become the first manufacturer to have a tandem panel independently certified under Underwriters Laboratories and International Electrotechnical Commission standards. First Solar of Phoenix has poured more than $2 billion into thin-film research. Tandem PV of Fremont, California, reports less than 1 per cent annual degradation in field tests.

China controls about 80 per cent of the global solar market. The industry’s own trade association believes American tandem technology is level with China’s, perhaps ahead. If there is one place where the United States might actually win back a manufacturing industry from Beijing, this is it.

And how is Washington helping? By making it harder to build solar farms. Republicans in Congress and the Trump administration have tied federal permits for new solar projects in knots while promoting fossil fuels and nuclear. The single most promising answer to Chinese industrial dominance is a technology built in Georgia by a Korean company with a German research lab, and the administration that claims to be waging economic war on China is standing on its air hose.

This is the deeper incompetence, the one that outlasts any monthly figure. Beating China in manufacturing was always going to be won in laboratories, on factory floors and in permitting offices, by out-inventing and out-building, not by taxing American consumers on the way in. Tariffs are a wall. Tandem solar is a ladder. Mr Navarro has spent his career selling walls.

The Canadian Footnote

From up here, we have our own memories of the professor.

In June 2018, Mr Navarro announced on American television that there was “a special place in hell” for Justin Trudeau, after our Prime Minister said, accurately and in public, that Canada would respond to American steel and aluminium tariffs. He later apologised. In February 2025, as the Financial Times reported, he floated expelling Canada from the Five Eyes intelligence alliance, and the Daily Telegraph reported that he pushed American negotiators to reopen the question of the Canada-US border itself. Canada’s response, at the time, was to stop talking to the White House until people with actual portfolios were confirmed.

There is a pattern. When the President’s tariff pause of April 9, 2025, finally arrived, The Wall Street Journal reported that Scott Bessent and Howard Lutnick secured it by meeting the President in the Oval Office while Mr Navarro was in another meeting down the hall. The adults, in other words, had to wait until the professor left the room. John Bolton, no stranger to confrontation himself, once remarked that if you left Mr Navarro alone in a room for an hour, you would return to find him arguing with himself.

That, I think, is the kindest summary available. Peter Navarro has spent twenty months arguing with the evidence, with the courts, with the bond market, with his own 1984 self and with every American ally that sells anything worth buying. The evidence has now issued its verdict in the one currency he says he respects.

Stephen Leacock, who headed the department of economics and political science at McGill for more than three decades, could have built an entire Mariposa sketch around a trade adviser who invented his own expert witness. He would also have recognised the ending. The man who chose the scoreboard has lost on it.

Ron Vara was unavailable for comment.
