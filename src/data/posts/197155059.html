There is a reliable way to lose your job in the Trump administration. It isn’t incompetence — incompetence is a credential. It isn’t corruption — corruption is a lifestyle. It isn’t even disloyalty, exactly, because loyalty in this White House has never been about devotion to a person. It’s about devotion to a transaction.

Marty Makary’s sin was simple. He tried to do his job. On Tuesday, he resigned rather than stop doing it.

The Commissioner Who Said No

Dr. Marty Makary, Johns Hopkins surgeon and former FDA Commissioner. One of the administration’s more credentialed appointees, now reportedly on his way out for resisting flavored e-cigarette approvals.

Dr. Marty Makary — Johns Hopkins cancer surgeon, bestselling author, one of the few Trump appointees who arrived at his confirmation hearing with an actual curriculum vitae rather than a donor receipt — was the Food and Drug Administration commissioner for just over a year. By all accounts, including those of people who disagree with much of what he did, he was one of the administration’s more credible appointments.

That sentence alone should have told him he was in trouble.

On May 8th, multiple outlets — the Wall Street Journal, Bloomberg News, Politico, CNN, and STAT — reported that President Trump had signed off on a plan to fire Makary. When asked directly, Trump offered his customary tell: “I’ve been reading about it, but I know nothing about it.” The FDA reversed itself on flavoured e-cigarettes within hours.

On Tuesday, May 12th, Makary resigned. According to four people familiar with the matter, he told those close to him that he could not in good conscience approve flavoured vapes, given their appeal to young people, and would not do something he did not believe in.

Trump, departing for China, wished him well with the kind of eulogy this administration reserves for the recently discarded: “Marty’s a terrific guy, but he’s going to go on and he’s going to lead a good life. He’s a great doctor, and he was having some difficulty.”

Having some difficulty. That is the Presidential epitaph for a man who refused to sign off on mango-flavoured nicotine for teenagers.

The Story Within the Story

To understand why the head of the FDA just walked out the door, you need to understand a $6 billion market, a handful of donor cheques, and a fruit flavour.

E-cigarettes were supposed to be a public health tool. The idea was elegant and, in its way, genuinely compassionate: roughly 28 million Americans still smoke cigarettes, and cigarettes remain the leading cause of preventable death in the United States. If you could transition those smokers to a less harmful delivery system — one that gave them the nicotine without the tar, the carbon monoxide, the combustion byproducts — you could save hundreds of thousands of lives.

The FDA’s scientific review process was designed around a single question: Does this product help existing smokers quit without recruiting a new generation of nicotine users?

Flavored disposable vapes — mango, blueberry, strawberry ice cream, etc. — now central to the battle between youth protection and adult harm reduction.

That second clause is where the trouble lives. Because flavours — mango, blueberry, strawberry ice cream, blue raspberry — don’t solve the quitting problem. They solve the starting problem. They make nicotine pleasant for people who have never tasted it before. They are, in the most precise regulatory language, “underage-appealing elements.”

I know this not from reading studies, though the studies exist. I know it because I was fifteen years old in the 1960s, standing outside a high school in Ontario, smoking menthol cigarettes because they tasted better than the regular ones. Menthol was my mango. It was the flavour that made the poison go down easy. I smoked for years. I quit after my daughter was born, relapsed, quit again, and have now been smoke-free for roughly thirty years. I am seventy-four. I have no illusions about how much damage those years did to my body, or how much worse it would have been had I not stopped.

So when I tell you that allowing flavoured e-cigarettes is wrong, I’m not speaking from a policy brief. I’m speaking from a body that remembers.

The Market That Couldn’t Wait

Here is what happened while the FDA was trying to do science.

The agency has been working through more than 25 million applications for e-cigarette authorization. It has approved only a few dozen. The companies that played by the rules — Reynolds American, Altria, Juul — were authorized to sell only tobacco- and menthol-flavoured vapes. No candy. No fruit. No dessert.

But the illicit market didn’t wait. Chinese manufacturers flooded the United States with disposable vapes in every flavour a teenager could want, and those products captured the majority of the $6 billion American e-cigarette market. The companies that followed the rules watched their competitors — the ones breaking the law — eat their lunch.

This created two problems, and the Trump administration solved only the one that came with a cheque attached.

Problem one: illicit Chinese e-cigarettes are unregulated, untested, and potentially dangerous. This is a genuine public health concern. Problem two: the major tobacco companies — the ones who are reliable donors to Trump’s MAGA Inc. PAC and have contributed to pet projects including the planned White House ballroom — want to sell flavoured vapes from prime shelf space in gas stations and convenience stores across America.

And here is where intellectual honesty requires a pause.

There is a credible, evidence-based case for flavoured e-cigarettes. In the United Kingdom, public health authorities have broadly concluded that vaping is substantially less harmful than smoking — some estimates place the reduction in risk at around 95 percent. Flavours are part of why switching works. Adult smokers who have spent decades on combustible tobacco are more likely to stay on an e-cigarette if it tastes like mango than if it tastes like the thing they’re trying to quit. The harm reduction case is real, it is supported by serious science, and dismissing it entirely would be dishonest.

But here is the thing: the FDA’s scientific review process was designed to answer exactly this question — whether a specific flavoured product could deliver the cessation benefit to adults without becoming a recruitment tool for adolescents. That process was painstaking, slow, and incomplete. More than 25 million applications were filed. A few dozen were approved. The science was being done.

What happened on May 9th was not the conclusion of that process. It was the abandonment of it. The question of whether mango-flavoured vapes can help adult smokers without hooking teenagers is a legitimate scientific question with a legitimate scientific answer — and the administration decided it didn’t want to wait for that answer, because the donors were waiting for a different one.

On Friday, May 9th, the FDA issued a new guidance that addresses both problems — but only one of them honestly. The agency said it would take steps to remove illicit products from the market (enforcement it has been failing to execute for years) and would allow products in “advanced stages of review” to be marketed immediately, using a doctrine called “enforcement discretion” — a polite way of saying the agency plans to set aside its own rules.

The Gift

Mitch Zeller, who served as the FDA’s tobacco chief under both Democratic and Republican administrations, was characteristically blunt. He called it “a gift to companies that are eligible” while “allowing illegal products to remain on the market because the FDA is going to look the other way.”

Brian King, another former FDA tobacco chief now at the Campaign for Tobacco-Free Kids, noted the obvious: the guidance benefits larger companies far more than smaller ones. Reynolds American. Altria. The donors.

The guidance was issued without public comment or rulemaking. It makes an end run around rules the FDA had previously defended all the way to the Supreme Court. And it was announced hours after the White House signalled it was firing the commissioner who had been resisting it.

The FDA did include a fig leaf — it said it would continue to take action against products with “underage-appealing elements,” meaning vapes shaped like video game controllers or disguised as highlighters. But as Tony Abboud of the Vapor Technology Association cheerfully noted, the agency is now “focusing on these other features which are truly child appealing, rather than just focusing on flavours.” Translation: the flavours won. The industry’s position — that a mango-flavoured nicotine delivery device is not inherently appealing to children — is now federal policy.

U.S. teen vaping rates have declined significantly since the 2019 peak. The question now is whether reopening flavored products will reverse that progress.

The Irony

Here is what makes this particularly bitter. Adolescent vaping rates have been falling. The combination of enforcement, education, and the first Trump administration’s 2019 partial ban on flavoured cartridge-based vapes was working. In 2019, about a quarter of high school seniors reported vaping in the previous month. That number has dropped steadily since.

This policy doesn’t solve a problem. It reopens a door that was closing.

And the commissioner who tried to keep it closed — who resisted for months, whose deputy issued a memo in February delaying flavoured vape approvals, who told those close to him he could not in good conscience do what the White House demanded — chose to walk rather than comply.

Lawrence O. Gostin, a Georgetown Law professor who studies the FDA, delivered perhaps the most damning summary of Makary’s tenure: “On vaccines and mifepristone, Makary rarely prioritized rigorous evidence. Ironically, his one stand for high-quality science — on flavoured vapes — created the friction with the White House that contributed to him losing his job.”

Read that again. The one time he chose science, it cost him everything.

The Vacancy Architecture

Makary’s replacement tells you what comes next. Kyle Diamantas, the FDA’s top food regulator, was named acting commissioner. He came to the agency from Jones Day, the law firm where he represented Abbott Nutrition, the infant formula company. The revolving door doesn’t even bother to revolve anymore — it just stands open.

Makary’s departure is not an isolated event. It is another brick removed from a wall that is already failing.

Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s Department of Health and Human Services currently operates without a permanent CDC director. Without a surgeon general. Without a top vaccine regulator at the FDA — Dr. Vinay Prasad, Makary’s handpicked director of gene therapies, stem cell treatments and vaccines, was targeted by the right-wing influencer Laura Loomer, resigned under pressure, was brought back, and then left the agency again in recent weeks. Neither of the agency’s main divisions regulating the biopharma industry is led by a permanent official. The FDA itself has lost more than 4,000 staff members — roughly a fifth of its workforce — through layoffs and voluntary departures.

This is not dysfunction. This is architecture.

Every vacancy is a steering wheel with no hands on it. Every acting director is someone who knows they can be replaced tomorrow. Every unfilled Senate-confirmed position is a signal that the administration prefers empty chairs to occupied ones — because empty chairs don’t say no.

Nathan Cortez, a law professor at Southern Methodist University who studies the FDA, noted that finding a permanent replacement will be a major challenge: the new commissioner will have to walk a tightrope between what the administration wants and what federal law actually requires of the agency. Historically, the expectation is someone with an M.D. or PhD and real scientific credentials. But in this administration, scientific credentials are what got the last commissioner pushed out.

Makary said no. On one issue, on one product category, for reasons grounded in science and public health, he said no. And then he said goodbye.

The List Grows

And so the Enemies List gains another name. Not because Marty Makary was a hero — his tenure had its own controversies, its own chaos, its own critics from every conceivable direction. He angered the anti-abortion community by slow-walking a mifepristone study. He frustrated pharmaceutical companies and their investors with drug rejections his team defended on television. He drew criticism from public health leaders for pandering to anti-vaccine activists with an unsupported memo claiming deaths related to Covid vaccines. He allowed the renewed use of unproven peptides favoured by RFK Jr. He sat in a frigid plunge pool with a wellness influencer. As Diana Zuckerman of the National Center for Health Research put it, “He has offended almost everyone involved in FDA issues, which is not easy to do.”

But he did not resign over any of those things. He resigned because Reynolds American and Altria write cheques, because the White House wanted flavoured nicotine on the shelves, and because when told to approve products that would make it easier for fifteen-year-olds to start a habit they’ll spend decades trying to quit, he said he could not do it in good conscience.

In this administration, conscience is a resignation letter.

The flavour of loyalty, it turns out, is mango.
