I. The Texts From Washington

Lila Shroff, writing in the Atlantic on July 9, 2026, opens with something almost too on the nose to invent: a Labor Department AI-literacy course, delivered by text message, that tells enrollees to ask a chatbot for “side hustle ideas” and to doodle a hippo so an AI can guess whether it’s a hippo or “a lumpy potato.” This is, per an agency press release, meant to equip Americans with “foundational AI skills” for the transition ahead. It is also, as Shroff puts it, the kind of underwhelming response that follows from a country that has spent years watching AI’s jobs threat approach like a deer in headlights — long on catastrophic messaging, short on anything that resembles a plan. Dario Amodei’s own warning, issued earlier this year, still stands unanswered by policy: AI, he said, “isn’t a substitute for specific human jobs but rather a general labor substitute for humans.”

What’s changed in the past several weeks, Shroff argues, isn’t the plan. It’s the show of having one. And the show, on close inspection, tends to serve the people staging it.

II. Sanders, Altman, and the Stake Nobody Quite Wants to Give Away

The clearest test case is Bernie Sanders’s AI Sovereign Wealth Fund Act, introduced last month, which would hand the federal government a 50 percent stake in major AI companies including Anthropic and OpenAI, with an appointed commission holding voting shares explicitly meant to “stop bad decisions that will reap massive job loss.” This is standard Sanders material — Big Tech oligarchs, public ownership, the usual targets. What isn’t standard is that one of the alleged oligarchs asked for a meeting about it.

Sam Altman met with Sanders after the bill’s announcement and reportedly told him that while he doesn’t endorse the specific plan, he supports the underlying idea of public ownership of AI companies — a position Altman has been circling since before ChatGPT existed. In April, OpenAI itself endorsed a “public wealth fund” giving every citizen “a stake in AI-driven economic growth,” and according to the Financial Times, Altman has more recently discussed public ownership of OpenAI and its competitors directly with the Trump administration, with the company reportedly floating a 5 percent stake.

It is worth sitting with what this actually resolves and what it doesn’t. Sanders’s fund would start by paying roughly $1,000 per American annually — not nothing, but nowhere near a response to mass unemployment, if mass unemployment is what’s coming. Public ownership doesn’t obviously prevent the layoffs the fund is meant to cushion; it mostly changes who holds the equity while the layoffs happen. And as Shroff notes, public ownership could just as easily dilute the political appetite for the more onerous regulation that might actually slow the displacement down — a government that owns half your company has a stake in that company’s stock price, not only in your workforce. Anthropic, for its part, says it wants to research “AI sovereign wealth funds” as a possible response to extreme unemployment, which is a company keeping every future option, including the ones it might eventually resist, open for study rather than commitment.

III. Claude Corps and the Loop That Doesn’t Close

The clearest illustration of the pattern is the one buried in the dollar figures. Anthropic has committed $350 million toward labor-transition research and policy trials, and roughly half of it is going to a new national fellowship called Claude Corps, which will pay early-career workers $85,000 each to spend a year embedding Claude across at least 400 nonprofits.

Sit with the mechanism for a second. The dependency and apprenticeship problems this series documented in Part 37 and Part 47 describe entry-level workers losing the grunt work that used to train them, because AI now does it faster and cheaper. Claude Corps responds to that same displacement by hiring young people — paid a salary well above what many of the jobs being displaced ever offered — to do the work of getting more nonprofits, and by extension more of their staff and clients, onto Claude in the first place. The solution to AI threatening entry-level work is to employ a smaller number of entry-level workers to accelerate the adoption of the AI that threatens it. This is not necessarily bad-faith engineering on anyone’s part. It is what happens when the same company is simultaneously the disruptor and the first responder, and nobody outside the company is setting the terms of the response.

OpenAI’s foundation, which owns roughly a quarter of the company, is running a parallel effort: $250 million in grants and partnerships aimed at the same transition, plus its own internal team to seed ideas. Both organizations describe interest in measuring how AI actually affects labor markets and in unemployment-insurance reform — worthwhile questions, and ones that, as Shroff notes, economists genuinely don’t have settled answers to yet. AI has not produced the mass layoffs some feared, at least not so far, and serious economists remain split on whether it will. But that uncertainty is precisely why it matters who is funding the measurement of the problem, and what incentive that funder has in how the measurement comes out.

IV. Raise Us and the Warp Speed Comparison That Doesn’t Hold

The most institutionally serious effort Shroff describes is Raise Us, a new nonprofit led by Gina Raimondo and former Indiana governor Eric Holcomb, which has raised more than $500 million from a mix of philanthropies — the Rockefeller Foundation, Melinda French Gates’s Pivotal, Emerson Collective — and tech money, including Anthropic, Amazon, Microsoft, and the OpenAI Foundation among its founding partners. Utah, Connecticut, Arkansas, and Maryland have signed on as initial state partners to pilot interventions like wage insurance, which David Autor — an MIT economist advising the group — describes as showing “some positive evidence” but never tested at meaningful scale in the U.S. Susan Athey, a Stanford economist unaffiliated with the effort, calls the resulting policy evidence “undersupplied” and “extremely valuable.” Both of those assessments can be true, and the project can still be worth doing.

What should give a reader pause is the framing Brad Smith, Microsoft’s vice chair, offered Shroff directly: that Raise Us resembles the public-private partnerships that formed around Operation Warp Speed during the pandemic. Shroff’s rebuttal is the sharpest line in the piece, restated rather than quoted here: it would be as though the vaccine makers involved in Warp Speed had also been the ones engineering more dangerous viruses on the side. The comparison Smith wants — industry stepping up in a crisis it didn’t cause — inverts the actual relationship. The AI industry is racing to automate the same jobs it is now organizing philanthropic capital to cushion the loss of. Both things can be sincere. Neither erases the other.

V. The Governance Gap, Restated in Dollars

This series has spent sixty-two parts arguing that the Asimov Gap is what happens when the pace of deployment outruns the pace of law — when decisions about what AI is allowed to absorb get made by whoever is doing the deploying, because no one else has the authority or the urgency to make them instead. Shroff’s reporting shows what that gap looks like once the companies themselves notice the backlash coming and decide to get ahead of it. The response is not regulation. It is not law. It is philanthropy, fellowships, wealth-fund proposals, and state-level pilot programs — all of it funded, staffed, and in several cases designed by the same handful of companies whose deployment decisions created the problem being addressed.

None of this makes the individual efforts worthless. Wage insurance might work. Better labor-market measurement is genuinely undersupplied, as Athey says. A federal stake, watered down or not, is at least a mechanism nobody has tried. But every one of these responses is voluntary, reversible, and shaped by the donor’s continuing interest in the technology’s uninterrupted growth — which is a different thing entirely from a governance framework that exists whether or not the companies involved feel like funding it this quarter.

Shroff’s closing observation is the one worth sitting with longest: shepherding a smooth labor transition is genuinely in Silicon Valley’s self-interest, given the brewing backlash and the risk of more dramatic federal action. But several of the industry’s own transition efforts, including Claude Corps, are explicitly designed to deepen dependence on the same tools driving the displacement they’re meant to soften. Marcus Chen is still at the beach. Somebody is now getting paid $85,000 a year to make sure more people join him there, one nonprofit at a time — whether or not there’s a rung left underneath for them to climb back up.
