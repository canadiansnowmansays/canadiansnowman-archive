In the autumn of 1998, a slightly overweight Korean boy enrolled at the Schule Liebefeld Steinhölzli, a German-speaking public school in a quiet suburb of Bern, Switzerland. He registered under the name Pak Un. He told classmates he was the son of a North Korean embassy official. He wore Nike Air Jordans, spoke limited German, and had devoted an entire room of his apartment to Chicago Bulls memorabilia — posters, jerseys, pennants, a shrine to a basketball team playing on the other side of the world that he had followed with the intensity of a devotee.

His classmates liked him. Joao Micaelo, the son of Portuguese immigrants who sat next to him in class, remembered helping him with German in exchange for help with maths. His PE teacher, Marco Imhof, recalled a boy who played basketball with passion if not always with polish, and who gave back as good as he got in trash talk. Another teacher remembered him as having a good sense of humour, getting on well with everyone, including students from countries that were technically enemies of his own. He walked to school unaccompanied. He seemed, by all accounts, like a reasonably normal teenager trying to fit in somewhere unfamiliar and not quite managing it, which is to say: like a reasonably normal teenager.

His name was Kim Jong-un. He was the son of Kim Jong-il, the supreme leader of the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea. And the gap between the boy his classmates knew in Bern and the man who would eventually execute his own uncle on live television, send troops to fight in Ukraine, and stand on a ceremonial balcony in Beijing as the acknowledged equal of Vladimir Putin — that gap is the subject of this essay.

The Hidden Prince

Kim Jong-un was born on January 8, 1984, the second son of Kim Jong-il’s third wife, Ko Yong-hui. The maternal line was the first complication. Ko Yong-hui had been born in Osaka, Japan, the daughter of a Korean textile worker who had emigrated during the colonial period. Under North Korea’s songbun classification system, Japanese origins carried significant stigma — Japan was the colonial oppressor, and Koreans who had lived there were categorized with suspicion, as potential tainted elements. Ko Yong-hui’s background made her, in the formal ideology of the state she had married into, a figure of inferior class.

Kim Jong-il kept her largely hidden. He did not formally introduce her or their children to Kim Il-sung, who died in 1994 without having publicly acknowledged his grandson as a family member. There are no known photographs of Kim Jong-un with his grandfather. The boy who would eventually claim the mantle of the Kim dynasty — whose propaganda would eventually describe him as the natural culmination of a sacred bloodline — spent his earliest years as a secret, unacknowledged, existing outside the official narrative of the family he had been born into.

The psychological freight of this origin is not incidental. North Korean experts who have studied Kim’s consolidation of power consistently note the particular ferocity with which he has dismantled anyone who might represent an alternative claim to authority, and the personal intensity with which he has pursued the construction of his own legitimacy. A man who spent his childhood hidden from his grandfather — excluded from the dynasty’s public face precisely because of his mother’s background — might be expected to pursue recognition with unusual urgency once that recognition was finally within reach. The execution of his uncle, the assassination of his half-brother, the relentless accumulation of titles and powers — these are not merely the standard equipment of authoritarian consolidation. They carry the specific emotional signature of a man determined to prove that he belongs where he stands.

The Swiss Education

Kim Jong-un arrived in Switzerland in the early 1990s, initially attending a French-speaking school in Bern under the care of his aunt and her husband, who had been appointed his guardians by Kim Jong-il. He returned to North Korea and then came back, enrolling at Liebefeld Steinhölzli in 1998, this time in the German-language stream, accompanied by his older brother Kim Jong-chul and younger sister Kim Yo Jong — the three of them living together in a Bern apartment with North Korean minders who posed as domestic staff.

The Switzerland years are, by the account of everyone who encountered him, genuinely formative in ways that complicate the standard portrait of Kim as a creature of North Korean ideology and nothing else. He absorbed Western popular culture with an appetite that has never entirely disappeared — the basketball obsession that brought Dennis Rodman to Pyongyang, the film enthusiasms that echo his father’s, the apparent comfort with informal social interaction that his Swiss classmates noted and that sits oddly alongside the absolute formality demanded by his position at home.

He also absorbed something harder to quantify: the experience of being ordinary. For the years he spent at Liebefeld Steinhölzli, Kim Jong-un was not a prince. He was Pak Un, a slightly awkward Korean kid who needed help with his German and gave it back in maths, who trash-talked on the basketball court and laughed at jokes and walked to school alone. No bodyguards. No portraits. No rituals of submission from every person he encountered. The contrast with the world he returned to — in which every human being in his presence was required to perform total deference to his authority — is not one that can have been easy to manage psychologically, even for a young man who had been preparing his whole life for absolute power.

What he took from Switzerland and what he left there are questions that analysts have wrestled with ever since he assumed power. The evidence is ambiguous. He has demonstrated an ability to engage informally with people he chooses to engage with — Rodman being the most vivid example — that suggests the Swiss years left some genuine residue of social ease. He has also demonstrated a capacity for violence against people he once trusted that suggests whatever residue remained, it was not the most important thing about him.

The Friend for Life

On February 26, 2013, Dennis Rodman arrived in Pyongyang with a group of Harlem Globetrotters and a Vice Media film crew, as part of what the organizers called “basketball diplomacy.” It was, by any measure, one of the more surreal diplomatic encounters in the history of the Korean peninsula — a five-time NBA champion, famous for his eccentric personal life and his championships alongside Michael Jordan, sitting courtside at an exhibition game in the capital of one of the world’s most closed societies, next to the thirty-year-old supreme leader of that society, watching basketball and apparently having a genuinely good time.

The connection was not as random as it appeared. Kim Jong-un had been a devoted follower of the Chicago Bulls during his 1990s adolescence — the dynasty years, when Jordan and Pippen and Rodman won three consecutive championships and became arguably the most famous sports team on the planet. Kim had apparently made overtures to Michael Jordan himself, whose representatives politely declined. Rodman arrived instead, and the chemistry was immediate.

They watched the game together. They talked. Rodman, with the guileless directness that had characterized his entire public life, told Kim he had “a friend for life” in front of the assembled North Korean crowd. He was not performing. He meant it. And Kim, for his part, appeared to mean it back — or at least to find in Rodman something that his position at home made almost impossible to find: someone who talked to him like a person rather than a god.

Rodman visited five times between 2013 and 2017. On the 2014 trip, he sang “Happy Birthday” to Kim Jong-un at a celebration game featuring retired NBA players including Vin Baker, Craig Hodges, and Cliff Robinson. He referred to Kim as his “best friend.” The visits were widely criticized in the West — human rights organizations pointed out the grotesque incongruity of an American celebrity providing diplomatic cover and personal validation to a regime operating concentration camps — and Rodman handled those criticisms with varying degrees of grace. During one CNN interview, he implied that Kenneth Bae, an American missionary imprisoned in North Korea at the time, might have deserved his fate — then apologized the next day, blaming the comment on pressure and alcohol.

What the Rodman visits revealed about Kim Jong-un is not primarily about basketball or friendship. It is about the particular loneliness of absolute power. Kim Jong-un is surrounded at all times by people who are required, on pain of death, to treat him as infallible, divine, and beyond criticism. No one can tell him a joke at his expense. No one can look him in the eye without performing submission first. No one can disagree with him about anything without risking consequences that range from professional ruin to execution. The Swiss boy who trash-talked on the basketball court and helped his classmate with maths lives in a world, as the supreme leader, in which authentic human interaction is essentially impossible.

Rodman offered him something close to it. That Kim valued it enough to welcome him back five times tells you something real about the man beneath the mythology — not something exculpatory, not something that mitigates what he has done and continues to do, but something human, and therefore more disturbing than the cartoon villain version of him is.

The American Who Died

In January 2016, while Rodman’s friendship with Kim was still active — he would visit again in 2017 — a twenty-two-year-old University of Virginia student named Otto Warmbier arrived in Pyongyang on a five-day tour organized by Young Pioneer Tours, a company that specialized in travel to North Korea. He was bright, curious, enthusiastic, exactly the kind of young American who reads about a forbidden place and decides the risk is worth it for the experience.

On January 2, 2016, at Pyongyang’s Sunan International Airport, as his tour group prepared to board a flight to Beijing, Warmbier was detained. The charge: attempting to steal a propaganda poster from the Yanggakdo Hotel where the group had been staying. He was accused of committing “a hostile act” against the state. At a one-hour trial in March 2016, during which he gave a tearful, scripted-sounding confession — “I have made the worst mistake of my life!” — he was sentenced to fifteen years of hard labour.

Shortly after that sentencing, Otto Warmbier fell into a coma. North Korea said nothing about this to anyone for over a year. His family received no information. The American government received no information. The world went about its business while a twenty-two-year-old American lay in a coma in a North Korean hospital, having suffered — from causes that remain officially undetermined — extensive loss of brain tissue in all regions of his brain, consistent with a prolonged deprivation of oxygen.

In June 2017, North Korea released him. He was flown home to Cincinnati on a medical evacuation flight, arriving in a state of unresponsive wakefulness — eyes open but unseeing, body contracted, showing no signs of understanding language or his surroundings. His family, seeing him for the first time in seventeen months, saw what the North Korean state had returned to them. Six days later, on June 19, 2017, Otto Warmbier died.

North Korea claimed he had contracted botulism and fallen into a coma after taking a sleeping pill. American doctors found no evidence of botulism. They found evidence of catastrophic brain injury from oxygen deprivation of unknown cause, dating from approximately April 2016 — weeks after his sentencing. What happened to Otto Warmbier in North Korean custody will never be fully known, because North Korea will never say, and the body was not subjected to a full autopsy at the family’s request. The coroner’s report cited “chronic deficiency of oxygen and blood supply to the brain” as the cause of death. The family used the word torture. The medical evidence is consistent with that characterization while falling short of proving it.

What is beyond dispute: a twenty-two-year-old man went to North Korea on a tourist visa, was accused of trying to take a poster off a hotel wall, and came home dead.

The Two Faces

The juxtaposition of Rodman and Warmbier — not deliberately staged, not arranged for rhetorical effect, simply two things that happened in the same years to two Americans who crossed Kim Jong-un’s path — is the starkest possible illustration of what kind of state North Korea actually is and what kind of man actually runs it.

In the Rodman relationship, Kim is recognizable: a basketball fan with a sense of humour, capable of genuine warmth, enjoying the company of a man who talks to him without either performing reverence or delivering policy demands. The Swiss schoolboy is somewhere in that relationship — the kid who helped his classmate with maths, who trash-talked on the court, who laughed at jokes.

In the Warmbier case, something else is visible. Not rage, not ideology, not the specific calculated brutality of the Jang Song-thaek execution — just the casual, administrative indifference of a system that took a young man’s life for a poster and then said nothing about it for more than a year, because it saw no particular reason to say anything. The life was not significant enough to warrant an explanation. The family’s grief was not significant enough to warrant information. The world’s opinion was not significant enough to warrant concern.

That indifference — not hatred, not ideology, just the blank disregard of a system for which individual human lives outside its own hierarchy have essentially no weight — is the most accurate portrait of the state Kim has built and that he embodies. He can be warm to the people he chooses to be warm to. He is not warm to the world.

Jang Song-thaek, the uncle who had been instrumental in Kim’s rise to power, who had walked alongside his nephew as his father’s hearse moved through Pyongyang, who had accompanied Kim on 106 official visits in 2012 alone — was arrested in December 2013, dragged before a military tribunal in a scene broadcast on state television, and executed. The charge was attempting to overthrow the state. The real charge, in the judgment of analysts who have studied the case, was existing as a potential alternative power centre and offering unsolicited advice to a young leader who had decided that unsolicited advice was indistinguishable from insubordination.

Kim Jong-un was twenty-nine years old. He had been supreme leader for two years. He had already decided who he was.

The Consolidation

Between 2011 and 2016, Kim systematically dismantled the power structures that might have constrained him and replaced them with structures that could not. Senior military officers who had served under his father were removed, demoted, or executed. Jang Song-thaek, the most powerful non-Kim figure in the state, was eliminated. Kim Jong-nam, the elder half-brother who might theoretically have represented an alternative dynastic claim, was assassinated in February 2017 at Kuala Lumpur International Airport by two women who wiped his face with a nerve agent and walked away — an operation so brazen that it could only have been authorized at the very top.

The Kim who emerged from this consolidation period was not the naive, untested young leader that outside analysts had initially assessed. He was something the Kim dynasty had not quite produced before: a third-generation authoritarian who had grown up in the shadow of two giants and had been determined, since before he took power, to exceed them both — not in the ideology they had built, not in the mythology they had constructed, but in the thing that, when stripped of all its ideological dressing, actually mattered: the unambiguous, unconstrained, absolute exercise of personal power.

The Swiss schoolboy who liked basketball and helped his classmate with maths grew up to run one of the most totalitarian states in human history with a hand noticeably firmer than either of his predecessors. He did not do this reluctantly or gradually. He did it immediately, systematically, and with a clarity of intention that suggested the consolidation had been planned long before it was executed.

The hidden prince became the supreme leader. The supreme leader became something harder to name — a man who contains, in the same person, the basketball fan and the executioner, the friend for life and the state that returned Otto Warmbier home to die.

Understanding that both of these things are true, simultaneously and without contradiction, is prerequisite to understanding anything else about what North Korea has become under his rule.
