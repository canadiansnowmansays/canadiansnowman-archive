How Kim Il-sung was installed by Moscow, outmanoeuvred every rival, and built from the wreckage of colonialism and the architecture of Stalinism a state unlike anything the world had seen before — or has seen since.

On the night of August 10, 1945, two young American colonels sat in a Washington office with a map of Korea and thirty minutes to solve a problem. Japan had just surrendered. The Soviet Red Army was already pouring across the Manchurian border and would reach the Korean peninsula within days. The United States needed a dividing line — somewhere to separate the Soviet occupation zone from the American one before the Soviets claimed the whole peninsula by default.

The colonels had no particular expertise in Korean geography. They had an atlas and a deadline. One of them — Dean Rusk, who would later become Secretary of State under Kennedy and Johnson — suggested the 38th parallel. It was a round number. It placed the capital, Seoul, in the American zone. It split the peninsula roughly in half. The Soviets, to American surprise, accepted it immediately.

That improvised line, drawn in thirty minutes by men who had never been to Korea, would determine the fate of tens of millions of people for the next eighty years. It separated families who would never see each other again. It created two states that would fight a devastating war and then freeze into a permanent armed standoff. It bisected farms, rivers, and villages with the indifference of a bureaucratic stroke — in some places, the line ran through houses, leaving one room in the North and another in the South. And it drew a boundary that, in the northern half, a thirty-three-year-old Korean captain named Kim Song-ju — soon to be known to the world by a nom de guerre, Kim Il-sung — would spend the rest of his life trying to erase.

The line also encoded, from its very first moment, the fundamental insult that North Korean ideology would spend decades converting into fuel: Korea’s fate, once again, had been decided by foreigners who drew lines on maps without asking the Koreans what they thought.

The Arrival

Kim Il-sung returned to Korea on September 19, 1945, arriving by Soviet ship at the port of Wonsan with sixty-six other Korean officers of the 88th Brigade of the Red Army. He had spent the war years in the Soviet Far East, training under Soviet command, separated from the peninsula he had left as a child. He was thirty-three years old, spoke Korean with a slight Russian accent, and was almost entirely unknown to the Korean public.

The Soviets had a problem to solve. They needed a Korean leader for the northern zone — someone with anti-Japanese credentials, communist sympathies, and sufficient malleability to work within Moscow’s framework. The obvious candidate was Cho Man-sik, a widely respected Christian educator and nationalist who had spent the colonial period in Korea itself, resisting Japan through peaceful means and earning genuine popular admiration. The Soviets tried him first. In January 1946, when Cho Man-sik refused to endorse the Moscow Conference’s proposal for a five-year international trusteeship over Korea — a plan most Koreans despised as simply trading one form of foreign oversight for another — the Soviets placed him under house arrest. He would never be released. His fate after that disappears into the silence of the North Korean state.

With Cho Man-sik neutralized, the Soviets turned to Kim. He had been identified as a potential leader by Lavrenti Beria, Stalin’s security chief. He had military experience, anti-Japanese credentials however embellished, and the crucial advantage of being known to and trusted by Moscow. On October 14, 1945, Soviet occupation authorities introduced Kim to the North Korean public at a mass rally in Pyongyang — presenting him as a great guerrilla hero, the liberator of Korea, a man whose exploits against the Japanese had become legend.

The crowd was somewhat confused. The legend they had heard of was older, more formidable. This man was young, round-faced, wearing a Soviet-style suit that didn’t quite fit. Some in the crowd murmured that this couldn’t be the real Kim Il-sung. The real one had defeated armies. Whoever this was, he looked like a student.

He was not a student. He was, as the world would discover over the following decades, one of the most ruthless political operators of the twentieth century.

The Purges

Kim Il-sung arrived in North Korea as the Soviet’s preferred candidate, but preference is not power. Power had to be built, methodically, from the ruins of the colonial state and the fractured landscape of Korean communism. It took him less than five years to achieve total dominance, and he achieved it through a combination of genuine political skill, strategic land reform that purchased popular loyalty, and systematic elimination of every rival faction that might challenge him.

The Korean communist movement of 1945 was not a monolith. It was a collection of competing factions, each with its own base of support, its own external patron, and its own claim to legitimacy. There was the Domestic Faction — communists who had stayed in Korea during the colonial period, working underground, suffering imprisonment and torture in the name of resistance. There was the Yan’an Faction — Korean communists who had fought alongside Mao’s forces in China and returned with the prestige of the Chinese revolutionary tradition behind them. There was the Soviet Faction — Koreans who, like Kim himself, had spent the war years in the USSR and returned with Moscow’s backing. And there were the nationalists, the Christians, the moderate democrats — people like Cho Man-sik who had imagined that liberation from Japan might mean something other than the installation of a new authoritarian system.

Kim eliminated them all, in sequence and with patience.

The nationalists went first, sidelined and then arrested as the political framework was reorganized along Soviet lines. In March 1946, Kim’s government enacted sweeping land reform — redistributing land from landlords to peasants without compensation, a move that was genuinely popular with the rural majority and gave Kim a base of support independent of Soviet bayonets. Industries previously owned by the Japanese were nationalized. A Soviet-style constitution was drafted. The infrastructure of a totalitarian state was assembled with remarkable speed.

Then Kim turned on his communist rivals. The Domestic Faction was accused of collaborating with Japanese colonial authorities — a charge with enough truth in it to be useful and enough flexibility to be applied selectively. The Yan’an Faction, which had its own military commanders and its own ideas about how North Korea should be governed, was broken in stages — its most capable general, Mu Chong, was expelled from the party as a scapegoat for military failures during the Korean War. The Soviet Faction, which had been Kim’s earliest base of support, was dismantled once it was no longer needed, its members ousted from leadership positions in the early 1950s.

By the time Kim had finished, there were no factions. There was only Kim.

The God-King Project

What Kim built in the ruins of his rivals was not simply a communist state on the Soviet model. It was something stranger and more total — a political religion with Kim himself as its deity.

The cult of personality that surrounded Kim Il-sung was the most elaborate and comprehensive in the history of communist states, surpassing even Stalin’s and Mao’s in its theological ambition. It began modestly enough — portraits in public buildings, laudatory articles in state newspapers, the standard equipment of Stalinist governance. But Kim and his ideologues pushed it far beyond anything the Soviet model had contemplated.

Kim was not merely the Great Leader. He was the Father of the Nation, the Sun of the Nation, the Lodestar of the Twenty-First Century. His birthplace outside Pyongyang became a sacred site of pilgrimage. His mother was declared the Mother of Korea, her image venerated alongside his. His father’s association with anti-Japanese activism was amplified into heroic legend. His great-grandfather was retroactively credited with leading the attack on an American gunboat that had sailed up the Taedong River in 1866 — a claim with no historical evidence, but useful for establishing the Kim family as perpetual defenders of the peninsula against foreign aggression.

The ideology underpinning all of this was juche — self-reliance. First articulated in a 1955 speech, juche asserted that North Korea must be the master of its own destiny, dependent on no foreign power, guided by its own wisdom and strength. It was, in its origins, partly a practical political move: Kim was distancing himself from Moscow at a moment when the Soviets, under Khrushchev, were denouncing Stalin’s cult of personality and making clear that Soviet-bloc states were expected to follow suit. Kim had no intention of following suit. Juche gave him the ideological framework to refuse — if Korea was self-reliant, it did not need to take its cues from anyone, including the Soviet Union.

Over time, juche evolved from a political philosophy into something closer to a state religion. It provided the answer to every question a citizen might ask: why is the country poor? Because self-reliance requires sacrifice, and sacrifice is noble. Why is the outside world hostile? Because it has always been hostile to Korean independence, from the Japanese colonialists to the American imperialists. Why must the leader be obeyed without question? Because the leader embodies the collective will of the Korean people, and to question him is to question Korea itself.

The songbun system — the hereditary loyalty classification that divided every North Korean citizen into core, wavering, and hostile categories based on their family’s political history — was the administrative enforcement mechanism beneath the ideological superstructure. Your grandfather’s perceived loyalty to the Japanese colonial administration could determine your own access to food, education, and housing three generations later. The system created a population stratified by inherited suspicion, in which upward mobility required constant demonstration of devotion to the Kim family and downward mobility could be triggered by a single wrong word.

In practice, songbun governed every significant transaction in a North Korean life. University admission, job assignment, military posting, access to rationed food, permission to live in Pyongyang — all of it filtered through a classification that citizens could not see, could not challenge, and could not escape. The child of a man who had served in the Japanese colonial police was classified hostile regardless of the child’s own conduct. The grandchild of a landlord dispossessed by the 1946 land reform carried that stain into every application form. Conversely, the descendants of anti-Japanese guerrillas and Korean War heroes enjoyed privileged access to the system’s scarce goods. Loyalty was not merely demanded in the present. It was inherited from the past and projected into the future — a perpetual accounting of bloodline and biography that bound each generation to the political choices of the ones before it. The Organization Guidance Department of the Korean Workers’ Party maintained these records and quietly controlled who rose, who stagnated, and who disappeared. Many experts believe this department, rather than any formal government institution, was the true centre of power in North Korea from the 1960s onward — the invisible spine around which the visible theatre of governance was arranged.

The Instrument and the Institution

There is a persistent temptation in Western analysis to view Kim Il-sung as simply a Soviet puppet — a compliant instrument of Moscow’s Cold War strategy, installed and maintained by foreign power, lacking any genuine domestic base. The record does not support this reading.

Kim was installed with Soviet backing. That is true and important. But he outgrew his installation with remarkable speed. He played China against the Soviet Union and the Soviet Union against China throughout the Korean War and its aftermath, extracting maximum material support from both while refusing to be wholly subordinated to either. When Khrushchev demanded de-Stalinization, Kim refused. When the Yan’an Faction, backed by China, attempted a coup against Kim at the 1956 Korean Workers’ Party Congress — a genuine attempt to remove him — he crushed it with such thorough brutality that China and the Soviet Union, who had quietly encouraged the attempt, backed down rather than escalate. He was not a man you could remove.

By the time he had consolidated absolute power in the late 1950s, Kim Il-sung was no longer the Soviets’ Korean. He was simply Korea’s Kim. The state he had built was not a satellite. It was a hermit kingdom reborn — closed, self-referential, hostile to external influence, and governed by a man who had internalized the lessons of every foreign subjugation Korea had ever suffered and drawn from them a single, iron conclusion: never again would a foreign power choose Korea’s leader.

The irony that a foreign power had chosen him was not lost on historians. Whether it was lost on Kim is another question entirely.

What He Left Behind

Kim Il-sung died on July 8, 1994, after fifty years of absolute rule. He left behind a state of extraordinary internal coherence and extraordinary external dysfunction — a political system so perfectly designed for the perpetuation of his family’s power that it has now, thirty years after his death, successfully transferred that power through two more generations without a single serious challenge to the dynastic principle.

He also left behind a nuclear weapons programme in its earliest stages, an ideology of self-reliance that had become a rationale for mass poverty, and a population so thoroughly insulated from outside information that millions of people genuinely mourned his passing with a grief that outsiders struggled to interpret as anything other than performance. Some of it was performance. But not all of it. Kim had built something real from the colonial wound and the Stalinist toolkit — a national identity so total, so immersive, so woven into every aspect of daily life, that for many North Koreans it was not distinguishable from reality itself.

His grandson is still building on that foundation. The tools have changed — nuclear warheads instead of Soviet rifles, cryptocurrency theft instead of gold smuggling. But the project is the same: make Korea impossible to absorb, impossible to ignore, and impossible to destroy.

The Soviet’s Korean became Korea’s eternal president. The eternal president became a god. The god’s grandson is now a peer of Putin and Xi, standing on a balcony in Beijing, clapping.

The foundation holds.
