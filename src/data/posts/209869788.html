It’s tempting, watching SpaceX sign carrier after carrier across four continents, to assume the losers in this story are obvious: the terrestrial carriers being disrupted, the traditional telecom order being upended by a rocket company that decided phones were next. Parts I through IV should have already complicated that picture. Rogers isn’t a victim of Starlink — it’s a willing, profiting partner. Airtel Africa isn’t losing anything; it’s monetizing a customer base it already had. The disruption in this story isn’t happening to the companies signing the deals. It’s happening one level away, to businesses whose entire reason for existing was standing in the gap that direct-to-cell is now quietly closing.

The businesses built entirely on the gap

Start with the companies whose founding premise was coverage where the Big Three couldn’t or wouldn’t go. In Canada alone, this is a longer list than most people realize: Iristel and its Ice Wireless brand serving Nunavut, the Northwest Territories, and Yukon; SSi Canada’s Qiniq network doing the same for Nunavut specifically; TBayTel covering Northwestern Ontario; SaskTel holding Saskatchewan as a provincial monopoly precisely because building conventional infrastructure across that province’s low-density geography never attracted the national carriers in the first place. Every one of these companies’ core competitive advantage is the same sentence: we go where Bell, Rogers, and Telus don’t.

Direct-to-cell attacks that sentence directly, and it does so without requiring any of these regional carriers to do anything wrong. Rogers doesn’t need to build a single tower in Nunavut to start offering its customers satellite coverage there. Once national carriers can plausibly claim “coverage everywhere in Canada” through a satellite add-on, the entire commercial rationale for choosing a northern-specialist carrier over a national one starts to erode — not because the regional carrier’s terrestrial network got worse, but because the national carrier’s satellite layer made the geographic gap that justified switching to a regional specialist in the first place largely disappear.

This is a slower, quieter disruption than the dramatic “fourth carrier enters the market” story SpaceX’s US ambitions generate, but it may end up mattering more to more people, because it’s happening in dozens of countries simultaneously, to companies too small to make headlines when they lose customers one at a time.

It’s worth being precise about the mechanism here, because it isn’t head-to-head price or service competition in the usual sense. A regional carrier like Iristel doesn’t lose a customer because Rogers undercuts its price in Nunavut — Rogers likely never bothers matching Iristel’s price at all. It loses the customer because the entire reason someone chose a northern specialist in the first place — “my national carrier doesn’t reliably work up here” — stops being true, at which point the customer has no remaining reason to pay a specialist’s premium or tolerate a specialist’s smaller retail footprint down south. The disruption arrives sideways, through the erosion of a justification, rather than head-on through a competing offer.

TerreStar, Skylo, and the wholesale suppliers squeezed from both sides

There’s a subtler casualty hiding in this same space: the wholesale satellite-connectivity suppliers who built a business model around selling coverage to existing carriers, rather than building their own retail brand. TerreStar Solutions, working with Skylo, positioned itself explicitly as a business-to-business supplier — its own CEO has described the company’s ambition as selling satellite services to Bell, Telus, or Rogers, rather than competing with them directly. Telus itself ran a satellite-to-smartphone trial with TerreStar and Skylo back in 2023, and TerreStar holds genuine nationwide 2-GHz spectrum, which ought to make it an attractive wholesale partner.

The problem is timing, not technology. By the time TerreStar’s offering matured, Rogers had already signed with SpaceX, and Bell and Telus had already aligned with AST SpaceMobile. A wholesale supplier’s entire value proposition depends on being the answer to a question a carrier hasn’t already answered, and in Canada’s case, all three major carriers had already answered it before TerreStar’s commercial service was ready to compete for the business. This is the sharpest possible illustration of a lesson that runs through this whole series: in a market this fast-moving, being early to a partnership matters more than being technically superior, because the carrier relationship, once signed, isn’t easily reopened.

There’s a related casualty worth naming briefly: the dedicated satellite Internet-of-Things connectivity providers who built businesses around tracking shipping containers, agricultural equipment, and remote industrial sensors — a market that assumed cellular coverage simply wouldn’t reach those assets. SpaceX has already demonstrated CAT-1 IoT device connectivity over its direct-to-cell network during testing, which points toward the same commoditization pattern playing out one tier below consumer messaging: a specialized industry built on filling a coverage gap, watching that gap get folded into a mainstream carrier’s standard feature set rather than remaining a business someone has to pay a premium provider to solve.

Dedicated hardware, and the disruption that hasn’t quite arrived

The most visible casualty ought to be the satellite messenger hardware business — Garmin’s inReach line, Zoleo, and the standalone satellite communicators built for hikers, sailors, and backcountry travellers who’ve spent two decades paying a premium specifically for connectivity their phone couldn’t provide. Direct-to-cell, on its face, makes the entire category obsolete: why buy and subscribe to a separate device when your existing phone can talk to a satellite directly?

The actual picture is more interesting, and less one-sided, than that framing suggests. Independent testing in 2026 found that dedicated satellite messengers still meaningfully outperform smartphone direct-to-cell integration on two dimensions that matter most in a genuine emergency: signal acquisition speed and battery endurance. A dedicated device built around a small, efficient antenna and a battery that lasts weeks rather than a day is still, mechanically, a better tool for the specific job of “I am in trouble and my phone is nearly dead.” Garmin hasn’t responded to the threat by disappearing — it responded by restructuring its subscription pricing to eliminate the punitive “pay when not using it” fees that used to frustrate seasonal users, and by partnering more closely with Iridium’s own network rather than competing against it. Apple, for its part, chose to integrate Globalstar’s satellite network directly into the iPhone rather than build or acquire its own capability, hedging its bets in exactly the multi-vendor way Veon does with wholesale carrier deals in Part II.

The honest read is that this category isn’t being destroyed so much as it’s being squeezed into a narrower, more specialized niche: serious backcountry users and professionals who need genuine reliability will likely keep paying for dedicated hardware, while the much larger population of casual hikers and day-trippers who only wanted “some way to text if something goes wrong” will migrate to whatever their existing phone plan already includes for free or near-free. That’s still a real disruption — it shrinks the addressable market for companies like Garmin’s satellite division substantially — but it’s a slower, more survivable one than “the entire category disappears.”

There’s a further complication worth naming: the underlying satellite networks themselves are getting tangled together rather than staying cleanly separated by category. Garmin’s inReach line runs on Iridium’s constellation, and the two companies’ commercial relationship has grown close enough that Iridium now hosts Garmin’s own inReach product pages directly on its site — a level of integration that blurs the line between “dedicated hardware maker” and “satellite network operator” considerably. Meanwhile, emerging non-terrestrial-network standards like Skylo point toward a longer-term convergence where the distinction this section has been drawing — dedicated messenger versus smartphone direct-to-cell — stops being a meaningful dividing line at all, and the real competitive question becomes which underlying constellation a given device or carrier chooses to ride on, regardless of what the retail product looks like on the outside.

The premium satellite-phone business, and the customers who can’t afford to be wrong

One tier further up sits the traditional satellite phone industry — Iridium’s own handset business, and companies like Inmarsat serving maritime, aviation, and enterprise customers who’ve paid a serious premium for guaranteed, dedicated satellite communication for decades. This is the segment direct-to-cell threatens least, and for a telling reason: the customers paying for a true satellite phone aren’t paying for convenience. They’re paying for certainty in situations where “usually works” isn’t good enough — a cargo ship mid-ocean, a research station in Antarctica, a disaster-response team that cannot afford a dropped connection during a rescue.

Direct-to-cell, as this series has documented repeatedly, is explicitly a supplemental technology — SMS and basic data today, with voice and broader data still maturing. That’s an entirely different value proposition from a dedicated satellite phone’s always-on, purpose-built reliability. The premium satellite-phone market will likely feel real pricing pressure at its lower edge, where casual users who only wanted occasional emergency backup migrate toward cheaper smartphone-based options. But its core enterprise and mission-critical customers have a different problem entirely, and it’s the same one Shotwell described SpaceX itself chasing in Part II’s discussion of enterprise Starlink growth: reliability sells at a premium regardless of what’s happening in the consumer tier below it.

The MVNO ecosystem’s disappearing differentiator

Finally, there’s a subtler casualty inside the telecom industry itself, and it follows directly from the Japan case study in Part IV. Once KDDI, NTT Docomo, and SoftBank all run functionally identical Starlink-powered satellite coverage, competition on that dimension evaporates — which is exactly why KDDI pivoted to differentiating on service design (its dedicated SOS centre, its IoT push) rather than network claims. The same dynamic threatens a slice of the broader MVNO ecosystem discussed in earlier parts of this series: small carriers and resellers whose marketing leaned on “we cover more of the country than you’d expect” lose that differentiator entirely once satellite coverage becomes a checkbox every carrier, large or small, can offer as a wholesale add-on. Coverage stops being a competitive axis and becomes table stakes — good news for consumers, but a real problem for any brand whose entire pitch was built on a gap that’s rapidly closing.

The pattern beneath all of it

Line these five categories up and a single principle emerges: the businesses genuinely at risk from this expansion aren’t the incumbents SpaceX is trying to compete with directly. They’re the businesses whose value proposition depended on a gap — geographic, technical, or competitive — that direct-to-cell is in the process of closing. A gap-filler’s entire business model is a bet that the gap stays open. Direct-to-cell’s whole premise is closing gaps as fast as regulatory approval allows. Those two facts cannot coexist indefinitely, and the businesses built entirely around the gap are the ones with the least room to adapt.

It’s worth being fair to the counter-argument, too, because not every gap-filler is doomed equally. The businesses that survive this transition best will likely be the ones that can reposition around a genuinely different axis than pure coverage — Garmin’s pivot toward battery life and signal speed as its differentiator, TerreStar’s ambition to become a wholesale supplier rather than a retail competitor, the premium satellite-phone industry’s continued focus on mission-critical reliability over convenience. The businesses most at risk are the ones with no second axis to retreat to at all — a regional carrier whose entire pitch really was just “we work where the national carriers don’t,” and nothing else.
