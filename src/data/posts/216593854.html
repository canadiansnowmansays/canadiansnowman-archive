Stephen Gordon

I count my essays. Readers of this series know that by now, and know that every so often the count lands on a number that was already a year before I got to it, and I stop whatever I was doing and write about it.

This is essay 1,759, and it is one of the personal ones. I want to say that plainly at the top, as this series does. Not grave in the manner of 1,347 or 1,633 — though men die in it, and I will count them honestly — but personal, because every essay in this project has been written from a particular desk, in a particular city, in a particular language, under a particular flag, and all four of those particulars were decided in about fifteen minutes on a September morning in 1759, on a farmer’s field above the St. Lawrence River, a decade and a half before the country to my south existed and a century and change before mine did. I have written fifteen hundred essays about other people’s hinges. This is the entry where the count arrives at my own.

The situation, kept brief. By 1759 the Seven Years’ War — the actual first world war, fought on five continents, whatever the branding departments of later conflicts may claim — had brought a British fleet and army up the St. Lawrence to Quebec, the fortress capital of New France. The British commander was James Wolfe, thirty-two years old, sickly, difficult, and running out of season; the French commander was the Marquis de Montcalm, a professional soldier who had spent the summer declining, correctly, to be lured out from behind his walls. Quebec sits on a promontory; the river approaches were guarded; the cliffs upstream were presumed unclimbable; and the arithmetic of a Canadian September was on France’s side, because the fleet had to leave before the ice, and everyone on both sides could read a calendar. Montcalm did not need to win. He needed to subtract days, and the days were subtracting themselves.

What Wolfe found — and the historians still argue over how much was desperation, how much design, and how much a dying man’s indifference to odds — was a path. A cove upstream, the Anse-au-Foulon, where a track ran up the cliff face, thinly picketed because no serious force was expected to attempt it. On the night of September 12–13, 1759, British boats drifted down on the ebb tide in the dark, a French-speaking officer answered a sentry’s challenge convincingly enough to buy the minutes that mattered, and by dawn Wolfe had several thousand regulars standing in line of battle on the Plains of Abraham — the farm fields of one Abraham Martin, whose name thereby entered history because he had once owned a pasture — a mile from the city walls, on the one piece of ground Montcalm could not ignore.

And here the count compresses, which is why this essay exists. Montcalm, judging he could not wait for the detachments that were hours away — another disputed decision; the fraternity of hindsight has been second-guessing it since approximately lunchtime that day — attacked at mid-morning with the force at hand. The French line advanced; the British line, under orders of a discipline that still reads coldly on the page, held its fire until the range closed to a few dozen paces; and then it delivered what military historians have called the most perfect volley of the age — a single crashing sheet of fire, reloaded, repeated, and followed by the bayonet. The battle, as a contest, lasted about fifteen minutes. Both commanding generals were mortally hit — Wolfe dying on the field in the moment of victory, in the tableau that Benjamin West would later paint into imperial scripture, and Montcalm carried back through the gates to die before the next dawn. Quebec capitulated five days later. Montreal followed the next year, and at the Treaty of Paris in 1763, France ceded Canada — the whole vast watershed of it — to Britain, and kept instead her sugar islands, which were, by the sober accounting of the day, worth more.

Fifteen minutes. I have checked the figure against the serious histories and it holds within the tolerance this series permits: the morning was hours, the campaign was months, the war was seven years, but the decision — the interval in which the future of half a continent moved from one column to the other — fits inside a quarter of an hour. It is the shortest number on this series’ entire map, and I have been walking toward it since essay 1,066, and now that I am here I find what I always find at the hinges: the number is small and the freight is not.

Because here is what was actually decided in those fifteen minutes, stated from the desk where I feel it. It was decided that this essay would be written in English. It was decided that the sixty-some thousand French subjects then farming the St. Lawrence valley — the Canadiens, whose descendants number in the millions and whose language fills my country’s second column on every cereal box in my kitchen — would become a conquered people inside a British empire, and that Britain, having conquered them, would face the question that has defined my country ever since: what do you do with an addition you took by subtraction? And the answer Britain groped toward — haltingly, self-interestedly, with the American colonies turning mutinous to the south and no appetite for a second front — was the Quebec Act of 1774: the conquered keep their church, their civil law, their language, their land tenure. Readers of essay 1,707 will recognize the instrument instantly. It is the Scottish settlement, applied to a people who never signed it: a consolidation with protected subsidiaries, entrenched not by treaty between equals but by the cold prudence of an empire that had learned addition’s price. Out of that expedient — resented by the American colonists, litigated by every Canadian generation since, renegotiated in my own lifetime on the floor of arenas and in the small print of constitutions — came the country I live in: two languages, two legal traditions, one chronically reopened ledger, a federation that survives, exactly as 1707 taught, as long as the Equivalent keeps arriving. The fifteen minutes did not just transfer a colony. They created the problem whose continuous, imperfect, unfinished solving is Canada. I am not being wry. It is the least wry sentence in eighteen hundred essays: my country is what the fifteen minutes made necessary.

And I owe the conquered their number, because it is the most defiant figure in this essay and it belongs to them. In 1759 the Canadiens were perhaps sixty or seventy thousand souls, facing a British America of well over a million — a demographic ledger of twenty to one, in an age when conquered minorities of that proportion were routinely dissolved into their conquerors within a few generations, as a matter not even of policy but of arithmetic. The arithmetic failed. It failed because the Canadiens made the counting itself an act of resistance — la survivance, they came to call it, survival as a discipline — the parish registers kept, the language kept, the law kept, the children, above all, counted and raised in French at birthrates that demographers still cite with a kind of awe, until the sixty thousand had become millions and the twenty-to-one had been argued to a standstill. And the province those millions built stamps its verdict, to this day, on every automobile it licenses, in the official motto of Quebec: Je me souviens. I remember. An entire jurisdiction whose motor-vehicle registry doubles as a statement of mnemonic defiance. This series has spent eighteen centuries establishing that the count is how we say the days mattered. Quebec put it on the licence plates. I have nothing to teach a province like that; I can only file the tribute and note that the Numbers Game has been played on the shores of the St. Lawrence, at championship level, since before this country had a name.

And the year knew it, somehow. Sixteen fifty-nine — forgive me: 1759 — was christened by the British themselves the annus mirabilis, the year of victories, Minden and Lagos and Quiberon Bay and Quebec, the church bells of England worn thin with ringing; the second year of miracles in this series, and how telling that the first, essay 1,666, belonged to a solitary man with a prism, and this one to fleets. But the entry I cherish is quieter. In the very months that the empires were settling accounts on the Plains of Abraham, there was published in Europe a short satirical novel by Voltaire called Candide — 1759, the same year, the coincidence is exact — in which the narrator dismisses the entire North American war as two nations bankrupting themselves fighting over a few acres of snow. Quelques arpents de neige. It is the most famous sneer in the history of my country, published in the season of the battle itself, and I have spent my whole reading life composing the reply, and the reply is this essay, and it amounts to: the acres were underpriced, monsieur, and so was the snow. France kept the sugar. The sugar islands are lovely and I mean them no harm. But the few acres of snow are now the second-largest country on Earth, and this morning I rode an e-bike across a sliver of them beside a Siberian dog, in the only nation in the Americas where the conquered of 1759 still adjudicate the constitution in their own language, and I would not trade the ledger. Voltaire was the finest auditor of his century and he expensed the wrong asset, and it comforts me more than I can say that even he could misprice a future. It is the standing hazard of the trade.

Dior walked the field with me once. I should tell you that, because it is the only primary research this series has ever conducted. Three years ago, in Quebec City, on the battlefield park where the Plains are kept — mowed, monumented, jogged across by citizens of the country the fifteen minutes made — she moved over the grass with her nose down, reading whatever the ground still says in the languages she reads, and she stopped at no monument, because monuments are addressed to us, and she paid out her attention instead on the wind coming up off the river, which is the one witness still on site. Wolfe has an obelisk; Montcalm has a memorial he shares, in the end, with his enemy — the city raised a column inscribed to the courage of both, which may be the most Canadian object in existence — and the wind has the river, and Dior, who does not know fifteen minutes from fifteen years, sat down on the decisive ground of my civilization and scratched her ear. I have thought about that often. The field has returned to being a field. That is not amnesia. That is what winning the peace looks like, two hundred and sixty-seven years on: grass, joggers, a dog scratching, two languages on the interpretive signs, and the count kept — honestly, in both columns — by essays like this one.

The count moves on, as it does, and it stays in the family of wonder for a while, which after this entry I think we have earned. The next landmark is essay 1,769, and it is one of the finest numbers on the map: the year the entire species, for the first time, performed a single coordinated measurement — dozens of expeditions, rival empires cooperating in the middle of their rivalries, all timing the same small black dot crossing the sun, to compute the one number nobody had: the actual size of everything. A Yorkshire farm boy named Cook carried one of the stopwatches. The scurvy ledger of essay 1,522, I am pleased to report, finally gets its correction.

Fifteen minutes on a field. Every essay in this series since has been, in the strictest sense, a dispatch from the outcome.

See you at 1,769, when the whole species takes a measurement.
