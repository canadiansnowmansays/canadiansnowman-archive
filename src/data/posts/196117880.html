In 1421, the largest fleet the world had ever assembled left the port of Nanjing and sailed into the unknown.

It was not the unknown to the Chinese. They had been trading across the Indian Ocean for centuries. But the scale of what Admiral Zheng He commanded that year has no parallel in the history of exploration. The fleet numbered somewhere between 200 and 300 vessels. The largest ships — the treasure ships, the baochuan — were roughly 400 feet long, dwarfing anything that floated in European waters. Christopher Columbus would not cross the Atlantic for another 71 years. When he did, his three ships combined would have fit on the deck of a single Chinese treasure vessel.

Zheng He sailed to Southeast Asia, the Indian subcontinent, the Persian Gulf, and the east coast of Africa. He did not conquer. He did not establish colonies. He did not claim territory in the European sense. He collected tribute, exchanged gifts, and returned with exotic animals — a giraffe famously presented to the Yongle Emperor, who took it as a sign of divine approval. The purpose of the voyages was not expansion. It was demonstration.

China already knew it was the centre of the world. The voyages were a reminder to everyone else.

Admiral Zheng He and the treasure fleets. Not conquerors, but emissaries of the Middle Kingdom, returning with tribute and wonders — including giraffes that the Yongle Emperor saw as heavenly approval.

The name tells you everything. Zhōngguó — 中国 — the Middle Kingdom. Not a kingdom in the middle of other kingdoms. The Middle Kingdom as cosmological fact: the country at the centre of civilisation, surrounded by tributary states and barbarians in descending order of cultural attainment. The emperor was the Son of Heaven. His court was the axis around which the known world revolved. Foreign rulers did not have diplomatic relations with China. They had tributary relations — they sent envoys bearing gifts, performed the kowtow, and received imperial recognition of their authority in return.

This was not arrogance in the Western sense. It was a worldview, and for most of recorded history, it was defensible as fact. China was, by any measurable standard, the most advanced civilisation on earth for the majority of the past two millennia. The question worth examining — the question that explains everything Xi Jinping does today — is how a country holding that position for that long came to spend the 19th century being carved up by foreign powers, and what the memory of that fall has produced in the country trying to return.

Chang’an under the Tang (618–907), the largest and most cosmopolitan city on Earth. While Europe was in relative darkness, merchants, monks, and scholars from across Asia converged on the true centre of civilisation.

The Tang dynasty, which ruled from 618 to 907, gave the world woodblock printing, gunpowder, and the first paper money. Chang’an, the Tang capital, was the largest city on earth — a cosmopolitan metropolis of perhaps one million people at a time when London was a muddy trading post of perhaps 10,000. Merchants, monks, diplomats, and scholars from across Central Asia, Persia, India, and Korea crowded its markets. The Silk Road passed through it. The world came to China, not because China invited it, but because China was where the things worth having were made.

The Song dynasty (960–1279) — builders of the world’s first standing professional navy, inventors of the compass, mechanical clock, and suspension bridge. In 1078, Song iron production reached 125,000 tonnes annually, a level England would not match until the Industrial Revolution. Bustling commercial cities like Kaifeng and Hangzhou thrived on paper money, massive internal trade, and Indian Ocean networks.

The Song dynasty, which followed, produced the compass, the mechanical clock, and the suspension bridge. Song China had the world’s first standing professional navy. Its iron output in 1078 has been estimated at 125,000 tonnes per year — a figure England would not match until the 18th century, during the height of the Industrial Revolution. Song merchants traded across the entire Indian Ocean network. Song paper money made transactions possible at a scale that would not appear in Europe for centuries.

Ming imperial court in the Forbidden City. The dynasty that launched the largest fleets in history soon chose to recall them. What began as a demonstration of centrality became a deliberate withdrawal from the world beyond the tributary system.

The Ming dynasty — the dynasty of Zheng He’s voyages — inherited this accumulated achievement and briefly turned it outward. But the Yongle Emperor who commissioned the great fleets died in 1424, three years after the largest of the voyages departed. His successors were Confucian administrators who viewed the voyages as expensive vanity. The treasure ships were ordered dismantled. The records of the voyages were, in some accounts, deliberately destroyed by court officials who considered outward expansion a distraction from internal governance. China turned inward.

This is the first great irony of Chinese history: the country that had the capacity to project power across the known world decided not to. Not because it lacked the ships or the navigators or the organisational capacity. Because the Confucian worldview that governed the court did not value what the voyages were demonstrating. The world already knew China was the centre. Why spend more treasure proving it?

The decision to turn inward was not catastrophic in the 15th century. China remained the world’s largest economy by a significant margin. The Ming dynasty governed a population of perhaps 200 million people — more than all of Europe combined. The internal market was vast enough to sustain sophisticated manufacturing, complex financial instruments, and intricate regional trade networks without needing external expansion.

What the inward turn foreclosed was adaptation.

The countries that were turning outward in the 15th and 16th centuries — Portugal, Spain, the Netherlands, eventually Britain — were not richer or more sophisticated than China. They were hungrier, more competitive with each other, and operating under a different logic. European states existed in a permanent condition of rivalry. That rivalry drove innovation in military technology, navigation, shipbuilding, and eventually in industrial production. The pressure of competition produced a pace of change that China, operating as the unchallenged centre of its world, had no equivalent incentive to match.

There is a paradox here that deserves a moment’s attention. China invented gunpowder. Chinese alchemists discovered it in the 9th century while searching for an elixir of immortality — which is as good a metaphor as history provides for the gap between intention and consequence. For several centuries, China led the world in the military application of gunpowder: fire arrows, bombs, early cannons. Then the technology transferred westward along the Silk Road, and European metallurgists — driven by the relentless pressure of interstate warfare — refined it into something qualitatively different. The cast-iron cannon that British warships carried into Chinese waters in 1839 was descended from Chinese invention and had been improved beyond Chinese recognition by the competitive pressure of European conflict. China invented the tool. Europe weaponised it. The irony is not lost on Chinese historians.

The same pattern repeated in ship design, in navigation, in eventually in industrial organisation. Not because Chinese engineers and scholars were less capable — the Song dynasty’s iron output alone disproves that — but because the institutional logic of the Middle Kingdom created no pressure for the kind of compounding innovation that European rivalry demanded. The court rewarded stability. It rewarded Confucian administration. It did not reward disruption, and disruption is what the Industrial Revolution was.

By the 18th century, the gap had become consequential. When Lord Macartney arrived in Beijing in 1793 carrying British manufactured goods — clocks, telescopes, scientific instruments — and requesting trading rights, the Qianlong Emperor replied in terms that have echoed through Chinese historical consciousness ever since: “We possess all things. I set no value on objects strange or ingenious, and have no use for your country’s manufactures.”

We possess all things… I set no value on objects strange or ingenious.” Qianlong Emperor to King George III, 1793. A civilisation so secure in its centrality that it saw no need to engage with the hungry rivals on its periphery.

Macartney’s mission failed completely. He refused to perform the kowtow — the full prostration required of tributary envoys — and the emperor refused to grant the trading rights the British East India Company wanted. The two civilisations met and found each other incomprehensible. Macartney returned to Britain with nothing. His journal described a country of impressive size and evident sophistication that had, in his assessment, stopped moving. He compared it to a first-rate man-of-war — magnificent, but decaying at anchor.

What Macartney could not see, and what the Qianlong Emperor could not see either, was that the anchor was about to be cut. Not by a diplomatic mission. By warships.

Forty-seven years later, British vessels arrived without a diplomatic mission. They had gunpowder too. Theirs had been weaponised into cannon that could destroy Chinese junks from distances those junks could not answer. The gap that had opened while China looked inward had become a military gap — and a military gap, in the 19th century, was settled quickly and without ceremony.

It is worth being precise about what China was in 1800, because the humiliation that followed is only comprehensible against that baseline.

China accounted for roughly 33 percent of global GDP in 1820, according to the Maddison historical estimates — the single largest share held by any country in the modern era. Its population was approximately 380 million people. Its silk, porcelain, and tea dominated global trade. European merchants had spent two centuries trying to find goods China wanted to buy in exchange for Chinese goods; they had largely failed, because China produced what it needed. The British solution to this trade imbalance — flooding China with Indian opium — was the proximate cause of the wars that followed. But the deeper cause was the gap between a civilisation that had believed itself the centre for so long that it had stopped watching the edges, and civilisations that had spent centuries being sharpened by competition and hunger.

The Qianlong Emperor’s letter to George III was not delusion. It was an accurate description of the world as it had existed for two thousand years. It became delusional in the decades after it was written, as the world changed faster than the court could perceive.

The first Opium War, 1839 to 1842, ended with the Treaty of Nanking — the first of the unequal treaties that would define the Century of Humiliation. Britain received Hong Kong island in perpetuity. Five ports were opened to British trade and residence. China paid an indemnity of 21 million silver dollars. The kowtow question — the ritual that Macartney had refused — was rendered moot. Nobody was kowtowing to Beijing anymore.

British warships destroy Chinese junks during the First Opium War (1839–1842). The technology China had invented centuries earlier — gunpowder — had been refined by European competition into weapons that ended the old order.

I want to be careful here about a version of this history that gets told in Western accounts and that strips the story of its full weight.

The standard Western framing treats the Opium Wars as a trade dispute that escalated, regrettable but understandable in the context of 19th-century imperial competition. What that framing obscures is the specific nature of the humiliation: a civilisation that had been the world’s most sophisticated for two millennia, that had genuinely believed — with substantial historical justification — that it occupied a unique position in the order of things, was defeated militarily by countries it had considered peripheral. Not merely defeated. Carved up. Made to watch foreign powers establish treaty ports on its soil, govern their own citizens under their own laws on Chinese territory, and conduct trade on terms the foreign powers dictated.

The Chinese term for this period — bǎinián guóchǐ, the Century of National Humiliation — is not rhetoric. It is a precise description of how the experience was encoded in national memory. The century runs from the Treaty of Nanking in 1842 to the founding of the People’s Republic in 1949. The foreign intrusions multiplied during those years: France took Vietnam, Britain took Burma, Japan took Korea and then large portions of China itself during the Second Sino-Japanese War. Russia extracted territorial concessions in the north. Eight foreign armies marched through Beijing in 1900 to suppress the Boxer Uprising and protect their legations. The Forbidden City — the literal centre of the known world, the residence of the Son of Heaven — was occupied by foreign troops.

Foreign troops inside the Forbidden City after the Boxer Uprising, 1900. The literal centre of the world occupied. This is the memory that still shapes every Chinese leader from Mao to Xi: what happens when the Middle Kingdom becomes weak.

Every Chinese leader since 1949 has governed in the shadow of that century. Every foreign policy decision, every territorial claim, every negotiating position has been shaped by the institutional memory of what happens when China is weak. Xi Jinping did not invent the restoration narrative. He inherited it from every leader before him and made it more explicit.

Which brings us back to Zheng He.

In 2005, China celebrated the 600th anniversary of the first treasure fleet voyage with a national commemoration that would have been incomprehensible to any previous generation. Zheng He became a national hero — his image on postage stamps, his story in textbooks, a museum dedicated to him in Nanjing. The state television documentary about his voyages framed them as evidence of China’s historically peaceful rise: China had the power to conquer and chose not to. It projected influence through trade and tribute rather than colonisation.

The historical revisionism in this framing is significant — Zheng He’s voyages were not entirely peaceful, and the tribute system was a form of dominance however it was decorated — but the political logic is clear. Xi’s China is telling a story about itself that begins with the treasure fleets, runs through the Century of Humiliation, and arrives at the present moment as the return of the rightful order. The Long Return, as I have chosen to call this series, is not a metaphor Xi would reject.

The 141 pages of the 15th Five-Year Plan — tabled in the Great Hall of the People on March 5, 2026 — are, among other things, a document about the restoration of centrality. The quantum networks, the humanoid robots, the rare earth dominance, the semiconductor ambitions: these are the 21st-century equivalent of the treasure ships. Not conquest. Demonstration. A reminder to everyone else of who occupies the centre.

Whether that return is achieved, how it is achieved, and what it costs — the Chinese people and the rest of the world — is what this series will examine, one century at a time.

I approach this history as a Canadian outsider who has visited China multiple times and found it consistently extraordinary. The scale of the cities, the quality of the infrastructure, the evident competence of the people running the systems — none of it is myth. But visitors to China see what the system permits visitors to see. The bullet trains are real. The skylines are real. What is not visible to a foreigner walking through Shenzhen or Beijing is the grip that produces them: the self-censorship that costs nothing to enforce because it costs everything to resist, the political system that can mobilise national resources at a scale no democracy can match, and the historical memory that makes that mobilisation feel, to many Chinese citizens, not like repression but like destiny.

I used to send my essays to a friend in Shenzhen. He never commented on the political ones. He never explained why. He didn’t need to. That silence is the system working exactly as designed — and it is the system that the treasure fleet voyages, the Qianlong Emperor’s letter, the Opium Wars, and the 141 pages are all, in their different ways, about.

The treasure fleets are gone. The 141 pages remain.
