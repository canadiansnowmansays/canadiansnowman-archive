Almost everyone reading this has had RSV. Most of us caught it before our second birthday, and most of us remember nothing about it, because for most of us it was a runny nose and a cough that our parents mopped up and forgot. Respiratory syncytial virus is the most ordinary of childhood infections, right up until it isn’t. In the United States it is the leading reason infants are admitted to hospital in their first year of life, and it kills thousands of older adults every winter. It rises as the weather cools. Which is to say: it is rising now, in the third week of October 2026, as I write this from a Toronto morning that has finally decided it is autumn.

This is the season when a health secretary earns his salary. There is an RSV vaccine for adults over a certain age, first approved by the FDA in 2023. There is a maternal vaccine for women late in pregnancy. There are monoclonal antibody shots for newborns that the CDC credits with keeping roughly eight in ten protected babies out of intensive care. A health secretary with two national television appearances in a single day — which Robert F. Kennedy Jr. had on August 2, 2026 — could do a great deal of good by saying, plainly, get the shot, protect the baby, protect your mother.

He used both appearances to tell America that RSV escaped from a laboratory.

What He Said, and Where

The first appearance was CNN’s State of the Union with Dana Bash. As FactCheck.org documented in its review of that interview, Kennedy described RSV as “the biggest killer of kids in this country today” and attributed it to the same kind of risky laboratory work he blames for COVID-19 and Lyme disease. Bash pushed back. Kennedy told her she was wrong. Later the same day, on Fox News, he dropped the qualifiers entirely and said simply that RSV came out of a lab.

Both claims are false, and neither is close.

On the first: RSV is not the leading killer of American children. Cancer kills more of them. FactCheck.org noted that RSV has historically been estimated to kill somewhere between 100 and 300 American children under five each year — every one of them a catastrophe for a family, and every one of them a reason to promote the antibody shots, not a statistic to be inflated into a slogan.

On the second: RSV did not leak from anywhere. It was found in a laboratory, which is a different thing, in roughly the way that Lake Ontario was found by Étienne Brûlé and not invented by him.

Tom Bartlett, writing in The Atlantic on October 15, 2026, has now traced exactly how Kennedy got from the first fact to the second fiction, and the route is worth walking, because it is the RFK Files in miniature.

What Actually Happened at Walter Reed

The kernel of truth is real, and it is the reason the myth has legs.

In October 1955, fourteen chimpanzees housed at an annex of the Walter Reed Army Institute of Research in Silver Spring, Maryland, came down with what looked like a common cold. Army researchers did what researchers do: they swabbed throats, drew blood, isolated a virus, and deliberately infected a second group of chimps to confirm what they had found. They called it the chimpanzee coryza agent — “coryza” being the medical word for a head cold. In February 1956, a lab worker who had handled the second group fell ill, and testing showed he had the same virus.

Stop the story there and Kennedy almost has a case. Chimps get sick, a worker catches it, a new pathogen walks out the front door and into the world. It is a tidy little parable about scientists playing with fire.

The trouble is that the story does not stop there. It keeps going, and every subsequent chapter points the other way.

What Happened Next

In 1957, Robert Chanock, Bernard Roizman and Ruth Myers at the Johns Hopkins School of Hygiene and Public Health published the paper that gave the virus its human identity. Their laboratories isolated two strains, named “Long” and “Snyder” after the children they came from, from Baltimore infants with pneumonia, and showed they were indistinguishable from the chimpanzee agent. A companion paper by Chanock and Lawrence Finberg, published in the same November 1957 issue of the American Journal of Hygiene, looked at how widely the virus had spread.

The answer was: everywhere. By age four, about 80 per cent of the Baltimore children tested already carried antibodies to it. Inmates at a federal prison in Ohio carried them too. A virus that had supposedly jumped from a chimp colony to one lab worker in February 1956 could not, in eighteen months, have quietly infected four-fifths of the toddlers in an American city and the population of a federal penitentiary without anyone noticing a pandemic. The Hopkins researchers drew the obvious conclusion: this was a human virus that had found its way into the chimpanzees, probably from their human handlers, not the reverse.

Then came the confirmation. In 2007, a team at Vanderbilt University, publishing in Modern Pathology, went back through the hospital’s autopsy log for children who died of bronchiolitis between 1925 and 1959, re-stained the preserved lung tissue with RSV-specific antibodies, and found the virus. One of the positive cases was a child who died in 1931 — nearly a quarter century before the chimps at Silver Spring caught their colds. The researchers described it as the earliest confirmed RSV infection on record.

The trail runs back further still. An 1819 medical treatise describes an “acute suffocative catarrh” in infants — a choking build-up of mucus that matches RSV’s signature presentation. Genetic analysis of the virus’s mutation rate suggests its ancestors have been circulating in humans for centuries.

So the honest sentence is this: in 1955 the U.S. Army discovered, in its chimpanzees, a virus that human children had been catching since before Kennedy’s grandfather was born. Calling that a lab leak is like calling the discovery of Uranus an act of planetary construction.

The Activist in the Footnote

Every conspiracy theory has a provenance, and this one’s is unusually easy to trace, because Kennedy footnoted it.

In his 2021 book The Real Anthony Fauci, Kennedy cites a 2021 article titled “From Chimpanzees to Children: The Origins of RSV,” published by Children’s Health Defense, the organisation Kennedy founded and chaired. Its author was Lyn Redwood, a nurse practitioner and long-time anti-vaccine activist who believes her son’s autism was caused by the mercury-based preservative thimerosal in his infant vaccines. Redwood was among the so-called “mercury moms” who recruited Kennedy to the vaccine cause around 2005, and he has credited her with bringing him into it. She went on to serve as president of Children’s Health Defense.

Redwood’s article does something more ambitious than Kennedy’s television version. It suggests that RSV-infected cells from the Walter Reed chimps may somehow have contaminated the polio vaccine, and that the tainted vaccine then seeded the virus into millions of American children in the 1950s. That speculation is load-bearing for Kennedy’s larger and more grotesque argument that the polio vaccine did more harm than polio did.

It also rests on an error so basic that a nine-year-old with a zoo membership could spot it. The kidney cells used to grow the early polio vaccines came from monkeys — rhesus and cynomolgus macaques. Chimpanzees are not monkeys. They are apes. Redwood’s article refers to the Walter Reed chimps as “sick monkeys,” collapsing two different branches of the primate family tree into one, and the entire polio-vaccine theory depends on the collapse. Remove the confusion and the bridge between the chimp colony and the vaccine factory simply isn’t there.

When Bartlett put the 1931 lung-tissue paper in front of Redwood, she held her ground: she did not accept that RSV had been circulating in humans before 1956. She did not respond when he pointed out the monkey-chimpanzee mistake. On the polio question, she retreated to the oldest position in the speculative business — that she had merely raised a question, and that anyone saying her article claimed the vaccine spread RSV was misrepresenting her.

Which brings us to the person who did exactly that. In The Real Anthony Fauci, Kennedy cites Redwood’s article for the proposition that RSV spread through polio vaccines. Her question mark became his full stop. The speculation went into the book as fact, and the book went onto the bestseller lists.

From Footnote to Org Chart

In most governments, the story would end there, as an embarrassing episode in a politician’s back catalogue. In this one, the footnote got a desk.

In June 2025, months into Kennedy’s tenure, reports surfaced that Redwood was being brought into the CDC to help oversee vaccine safety. Senator Patty Murray wrote to Kennedy on June 27, 2025, in a letter demanding he reverse the decision, noting Redwood’s leadership of Children’s Health Defense and its record of spreading vaccine falsehoods. The same month, after Kennedy fired all seventeen members of the CDC’s Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices, Redwood presented to the newly installed panel on thimerosal. She is now, according to Bartlett’s reporting, a senior adviser to the Secretary himself.

Readers of this series will recognise the shape. In “Friends in All the Wrong Places,” the Children’s Health Defense conference where Kennedy boasted of having a friend in the White House was held on the same day his department gutted the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force. The pattern is not that Kennedy occasionally listens to the wrong people. It is that he has systematically replaced the people who could correct him with the people who taught him what he already believes. Larry Anderson of Emory University, who has studied RSV for decades, laughed so long when Bartlett read him Kennedy’s claim that Bartlett had to wait for an answer. Anderson does not work for the Secretary. Redwood does.

Three Origin Stories, Zero True

The final act of this farce came when Bartlett asked HHS to clarify the Secretary’s actual position.

A department spokesperson replied that RSV “originated from a troop of infected chimpanzees,” and that it then spread to lab workers and from them to the general public — but, the spokesperson specified, not through vaccination.

The department’s official position now explicitly contradicts the Secretary’s bestselling book, which explicitly said the vaccine was the vector. Neither version is true. Both are being defended. And no one at HHS appears to have noticed that the Secretary’s own press office has quietly abandoned the polio-vaccine theory that was the whole point of the original story — the bit about vaccines, which was the bit that mattered to him.

This is the Obfuscate stage of the TACO pattern operating at its purest. Nothing is ever retracted. Positions are simply swapped out, the old one left lying where it fell, the new one delivered with the same confidence, and the burden of noticing the contradiction left to whoever has the patience to line them up. When the health secretary cannot keep his own fictions consistent, the problem is no longer that he is wrong about history. It is that his department has no mechanism for being right about anything.

Why a Sixty-Year-Old Chimp Matters

It would be easy to treat this as a curiosity — a strange little hobby-horse about a 1955 cold in a monkey house, sorry, ape house. It is not.

First, there is the season. Every minute Kennedy spends telling Fox viewers that RSV escaped from the Army is a minute he does not spend telling a 72-year-old in Ohio that there is a vaccine for it, or telling a woman thirty-four weeks pregnant that there is a shot that will protect her newborn through its first winter. The national-security frame this series has used since April — that Kennedy’s dismantling of public-health infrastructure leaves the country exposed — has never been more literal. The threat isn’t a hypothetical future pandemic. It is the ordinary virus arriving this month, met by a public whose health secretary has spent his airtime on folklore. Earlier this year, his FDA opened scrutiny of the very antibody shots that have been driving down infant RSV hospitalisations, prompting paediatricians to worry aloud about what happens if American babies lose that protection.

Second, there is the damage to a legitimate argument. The risks of gain-of-function research are a real debate, conducted in serious journals by serious people. I took that debate seriously in the Coda to my Fauci series, “The Sentence Fauci Wrote Himself,” which examined the 2011 defence of bird-flu experiments that Fauci co-signed with Francis Collins. When the Secretary of Health and Human Services invents a lab leak for a virus that predates the laboratory, he does not strengthen the case for oversight. He discredits it. Every scientist who has to spend an interview explaining that babies were dying of RSV in 1931 is a scientist not being asked about the experiments that genuinely deserve scrutiny.

Third, there is the method, which is the subject of this entire series. The RSV myth is a working model of how Kennedy builds a policy. A loyalist floats a speculation. Kennedy upgrades it to fact. The fact goes into a book, the book into a worldview, the loyalist into the department, and the worldview into federal policy. At no stage does anyone with the expertise to say that’s a monkey, not a chimpanzee get a vote. The vaccine-injury reporting clinic in “Paid by the Report,” the narrowed childhood vaccine schedule in “The Director Who Wasn’t in the Room,” the hollowed-out task force in “Friends in All the Wrong Places” — these are the same assembly line producing different products.

The Man Who Got the Chocolates

I’ll end with someone who was actually in the room.

Philip Roane was a young technician at Johns Hopkins in the mid-1950s, and he did a good deal of the laboratory work that identified the virus in Baltimore’s children. He was not listed as an author on the 1957 paper. For his trouble, Chanock gave him a box of chocolates and a bottle of Scotch. Roane went on to a long career and is now, at 98, a professor of microbiology at Howard University.

In 2025, nearly seventy years after the fact, Roane finally got his byline on RSV. He co-authored, with Arturo Casadevall, Thomas Shenk and Bernard Roizman, a history of the virus’s discovery in the journal mBio, written after Johns Hopkins was designated a “Milestone in Microbiology” site in May 2024 for the work. It tells the story as it happened: the chimpanzee coryza agent at Walter Reed, the Long and Snyder strains in Baltimore, and the slow recognition that the chimps had caught a human disease.

There is a lesson in the two men, the technician and the Secretary. One spent a lifetime doing the work and waited seven decades for the credit. The other did none of the work, borrowed his conclusion from a friend who couldn’t tell a monkey from an ape, and broadcast it to millions in an afternoon. Roane’s version is the true one. Kennedy’s is the one with the larger audience. The job of an honest public-health system is to make sure those two facts are not the same fact.

This month, as the cases climb, it isn’t.
