A week before the midterm elections, the President told a rally in Iowa that “at some point we’re going to have to give them the hammer.” A week after them, he put it down.

On Tuesday, November 3, as Americans voted, the New York Times reported that Central Command had a plan to strike Iran beginning as early as the next day. Julian E. Barnes, Natan Odenheimer, Eric Schmitt, Adam Rasgon and Dustin Volz explained the timing. The campaign needed three aircraft carriers, and there were three in the region; after early November, one would have to go home. Early November was also a stretch of moonless nights, the darkness American pilots prefer.

The carriers were there. The nights were dark. The order did not come.

Instead, on Monday, November 9, the President said the war “militarily is effectively over.” On Thursday he took his Vice-President off the negotiations and announced that he did not need a deal either. “We’ve got their money, Hundreds of Billions of Dollars, and we’ve got their Strait!” By Sunday Iran had fired drones at Bahrain twice in three days, and Europe was counting Iranian plots. Washington had decided to wait. Tehran, its foreign ministry said, was “not waiting for anyone.”

Effectively Over

The day after the President’s remark, in Paris, his Secretary of State turned it into policy. American economic pressure was now effective enough, Marco Rubio said, that “there’s really no need for major combat operations any longer,” as Michael Crowley reported for the New York Times. He told the Group of 7 foreign ministers the same thing.

It is not quite peace. Rubio said the United States might still use force if Iran “crosses certain red lines,” which he did not name. A deal was possible but not soon, because Iran’s conditions were “absurd.” He would not say what outcome Washington was pursuing. “You do bad things, bad things will happen to you. That’s the way to read it.” On Monday the President called the policy “just keeping it the status quo.” On Tuesday Rubio rejected the phrase: “No, not status quo. The economic pressure is going to get even worse.” Israeli officials and Iran hawks in Washington want more bombing. Asked whether Israel supported the new approach, Rubio said he did not know.

The Reassignment

“I have asked Vice President Vance to focus his complete and total attention on the Massive Corruption and Crime taking place in Democrat run Cities and States,” the President wrote on Thursday, as Luke Broadwater reported for the New York Times. “We don’t need a deal with Iran. Those days have long since passed.”

Readers will remember the letter. In Part CIV, Vance wrote through Qatari mediators to Gen. Mohsen Rezaei proposing direct talks he would lead. In Part CV, Tehran was reviewing “concrete proposals” on its nuclear programme for the first time in weeks, and Vance had become the Qatari track’s leading American supporter. That track now has no senior American at the head of it.

The timing invites the obvious reading. On the morning after the midterms, Vance wrote that “the reason we lost is the war.” The President said he had not minded: “A lot of people didn’t like his comments. But not me.” Nine days later, the man who blamed the war was taken off the one file that might end it. A Thanksgiving-week trip his staff had planned to four Latin American countries was cancelled over the same days.

Fairness requires this diary to note what the reassignment does not prove. It does not prove the President has chosen war. A president who says he no longer needs a deal may simply be telling the truth about his priorities. Tehran heard it differently. After the vote, Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi wrote: “American voters have made their choice. Time for the White House to make its own. Our 2-day proposal stands.” A week later the White House said it did not need the proposal.

The Waiting Game

David E. Sanger of the New York Times summed up the new policy in a sentence: “Declare it is over, even if not much has changed.” The administration now says it no longer needs an agreement removing Iran’s potential bomb fuel or limiting its missiles, two of the main reasons the President gave for going to war. The plan to send commandos to seize the roughly 440 kilograms of 60 per cent enriched uranium has been dropped, at least for now. Rubio’s approach, Sanger writes, is best described as “strategic patience.” Readers will remember the phrase. In Part CIV, Hamidreza Azizi of the International Crisis Group described Iran abandoning its own long-standing policy of strategic patience. Washington has now adopted the policy Tehran discarded.

Some of the President’s claims need correcting. Perhaps $100 billion of Iranian money is frozen around the world, not “hundreds of billions,” and only about $2 billion of it is in the United States. The strait has only partly reopened; every day, in Sanger’s phrase, is “a game of cat and mouse” as the Navy escorts tankers out. And the United States is not leaving. The blockade stays, with no plan to end it, which means the large, open-ended military presence the President’s allies said in the spring they wanted to avoid.

Then there is the uranium, the first reason the President gave for the attacks of February 28. “They have nothing,” he told 60 Minutes. “It’s buried under thousands of tons of rock.” It has been under that rock since June 2025, when he said Iran’s nuclear capacity had been “obliterated” and that Iran was “two weeks” from a bomb. The fuel has not moved. Only his description of it has. In the same interview he said that if Iran got “a nuclear weapon during my term, I would be excoriated.” As Conor Friedersdorf noted in The Atlantic, the two statements sit awkwardly together. Sanger asks the question this diary has asked for months: “If the nuclear material is not a threat, sitting exactly where it was in February, why did America go to war?”

“Trump began the war thinking he would be a hero,” said Karim Sadjadpour of the Carnegie Endowment. “He’s now trying to figure out how to end the war without looking like a loser.” He began with “maximalist goals” and now “seems to be preparing for a minimalist outcome: accepting that the nuclear material is still there, as long as it’s buried under rock.”

The Claim

Part of what kept the window open in the week of the vote was a piece of intelligence that divided the American government. Israel told Washington that Iran was considering chemical weapons, Barnes and his colleagues reported, and described the threat as more urgent than Iran’s nuclear ambitions. Some American officials took it seriously; Iran has stockpiled chemical-weapons components for two decades, and Dana Stroul, the Pentagon’s senior Middle East official under President Biden, said a country that feels “existentially pressured” might cross that line.

Others heard an echo of 2002 and Iraq’s weapons of mass destruction. Gulf governments briefed without evidence called it “unserious.” And Israel’s Home Front Command ran nationwide drills that week without making chemical weapons a significant part of them or issuing general safety reminders. A government that believed its citizens faced a gas attack would normally tell them. Some officials suggested a motive: Benjamin Netanyahu, short of a governing majority, might use a security emergency to force his opponents into a coalition. Iran, which lost thousands to Saddam Hussein’s gas in the 1980s, denies having such weapons. This diary cannot judge the intelligence. It notes that the claim arrived just before the strike window, from a caretaker prime minister fighting for his job, and that it did not move the President. Part CIV recorded that Netanyahu pressed for a strike before Israel’s vote and was refused by his own army chief. He appears to have tried again before America’s.

Iron Gate

What remains is the siege. It began at sea: a naval blockade of a country that moved about 90 per cent of its trade by water, then sanctions that grounded much of its air transport. Now Washington is going after what Erika Solomon, Eric Schmitt and Tyler Pager of the New York Times call Iran’s “last economic artery”: its land borders. Central Command’s new Task Force Iron Gate, 20 people based in Florida, is to stop weapons, sanctioned goods and money crossing them. It will not fight; its job is to push Iran’s neighbours to do it.

The neighbours do not want to. Turkey has largely refused. Iraq has been asked to close all but two of its crossings and has refused that too, closing one or two briefly as a gesture, to uproar at home. Pakistan has opened and closed crossings. Several neighbours warned of “civil insurrection” if they cut trade that border communities depend on. They also fear Tehran: Pakistan depends on Iranian fuel, and Iraq gets more than a third of the gas for its power grid from Iran. Russia is helping Iran lobby against “the so-called Iron Gate plan.” Ellie Geranmayeh of the European Council on Foreign Relations thinks the midterm result has given the neighbours licence to ignore Washington. “Iran is going to be their neighbor, they know they’re stuck with Iran for life.”

Not Waiting

The man running the siege has already felt it. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who has led the economic campaign since August, has moved onto a military base after Iranian threats against his life, as Maggie Haberman and Jonathan Swan reported for the New York Times. Officials concluded that Iranian plotting against current and former officials had reached a more advanced stage. The Treasury said he “will not be deterred.” The same article carries the sentence that matters most: American intelligence says Iran’s leadership is determined to hold on “at nearly any cost,” which suggests economic pain alone may not bring it down.

On Friday, the day after the President said he did not need a deal, Central Command shot down two Iranian drones heading for Bahrain, which hosts a major American base. It appeared to be Iran’s first attack on Bahrain since September, and the Revolutionary Guards called it retaliation for an American strike on a telecommunications tower in Iran. On Sunday they tried again, aiming, they said, at a radar at a Bahraini air base. No injuries were reported, as Vivian Nereim and Aaron Boxerman reported for the New York Times. The war that is “effectively over” also still includes sporadic American bombing along Iran’s southern coast.

Europe is paying too. On Veterans Day, at Arlington, the President promised that when the war ends “the entire world will be a much safer and better place.” That is not how it feels in Europe, Michael D. Shear reported from London for the New York Times. An indictment unsealed this week says a commander of Kataib Hezbollah, Iran’s Iraqi proxy, directed a foiled plot against R.A.F. Fairford from a New York jail; the United States has pulled its 12 B-1 bombers from the base, and this week evacuated as many as a dozen aircraft from R.A.F. Mildenhall over a suspected drone plot. Cyberattacks have spoiled supermarket food in France and seven other countries, and France has formally blamed Iran. “Iran is signaling very clearly to the Europeans that if the U.S. resumes the war, Europe is going to also become part of the target list,” Geranmayeh said. Fairness requires two caveats: Russia runs its own sabotage campaign in Europe, and Friday’s nationwide American ground stop was caused by ageing equipment, not a cyberattack. But Spain, Germany, France and Britain all refused to join this war, and they are paying for it anyway.

The Boogeyman

On Sunday the Times published an investigation that seems unrelated to this war and turns out to be about it. For four decades, Adam Goldman, Ronen Bergman, Farnaz Fassihi and Hwaida Saad reported for the New York Times, Israeli intelligence was convinced that Iran was behind the disappearance of Ron Arad, the airman captured in Lebanon in 1986. On that conviction, Israel seized Iranians. In 2006 it took General Alireza Asgari from an Istanbul hotel; three former American officials say he was interrogated for weeks and killed, and he knew nothing about Arad. In 2021 Mossad officers posing as movers put General Abdullah Saberi in a refrigerator box in Damascus; he too knew nothing. All this happened although Israeli reviews in 2006 and 2016 had concluded Iran was not involved. Those searching now believe Arad never left Lebanon.

“It was a collective blind spot,” said Avi Kalo, who led the military’s missing-in-action unit. “Iran was the boogeyman.” No one suggested anyone lied; they called it confirmation bias, the same habit, the Times notes, that left Israel underestimating Hamas before October 7 and predicting an uprising in Tehran when this war began. The story does not show that Iran is harmless; this week’s attacks show otherwise. It shows what certainty can do. This war began with one: that Iran’s uranium was an urgent threat only force could remove. The uranium sits where it sat in February, and the President now says it is nothing to worry about.

The Siege

Fairness requires this diary to welcome one thing. The President did not resume the bombing campaign, and a siege kills more slowly than a war. The generous reading is that he listened to the voters, to Gulf allies who begged him not to restart the war, and to advisers who warned the munitions would run out before Iran did. The less generous reading is the pattern this diary has recorded before: strikes approved and cancelled hours before they began, a war run on last-minute reversals that keeps every option alive and never pays for choosing one.

Either way, what is left is the fourth of the options Sanger and Schmitt set out last week, “muddle along,” which they said would “effectively allow Mr. Trump to avoid making a decision.” It has no end date. Its pain falls first on ordinary Iranians, then on neighbours asked to share it. “You do bad things, bad things will happen to you” describes punishment, not a strategy.

Friedersdorf recalls 2006, when a lame-duck Republican lost both houses over an unpopular war. Bush answered with the surge; Congress voted a withdrawal deadline, he vetoed it, and Congress then voted another $70 billion. The last American troops left Iraq this year. “Winning elections by campaigning against a war,” Friedersdorf writes, “is far easier than actually ending the conflict.” The new Congress could cut off the money in January. In June, a Republican Congress told the President to seek its approval or withdraw. He ignored it, and Congress let him carry on.

And a closed window can be reopened. The moon will be dark again in December. The third carrier will arrive. The President leaves for summits in the Philippines and China within days, where he will want to look undiminished. The voters asked for the war to end. What they have so far is a war with less bombing and no end date.

The Cost Ledger

The Pentagon’s last figure to Congress was $47 billion, not counting damage to American bases or the hundreds of billions consumers have paid in higher fuel prices. The new policy adds a blockade with no end date and the military presence needed to keep it. Europeans are paying for a war their governments refused to join, in fuel, groceries and heating. The Democratic Congress that takes office on January 3 is unlikely to add money for the war. Among this week’s costs inside the American government: a Treasury Secretary who can no longer safely sleep at home.

The Numbers

  • Three: aircraft carriers in the region on November 3. Zero: strikes ordered in the window.

  • Effectively over: the President’s description of the war, on November 9.

  • Nine: days between Vance’s “the reason we lost is the war” and his removal from the talks.

  • About $100 billion: Iranian money frozen worldwide, by some estimates. About $2 billion is in the United States.

  • About 440: kilograms of 60 per cent enriched uranium, under the rock since June 2025.

  • 20: staff assigned to Task Force Iron Gate, in Florida.

  • Two: border crossings Iraq has been asked to leave open. It has refused.

  • Two: Iranian drone attacks on Bahrain in three days.

  • 12: B-1 bombers withdrawn from R.A.F. Fairford.

  • 40: years since Ron Arad was captured. Israel now believes he was never in Iran.

  • 2006: the last time a president lost both houses over a war. His troops were not all home until this year.

And Narges Mohammadi, for whom this section is always kept. This week the American government decided it did not need a deal with Iran’s rulers and would rely instead on making Iranians poorer until those rulers give way. The intelligence says they will hold on at nearly any cost. The cost, as she has spent her life pointing out, is paid by the people who were never asked: the families watching the rial collapse, the workers whose wages buy less each week, the prisoners whose names the world forgets. Washington has decided to wait for Iran’s rulers to break. Iranians are the ones doing the waiting. She is still under sentences totalling 44 years for saying they deserve better.
