There is a certain kind of document whose meaning lies less in what it says than in how it was signed. On Tuesday, September 29, 2026, the day before Quantico, Pete Hegseth signed one.

It ordered the three service academies, West Point, the Naval Academy and the Air Force Academy, to stop granting tenure to civilian professors. The policy was modest by the standards of this series; it touched no one who already held tenure. The signature was not modest at all. And it opens the one front in Hegseth’s campaign that the earlier parts of this series have not yet examined: what he is doing to the education of the officers who will one day replace the generals he fired.

The Performance

The memo was a short document with a large signature. Eliot A. Cohen, a professor at Johns Hopkins and a former counsellor at the State Department, described it for The Atlantic on September 30, 2026, in “Pete Hegseth’s Magic Marker.” The Secretary’s jagged signature, in an oversized black Sharpie, took up as much space as two of the memo’s paragraphs, in what Cohen took to be a nod to the President’s own style. With the same marker, Hegseth had underlined two key sentences word by word. Cohen likened the whole thing to an angry note left by a grounded preteen on his parents’ dinner plate, and judged it all temper.

The words underneath matched the pen. As Matthew Adams reported for Stars and Stripes on September 29, 2026, in “Hegseth ceases civilian tenure appointments at service academies,” Hegseth wrote that the academies exist to prepare the next generation of officers to lead, and that keeping them focused on that warfighting mission meant addressing the “structural mechanisms that have allowed civilian academic norms to dilute our focus on lethality.” Tenure, he wrote, leads to “academic stagnation, faculty complacency and institutional inability to adapt to emerging needs.”

The memo did not say when the change would take effect, and it did not touch the existing tenured faculty. What it changed was the future: from now on, no civilian scholar who joins an academy will be able to earn the security that civilian scholars everywhere else in American higher education regard as the mark of having arrived.

The Paperwork

Who teaches at the academies. Civilian professors are not a fringe presence. By Stars and Stripes’s figures, they make up about 27 percent of the faculty at West Point and about 30 percent at the Air Force Academy. The Naval Academy has 284 civilian faculty, 217 of them tenured and 67 on the tenure track, roughly half of its teaching staff. They teach the subjects that uniformed officers, rotating through for two or three years, cannot cover in depth: the languages, the hard sciences, history, literature, economics.

The arrangement is deliberate: a permanent core of professional scholars beside a rotating cast of officers. Joe Walsh, reporting for CBS News on September 29, 2026, in “Hegseth directs military service academies to immediately stop giving tenure to civilian professors,” noted that a former Naval Academy superintendent, Vice Admiral Yvette Davids, had described tenure as a recruiting tool: it is how the academies attract scholars who could otherwise go to Princeton or Stanford.

What Hegseth said he would do. None of this is a surprise. At his confirmation hearings in January 2025, Hegseth said he favoured replacing civilian professors with uniformed officers and complained of “civilian professors that came from the same left-wing, woke universities.” The tenure memo is the second half of a sentence he began twenty months ago.

The record in between. The academies have been reshaped steadily since. In May 2025, Hegseth ordered a review of library books touching on diversity and critical race theory. In April 2025, the Air Force Academy quietly replaced its mission statement. The old version began: “To educate, train and inspire men and women to become officers of character.” The new one promises to “forge leaders of character” who will lead the Air Force and Space Force “as we fight and win our Nation’s wars.” The verb educate is gone. According to accounts at the time, the faculty learned of the change from the academy’s website.

Civilian teaching posts at the Air Force Academy were cut in 2025. As Greg Hadley reported for Air & Space Forces Magazine on February 6, 2026, in “Air Force Academy Leaders Ousted in Shake Up,” those cuts prompted a review by the Higher Learning Commission, the academy’s accreditor. An internal survey found that 72 percent of staff said the campus climate had worsened. The superintendent, Lt. Gen. Tony Bauernfeind, was told to retire after less than two years, on track for one of the shortest superintendencies in the academy’s history. The new dean of faculty is a former F-15E pilot who ran autonomy research at DARPA: an accomplished officer, but an unusual choice to run a college.

At West Point, the most vivid case is a single professor. Adam Kalkstein had taught geography and climate science to cadets for seventeen years and held tenure as a full professor. In September 2025, according to his lawsuit, faculty were told they could no longer teach about human-caused climate change, in line with a Hegseth directive from March 2025 against what the Secretary called the “climate distraction.” Kalkstein objected. As Elias Guerra reported for WAMC on September 5, 2026, in “West Point Professor says he’s being pushed out for teaching human-caused climate change,” he told the dean in November 2025 that following the order made him “a liar and a fraud.” After the spring 2026 semester his post was eliminated in a reduction in force. He is suing in federal court in New York, alleging a violation of his First Amendment rights. West Point has said the cut was a routine reduction.

Kalkstein’s case illustrates exactly what tenure is meant to prevent. A tenured professor ordered to stop teaching established science can refuse and keep his job. Without tenure, the only protection is the hope that one’s dean agrees with the science.

The wider campaign. The academies are one part of a broader assault on officers’ education. In February 2026, the Pentagon cut ties with Harvard. “Harvard is woke; The War Department is not,” Hegseth declared. Weeks later, as Caitlin Yilek reported for CBS News on February 27, 2026, in “Hegseth says Pentagon cutting ties with top universities, calling them ‘woke breeding grounds’,” he announced that officers would no longer attend graduate programmes at Princeton, Columbia, MIT, Brown, Yale and others from the 2026–27 academic year, and ordered a review of the war colleges. Hegseth holds degrees from both Princeton and Harvard.

The replacement. At Quantico, on the day after the tenure memo, Hegseth unveiled what is meant to fill the gap. As Rebeka Zeljko reported for the Daily Caller on September 30, 2026, in “Hegseth Announces New ‘Patriotic’ Officer Training Program In Partnership With Select Universities,” a new “America’s Corps of Cadets” will expand officer training through five partner schools: Louisiana State, Liberty, Tuskegee, Mississippi State and Hillsdale. They will be designated “Distinguished Military Colleges” with priority for commissioning. Hegseth described mainstream universities as “anti-American, DEI-worshipping, woke factories” and said the country needed leaders who understood “the faith of Jerusalem, the philosophy of Athens, the power of Rome, and the freedom of Philadelphia, circa 1776.”

There is no objection here to LSU, Mississippi State or the historic Tuskegee, all of which have long commissioned fine officers. The point is the pattern. Civilian scholars inside the academies are to be made less secure. Civilian scholars at elite universities are cut off. And civilian scholars at a short list of institutions chosen by the Secretary, including two with explicit ideological commitments, are elevated. The department is not removing civilians from officer education. It is choosing which civilians it trusts.

The Bill

The scholars who won’t come. The cost of the tenure memo will not appear this year. It will appear in hiring rounds over the next decade, when a promising young historian or physicist weighs a tenure-track post at a civilian university against an untenured job at an academy, in a system that cut the post of West Point's only climate scientist after he refused to stop teaching that climate change is human-caused. West Point calls that a routine reduction in force; a young scholar deciding where to build a career will draw a simpler conclusion. Some will still choose the academies, out of patriotism. Many will not. The academies will slowly get the faculty that the memo’s terms can attract.

The accreditors. The service academies are accredited colleges, and their degrees matter. The Air Force Academy’s 2025 faculty cuts have already drawn an accreditation review. Accreditors care about exactly the things the memo weakens: a stable, qualified faculty and academic freedom. It would be one of the stranger outcomes of this administration if the academies whose mission is to produce officers found their degrees questioned because the Secretary objected to their professors.

The cadets. Cohen’s central argument is the strongest one, and it should be put plainly. Military education is still education. The academies do not exist to teach tactics; the Army, Navy and Air Force do that later, and better. They exist to produce officers who can think: who have read history and know what happens when armies overreach, who understand enough science to question a briefing, who have read enough literature to understand people under pressure. Many of the most admired American commanders of the last half-century, including Jim Mattis, David Petraeus, James Stavridis and William McRaven, were notable readers. None of them earned their reputation with push-ups.

The one percent. The memo is part of a larger message about who soldiers are and how they relate to everyone else. Days after Quantico, Hegseth told cadets at the Air Force Academy that they were “the real one percent,” with a perspective most Americans lack. That remark was reported by Cohen in a second Atlantic piece, “Hegseth’s One Percent,” on October 5, 2026. Cohen’s answer is historical. America’s largest and hardest wars were won by citizen armies: militia, volunteers and draftees who went when called and wanted to go home. George C. Marshall and Dwight Eisenhower, he notes, insisted that a soldier is a citizen first. Put that next to the tenure memo, the cut ties with elite universities, and a Corps of Cadets drawn partly from Liberty and Hillsdale, and a pattern appears: an officer corps educated in a narrower range of places, by a narrower range of teachers, and told that it is better than the society it serves.

The irony. The administration has often complained, with some justice, that many university faculties are ideologically uniform. Cohen points out the contradiction: the remedy now being applied to the academies produces uniformity by other means. A faculty without tenure, teaching under a mission statement that dropped the word educate, in an institution where a scientist's post was cut after he refused to drop established science from his teaching, is not a faculty that will produce many dissenters. Whether or not that is the intention, it is the likely result. It is also the opposite of what a military facing a fast-changing war needs from its young officers.

Cohen’s verdict. Cohen does not hide his. He expects Hegseth to be gone within two years, quite possibly less. And in Cohen’s words, not this series’, Hegseth is “the worst civilian leader of the American military since John Floyd in 1860.” Floyd was President Buchanan’s secretary of war and went on to become a Confederate general; Cohen calls him a traitor. It is a ferocious comparison from a scholar who served in a Republican administration and who has taught at the war colleges. It is also, by Cohen’s own account, a forecast about cleanup: whoever follows Hegseth will need to repair not only the officer corps and the budget, but the intellectual foundations of the force.

The Sharpie signature will be easy to forget. Signatures usually are. The memo underneath it will not, because its effects are slow and cumulative and land on people who are now eighteen. In a decade they will be captains, majors and lieutenant colonels. Some of them will have been taught by scholars who chose the academies anyway. Others will have been taught by people the Secretary preferred. The country will find out which education served it better at the worst possible time: in the next war.
