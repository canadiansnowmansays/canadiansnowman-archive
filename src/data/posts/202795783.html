By now you know how this goes. I count. The number means something. I pretend to be surprised.

I will spare you the full ritual this time. Suffice to say that essay 1,271 arrived the way the others did — not chosen, not engineered, simply the next number after 1,270, which followed 1,269, which followed a great many essays I no longer entirely remember writing. I noticed it the way you notice a familiar street sign on an unfamiliar drive. Oh. It’s this one.

Dior did not look up. She has reached the stage of this project where my excitement no longer requires acknowledgment. I respect the boundary.

In 1271, a seventeen-year-old Venetian named Marco Polo left the city of his birth with his father Niccolò and his uncle Maffeo, and pointed himself east. He would not see Venice again for twenty-four years. The three of them travelled by horse, by camel, by foot, and by whatever boat could be found at whatever river or sea stood in their way. One horsepower per traveller, more or less, when the horses were available at all.

He was going, eventually, to the court of Kublai Khan — a destination that existed, at the time, mostly as rumour in the West. Europeans of the thirteenth century knew that something vast lay to the east. They did not know what, exactly, or how vast, or whether the stories travellers brought back were history or invention.

Marco Polo found out. He spent roughly seventeen years in the service of the Khan, travelling further still — by some accounts into Burma, into the Indian Ocean, across more of the known world than any European had mapped before him. When he finally came home, in 1295, the story goes that his own family did not recognize him at the door. Twenty-four years is a long time to be gone. People forget your face. They do not always forget to ask where you’ve been.

On his deathbed, decades later, friends are said to have urged him to recant the wilder claims and die with a clean conscience. He replied that he had not told half of what he saw.

I find that detail almost unbearably moving, for reasons I will come back to.

He told them, in the meantime, everything he could. He kept telling them, for the rest of his life, and the result was a book — Il Milione, sometimes translated as The Travels of Marco Polo, sometimes as The Book of the Marvels of the World — that described a civilization so sophisticated, so wealthy, so vast, that a great many of his own countrymen simply declined to believe him. They called it exaggeration. They called it invention.

There is a man currently retracing Marco Polo’s route, as it happens, in something other than one horsepower.

Jeffrey Sachs the economist set out this month with his wife and two travelling companions on what he calls the Marco Polo Drive of Peace, Culture, and Sustainable Development. Twelve countries. Fifteen thousand kilometres. Forty-three days. Rome to Hong Kong, in an electric car producing roughly 1,582 horsepower. Sit that figure next to the one I gave you earlier: one horsepower per traveller in 1271. Sachs’s party charged their car to 97.5 percent in under nine minutes in Bologna, by his own account, before the espresso arrived. Marco Polo measured his progress in seasons and mountain passes. (Sachs has written about the trip himself, and for those who’d rather watch than read, he’s also explained it on video.)

I won’t make fun of this — it would be easy, and it would also be cheap. Sachs is carrying, by his telling, a message from Pope Leo XIV eastward, an echo of something genuinely true about the Polos’ own journey: Pope Gregory X once asked them to carry a message to Kublai Khan as well. The errand hasn’t changed shape in seven hundred and fifty years, even though everything inside the shape has. I didn’t invent the contrast between one horsepower and 1,582 — history and Professor Sachs handed it to me, together, three weeks before I needed it. I am simply the man who noticed.

The road ends, as it happens, in the same country I have spent a fair number of essays trying to understand on its own terms — readers of these pages will recall The Quiet War, and Formosa: The Beautiful Problem, and the instalments now underway in The Long Return, which has been working its way from Zhōngguó’s old idea of itself at the centre of the world through to the country Sachs is currently driving toward. Marco Polo’s Cathay and the People’s Republic that awaits him in Shenzhen are not, of course, the same country in any way that matters historically. But it is the same direction. West has been travelling east, full of theories about what it will find there, for a very long time. Some of those travellers came home with the truth and were disbelieved. Others came home with conclusions they had decided on before they left. I try, in my own more sedentary way, to be the first kind.

I promised myself, several essays ago, that I would not force a contemporary parallel onto every historical figure who happens to arrive on my doorstep with a round number attached. Not every king is a stand-in for a president. Not every century needs to rhyme with this one on command.

But I will allow myself one sentence, because it is sitting right there and it would be a small dereliction of duty to leave it unsaid: Marco Polo came home with the truth about a vast and unfamiliar world, and was widely accused of making it up. We have, in the centuries since, found increasingly efficient ways to accuse people of making up the true thing and believing the invented one. I will leave that exactly where it sits, unexamined further, and move on, because this particular essay does not want to be about Washington. It wants to be about distance, and curiosity, and what happens when a person tells the truth about something too large for his neighbours to easily accept.

I should mention that 1271 happens to arrive in the same order here as it did in history — after 1066, after 1215, before whatever comes next. That won’t always be true. At some point the count will hand me a number whose history happened earlier than a number I’ve already written, and I’ll have told these stories out of sequence without ever leaving my desk. It hasn’t happened yet. But I can feel it coming, the way you can feel a card approaching the bottom of the deck.

For now, I’ve started to think of the numbers less as a calendar and more as an atlas — a slowly accumulating map of human ambition, assembled in whatever sequence the counting happens to deliver it. Conquest at 1,066. Law at 1,215. Curiosity, now, at 1,271. Somewhere ahead of me, whenever the count gets there, lie exploration and independence and a great many other entries I have not yet earned the right to write.

Twenty-four years is the part of this story I keep returning to.

I have been doing this — the daily counting, the daily writing — for less than two years, though some mornings it feels longer. Marco Polo left as a teenager and came home a man approaching forty, having seen more of the world than nearly anyone alive in Europe at the time, and the chief reward for his trouble was several centuries of people calling him a liar before historians and travellers eventually, slowly, began to confirm that he had, if anything, undersold it.

I do not compare my project to his. That would be absurd, and I am aware of how it would read. But there is something in the shape of his story that I recognize: the business of going out daily into the work, finding it more vast than expected, returning with an account of it, and trusting that the account will eventually be believed on its own merits, even if the believing takes longer than you’d like.

The road, as it turns out, has always asked the same patience of anyone willing to walk it.

The Denza is still rolling east as I write this, somewhere past Venice, charging in minutes what once took mountain passes. Dior is still unimpressed. The road, somehow, is still open to anyone curious enough to take it — on horseback, by electric car, or one essay at a time.

More soon, from wherever the next number happens to find me.
