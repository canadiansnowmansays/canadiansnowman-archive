By Stephen Gordon

Part LII of this series ended on a question Senator Peter Welch could not get F.B.I. Director Kash Patel to answer plainly: would the bureau send agents to polling places, yes or no. Patel offered a description of where personnel were stationed instead of a commitment about where they wouldn’t be sent. In the weeks since, that ambiguity has stopped being hypothetical. It has a name — the administration itself calls it the “Polling Place Policy” — a paper trail, a set of documented incidents, and now two separate federal lawsuits trying to get a court to close the gap the F.B.I. director wouldn’t.

The Policy Gets a Name

On September 1st, Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin confirmed and clarified what plaintiffs in Denver’s lawsuit call the Polling Place Policy: a standing authorization for armed Immigration and Customs Enforcement officers to conduct enforcement operations at polling places, including serving warrants and detaining people suspected of being in the country illegally. This did not emerge from nowhere. Earlier this year, then-White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt was asked directly whether ICE agents might appear near polling locations in November and answered that she “can’t guarantee” they wouldn’t. Plaintiffs in the Denver case point to that comment as the precursor to the policy Mullin later confirmed outright.

The Denver lawsuit, filed September 10th in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia, is a more developed and more factually loaded complaint than the later N.A.A.C.P.-led suit. Its plaintiffs are the City and County of Denver, the League of United Latin American Citizens, Common Cause, the Common Cause Education Fund, and UnidosUS, represented by the Democracy Forward Foundation and, for Denver specifically, the Public Rights Project. Denver’s Clerk and Recorder, Paul López, did not mince words about why the city joined: “Denver is the vanguard of democracy, and with cast-iron certainty we will protect our free and fair elections from any and all attempts at federal intimidation and intrusion.”

What makes this complaint harder to wave away as speculative is that it does not rest on the possibility of future conduct. It cites specific incidents already on the record: armed ICE agents conducting operations at polling places in San Antonio, Texas; Ventura County, California; and Syracuse, New York, in May and June of this year — months before Mullin’s September confirmation of the broader policy. The complaint also cites a ProPublica investigation finding that more than 170 U.S. citizens have been detained by ICE while “peacefully exercising their First Amendment rights,” and invokes two names directly: Renée Good and Alex Pretti.

Both were killed in Minneapolis during Operation Metro Surge, a large-scale immigration enforcement operation that ran from early December through mid-February. Good, 37, was shot by an ICE agent on January 7th while stopped in her car during a protest against the operation; witnesses disputed the Department of Homeland Security’s account that she had tried to ram agents. Pretti, also 37 and an ICU nurse, was fatally shot on January 24th by Customs and Border Protection agents while attempting to help a bystander who had been pepper-sprayed. Neither had a criminal record. Neither was the target of any warrant. As of this writing, no ICE or CBP agent has been criminally charged in connection with either death, or with any of the nine deaths the Associated Press has tallied across immigration enforcement operations since the administration returned to office. That is the evidentiary backdrop the Denver complaint is invoking when it argues that a reasonable voter, aware of this record, would rationally fear an encounter with armed federal agents at a polling place — not an abstraction about hypothetical intimidation, but a documented pattern in which federal agents involved in fatal encounters with U.S. citizens have not, to date, faced consequences.

The Statute, and Its Weak Point

Where the N.A.A.C.P. coalition’s suit leans on Section 11(b) of the Voting Rights Act — the general prohibition on voter intimidation, threats, and coercion — Denver’s complaint reaches for something older and narrower: an 1865 statute, now codified at 18 U.S.C., that bars federal officials from bringing “armed men” to “keep the peace at the polls” where an election is held. The law contains one exception, carried over from the Reconstruction-era circumstances that produced it: armed federal officers may be present only “to repel armed enemies of the United States.” Denver’s lawyers argue immigration enforcement plainly does not fall within that exception. The Trump administration, should it choose to contest the point rather than simply deny any plan exists, would presumably argue the opposite, or argue that ICE officers executing a warrant are not the “troops” the 1865 Congress had in mind.

This is worth stating plainly, because it is the one place where this piece owes the administration’s side a fair hearing rather than an assumption: the statute was written in 1865 to stop the U.S. Army from being used to intimidate freedmen at Southern polling places, and reasonable observers can disagree about whether a modern civilian immigration enforcement agency, executing a criminal warrant, is the kind of “armed men” the statute contemplated. A commentary published by the conservative outlet Heritage Review made a version of this argument directly, calling the plaintiffs’ reliance on the “troops at polls” law a “structural problem,” on the grounds that the statute was written specifically to prevent the federal government from using organized military force to intimidate voters as a body — not to reach individual law-enforcement encounters incidental to voting. Whether that distinction holds is exactly the question a court will now have to answer, not a settled one this series can wave past. What is not in genuine dispute is Mullin’s own description of when his agents would show up — “if there is a threat to that polling place... or we’re serving a warrant” — which leaves the administration itself defining, unilaterally and after the fact, what counts as a threshold triggering the very presence two lawsuits are trying to prevent.

The Foreign Hand

While that fight plays out in the courts, U.S. intelligence officials have confirmed something adjacent and, in its way, more concrete: Russia is, once again, running a covert influence campaign aimed at American confidence in the midterms. Classified assessments described to reporters this month found the Kremlin has authorized a digital operation to seed and amplify domestic division around the election — smaller and less coordinated, officials say, than some of Moscow’s previous efforts, and secondary to its focus on the war in Ukraine, but active nonetheless. Iran and China have each been assessed as possible but less certain participants, with Beijing in particular judged more likely to stay cautious for fear of getting caught.

Here the stated goal and the observed pattern pull in different directions, and the gap between them is worth naming precisely rather than smoothing over. Some officials say Russia’s aim appears to be sowing chaos broadly rather than favouring either party. But the actual examples independent researchers have traced to the campaign tell a narrower story. A network linked to a known Kremlin operation — researchers call it Operation Overload, or Matryoshka — has spent the past week posting AI-generated videos on X and Bluesky attacking Democratic Senate candidates specifically: Jon Ossoff in Georgia, Sherrod Brown in Ohio, Roy Cooper in North Carolina, Troy Jackson in Maine, Chris Pappas in New Hampshire, Mary Peltola in Alaska, Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan, James Talarico in Texas, and Joshua Turek in Iowa — nine of the candidates whose races will decide whether Republicans keep the Senate. One post mimicked a New York Times video, using an image of actress Jamie Lee Curtis with fabricated subtitles attacking Democrats over transgender-rights policy; her publicist called it flatly false. By the end of that single day, the post had been viewed nearly 200,000 times. The themes running through the campaign — corruption, antisemitism, support for transgender rights — are, as reporters noted, the same ones Mr. Trump and his party have used against the same candidates.

So the “sow chaos generally” framing and the target list are both true at once, and the honest reading is not that officials are wrong about Russia’s motive, but that a chaos operation and a one-sided operation are not mutually exclusive: undermining confidence in an election and tilting its outcome toward a preferred side can be the same campaign, running through the same accounts, at the same time.

What the Administration Said It Was Worried About

This is where the season’s two chaos stories fold into each other in a way that is difficult to read as coincidence. In July, President Trump gave a prime-time address on election security in which he claimed there were “severe vulnerabilities” in American voting systems and accused officials of a yearslong “cover-up” of foreign interference by China and others. He ordered a task force to declassify supporting documents. When those documents were released, heavily redacted, they did not show major new weaknesses in the country’s election infrastructure, and did not demonstrate that any foreign government had actually manipulated a vote.

Measured against what has actually surfaced since — a real, confirmed, ongoing Russian influence campaign, targeting nine Democratic Senate candidates by name, using fabricated video attributed to real news organizations — the July address reads less like an early warning and more like misdirection that turned out, almost by accident, to be aimed at the wrong threat in the wrong direction. The administration spent months alleging a cover-up of foreign interference it could not substantiate, while an actual foreign interference operation ran in public, on platforms the administration has shown no particular urgency about policing, against a target list assembled entirely by the people running the campaign.

None of this required any conspiracy between the administration and Moscow to produce the effect this series keeps returning to. It only required the same thing it always requires: an unresolved sentence, left unresolved on purpose, doing work that a resolved one could not do. Patel would not tell Welch that agents wouldn’t be at the polls. Mullin confirmed a named policy and then defined its own trigger. Trump would not let a substantiated finding replace an unsubstantiated accusation, even once the documents he ordered released failed to support it. And into every one of those gaps, something moved — two lawsuits, filed nine days apart, trying to force two different federal courts to name what the ambiguity itself constitutes under two different statutes, and a Kremlin-linked operation that did not wait for anyone’s permission to start filling the space with fabricated video of a dead-serious kind.

The lawsuits may or may not succeed before Election Day; both rest on legal theories — VRA intimidation, an 1865 anti-troops statute never designed with ICE in mind — that are, in different ways, being asked to stretch further than either was originally built for. The Russian operation, per the researchers tracking it, is not slowing down. “We’re definitely expecting it to ramp up,” one analyst said. “I don’t think they’re going to stop.” Whatever the courts eventually decide about polling-place policy, that much appears to already be true.
