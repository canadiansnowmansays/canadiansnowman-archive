Postmark: A cornfield, somewhere in the Midwest. A tractor moves in perfect rows, no one in the cab. Sixteen cameras watch the horizon. The farmer is at his daughter’s football game.

Every postcard in this series so far has chased the same silhouette: two legs, two arms, a head, the uncanny outline of ourselves. This one does not. The most consequential robot in American agriculture in 2026 has no legs at all. It is a tractor — the same green machine that has sat in a farmer’s shed for a century — except that the seat is empty, and it is plowing a quarter-section of Iowa without a hand on the wheel.

This is worth pausing on before anything else, because it corrects an assumption this whole series has let ride unchallenged. The humanoid form matters where the world was built for human bodies — an airport, an operating room, a kitchen. But agriculture was never built for human bodies. It was built for the horse and then the tractor, and the tractor has simply been taught to drive itself. There is a lesson buried in that fact, and I want to sit with it before the field robots earn their postcard: the robots that will remake the most work, the fastest, may be the least humanoid of all. Sometimes the future does not need to look like us. It only needs to know where the row ends.

The tractor that learned to see

As Farm Progress reported, the transformation has moved in stages that read like the story of autopilot before it. Through the last decade, GPS-guided steering did the straight lines while a driver still sat in the cab for the turns and the edge cases — the fence line, the stray dog, the neighbour’s kid on a four-wheeler. What changed between 2024 and 2026 is that the edge cases themselves became automatable. John Deere’s second-generation autonomy kit, unveiled at CES, wraps a tractor in sixteen individual cameras arranged for a full 360-degree view, paired with ruggedized processors and machine-learning software developed through Deere’s acquisition of a company called Blue River. As one industry account put it, the kit now handles headland turns, obstacle avoidance, and full implement control without a human in the loop — true driver-optional operation, not merely driver-assisted. The company’s chief technology officer has been characteristically blunt about the goal, describing it as a way to reduce dependency on unskilled labour at exactly the moments — planting, tillage, harvest — when farms need the most hands and can find the fewest.

The scale of that need is a number worth sitting with. As John Deere itself has stated, the American Farm Bureau Federation estimates something on the order of 2.4 million farm jobs that need filling every single year in the United States alone — not once, but annually, a bucket with a hole in the bottom that the agricultural labour market has never fully patched. This is the demographic arithmetic Japan’s airports are already living through, transplanted to an American cornfield to an American cornfield: a shrinking, aging, urbanizing labour pool set against work that must happen on nature’s schedule, not a factory’s. A field ready for harvest does not wait for a worker to be found. The tractor that drives itself is agriculture’s answer to the same arithmetic that put humanoids on the tarmac at Haneda.

And the retrofit economics matter as much as the new-machine story, because they determine who actually gets to use this. As Farm Progress reported, Deere’s own technology chief made a point of extending autonomy not just to machines rolling off the factory floor but to tractors farmers already own, through a camera kit installable on 8R and 9R models from 2020 onward — because, in his words, you shouldn’t have to buy a new machine to get the benefit. A Nebraska company called Sabanto sells kits that turn newer John Deere, Fendt, and Kubota tractors into robots regardless of brand loyalty, and Agco and CNH Industrial have each announced their own retrofit or partnership plays. This is the democratization instinct Part XI found in the garage humanoid, arriving instead through the used-equipment lot — the recognition that a transformative capability locked inside only the newest, most expensive machines helps only the wealthiest farms, and that the labour crisis is sharpest precisely on the operations that cannot afford to replace their whole equipment line.

The laser and the berry

Autonomy is only half the field robot’s story; the other half is precision, and here the machines do something no human hand ever could at any price.

As Robotics and Automation News reported, John Deere’s See & Spray system uses machine vision to distinguish a crop plant from a weed in real time and fire herbicide only at the weed — and the results, across corn, soybean, and cotton operations, have run to an average fifty-nine percent reduction in herbicide use, with over a million acres treated by 2024 alone. The yield effect is almost a footnote and is nonetheless remarkable: three to four more bushels an acre, because the crop itself is under less chemical stress. A competing approach from a startup called Carbon Robotics skips the herbicide question entirely — its LaserWeeder identifies weeds and kills them with a targeted laser, no chemical at all, mounted on a kit that turns an ordinary tractor autonomous in the same stroke. Robots, in other words, are not just replacing the driver. They are doing something the driver never could: seeing every single plant in a field, one at a time, and treating each according to what it actually is.

The harvesting numbers tell an equally instructive story, and this is where the honest limits of the technology show most clearly. As one industry guide detailed, robotic berry-picking systems like Harvest CROO can manage roughly four to eight flats of strawberries an hour per picking arm, against eight to twelve for a skilled human picker — the robot is still, task for task, the slower worker. But the robot runs twenty hours a day without a break, a meal, or a wage that rises with scarcity, and in a labour market where the human pickers simply are not available at any wage, the comparison that matters is not robot-versus-human but robot-versus-nothing. High-value, labour-scarce crops — strawberries, wine grapes, orchard fruit — are where the robotic case is strongest precisely because the alternative is not a cheaper human. It is an unharvested field.

The furrow that isn’t on the map

This series has learned, across seventeen postcards, to distrust a demo the moment it looks too clean, and the field robot deserves the same scrutiny this series applied to the marathon-winning humanoid in Part XVIII. A tractor that drives a premapped quarter-section under fair skies is doing something genuinely difficult. A tractor that copes with a washed-out culvert after an unexpected storm, a fence a strong wind knocked flat overnight, or a dog that wanders into the row from the next farm over is doing something else entirely — and it is in exactly that gap, between the mapped field and the field as it actually is on any given Tuesday, that the autonomous tractor’s real test is still being run.

The industry’s own language is candid about this. Deere’s leadership speaks of full autonomy across the entire corn-and-soybean production cycle as a target for “the balance of the decade” — 2030, not next spring — and the machines being sold today are explicitly framed as driver-optional rather than driver-eliminated, retaining a remote human who can be summoned the moment a camera flags something the model has not seen before. This is the same architecture Part XIV found in China’s ten-thousand-robot factory-floor directive and Part XVII found in the home humanoid’s teleoperated Expert: the machine handles the routine, and a human — increasingly, a human watching several machines from a control room rather than riding in any one cab — remains one alert away from every field, ready to take over the instant reality deviates from the map. The autonomous tractor has not eliminated the farmer. It has turned the farmer into a supervisor of a fleet rather than an operator of one machine, watching dashboards instead of a windshield.

There is also a smaller, stranger counter-current worth noting, because it complicates the single-large-machine story this postcard has told so far. Alongside Deere’s half-million-dollar autonomous 9RX sits a growing fleet of much smaller, cheaper robots — lightweight electric platforms built by startups, designed not to replace one big tractor but to work in coordinated swarms across a field, each machine doing a narrow task and none of them irreplaceable if one breaks down. It is the same tension Part XI explored between the expensive commercial humanoid and the cheap, distributed, hackable open-source alternative, transplanted to the furrow: does the future of the farm belong to one enormous, capable, proprietary machine, or to a swarm of small, cheap, replaceable ones that fail gracefully because no single failure stops the harvest? Both models are being funded. Which one wins may determine whether the next generation of farms is owned by the equipment manufacturer or by the farmer who bought the parts.

The world this feeds

Zoom out from any single farm, and the field robot’s stakes turn out to be larger than any postcard about a battlefield or a stage, because this is the one territory in the whole series where the technology’s success or failure is measured, ultimately, in whether enough people eat.

The number that frames the entire enterprise is blunt: the planet crossed eight billion people in 2023 and keeps climbing, while the land available to feed those people is not expanding and the labour willing to farm it keeps shrinking in every wealthy nation at once. This is not a problem that can be solved by paying farmworkers more, because in much of North America and Europe there are, at almost any wage, simply not enough people choosing that work — the same demographic current traced through Japan’s airports in this series runs directly through Iowa’s cornfields and the Fraser Valley’s berry farms. A robot that can run twenty hours without tiring, precisely targeting only the weed and sparing the crop, is not merely a labour-saving convenience in this light. It is one of the more serious candidates for how an aging, urbanizing world keeps its granaries full at all.

There is a version of the future in which the autonomous tractor is remembered the way we now remember the mechanical reaper or the tractor itself at the turn of the last century — not as a curiosity, but as the invention that quietly kept pace with a hungrier planet while everyone was still marvelling at robots that could dance. This series has spent its second wave watching machines that perform on stages and battlefields and racetracks, drawing crowds and headlines in equal measure. The tractor plowing an empty field at dawn, with nobody watching and no camera crew present, may turn out to be doing the more consequential work of the entire humanoid century — it simply will never trend.

What the horse remembers

From the balcony, the field robot is the quietest and perhaps the most important postcard this series has filed, because it is solving the oldest problem humans have ever had — food enough, grown reliably, without depending on hands that may not show up — with the least dramatic machine imaginable.

There is a specifically Canadian resonance here that I do not want to pass by. Canada is a country whose entire settlement pattern was drawn by the tractor’s predecessor — the plough horse, the combine, the vast mechanized farms of the Prairies that fed a young nation and then much of the world. We have always solved our agricultural labour problem through machines rather than people, because the distances were too great and the population too thin for any other answer. The autonomous tractor is not a rupture in that story. It is its next chapter, written by the same impulse that put the first steam thresher on a Saskatchewan quarter-section a century ago: if the hands are not there, teach the machine to do without them.

And there is a genuine, hopeful case here that this series has not always had the luxury of making without qualification. Unlike the battlefield robot or the surveillance-adjacent home humanoid, the autonomous tractor’s stakes are refreshingly uncomplicated: it grows food more precisely, with less waste, less chemical, and no risk to a human life at all. The worst-case failure of a driverless combine is a ruined row of corn, not a wrongful death or a privacy breach. This is, among all seventeen postcards this series has filed, one of the very few where the wonder can be embraced almost without a shadow — which is itself worth noticing, because it tells us something about which automations we should worry over and which we should simply welcome.

The tractor drives itself now, up one row and down the next, seeing every weed and sparing every crop plant the herbicide it does not need, while the farmer who owns it is somewhere else entirely — at a football game, at a table, doing the human things the machine has finally, quietly, made room for. Rosie never came to the kitchen the way the Jetsons promised. But something very like her arrived in the field first, and nobody built her a face.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one came from a furrow, and no one was watching it get there.
