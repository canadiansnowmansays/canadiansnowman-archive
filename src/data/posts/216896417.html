Introduction

My great-uncle Art was gassed in the First World War. He survived it — his lungs scarred but functioning — and came home to a country that had just helped build, however reluctantly, an entirely new kind of international institution: a League of Nations, founded on the proposition that if enough countries simply agreed, on paper, never to do this to one another again, they might actually manage it. He later took up smoking, heavily, the way a great many of that generation did, on the theory, I suppose, that having survived a gas attack a man had earned the right to fill his own lungs with whatever he pleased. He died of lung cancer decades on. I mention this not because it is a remarkable story — it is a depressingly common one, repeated in some form in nearly every family of that era, mine plainly not excepted — but because it is the story that sits underneath everything I am about to write. The League of Nations was not an abstraction to the people who built it. It was the promise extracted from a generation that had just watched millions of young men gassed, shelled, and drowned in mud, and had decided, not unreasonably, that there had to be a better way of settling arguments between great powers than doing that again. The people who wrote the League’s covenant were not naïve idealists floating above the carnage. Many of them had just climbed out of it.

There is a second thread in my family that runs closer to the institution itself, and it is the one that finally tipped this from an idea I had toyed with into a series I felt I had to write. Hugh Llewellyn Keenleyside — a relative on my mother’s side, close enough that I grew up calling him great-uncle Hugh, though the family has never entirely agreed on the precise degree of the connection, tracing back through a shared great-grandfather’s two marriages — was born in Toronto in 1898, which makes him almost exactly my other great-uncle’s contemporary. He served in the Canadian Expeditionary Force in that same war. He came home, took a degree at the University of British Columbia, finished a doctorate in the United States, taught history for a few years, and in 1928 sat one of the very first examinations ever held by Canada’s newly formed Department of External Affairs — one of only two candidates accepted out of 256 who wrote it, the other being a young history instructor and athletic coach at the University of Toronto named Lester Pearson. Keenleyside went on to open Canada’s first diplomatic mission in Japan in 1929, serve during the Second World War as one of the few voices in the Canadian government willing to protest openly the internment and property seizures of Japanese Canadians, become Canada’s first ambassador to Mexico, and then — the detail that matters most for what follows — spend 1950 to 1958 as director-general of the United Nations Technical Assistance Administration, one of the quieter, less glamorous arms of the organisation that Pearson and men like him had helped build after the League collapsed. In 1959 he briefly served as UN Under-Secretary-General for Public Administration before returning home to run British Columbia’s power authority. Keenleyside spent his life, in other words, standing on both sides of the hinge this series is about: a young soldier in the war that produced the first attempt at collective security, and decades later a senior official inside the second attempt — the one that is still, just barely, standing.

I am not writing this series because I have a family connection to the United Nations. I am writing it because I apparently cannot avoid one, and because that has left me with an unusually personal stake in a question I suspect most people have stopped asking with any real seriousness: does the thing actually work? Not “is it perfect” — nobody serious argues that — but does it, on balance, across eighty years, do more good than harm, and is the harm it fails to prevent a design flaw or simply the price of getting the great powers into the room at all.

Why now, and why the long way round

The easy answer to “why now” is sitting in front of me as I write this: the 81st session of the UN General Assembly opened this week in New York, with President Trump due to address the gathering, a security agreement on Greenland’s future scheduled for signature, and a full calendar of leaders’ meetings on Iran, Venezuela, and China layered on top. That alone would be worth a dispatch or two, and it will get one — the next entry in this series is a real-time account of what actually happens in that hall this week, written before I know how it ends, because I think there is value in a prediction on the record rather than a tidy retrospective assembled after the fact.

But a single week of speeches, however eventful, is not a series. What earns eighty years of attention is the pattern the week will almost certainly confirm: a Security Council built in 1945 around the assumption that five great powers could be persuaded to bind themselves to a system precisely because each of them retained a veto over anything that bound them too tightly. That was not an oversight. It was the price of American participation, without which the whole project would have repeated the League’s founding failure — an organisation designed largely by an American president whose own country never joined it. The veto was the fix for that problem. It bought in Washington, Moscow, London, Paris, and eventually Beijing, and it has held the institution together, after a fashion, for eight decades.

It has also been, ever since, the reason the Council goes silent on precisely the issues where the world most needs it to speak. Ask the Security Council to authorise action against a small, friendless state and it can move with real speed. Ask it to do anything at all about a war involving one of the five permanent members, or a client state one of them has decided to protect, and the mechanism seizes up on contact. Hungary in 1956. Afghanistan in 1979. Syria for most of the past decade. Ukraine since 2022. In each case the same five-sided lock closes for the same structural reason: the contentious East-West questions are, almost by definition, the ones where a permanent member has skin in the game, and a permanent member with skin in the game will use the veto it was promised in 1945 rather than accept a resolution that binds it. That is not the Council malfunctioning. That is the Council working exactly as designed, which is the uncomfortable part.

That contradiction — a body built for collective security that structurally cannot act against its own most powerful members — is the spine of this series. Everything else that follows: the founding, the failures, the odd bursts of genuine and under-credited success, the theatre and the farce, hangs off it.

The shape of what follows

After this week’s dispatch, the series goes backward before it goes forward. It begins with the League of Nations: Woodrow Wilson’s design, the U.S. Senate’s refusal to ratify American membership in it, and the structural weakness that followed from an organisation with no enforcement mechanism and no reliable buy-in from the power that had proposed it. It follows the League through the tests that broke it — Manchuria, Abyssinia, the remilitarisation of the Rhineland — each one a case study in what collective security looks like when nobody is actually willing to collect.

Then San Francisco, 1945, and the deliberate choice to build the veto into the Charter’s foundation rather than pretend it away. From there the series works forward through the Cold War deadlock the veto produced, a chapter on the Assembly floor as theatre — Khrushchev’s shoe, real and embellished in roughly equal measure; Castro’s four-and-a-half-hour speech the same session; Arafat’s holster; Ahmadinejad’s Columbia University performance during his own UN week; Mr. Trump’s escalator and teleprompter, more recently, at last year’s opening — because the spectacle is part of the record too, and worth examining for what it reveals as much as what it distracts from. It covers the Korean War loophole that only worked because the Soviets happened to be boycotting the Council that month, the peacekeeping and development successes that don’t depend on Security Council consensus and have quietly outlasted every political crisis of the organisation’s history, and the failures — Srebrenica, Rwanda, the 2003 Iraq invasion that proceeded without Council authorisation at all — that show what happens when the system’s design flaw stops being theoretical.

It closes with where things stand now: Syria, Ukraine, a Council that has spent years demonstrating it cannot act against one of its own permanent members, and the long-stalled argument over expanding the P5 to include India, Japan, Germany, or Brazil — a reform nobody currently holding a veto has any real incentive to grant, whatever polite language gets used in the General Assembly’s reform debates. The concluding essay brings the whole arc back to the present, closing the loop with whatever this week in New York actually produces, read against eighty years of a pattern it will either confirm or, more surprisingly, break.

I want to be honest about the balance I expect this series to strike, because I think it is worth stating before the history starts rather than discovering it essay by essay. This is not going to be an obituary for the United Nations, and it is not going to be an apologia for it either. The organisation that let Rwanda happen while peacekeepers stood by under an impossible mandate is the same organisation whose technical and development agencies have spent seventy years doing unglamorous, effective work that almost never makes a headline — work my own family had a hand in, which is precisely why I am wary of either extreme. The temptation, writing about an institution this large and this old, is to reach for a verdict: the UN is a toothless talking shop, or the UN is the last line against barbarism, take your pick. I don’t think either verdict survives contact with the actual record, and I’m not going to pretend otherwise for the sake of a tidier thesis.

A note on the title

Alfred Tennyson wrote, in 1842, of a future “Parliament of man, the Federation of the world” — a phrase the internationalists of both the 1920s and the 1940s leaned on more than once, usually without much irony, as if the line itself were a blueprint rather than a hope. I am borrowing it with rather more irony than they intended, because the honest history of the institutions built to realise it is one of genuine, if partial, achievement sitting uncomfortably alongside genuine, repeated failure — and because I suspect Tennyson, like most poets asked after the fact to draft a security architecture, would have been the first to admit he hadn’t thought through the veto problem. He wrote the aspiration decades before anyone had to sit in a chamber and actually negotiate who got to hold it up.

That, in the end, is the honest premise of this series: an aspiration old enough that a Victorian poet could articulate it in verse, an institution built twice — once badly, once somewhat better — to try to realise it, and a family history that happens to run through both attempts closely enough that I no longer feel I have the option of writing about this from a comfortable distance. My great-uncle paid for the League’s absence in his lungs. My great-uncle Hugh spent a career trying to make the UN’s quieter half actually function. I don’t know yet how this week in New York ends. I have a fairly strong suspicion, given last year’s performance, of roughly what Tuesday’s speech will sound like, and I suspect I am not alone in that. We’ll find out together, and then we’ll go back eighty years — further, really, back to a war that gassed my great-uncle and commissioned the whole project in the first place — to work out how we got here, and whether the parliament of man ever had a realistic chance of being anything more than a very large room where the powerful occasionally agree to disagree politely.
