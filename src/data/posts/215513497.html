Six carriers, six filings, one destination: that was the first two instalments. This one is about everyone else who put something on the record for CRTC Notice of Consultation 2026-134 — a wholesale-dependent competitor, the organisation that actually administers the Code day to day, three consumer and public-interest advocacy groups, and roughly a hundred individual Canadians, several of them arguing points sharp enough to stand beside anything a lawyer filed. Among all of it, exactly one institution ever names advance billing at all — and even that one stops short of asking the Commission to end it.

What you’re about to read: Two more consumer groups — neither mentions advance billing either. A line-by-line audit finding six places where the Commission’s own draft quietly weakens existing protections, unmarked as changes. And 81 individual Canadians, 85 percent of whom raised exactly the issue no institution ever formally asked the Commission to fix.

TekSavvy: fighting the industry’s fight, from the other side

TekSavvy’s August 11, 2026 intervention (Consumer Intervention 196), filed by VP of Regulatory and Carrier Affairs Andy Kaplan-Myrth, is the shortest and most narrowly targeted filing in the proceeding — nine pages, one ask. TekSavvy is a wholesale-based competitor, bound today by only one of the four existing codes, and its entire submission argues for keeping it that way: don’t extend the harmonised Code beyond the nine large facilities-based providers the Internet Code already covers.

Three arguments carry that ask, and the first is the most interesting: TekSavvy argues the Commission has spent two years restricting the fees carriers can charge customers at retail while leaving the equivalent charges carriers pay each other at wholesale untouched or rising — so a refund mandated at retail with no matching refund from the wholesale carrier underneath it is a real, uncompensated cost. TekSavvy never says “advance billing.” But the shape of the argument is identical to this whole campaign’s core claim, aimed the other way: a mismatch between who’s asked to eat the cost of money moving before the underlying obligation is settled, and who actually caused it. The industry treats a wholesale timing mismatch as a real cost. It does not treat the retail version — advance billing — the same way. Worth asking why.

The CCTS: the institution TELUS wants stripped of its job

The CCTS (Consumer Intervention 195) administers the existing codes and would administer the new one, which makes its filing worth reading specifically against TELUS’s Section 8 argument that the CRTC has unlawfully delegated quasi-judicial authority to it and should either strip it of that role or make its rulings appealable. The CCTS doesn’t answer that argument. It doesn’t cite the Order-in-Council or Participation Agreement that establish its authority, and it doesn’t engage TELUS’s theory at all — it simply restates the Commission’s preliminary view that the current roles should continue. Whether that’s caution or a calculated non-engagement, the effect is the same: the CCTS declines to defend itself, and PIAC does it instead, further down.

What the CCTS does bring is the most granular evidentiary picture in the proceeding, because it’s the party that reads every complaint. Its filing documents a real complaint involving a 57-page customer agreement covering three wireless lines — four separate Critical Information Summaries scattered across four different pages, three distinct 24-month device financing agreements, three device-return-option agreements each with their own terms — and makes the point, without editorial flourish, that its own trained staff find agreements like this hard to follow. The complaint data backs it up: small business complaints were 43 percent billing-related; home phone complaints still ran to 3,279 issues in 2024-25 despite the sector’s decline, with unexplained price-increase complaints up 60 percent year over year and breach-of-contract complaints up 178 percent. And on implementation timing, the body that would actually have to run the new Code asks for six months — a quarter of what Bell and TELUS say they need.

Two more consumer advocates, and neither one raises it either

PIAC wasn’t the only public-interest voice on the record. Union des consommateurs (Consumer Intervention 163), a Quebec-based network of 16 local consumer-rights groups, filed its own 24-page intervention on August 11, and the Samuelson-Glushko Canadian Internet Policy and Public Interest Clinic (CIPPIC) (Consumer Intervention 170), a University of Ottawa public-interest law clinic, filed the same day. Both are substantial, technically serious documents. Neither mentions advance billing once.

UC’s organizing principle is nivellement par le haut — “levelling up”: wherever the four existing codes disagree, the harmonised Code should adopt whichever version protects consumers more, not whichever is administratively lighter. Its signature, most concretely argued position is that device financing and rental contracts should be fully decoupled from the underlying service contract, so a customer can cancel or switch plans without triggering an accelerated device balance. UC backs this with a real example: a customer on a 24-month contract for a $1,300 device, financed at $811.09 with a $489 return-option balance, who cancelled with five months left and was billed $657.89 combined — more than half the device’s value — to leave early. UC cites the Competition Bureau’s own 2019 submission making the identical point about device- and service-contract entanglement acting as a switching barrier. UC also wants prepaid wireless customers — who skew older and lower-income — brought inside protections currently reserved for postpaid customers, no small-ISP exemption, and a hard six-month implementation window, matching the CCTS and undercutting every carrier’s 18-to-24-month ask by a wide margin.

CIPPIC’s contribution is different in kind from every other filing on the record, including the other consumer groups’: rather than proposing new protections, it forensically audits what the Commission’s own Working Document quietly removed relative to the four codes it consolidates, and catches six downgrades the drafting nowhere marks as substantive. The guaranteed minimum usage during a customer’s trial period — historically at least half the monthly allowance, doubled for customers with disabilities — simply disappears, leaving a 30-day trial window with no floor on how much of the service a customer is actually allowed to test. The device-unlocking obligation, which used to cover any device locked to a carrier’s network, gets narrowed by the insertion of a single word (”such”) to cover only devices the carrier itself supplied. The Internet Code’s rule letting a customer name their own cancellation date — useful for coordinating a switch with no service gap — is silently swapped for the weaker Wireless Code default, where cancellation only takes effect once the provider receives notice. The security-deposit ceiling disappears entirely, the guaranteed interest rate on deposits drops, and mandatory deposit reviews are cut from twice a year to once. And the explicit statement that “a failure to pay arrears is not, by itself, fraud” — the same protection Ysobella Phillipe independently flagged as missing, below — simply isn’t carried forward from the Deposit and Disconnection Code into the new draft. None of this is industry lobbying for less. It’s the Commission’s own consolidation, as drafted, quietly landing on the weaker option in six separate places.

CIPPIC also converges independently with UC on decoupling device financing from cancellation penalties, backed by its own data points: CCTS complaints hit 23,647 in 2024-25, up 17 percent, then up a further 61 percent year-over-year at mid-year; only 3 percent of Canadians can correctly identify the existing codes; awareness has fallen from 20 percent to 14 percent since 2022 despite mandatory promotion requirements already in force — a finding that reads less as a knock on CIPPIC and more as an indictment of how little the Commission’s own existing promotion rules have actually accomplished.

Then there’s the one consumer group that does raise advance billing — just not as its own ask.

PIAC: the coalition finally shows up on the page

PIAC’s August 14, 2026 intervention (Consumer Intervention 194) is the document that changes what this campaign actually is. Up to this point, this was one Canadian’s regulatory complaint, backed by press coverage and a handful of allies contacted directly. PIAC’s filing is the moment it becomes part of the record in someone else’s voice.

Reviewing the individual interventions filed in this proceeding, PIAC names me directly — under my full legal name, Stephen Lawton, as it appears on my CRTC filing, rather than the byline I write under here — cited to “Consumer Intervention 14.” They walk through the Fido story almost beat for beat: the December 2025 mid-cycle churn to Koodo, the account balance frozen at $42.38 instead of the roughly $4.10 actually owed, the eliminated inbound-call line, the AI chat system blocking access to a human agent, the callback denied because the account was already cancelled, the $1.22 late fee on top of it all. PIAC quotes my actual ask verbatim — a Code provision requiring carriers to bill in arrears for service actually provided — and my line that the Commission has, until now, regulated only the consequences of advance billing, through the prorated-refund requirement, without ever asking whether the underlying practice should exist. PIAC separately cites Lev Jaeger, who asked the Commission to stop carriers billing in advance and make payment records update as fast as usage data does, and Robert de Salis, who put it more plainly than either of us managed: he always seems to be the one paying, and the provider always gets to hold his money.

What PIAC does not do is adopt a ban on advance billing as one of its own recommendations. Its own priorities and its own clause-by-clause redline both leave the point untouched, exactly as the carriers, TekSavvy, the CCTS, UC, and CIPPIC all did. The honest reading isn’t that PIAC dismissed the issue — naming four consumers by intervention number is not a small gesture from an organisation with sixty-two pages of its own priorities to cover. It’s that the practice remains, after everything, something individual Canadians raised and a national advocate acknowledged, without a single institution turning it into a formal ask.

The rest of PIAC’s filing is the clearest single rebuttal to the industry’s version of “harmonisation” anywhere in the proceeding — the same principle UC calls levelling up and CIPPIC’s audit defends by omission: where a protection could reasonably apply across services, the strongest version should become the standard, not the weakest. PIAC proposes inserting exactly that into the Code’s stated Objectives, adding “and importantly to strengthen consumer protections” to the founding text. Whether that phrase survives to the final version is close to the whole fight in miniature.

What the individual record actually shows

I went back through the roughly 140 individual interventions on the record as of this writing, reading in sequence rather than relying on PIAC’s sample, and the picture is more striking than “four Canadians raised it.” Advance billing appears nowhere in the first thirteen filings. Starting at intervention #14 — mine, filed the day the Toronto Star piece ran — and running for roughly the next hundred submissions, the overwhelming majority of individual Canadians who address billing practices at all name advance billing specifically, in almost every case unprompted and in their own words: pay after the service is delivered, not before; stop making customers chase refunds; treat telecom like every other utility. Several of these submissions are genuinely substantive. One, filed by a self-identified accountant, works through the practice under IFRS 15 and ASC 606 — the accounting standards that govern when a company is allowed to book revenue as earned, essentially: not before the service is actually delivered — arguing that carriers are booking cash they haven’t actually earned. Another proposes that the CCTS’s own per-complaint regulatory fees give carriers a direct financial incentive to switch billing models, calling it a genuine “win-win.” Only one clear dissent turned up in the entire sequence — an intervention arguing against CRTC regulation generally, not defending advance billing on its merits.

Putting a number on it: excluding my own founding intervention, 81 other individual Canadians filed on the record between intervention #14 and #135, the stretch where billing practices dominate the individual record. I counted a filing as support if it asked to end advance billing, to bill in arrears, to pay only after service is delivered, or to stop making customers prepay and then chase a refund; I did not count general bill-shock or affordability complaints that raised no timing question at all. By that rule, 69 of the 81 — roughly 85 percent — supported ending advance billing, almost always unprompted and in their own words. One dissented, arguing against CRTC regulation generally rather than defending the practice itself. The remainder addressed other issues without taking a position either way. That’s not a sample PIAC selected to illustrate a point. That’s most of the people who showed up.

Past roughly intervention #100, the subject matter broadens — hold times, device-financing disputes, “unlimited” plan marketing, general affordability — and advance billing stops being the dominant theme, even as the individual record keeps running well past #135. But several of the later filings are worth naming on their own terms. Ysobella Phillipe filed a rigorously argued submission on how the Code’s entire “account holder” architecture can be turned into a control mechanism by an abusive partner against a device user who depends on the same account for access to police, counsel, or a doctor, with eight fully drafted amendments addressing it — including, independently of CIPPIC, the same missing “failure to pay arrears is not fraud” safeguard. Josh Killham filed a complete, technically sound case for extending the existing “unsolicited services” rule to television, so a customer can’t be billed indefinitely for a discretionary channel package they never selected and were never told they had. And Émile Arsalane, filing with arguments prepared alongside Marc Nanni, catalogued conditional pricing penalties, dark-pattern payment interfaces, post-cancellation billing lockouts, and disguised activation fees, with a specific proposal — interest plus a daily penalty — for exactly the kind of refund delay this campaign is about, even without naming advance billing directly.

None of that changes the headline finding. Eleven institutional parties are now confirmed on the record — six carriers, TekSavvy, the CCTS, PIAC, UC, and CIPPIC — and precisely one of them, PIAC, ever writes the words “advance billing” down, and only to report what individual Canadians said, not to adopt it. Where it stands heading into November 30

Bell, TELUS, Rogers, Cogeco, and PIAC have all requested to appear at the hearing in Gatineau; the CCTS is appearing as well, and Union des consommateurs and CIPPIC have both requested to appear. Quebecor has only reserved the right, and SaskTel and TekSavvy have said they don’t intend to appear, though both keep the option open. An intervener requesting an oral appearance has to explain, on the form itself, what a hearing would add that the written submission doesn’t. I considered mine extensive and self-explanatory enough that I didn’t pursue it — that, and a plain preference to keep some matters private rather than aired in cross-examination by counsel for six carriers, was reason enough to let the written record speak for itself.

Whether a reply-comment phase opens after the hearing remains unconfirmed. Until it does, the written record is what exists: six converging carrier filings, a wholesale competitor arguing a structurally similar grievance from the opposite direction, an administrator documenting the real cost of contract complexity, three consumer and public-interest groups converging from three different routes on the same “don’t weaken what already exists” principle, and 85 percent of the individual Canadians who filed on billing practices, saying overwhelmingly and mostly in their own words, that being billed for service you haven’t received yet is a problem worth fixing.

Here’s the only forecast I’ll make, and it isn’t about the outcome: ignoring that stretch of the individual record would now be a choice, not an oversight.
