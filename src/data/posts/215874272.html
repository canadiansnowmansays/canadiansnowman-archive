Every neighbourhood eventually produces one: the fellow who walks the block admiring your storefront a little too closely, who mentions, almost in passing, how much trouble a broken window could cause, and who happens to know a fellow who could make sure nothing like that ever happens to you. He does not call it extortion. He calls it a relationship. Seven weeks into this instalment of “The Enforcers,” the relationship has matured into something the family is finally willing to put in writing — laminated, tri-folded, and left on the table of every institution in the country that has ever needed something from Washington.

FAMILY PROTECTIVE SERVICES A Division of the Executive Branch RATE CARD & CLIENT TESTIMONIALS (Please Do Not Remove From Premises — Or Do. We’ll Send Someone.)

OUR PHILOSOPHY

At Family Protective Services, we believe every institution deserves peace of mind. Universities, performing-arts centres, blue-state mayors — all of you have something valuable, and all of you have, at one time or another, said something we found disrespectful. We don’t hold grudges. We hold leverage, which lasts longer and compounds interest. Below, please find our three service tiers, illustrated with case studies from satisfied — or at least settled — clients.

BRONZE TIER: THE COURTESY VISIT “We noticed your city has a crime problem. We’d hate for it to get worse.”

The Bronze package requires no payment up front — only an invitation, real or invented. Baltimore’s Governor Wes Moore learned this on August 24, 2026, when he wrote the President a letter proposing a public safety walk through the city that fall. Mr. Trump replied on social media that he would “much prefer” Mr. Moore clean up his own “crime disaster” first, and added that if the Governor ever did need help — the way, he noted, Gavin Newsom supposedly had in Los Angeles — he would happily send in “the troops.” The offer, framed as a favour, functioned as a diagnosis, a warning, and an implied bill, all delivered in the same paragraph.

Baltimore’s application remains pending, mostly because Mr. Moore has declined to apply. It is, in this respect, still ahead of Memphis, which received a deployment last September without applying, and St. Louis, which Mr. Trump added to the account almost as an afterthought — “we’ll get to St. Louis also” — the way a landlord announces which building is next.

Washington, D.C., meanwhile, was not so fortunate: it received the full course unbidden and has never been permitted to cancel. As the Associated Press reported this July, the Pentagon confirmed it was extending the Guard’s D.C. deployment — originally justified as an emergency response to a “crime emergency” the city’s own numbers didn’t support — through Inauguration Day 2029, “or until terminated by the President.” The Congressional Budget Office puts the running cost above three million dollars a day. Family Protective Services does not itemise its invoices. It simply keeps the meter running until someone with subpoena power asks it to stop.

SILVER TIER: THE INSTITUTIONAL REALIGNMENT PACKAGE “That’s a lovely endowment you’ve got there.”

For clients whose vulnerability is measured in federal grant dollars rather than crime statistics, Family Protective Services offers the Silver package: a frozen funding stream, a list of demands concerning admissions and hiring practices, and a settlement figure that restores the funding once the client has publicly agreed the arrangement was their idea. Columbia set the template in the summer of 2025 with a $221 million payment. Cornell followed that November, agreeing to pay $30 million directly to the federal government and invest a further $30 million in agricultural research, in exchange for the restoration of over $250 million that had been withheld. University President Michael Kotlikoff, in the traditional gesture of the newly released hostage thanking his captor’s professionalism, wrote that Cornell looked forward to “resuming the long and fruitful partnership with the federal government.” Both settlements remain the working template; nothing about the arrangement has needed updating since.

Family Protective Services would like to note, for transparency, that the partnership was never actually interrupted from its side. It simply declined to release funds Congress had already appropriated until the client agreed the withholding had been reasonable all along. This is not a threat. It is, as the paperwork always says, a resolution.

PLATINUM TIER: THE LEGACY NAMING RIGHTS PACKAGE “Nice cultural institution you’ve got there. Shame if it stayed under a tarp forever.”

Our flagship offering, and the subject of today’s client spotlight. Since June, the John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts has occupied an unusual position in our portfolio: a client who insists it isn’t a client at all, even as its own board keeps re-applying for the service it was ordered to cancel.

The facts, briefly, for readers joining mid-instalment. In May, U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper ruled that the Kennedy Center's board had exceeded its authority when it added the President's name to the building in December, and ordered the letters removed — a process completed in June, leaving the façade wrapped in scaffolding and tarpaulin for the balance of the summer. In August, the board — stacked, as Axios reported, with the President's own appointees, who then elected him its chair — voted 20 to 3 to try again, proposing an inscription reading "Restored and Renovated by President Donald J. Trump" beneath the existing sign, along with a plan to rename the plaza out front in his honour. When Judge Cooper asked, at a hearing, why the board was in such a hurry to install it by September 8th, government counsel could only explain that the date matched the board's own vote — which is a bit like a debtor explaining he needs the money by Thursday because that's when he wrote the IOU.

The tribute logic was not left to subtext. Justice Department lawyers told the court that blocking the inscription would cause donors to flee and "structural rehabilitation to stop," warning there would "simply not be the expertise or funding necessary to rebuild and renovate this decimated structure" without it. Translated out of the language of litigation and into the language of the protection racket, the argument runs: it would be a shame if this beautiful national memorial fell down, and it just so happens that only one thing can save it. Lawyers for Representative Joyce Beatty of Ohio, an ex officio board member who has fought the renaming from the start, put it more plainly, accusing the administration of effectively "taking the center hostage."

THE SEPTEMBER 15 RULING

That afternoon, the claim was tested, and the claimant lost. Judge Cooper ruled that the proposed inscription — along with the renamed plaza — would violate his earlier order exactly as the original renaming had, calling the board's attempt to distinguish "restored and renovated by" from a plaque baldly reading "Trump" a matter of "linguistic gymnastics." "Defendants cannot install memorials for President Trump," he wrote, without Congress's blessing — a body, the ruling notes, that built the place for a different, deceased president and never authorised a second name on the wall. He left the door open only a crack, on a separate proposal to credit a still-hypothetical hundred-million-dollar endowment fund, while making plain he expected to close that door too, on the same grounds, "just as plainly."

It is worth remembering, while the ink is still wet on this one, that the Kennedy Center has already lost money defending the racket once this year: a federal judge awarded jazz musician Chuck Redd more than a quarter of a million dollars in legal fees last month, after the Center itself sued him for cancelling a performance in protest of the rebrand. Family Protective Services notes that suing your own critics for declining to perform under your new sign is not, strictly, part of the Platinum package — but it is the kind of complimentary enthusiasm that keeps repeat clients coming back, whether they want to or not.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Q: My institution has done nothing wrong. Why have I received a visit? A: Wrongdoing is not a precondition of service. Neither, strictly, is a legal theory. As this newsletter documented in July, government lawyers have already conceded in open court that a prior round of grant terminations tracked nothing but which states voted for the President’s opponent — a rationale three separate federal judges, in three separate districts, have each rejected as unconstitutional this year alone. Family Protective Services considers three independent rejections not a pattern but a coincidence, and reserves the right to keep testing that theory before a fourth judge, a fifth, and however many it takes.

Q: The settlement figure seems arbitrary. Who determines it? A: The client does, eventually, after several rounds of negotiation in which only one party is permitted to withhold funds Congress already appropriated. This is known internally as “market pricing.”

Q: What happens if I win in court? A: You may, as the Kennedy Center’s board has now done twice, simply try the same manoeuvre again in a slightly different font. Family Protective Services considers a losing ruling to be less a verdict than a rough draft.

FINE PRINT

Family Protective Services reserves the right to characterise any of the above as “programmatic realignment,” “restoring accountability,” or “a perfectly reasonable basis” for reappraising decisions after the fact, per a phrase government attorneys have already used in a related grant-termination case and which we have taken the liberty of adopting as a house slogan. Clients dissatisfied with service tiers are reminded that a fourth is available upon request, and that we do not, as a rule, discuss the fourth tier in writing.

STOP PRESS: THE HOUSE COMMENTS ON TIER 4

Above, Family Protective Services noted that it does not, as a rule, discuss the fourth tier in writing, and that the house has never had to. On the night of Wednesday, September 17, the house did not so much discuss it as display it — on a poster board, through the window of Air Force One, to a runway full of photographers holding lenses long enough to read the caption from the tarmac. The caption, in red capitals sized for a client with no depth perception, read: KENNEDY CENTER DEMOLISHED.

Minutes after the September 15 ruling, the board voted to close the building outright, citing a partial ceiling collapse and, not for the first time, its own finances — finances Family Protective Services would note were considerably healthier before the client agreed to this arrangement in the first place. Representative Beatty’s lawyers, already in court over the closure, amended their filing within hours to ask the judge to make “crystal clear” that no demolition may proceed without notice. A judge obliged only partway, on Thursday, September 18: no emergency hearing, but a reminder that the administration had already promised, in an earlier round of this same litigation, to give thirty days’ notice before bringing anything down. Family Protective Services considers thirty days a generous grace period, and notes that the client is welcome to spend it however it likes.

The reversal is worth dating precisely, for clients keeping their own file. In February, when this arrangement began, Mr. Trump told reporters, “I’m not ripping it down.” By September 17, touring North Carolina hours before the poster board appeared, the line had become: without recognition, “it’s going to close. It’ll end up being ripped down.” Seven months is not, by the standards of this administration’s other renovation projects, a long wait. The White House ballroom construction was also promised not to “interfere” with the East Wing, before the East Wing came down anyway; the limestone friezes atop a certain Fifth Avenue building were also, once, promised preservation, in 1980, before they were not preserved. Family Protective Services does not call this a pattern. It calls it a house style.

This piece is a work of satire. The account of the Kennedy Center litigation — the May and September rulings, the board's August vote and its reasoning, the Justice Department's "donor flight" argument, the Redd fee award, the board's closure vote, the Air Force One poster-board photographs, Mr. Trump's February and September statements on demolition, and Representative Beatty's amended filing and the judge's thirty-day notice order — is drawn from contemporaneous reporting and court filings and is described accurately. The Cornell and Columbia settlement figures are likewise drawn from the universities' own announcements. The "Family Protective Services" framing device is the author's own, offered because the government's own account of its bargain with the Center — restore the name or watch the building fail — already reads like a shakedown with a marble lobby.
