On August 20, 2026, Hui Ka Yan stood in a courtroom in Shenzhen in a navy blue shirt, flanked by two police officers, and heard a judge sentence him to life in prison. It was the first time the public had seen him since he vanished from view in 2023. His hair, once the jet black favoured by Chinese business elites photographed at Politburo-adjacent functions, had gone white. As reported in The New York Times, The Shenzhen Intermediate People’s Court found that between 2016 and 2021, Hui and the company he founded — Evergrande, once China’s largest property developer — had engaged in large-scale financial fraud, misuse of funds, fund-raising fraud, and the illegal taking of public deposits. He was ordered to forfeit every personal asset he had. The court fined Evergrande Group and its real estate subsidiary a combined $2.35 billion, stripped Hui of his political rights for life, and — across two Shenzhen courts, in the same session — sentenced fifty-six other people, including Hui’s two sons, to terms ranging from twenty-two months to eighteen years.

Read on its own, it is a corporate-fraud verdict: a fallen tycoon, an overleveraged company, a court settling accounts thirteen years after the accounts stopped balancing. Read against the fifteen parts that preceded it, it is something more specific — the moment the property crisis this series has been carrying as background risk since Part XI stopped being background and became a verdict with a defendant’s name attached.

Hui founded Evergrande in 1996 in southern China, at the exact moment the patient decades described in Part XI were beginning to move people out of villages and into cities at a pace no country had previously attempted. Over the following two decades, China’s urban population swelled by roughly 400 million — the migration Part XVI’s addendum traces into studio apartments and green buttons and dazi companions. Somebody had to build where those 400 million were going to live. Evergrande built more of it than almost anyone.

The mechanism was not exotic. Local governments, whose formal tax base had been narrowed by reforms in the 1990s, discovered that leasing land to developers was the most reliable source of revenue available to them, and leaned on it accordingly. Developers borrowed against the land and against presold apartments to finance the next phase of construction before the current phase was finished, a model that works exactly as long as prices keep rising and credit keeps flowing. Banks lent freely, because property-backed lending had never failed at scale in living memory. Households, cut loose from the danwei’s social scaffolding and told the market rewarded ambition, treated the apartment as the safest possible store of value — a belief the state did nothing to discourage, because the belief was load-bearing for the entire system. By some estimates, real estate and its related industries accounted for as much as a quarter of Chinese GDP at the arrangement’s peak. Evergrande, at its height, held more than $300 billion in liabilities and employed, directly and through its supply chain, hundreds of thousands of people. It also owned a soccer team and an electric vehicle company, because that is what unlimited leverage looks like when it is still, for the moment, working.

By 2020, Beijing had started to worry that the arrangement it had tolerated for two decades was becoming a threat to the financial system it was meant to serve. Officials imposed the “three red lines” — debt-ratio restrictions designed to force the most indebted developers to deleverage. For a company built on the assumption that credit would always be available to finance the next presale, the restrictions were not a correction. They were a cutoff. Evergrande defaulted in 2021. Home buyers who had paid in advance for apartments that were never finished protested in cities across the country. An internal investigation later found that executives had spent late 2020 arranging for third parties to take out loans against Evergrande subsidiaries as collateral, specifically to circumvent the borrowing restrictions the government had just imposed — the fraud the court ruled on this month, committed in the act of trying to outrun the tightening described in Part XIV.

What the court said about Hui is worth reading in full, because it is not really a statement about accounting irregularities. His and Evergrande’s actions, the court found, “seriously disrupted the socialist market economic order,” produced “exceptionally heavy economic losses,” and caused harm to society that is “extremely grave.” This is the same register the anti-corruption campaign in Part XII used against Zhou Yongkang and Bo Xilai: language that identifies a systemic failure and then locates its cause entirely inside one man’s conduct. The pattern holds here with unusual clarity, because the alternative explanation is available in this series’ own earlier parts. The land-leasing arrangement that gave local governments their revenue, the light-touch lending standards that let developers borrow against presales, the tacit state endorsement of property as the household’s primary savings vehicle — none of that was Hui Ka Yan’s invention. He built the largest structure the arrangement allowed to be built, and when the arrangement’s own architects tightened the rules without warning, the largest structure was the first to come down. A court can rule on what he did inside that arrangement. It cannot rule on the arrangement itself, because the arrangement was policy, and policy does not stand trial.

The sentence is nonetheless a message, and the intended audience is not really Hui Ka Yan, who is seventy-seven years old and will not live to test the appeal. The audience is every other developer still standing, every local official whose finances still depend on land sales, and every household still deciding whether property is a place to live or a wealth vehicle worth defending. The message is that the state, not the market, retains final authority over who gets ruined when a bet goes bad — the same authority Xi demonstrated over Jack Ma, over the tutoring industry, over the tech platforms whose founders discovered, one by one across his tenure, that success purchased no immunity from a Communist Party that had decided the terms had changed. Hui Ka Yan is simply the developer whose ruin happened to be largest.

The account the verdict cannot close is the one still owed to everyone who is not Hui Ka Yan.

The property crisis that began with Evergrande’s 2021 default has not resolved; it has metastasised into a slow-motion deflation that the government has spent five years trying and largely failing to arrest. Home prices in dozens of Chinese cities remain well below their 2021 peaks. Households that treated an apartment as a retirement plan have watched that plan lose a third or more of its value, with no clear floor in sight. Local governments that built their budgets on land-sale revenue have had to cut services, delay infrastructure, and in some documented cases stop paying civil servants on schedule. The investment-led growth model that built the factory floor Part XI described cannot simply be swapped for the technology-led model the 141 pages propose — not on the timeline the plan assumes, and not without a transition that asks a generation of households to accept that the wealth they were told to accumulate in concrete was never as solid as it looked.

This is where Part XVI left the property crisis: one risk among several the 141 pages do not fully account for, filed alongside the demographic headwinds and the succession problem as a condition to be managed rather than a story with an ending. The sentencing supplies the ending, or at least an ending — not a resolution of the underlying arrangement, but a ritual closing of one account, staged with the full theatre a one-party state can bring to a courtroom, so that the larger account can remain open a while longer without anyone having to say so.

A Canadian note belongs here, because I have written this series, from Part I to now, alongside the silence of a friend in Shenzhen who stopped replying to my essays somewhere around the time Hui Ka Yan disappeared from public view in 2023. I do not know whether he owned an apartment financed against an Evergrande presale, or whether his savings sat in a property that has not yet found its floor. I know that Shenzhen — the city Deng designated the first Special Economic Zone, the city where this sentence was handed down — is as good a place as any in China to have made that bet, and as good a place as any to be living with what the bet has cost.

The court gave Hui Ka Yan a life sentence and took everything he owned. It did not, and could not, give anyone their apartment’s value back. That account is still open. The next part of this series is about who is left holding it.
