On September 1, 2026, a letter from the United States Department of Justice arrived on the desk of Judge Sidney Stein in the Southern District of New York. It was twenty pages long, it was addressed to the sprawling consolidated litigation against OpenAI and Microsoft, and it carried the modest-sounding title of a “Statement of Interest of the United States.”

Sixteen days later, on September 17, the same courtroom received something rather less modest. Unsealed filings in The New York Times v. Microsoft and OpenAI showed an OpenAI researcher telling company president Greg Brockman about a way around the Times paywall. Brockman’s reply, as TechBrew summarised the filing, was “ah nice.” The same filings quoted a January 2023 internal memo from Brent Hecht, Microsoft’s director of applied science, who, in TechCrunch’s account of the unredacted record, described what his own industry was doing as “an astonishing theft of unprecedented proportions.”

So the sequence runs like this. First, the government of the United States told the judge that the copying was lawful and important to national security. Then the defendants’ own employees turned out to have called it theft in writing. Microsoft has said Hecht’s memo reflects one employee’s view and is not a legal analysis. That is fair enough. But it is a remarkable thing when the most damning description of the defendants’ conduct comes from the defendants, and the most eloquent defence comes from the Department of Justice.

Part IV of this series, “The Paper Trail,” ended with a prediction that Judge Stein’s ruling would push the industry’s real answer on fair use into 2027. That prediction still stands. What has changed is that the judge is no longer deciding alone. The executive branch has walked into the courtroom, pulled up a chair beside the defence table, and told everyone which verdict it expects.

What the Government Told the Judge

A statement of interest is filed under 28 U.S.C. § 517, which lets the Attorney General send a lawyer into any federal case touching the interests of the United States without asking the court’s permission. It is not binding. Judge Stein may read it, set it aside, and rule however the law requires. As Sterne Kessler’s client alert laid out, the government’s core position is that training an AI model on copyrighted material does not, in and of itself, violate copyright law. It borrowed its reasoning from Judge William Alsup’s June 2025 ruling in Bartz v. Anthropic: the purpose of copying a novel to build a model differs in kind from the purpose of the novel, which is to be read.

The filing then went further than a friendly brief needed to go. Sterne Kessler noted that it attacked the government’s own Copyright Office, alluding to the removal of the Register of Copyrights, arguing that her analysis deserved no deference, and calling her reasoning “threadbare.” The Register in question, Shira Perlmutter, was fired in May 2025 shortly after her office published a report suggesting that AI training might not always be fair use. A federal appeals court later ruled that the President had lacked the authority to remove her, and she was reinstated. The Justice Department is now asking a federal judge to disregard the expert office Congress created, having first tried to remove the expert.

The brief’s language was not restrained. The China Intellectual Property Lawyers Network’s summary of the filing records the government calling LLM training “transformative — spectacularly so.” It also cited executive orders from January 2025 and June 2026 declaring American AI dominance to be national policy, and warned that paid licensing would disadvantage American companies against foreign rivals that ignore American intellectual property law.

That last warning is worth holding on to. We will need it in a moment.

Alex Reisner, writing in The Atlantic on September 25, 2026, went looking for the evidence behind the national-security claim and found very little. The filing lists the military uses of AI: analysing intelligence, improving drones and robotic ships, recommending where to aim missile strikes. Reisner’s reasonable question is how a George Saunders short-story collection or a Bad Bunny music video contributes to target selection. The more relevant point in the brief is that language models help officials draw inferences from real-world facts, such as those reported in New York Times articles. In other words, the government’s security case rests partly on officials reading the newspaper through ChatGPT. The brief does not explain why that requires the newspaper to go unpaid.

Two Definitions of Theft

Now to the warning about foreign rivals who do not respect American intellectual property.

On July 21, 2026, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent went on Fox Business to discuss Chinese AI models. His concern was distillation, the practice of training one model on the outputs of another. As The Hill reported, Bessent said the administration was finding watermarks of American models inside Chinese ones and called it “unacceptable.” He promised action within weeks.

The action came on September 8. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, the National Security Agency and the FBI issued a joint advisory naming Chinese firms, including DeepSeek and Alibaba, as the source of high-volume distillation campaigns against American AI companies. Bessent followed up on X. As CNN reported, he warned that when Chinese firms run covert distillation attacks that “cross the line into IP theft,” sanctions and Entity List designations would follow.

Put the two positions side by side, and the government’s theory of intellectual property becomes clear. When an American company copies millions of newspaper articles, novels and songs without permission to build a commercial product, that is spectacular transformation and a national-security necessity. When a Chinese company copies the outputs of that American product without permission to build a competing commercial product, that is industrial-scale theft warranting sanctions.

It is possible to reconcile these positions in legal terms. The Chinese labs are accused of using fraudulent accounts and proxy networks to evade terms of service and export controls, which is a different legal wrong from copyright infringement. Distillation of machine output may not even be copyrightable, since there is no human author. A careful lawyer could argue the two cases for a week.

But the government is not being careful. It is using the moral vocabulary of theft for one case and the moral vocabulary of progress for the other. The deciding variable is not the conduct. It is whose property gets taken. Theft, in this administration’s working definition, is a matter of nationality.

The Shareholder at the Defence Table

There is another detail the statement of interest left out.

On July 2, 2026, the Financial Times reported that OpenAI had proposed giving the United States government a 5 per cent stake in the company. CNBC put the value at roughly $42.6 billion, based on OpenAI’s $852 billion valuation. Sam Altman reportedly pitched it as a way to share AI’s upside with the public, through a vehicle modelled on the Alaska Permanent Fund. Other reporting said his conversations included the President, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and, yes, Treasury Secretary Bessent. The talks were described as preliminary, and the arrangement would probably need an act of Congress.

Above the Law noticed the omission immediately. Its headline on the statement of interest observed that the Justice Department had told the court AI training was fair use and neglected to mention it was negotiating a stake in OpenAI. Even if the talks never produce a share certificate, the appearance is poor. A government in negotiations to own a piece of a defendant is not an ideal neutral voice on what that defendant owes the plaintiffs. A billion-dollar damages award against OpenAI would be, in the most literal sense, a hit to a prospective government asset.

A telecom professional learns early that the customer always notices who is standing behind the counter. A regulator that is also a shareholder is standing on both sides of it.

The Objection from Inside the Tent

What makes this episode more than another partisan skirmish is where the loudest objection came from.

Mike Davis is a conservative lawyer and strategist closely aligned with the Trump legal movement. He is not a man given to criticising this Justice Department. His Fox News op-ed opens by calling it the best Justice Department in modern history. He then asked it to withdraw the statement of interest.

In that op-ed, Davis argued that the government’s position would let Big Tech scrape and commercialise every creative work in America without permission or payment. He said conservatives have long demanded that AI policy protect what he calls the 4Cs: children, communities, conservatives and creators. He pointed out that the music industry already solves the problem through licensing, so that bars and restaurants can play music and musicians still get paid. And he gave the government’s national-security reasoning its most memorable summary, rejecting it as the proposition: “We must steal like China to compete against China.”

That line deserves a moment. The administration’s own allies can see that its argument amounts to adopting the conduct it condemns in Beijing and then calling it patriotism.

Davis’s objection is also the sturdiest one available, because it does not depend on liking the New York Times. It is a property-rights argument. Businesses pay for their inputs. An industry spending hundreds of billions of dollars a year on chips and data centres can afford to pay for books and articles. If it could not, that would be a strange sort of free market.

A Tool Built for Other Purposes

Reisner’s Atlantic piece usefully reminds readers what statements of interest have historically been for. The Obama Justice Department filed one in 2015 in Boise, Idaho, urging a court not to endorse arresting people for sleeping on the street. The Biden Justice Department filed one in 2023 defending transgender patients denied care under a new Kentucky law. Whatever one thinks of those positions, they used the government’s voice to defend individuals against more powerful institutions.

The September 1 filing reverses the direction. It uses the government’s voice to defend some of the most valuable companies in history against authors, journalists and musicians. And it does so at scale, in a consolidated proceeding that bundles the Times with book authors and other publishers.

Christine Bartholomew, a law professor at the University at Buffalo, told Reisner that the sheer volume of executive orders and statements of interest risks crowding out the courts’ ability to “develop responses independently.” She raised the separation of powers directly: the worry is not that the executive has an opinion, but that it is starting to dictate the interpretation.

Judges are not obliged to follow a statement of interest. But context matters. As Reisner notes, this is an administration that has attempted to impeach a judge who ruled against its immigration policy, and has made a habit of attacking individual judges by name. A non-binding suggestion from a government that punishes disagreement does not feel entirely non-binding.

The Disclosure

This series is written in open collaboration with Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, and I say so on my About page. That matters for this essay more than most, because Anthropic is threaded through it.

The distillation complaint that Bessent picked up began with Anthropic. As Fortune reported on February 24, 2026, the company alleged that DeepSeek, Moonshot AI and MiniMax had run about 16 million exchanges with Claude through roughly 24,000 fraudulent accounts to extract its capabilities. A further Anthropic threat report in September named Alibaba, Xiaomi and Zhipu as well, and put the combined activity at nearly 200 million exchanges. The legal basis for the claim lay mainly in terms of service and export controls, not copyright.

Anthropic is also the company that paid $1.5 billion to settle the book-piracy case described in Part V of this series. It is the defendant in Bartz, the ruling the Justice Department now leans on for its fair-use theory. For balance, CNBC reported in July that a source said the administration and Anthropic had not discussed a government stake in the company.

I raise this because the hypocrisy argument is only honest if it applies to everyone. The AI companies, all of them, would like two things at once: their own outputs treated as property worth protecting with sanctions, and everyone else’s work treated as raw material free for the taking. The government has simply adopted the industry’s preferred position as the national one. Naming the company whose model helped draft this essay does not weaken that point. It sharpens it.

The Argument Worth Having

None of this means the Justice Department is wrong on the law. That is the uncomfortable part.

There is a serious body of opinion, not all of it funded by Silicon Valley, holding that training a model is exactly the kind of transformative use fair use exists to protect. Techdirt, which has defended strong fair use for decades, ran a piece on September 8 arguing that the brief reaches the right conclusion but comes from a department with no credibility. Above the Law took a similar view, suggesting the brief stumbles toward the correct result. Judge Alsup held in Bartz that training on lawfully acquired books was fair use, while treating the pirated library copies as a separate problem.

That distinction, between how the material is used and how it was obtained, is where the September 17 filings bite. A court can accept that training is transformative and still ask how the training data got into the building. “Ah nice” is not a fair-use argument. Neither is stripping copyright notices from training data so that the model will not repeat them, which the unsealed filings also allege. The statement of interest largely glides past acquisition and focuses on the grand purpose of the finished model. The plaintiffs will make sure Judge Stein does not.

From this side of the border, one detail stands out. Canada’s copyright law does not have an open-ended American-style fair use doctrine. It has fair dealing, limited to a list of specific purposes set out in the statute: research, private study, education, parody, satire, criticism, review and news reporting. National security is not on the list. Neither is winning a race. A Canadian judge presented with this brief would need to find the purpose in the statute before weighing anything else, and would probably spend a quiet moment wondering which heading covers “missile targeting.”

What Happens Next

Judge Stein will rule on the summary-judgment motions in his own time, and whatever he decides will almost certainly be appealed. The statement of interest will not decide the case. It will be one document among thousands in a docket that already includes the internal memos of the defendants’ own staff.

What it has done is clarify where the American government stands. It does not believe copying is wrong. It believes copying is wrong when it happens to American companies, and necessary when American companies do it. That is not a theory of intellectual property. It is a theory of national advantage wearing intellectual property’s clothing.

The authors, journalists and musicians in these cases were never going to get a sympathetic hearing from the industry that used their work. They might reasonably have expected a neutral one from their own government. On September 1, 2026, they learned that it had already chosen a side, and that the side it chose was the one it might soon own a piece of.
