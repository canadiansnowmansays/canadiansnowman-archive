By Stephen Gordon June 11, 2026

Wednesday afternoon in the Oval Office: ‘We’re going to hit them hard again today’... and still hoping to strike a deal. The readout of the last voice in the room.

The question in the title is not mine. It belongs to roughly one hundred million Americans who have been watching the Iran war unfold since late February, trying to keep track of what the president said today and whether it contradicts what he said yesterday and whether either of those things corresponds to what the military is actually doing.

The answer, for the record, is yes. People have noticed.

CNN has catalogued at least thirty-eight separate occasions since late March on which President Trump has announced that a peace deal with Iran is imminent. Thirty-eight times. The number keeps climbing. At least three of those announcements were followed, within hours, by new American military strikes on Iranian territory. The most recent came on Tuesday, June 9th: peace is nearly at hand. By Wednesday morning: “They’ve taken too long to negotiate a deal that would have been great for them, now they will have to pay the price!!!” By Wednesday afternoon, to reporters in the Oval Office: “We’re going to hit them hard again today.”

A CNN analyst named Aaron Blake, attempting to characterize the pattern, offered the most honest summary available: Trump keeps claiming a deal is imminent, Blake wrote, “either because he’s delusional, trying to calm the financial markets or thinking he can will it into existence.”

Three options. All of them damning. And notably — not one of them is “because it’s true.”

I. The Question This Series Has Been Asking

Seventeen installments ago, in Part I of this series, I borrowed a question from MSNBC’s Lawrence O’Donnell: If Donald Trump gets dementia, how will we know?

The baseline problem, as this series has defined it, is that Trump’s cognitive baseline — his established normal — was already so far outside ordinary parameters that deterioration is uniquely difficult to detect. A man who has always been erratic provides natural cover for a man who is becoming more erratic. A man who has always confabulated provides cover for a man whose confabulation is accelerating. The noise obscures the signal.

Over sixteen installments, this series has built a taxonomy of five observable categories:

Disinhibition — the progressive removal of the internal governor between impulse and action. Posting until 4 AM. The Easter Sunday Truth Social post threatening to destroy Iranian civilization. The Oval Office outbursts that require senior military officials to walk back his statements within the hour.

Compulsion — the inability to stop. The posting marathons. The repetition. The rhetorical loops that keep returning to the same premise regardless of what was asked.

Grandiosity — the self-concept that has swelled past ordinary narcissism into something that appears delusional. Claiming to have motivated the Holy Spirit. Publishing AI images of himself as the Pope.

Confabulation — the confident, unconscious substitution of one reality for another. The April 29th press conference in which he recited Iran’s military destruction as though describing Ukraine, and stated that Ukraine’s currency “has no name.”

Anosognosia — the patient’s inability to perceive his own condition. Bragging about identifying a bear on a cognitive screening test as proof of fitness to command two simultaneous wars. Submitting to a White House physical while the 36 physicians’ statement was being entered into the Congressional Record.

Today, Part XVII introduces a sixth category. The Iran war has given us evidence for it that no press conference or Truth Social archive ever could.

II. A New Category: Perseveration

The Iran war is now Day 102. It was declared “pretty much complete” on Day 10.

Recall the sequence. On March 10th, CNN documented that Trump told CBS News “I think the war is very complete, pretty much” — on the same afternoon that the Defense Department’s rapid response account posted “We have Only Just Begun to Fight” on X, apparently without irony. On March 26th, he announced that the war was “progressing extremely rapidly, far ahead of schedule” and would end in “four to six weeks.” That deadline passed on approximately May 7th. The war continues.

This is not strategic ambiguity. Strategic ambiguity has a theory. It keeps adversaries off-balance by design, while the decision-maker privately understands the real landscape. What we are observing is something categorically different: a man who appears to genuinely believe the war is nearly over on Tuesday and genuinely believes it must be escalated on Wednesday, with no apparent awareness that these positions are in contradiction.

The clinical term for what happens when the feedback loop between action and consequence stops functioning is perseveration — the compulsive return to a fixed belief or behavior regardless of incoming information. The May psychological analysis of Trump’s Iran rhetoric by researchers reviewing his press conference transcripts found that the phrase “Iran cannot have a nuclear weapon” appeared, in various formulations, at least eight times in seventeen minutes — not as rhetorical emphasis but as the answer to questions about inflation, oil prices, and the China trip. The question asked was irrelevant. The response was invariant. The loop was closed.

Thirty-eight announcements of imminent peace. The loop is very closed.

But there is a mechanism worth examining — one that helps explain how the loop keeps resetting, how a man can announce peace on Tuesday and war on Wednesday and apparently experience no contradiction between the two.

III. The Last Voice in the Room

There is an observation about Donald Trump that has circulated among White House reporters, former advisors, and foreign diplomats since his first term, and that has been documented with increasing specificity in his second. It goes like this: his position on any given issue, at any given moment, tends to reflect the last conversation he had.

In the first term, this was managed as a feature. Advisors learned to schedule their meetings last. Foreign leaders learned to call at the right hour. The theory of management around Trump was, at its core, a theory of sequencing: whoever had his ear most recently had his agreement.

It was treated, at the time, as a personality quirk. An extreme version of the tendency all leaders have to be influenced by their most recent briefing.

This series wants to suggest a different frame.

In neurological terms, what is being described is increased suggestibility — a condition in which the patient’s working model of reality becomes progressively less anchored to stable internal memory and increasingly dependent on the most recent external input. The healthy brain retains what it has learned across a meeting, a day, a week, and updates its model incrementally. The suggestible brain — particularly in certain forms of frontal lobe deterioration — treats each new input as dominant, overwriting rather than integrating what came before.

The practical consequence is exactly what reporters have been observing for months: a president whose position on the Iran war is not a position so much as a readout of his most recent conversation. When Rubio or a Qatari mediator last had his ear, peace is imminent. When the military briefers or the overnight Truth Social loop had it, Iran must pay the price. When no one has had it and he is alone with the television at 3 AM, the posts begin.

This is not the same as being persuadable, or open-minded, or responsive to new information. A persuadable president updates his view when presented with better evidence. What is being described here is an inability to hold a view across time — to carry Tuesday’s assessment into Wednesday’s decision, and to recognize when they are in contradiction.

The thirty-eight peace announcements are not, on this reading, thirty-eight separate moments of wishful thinking or market manipulation. They are thirty-eight readouts from thirty-eight conversations with optimistic interlocutors — each one genuine in the moment, each one overwritten by the next voice in the room.

Which means the question is not only what is he thinking, but who did he last talk to — and whether anyone in that sequence has the stability, the consistency, and the standing to make the assessment stick.

The evidence of Day 102 suggests the answer is no.

IV. Wednesday, June 10th, 2026: The Evidence Arrives in Real Time

This series has, until now, worked primarily from archived material — press conference transcripts, Truth Social posts, documented medical assessments, congressional testimony. Part XVII has the unusual advantage of working from live evidence.

Consider what happened on a single day.

At some point Tuesday, Trump told reporters that peace with Iran was imminent. Tuesday evening, the United States launched strikes on five locations along Iran’s southern coast — Sirik, Jask, Bandar Abbas, Qeshm — in retaliation for the downing of a U.S. Apache helicopter near the Strait of Hormuz. Iran retaliated, launching drones at the U.S. Fifth Fleet in Bahrain and missiles at U.S. facilities in Jordan, Kuwait, and elsewhere. Nearly all were intercepted. No American casualties.

Wednesday morning, Trump posted on Truth Social: “They’ve taken too long to negotiate a deal that would have been great for them, now they will have to pay the price!!!”

Wednesday afternoon, to reporters in the Oval Office: “We’re going to hit them hard again today.”

Wednesday afternoon, also to reporters, apparently in the same session: he still hoped to strike a deal. He and Supreme Leader Khamenei, he has said separately, “seem to be getting along quite well.”

The United Nations Secretary General António Guterres, speaking to the Security Council on Wednesday, offered the external world’s verdict on this state of affairs: “The cease-fire is more like a lesser-fire — as we have seen with the escalating attacks and rhetoric over the last 48 hours.”

A lesser-fire. Declared by a man who has announced its end thirty-eight times.

V. “I Wanted to Say It So Badly”

22 ships, late at night, with no lights — announced because ‘I wanted to say it so badly.’ Disinhibition meets operational security

If the thirty-eight peace announcements are the structural evidence, the “22 ships” moment is the clinical exhibit.

On Wednesday, Trump told reporters in the Oval Office that the United States had been moving “22 ships, late at night, with no lights” through the Strait of Hormuz — a covert operation to transport oil past the Iranian blockade — and that Iran had “just figured it out.” He added, by way of explanation for why he was announcing a covert military operation to reporters: “I wanted to say it so badly, I didn’t want to ruin it.”

A senior U.S. military official was dispatched within hours to clarify what the president had actually meant.

Pause on the phrase: I wanted to say it so badly.

This series introduced disinhibition in Part II — the progressive removal of the internal governor between impulse and action. The governor’s function is not merely to prevent aggression or incivility. It prevents a commander-in-chief from announcing covert naval operations because he wanted to say it so badly.

Whatever strategic calculation existed for keeping those ships’ movements quiet, it was overridden not by a competing strategic calculation, but by an impulse. The loop between action and consequence — the basic cognitive architecture that connects “this information is classified for a reason” to “therefore I will not announce it to reporters” — did not close.

VI. The Sixth Category

Previous installments have documented the pattern in controlled environments: press conferences, TV interviews, social media posts made in the small hours of the morning. These are revealing, but they carry an inherent limitation. A man can be erratic in a press conference without that erraticism having immediate, measurable, lethal consequences.

The Iran war has removed that limitation.

Two concrete water tanks destroyed in Sirik County — 2.5 million litres capacity gone. Water cut to 20,000 people in 96°F heat. Consequence blindness

On Wednesday, June 10th, U.S. military strikes hit drinking water facilities in Sirik County, in Iran’s southern Hormozgan Province. Two concrete storage tanks — combined capacity 2.5 million litres — were destroyed. Water service was cut to 20,000 people in Kuhestak and ten surrounding villages. The temperature in Kuhestak on Wednesday was 96 degrees Fahrenheit. During summer months, Hormozgan province can reach 122 degrees. Iran’s Foreign Ministry called the strikes “a calculated war crime.” The New York Times verified video showing a damaged concrete structure with a collapsed roof bearing the painted inscription: “Water is the pulse of life.” Water service, it was later reported, was restored within twelve hours.

Earlier this week, Indian sailors aboard the tanker Marivex, disabled by U.S. forces enforcing the naval blockade, sent frantic voice messages for more than two hours before being rescued. “We have fire onboard, we have fire onboard. And vessel is sinking. Please help, please help… We are all crew Indian. 24 crew.” India subsequently summoned a U.S. diplomat to protest a second tanker strike involving Indian crew. U.S. Central Command has now disabled eight vessels since the blockade began on April 13th.

Oil is at $94 a barrel. U.S. inflation hit 4.2 percent in May — the highest since April 2023, driven almost entirely by energy prices, which have risen 23.5 percent over the year.

The sixth category, which Part XVI gestured toward as consequence blindness and which the Iran war now fully illuminates, is this: the apparent inability to perceive the downstream effects of one’s own decisions as relevant to the decisions that follow.

It is not that Trump doesn’t know about the water tanks in Sirik, or the Indian sailors on the Marivex, or the 39 cents more per gallon that every American driver is paying since February. The information is available. His briefers have it. The newspapers have it. He watches television.

The question — the Baseline Problem question — is whether any of that information is closing the loop. Whether it is arriving at the decision-making level as consequence rather than as noise.

The evidence of thirty-eight peace announcements, each followed by escalation, suggests the loop is not closing.

VII. What the Doctors Said

This series has been careful, across seventeen installments, to document what is observable rather than to diagnose what cannot be directly examined. That discipline is worth maintaining.

But it is worth noting what the people trained to observe these things have been saying.

In April, CNN reported that Rep. Jamie Raskin wrote to White House physician Sean Barbarella calling Trump’s statements on the Iran war “incoherent, volatile, profane, deranged, and threatening,” and explicitly linking his Iran rhetoric to questions about cognitive fitness — calling for a comprehensive cognitive evaluation and reporting the findings to Congress.

Dr. Vin Gupta, a medical analyst, listed the observable symptoms in a widely-circulated post: “Erratic. Can’t finish sentences. Often confused. Illogical train of thought. Word finding difficulties. Developing and worsening gradually over time.” His conclusion: the president is “exhibiting all the signs of dementia.”

Dr. John Gartner, clinical psychologist and founder of Duty to Warn, told The i Paper in February that Trump’s health had begun “rapidly deteriorating” and flagged specific concerns about cognitive capacity and the ability to manage complex international decisions.

None of these are diagnoses. All of them are observations from trained observers, looking at the same public record this series has been documenting.

The Raskin cognitive test request was, of course, declined. The White House physical in late May returned the customary finding of excellent health. The baseline problem, as always, is that the people who could answer the question are the same people for whom the answer is inconvenient.

VIII. The Question Lawrence O’Donnell Asked

We return, seventeen installments later, to where we began.

If Donald Trump gets dementia, how will we know?

The answer this series has been building, piece by piece, installment by installment, is that we would know the way we know anything: by looking at the pattern.

Disinhibition. Compulsion. Grandiosity. Confabulation. Anosognosia. And now — observable, documented, and carrying the weight of a war — the decoupled loop. The presidency in which the subjective experience of governing has become almost entirely disconnected from its objective effects.

Thirty-eight announcements of imminent peace. A covert naval operation announced because he wanted to say it so badly. A cease-fire that is, per the United Nations, more like a lesser-fire. Twenty thousand people without water in 96-degree heat. Day 102.

The question in this essay’s title was sarcastic. The people asking it — the senators, the military officials, the UN Secretary General, the analysts trying to keep track of whether it is a war day or a peace day — are not asking sarcastically.

They are asking because they are running a country, and an alliance, and a war, and they need to know.

The answer, for the seventeenth time, is the same.
