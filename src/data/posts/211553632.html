On June 17, 2026, Donald Trump signed a fourteen-point memorandum of understanding with Iran, an agreement meant to end six months of hostilities and set out a framework for the country’s economic reconstruction — a framework, notably, calling for at least three hundred billion dollars in redevelopment financing that the administration would coordinate through regional partners rather than fund directly itself. Two months later, Representative Ro Khanna, the Silicon Valley Democrat and ranking member of the House Select Committee on Strategic Competition with China, wrote an op-ed making the obvious point that undersells itself by being obvious: if Washington can produce a fourteen-point plan to rebuild Iran, it can produce a fourteen-point plan to rebuild Scranton.

He called it “Team America.” He published it on Fox News’s opinion page.

That second fact matters more than the first, and it’s worth sitting with why, because it’s the clearest test case yet of the argument I made two weeks ago in this series about vocabulary reclamation — that Democrats are increasingly choosing to contest patriotism and economic legitimacy on the terms their opponents set, rather than retreating to friendlier ground. Khanna didn’t just write a plan. He walked it into the other side’s living room and read it out loud.

What’s Actually In the Fourteen Points

Strip away the framing device and Khanna’s plan is a fairly standard progressive economic platform: Medicare for All, free childcare, housing guarantees, and — its most concrete and most controversial line item — passage of the Khanna-Sanders bill to tax billionaire wealth. The pitch is that a five-percent levy on the roughly nine hundred American billionaires holding a combined $8.2 trillion would raise something on the order of $4.4 trillion over a decade, enough to underwrite childcare, healthcare, housing, and free public college at scale. None of this is new; Khanna and Bernie Sanders have been pushing versions of a billionaire wealth tax for years.

What’s new is the packaging. The op-ed opens not with an appeal to fairness or a moral argument about inequality but with a direct comparison: the government just found the will and the money to draft a national rebuilding plan for a foreign adversary’s economy, and it hasn’t found the same will for its own. “It is time for a national economic development initiative that makes the economic success of every American family our highest priority,” Khanna wrote — a sentence that would sit comfortably in a Republican stump speech if you deleted the word “billionaire” from three paragraphs down. That’s the point. Khanna is not trying to sound like a progressive making a progressive case. He’s trying to sound like an America First nationalist making an economic case, and daring the reader to notice that the policies underneath the framing are the same ones Democrats have been running on for a decade.

Why Fox, Specifically

Publishing venue is never neutral, and Khanna has a long history of treating Fox News and its opinion pages as terrain worth contesting rather than conceding. He’s given interviews to Laura Ingraham, appeared on Sean Hannity’s program to defend colleagues under attack, and generally operated on the theory — which used to be a minority position among Democrats — that refusing to appear on hostile media just cedes the audience to whoever the network books instead. His timing here reinforces the point: the op-ed ran the same week Democratic strategist circles were absorbing Abdul El-Sayed’s one-point Michigan primary win, and the same week Bernie Sanders was publicly demanding that Democratic leadership ban Super PAC money from primaries — in other words, right in the middle of the party’s loudest internal argument about how aggressively to lean into economic populism.

The reception was instructive, and not entirely flattering to the theory. Khanna posted the piece to social media with the pointed framing that Vice President JD Vance had negotiated a fourteen-point plan to help rebuild Iran while Democrats have a fourteen-point plan to rebuild America — a tidy symmetrical jab. The replies were not converted swing voters. They were, by and large, exactly what you’d expect from a Fox-adjacent audience encountering a Democratic wealth-tax pitch: accusations of economic illiteracy, comparisons to the Inflation Reduction Act, and the reliable pivot to immigration and crime that greets almost any Democratic economic argument in that particular corner of the internet. Nobody’s mind changed in the comment section. Nobody’s mind was likely to.

The Israel Complication

There’s a second layer to Khanna’s positioning that the Fox op-ed conveniently sidesteps, and it’s worth naming because it cuts against the idea that his approach is simply the more disciplined, more moderate alternative to El-Sayed’s stacking problem. Months earlier, in an April appearance on ABC’s “This Week,” Khanna was pressed by Jonathan Karl on whether the Democratic Party was still a pro-Israel party, given that Khanna was one of a growing number of members who’d voted to halt military equipment sales to Israel. His answer didn’t hedge: Israel has a forty-five-billion-dollar defense budget, he argued, and the money would be better spent on healthcare and childcare at home. “I’m Team America,” he told Karl. “He seems to be more obsessed with the Middle East” — a line aimed squarely at Trump, but one that previews the branding he’d formalize in the Fox op-ed four months later.

The point is not that Khanna is wrong on the merits — that’s a separate, genuinely contested policy argument this series isn’t going to referee. The point is structural: Khanna’s own bundle of positions includes cutting aid to a key ally, which is precisely the kind of plank that gave El-Sayed’s Michigan opponents their most effective attack line. Khanna has simply sequenced his messaging differently. He leads with economic patriotism and lets the foreign-policy positions surface only when a interviewer forces the question, rather than putting “no aid to Israel” in the first line of a campaign ad, as El-Sayed’s own team did. Whether that’s principled message discipline or just better public relations is a matter of interpretation. It is, at minimum, evidence that the “stacking problem” I wrote about in Part I isn’t really about which positions a Democrat holds — it’s about how loudly and in what order they’re offered to an audience that hasn’t been persuaded of the underlying premise yet.

Not an Isolated Gambit

The Fox op-ed also isn’t a one-off stunt; it fits a pattern Khanna has been building for months, and the pattern is worth naming because it shows the “loyal opposition” instinct extends past national vocabulary into local, practical framing too. In Pennsylvania this summer, Khanna waded into a fight over a proposed data center by explicitly refusing partisan framing altogether: this isn’t a left-versus-right fight, he argued, it’s a right-versus-wrong fight about a large company jacking up local electricity costs while pocketing the profits. He made a similar move endorsing a Pennsylvania mayor’s race, and he’s continued needling his own party’s donor class the same way he needled Fox’s comment section — by insisting that the actual dividing line in American politics isn’t Democrat versus Republican but people versus concentrated power, a framing that lets him occupy “economic patriotism,” “Team America,” and “right versus wrong” simultaneously without any of them contradicting each other.

That consistency is, I think, the more interesting story than any single op-ed. Bernie Sanders was making a parallel argument the same week, publicly demanding that the Democratic National Committee ban Super PAC money from its own primaries and taking direct aim at AIPAC’s spending — the same sixty-million-dollar spending machine that very nearly sank El-Sayed in Michigan, discussed at length in Part I of this series. Khanna and Sanders are not identical figures, but they’re pulling on the same thread: the argument that the actual enemy isn’t a rival political tribe but an unaccountable donor class that both parties’ establishments have made their peace with. Wrapping that argument in the language of patriotism and national rebuilding, rather than class-conflict rhetoric, is precisely the vocabulary-reclamation strategy this series has been tracking since Part II — applied here to a substantially more concrete legislative vehicle than a hymn or a slogan.

What This Buys the Party

None of this means the Fox venue was wasted. Op-eds on hostile platforms rarely convert the immediate audience; that was never the realistic goal. What they do is signal, to the much larger audience of people watching the exchange secondhand — through screenshots, through cable news pickup, through the next round of coverage — that a Democrat was willing to make the case on the other side’s turf instead of retreating to a friendly podcast. That’s a Westminster instinct as much as an American one: a loyal opposition doesn’t win by only ever speaking to people who already agree with it. It wins, over time, by making its argument credible in venues where the incumbent party assumed it had a monopoly on the audience.

Whether “Team America” moves a single vote in November is close to unknowable and, frankly, beside the point this early. What it does is add a data point to the pattern this series keeps tracking: Democrats testing whether contested vocabulary — patriotism, economic nationalism, “Team America” itself — can be reclaimed through repetition and venue-crossing rather than through a single knockout argument. Buttigieg is doing it with “freedom.” Rob Sand is doing it with a hymn. Khanna is doing it with a spreadsheet and an enemy’s own comment section. It’s the same bet made three different ways, and none of the three has been tested by an actual election yet.

It’s worth remembering how recently this kind of Fox appearance was itself a controversy inside the party rather than a strategy. Khanna took real heat within progressive circles for a 2020 Ingraham interview in which he discussed forming issue-specific coalitions with conservatives skeptical of foreign intervention; the backlash was loud enough that Ingraham addressed it on air, insisting her door was “always open” to Democrats willing to talk. Six years later, the same instinct that drew criticism as a fringe indulgence is being deployed as a deliberate, party-relevant strategy in the middle of a hotly contested midterm cycle — evidence that the Overton window inside the Democratic coalition itself has moved, not just the one Republicans are accused of guarding.

The Honest Caveat

I’ll end where I ended Part II, because the caution bears repeating with a specific instrument attached to it this time. Rhetorical courage is not the same as electoral strategy, and publishing on Fox News’s website generates attention regardless of whether that attention is favorable. A cynic would note that Khanna’s op-ed got him talked about in exactly the circles that already knew who he was, while doing essentially nothing to address the coalition-math problem that actually decides midterm elections — the union households, the Black voters in Detroit and Flint, the rural interior counties that Part I identified as the places progressive insurgencies keep failing to reach. A fourteen-point plan on a hostile website is a messaging exercise. It is not, by itself, a theory of how you get from a one-point primary win to a general-election majority.

What it is, at minimum, is evidence that at least one prominent national Democrat has internalized the lesson this series keeps circling: that ceding vocabulary, and ceding venues, was never a neutral choice. Whether internalizing that lesson is enough to matter in November is a question this series will keep returning to as the results come in.
