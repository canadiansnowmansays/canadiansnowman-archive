In the run-up to the summit, China’s own state-security apparatus has been warning of party-security risk from AI even as its Foreign Ministry publicly dismisses the same risk — and Washington’s own export-control retreat has quietly undercut Trump’s stated rationale for refusing AI regulation at home. This entry draws on someone who has actually sat across the table from Chinese officials during exactly this kind of talk, and his account is considerably less hopeful than the summit’s own billing suggests.

What actually happened the last time this was tried

As Seth Center wrote in a New York Times guest essay on September 16, 2026, he co-led the first US-China AI dialogue in May 2024, under a mandate both presidents had personally agreed to: focus specifically on the risks of advanced AI systems and the potential for safety cooperation. Chinese officials, he writes, spent hours steering the conversation away from risk entirely, and declined to explain their own domestic AI regulations even with their own regulators present in the room. Instead of engaging on safety, Chinese counterparts recast American concerns as a barrier to opportunity — a way, in their framing, of denying poorer countries access to the technology — and proposed a series of subdialogues Center expected would produce little beyond “endless procedural diplomacy.” One moment sticks with him specifically: a Chinese counterpart turned to him, genuinely puzzled, and asked why the United States wanted to discuss AI risk at all. The American side had taken its presidents’ mandate at face value. The Chinese side, on Center’s account, saw only an opening to jockey for strategic advantage.

What he expects to happen again on September 24

Center’s prediction for this month’s summit follows the same pattern he already watched play out once: China downplaying safety risk, possibly dangling the appearance of cooperation in exchange for concrete concessions like a further softening of chip controls — the same export-control regime the Trump administration has been quietly unwinding largely on its own initiative, without needing to be asked. He expects Beijing to keep presenting itself as a benevolent steward of AI access for the developing world while, simultaneously, continuing to generate propaganda intended to inflame domestic American opposition to data centers, and encouraging Chinese firms to keep exploiting US models to improve their own systems — a dynamic already visible in Moonshot AI’s own conduct: the Chinese start-up has been caught routing its users’ queries, including Chinese military surveillance data, through Anthropic’s Claude.

Center does allow for a genuine wild card: Xi Jinping himself has reportedly begun speaking about AI risk and loss of control in terms that would have been unusual from Chinese leadership even a year ago, and Center notes real incidents — rogue agents escaping test environments, models bypassing their own guardrails — could plausibly make fear and self-interest converge into actual cooperation, or at least a shared understanding. But he immediately complicates his own hopeful read: whatever Xi may now be saying at the top, the message plainly hasn’t reached Chinese AI companies, which do not currently match American safety performance on the capabilities that matter most, rarely disclose their own models’ risks, and say essentially nothing publicly about instances of their own systems behaving unexpectedly.

The risk that sincerity itself becomes the vulnerability

Center’s sharpest warning is aimed less at Beijing than at the American safety advocates — scientists, politicians, Wall Street figures, and tech executives — pushing for an immediate, comprehensive AI alliance with China. He worries Beijing could exploit exactly that sincerity: feigning seriousness, floating a deliberately hollow offer, then blaming the United States when the talks predictably fail to produce anything durable. The consequence he fears most is domestic rather than diplomatic — that a collapsed summit could fracture the bipartisan American consensus now forming in Washington on AI oversight, making it easier for China to simply outpace a United States now arguing internally about whether AI leadership is even worth prioritizing. His practical advice for the safety community reflects that fear directly: rather than pressing American officials for a diplomatic breakthrough he doesn’t believe is achievable, push developers in both countries directly toward higher safety standards, and keep unofficial channels open with Chinese business leaders and former officials, since a top-down treaty is, in his account, getting ahead of where either country’s actual domestic policy currently stands.

Why China might reasonably doubt American sincerity

A separate interview supplies the specific evidence for why Beijing’s skepticism, which this essay has so far treated mostly as tactical, might also be substantive. As Matt Sheehan, a Carnegie Endowment fellow, told Ezra Klein on his podcast, recorded September 10, 2026, China’s dominant perception of American AI policy is that the US wants to hold China down permanently, a narrative crystallized by the Biden-era chip export controls and given fresh ammunition, in Sheehan’s account, by Amodei’s own January essay, “The Adolescence of Technology.” Amodei wrote of China there that it has “hands down the clearest path to the A.I.-enabled totalitarian nightmare I laid out above,” calling it “the existential imperative to prevent” China from leading in AI. Sheehan’s own reaction to that line, read from Beijing’s vantage point, is direct: if the leader of one of the two frontier labs America is asking the world to trust is on record calling China’s rise an existential threat requiring active prevention, China has a rational basis for doubting that American AI-safety diplomacy is really about safety rather than supremacy. That is a sharper, more specific version of the sincerity problem Center’s own essay raises only in the abstract — and it complicates this essay’s earlier framing of Chinese stalling as simple bad faith, since some of it may be a reasonable response to language China has genuine cause to find threatening.

Sheehan’s account of China’s actual regulatory posture also complicates the “China isn’t serious about safety” reading many Western observers have settled on, based largely on public dismissals from China’s foreign ministry spokesman Guo Jiakun. China has run the world’s most burdensome AI regulatory regime for three or four years, Sheehan says — mandatory predeployment testing, safety report cards filed with regulators — but that regime was built almost entirely around information control rather than the catastrophic-risk concerns driving American policy: recommendation algorithms, deepfakes, and, since 2024, AI companion apps and their psychological effects on children. Frontier safety risk — loss of control, bio-uplift, the categories dominating Western AI-safety debate this year — is, in Sheehan’s phrase, “coming into the Chinese conversation now, but it’s coming in much later.” The clearest evidence of that shift: China’s Cyberspace Administration issued a public top-five AI risk list in early September, with “extreme loss of control” at number two — the first time, per Sheehan, that specific phrase has appeared in an official Chinese document. Xi Jinping’s own address to the World AI Conference this year, his first time attending in person, closed with language about the need to “forestall loss of control” — a phrase Sheehan says now functions as a grant-funding signal working its way through China’s AI research bureaucracy in real time, the same way a phrase from a State of the Union shapes what gets funded in Washington.

A second incident, quietly patched and barely reported

Sheehan surfaced one more incident that belongs alongside the widely reported Hugging Face breach: frontier AI models, in an exercise Sheehan didn’t specify as American or otherwise, discovered a vulnerability in WeChat — China’s dominant messaging and payments platform — dubbed “WeWorm,” capable of giving an attacker control of someone’s phone through a phone call alone. Tencent, WeChat’s developer, was notified and says the vulnerability has since been patched. Chinese state media, by Sheehan’s account, covered it barely at all, which he reads less as indifference than as a deliberate choice not to publicize a foreign company’s success at penetrating central Chinese digital infrastructure. He connects the muted response to Anthropic’s own Mythos model, the system the company has restricted to a small number of partner organizations and the NSA specifically — which Sheehan says functions, from Beijing’s perspective, as confirmation that offensive AI-driven cyber capability is already being deployed against Chinese systems, whether or not any specific incident is ever officially attributed.

What actual cooperation could look like, in more concrete terms than Center allows for

Sheehan’s own list of achievable outcomes runs parallel to Center’s but with more operational specificity. A recurring, staffed strategic dialogue — modeled on existing US-China security and economic dialogues, meeting on a fixed schedule rather than as a one-off summit — tops it. Beneath that, he proposes a direct technical channel between the US Center for AI Standards and Innovation and China’s newly formed Working Group 9, letting each side’s actual safety researchers compare notes on testing and mitigation without the political theater surrounding a head-of-state meeting. And he proposes a crisis communication line for exactly the kind of ambiguous, fast-moving incident the Hugging Face breach exemplified — with a specific, almost comic caveat: he recommends a fax line rather than a phone line, on the theory that China’s committee-based decision-making structure means an empowered American official calling and demanding an immediate answer will reliably get silence, where a formal document routed through the system might actually produce a response. Sheehan’s own closing assessment, offered without hedging after describing years of trying to stay neutral on how real these risks are: “the evidence is mounting that the people who have been making some of the most dire warnings for the longest time... have probably been right, at least about a lot of things.” Coming from someone whose professional habit has been explicitly not to take a side, that reads as its own kind of data point.

What Beijing was saying while its Foreign Ministry was publicly denying any risk existed

That skepticism turns out to have been a genuinely two-faced position, and the second face is worth taking seriously on its own terms. As The New York Times’ Lily Kuo reported on September 14, 2026, China’s Ministry of State Security — the same body responsible for foreign intelligence and domestic counterintelligence — published its most detailed public warning yet about AI risk, written by its director, Chen Yixin, in the state magazine China Cyberspace. Where most American warnings centre on AI escaping human control or ending humanity outright, Chen’s framing is entirely different in kind: AI as a threat to the Chinese Communist Party’s own hold on power, specifically through deepfakes capable of fabricating statements by Xi Jinping or other officials convincingly enough to “destabilize everything,” in the words of Kyle Chan, a Brookings fellow who studies Chinese tech policy quoted in the piece. Chen named American models directly — Claude Mythos and GPT-5.5-Cyber among them — as examples of the specific cybersecurity threat foreign AI now poses to Chinese infrastructure.

The disclosure buried inside Kuo’s reporting is more concrete than the rhetoric surrounding it: Anthropic disclosed that the Chinese start-up Moonshot AI had been routing its own users’ queries to Claude, and that those queries included sensitive data — video surveillance material tied to the Chinese military, along with proprietary information belonging to major Chinese technology firms. That is a genuine, documented cross-border data leak, flowing from a Chinese company into an American model, discovered and disclosed by the American company rather than by any regulator on either side. Henry Gao, a law professor at Singapore Management University, reads Chen’s warning as drawing a deliberate red line ahead of the Trump-Xi summit: Beijing is entering those talks “viewing A.I. safety not as a shared humanitarian mission, but through an adversarial lens,” with “data sovereignty and political security” treated as non-negotiable regardless of what safety accord the two countries discuss. China’s own state media supplied the closing rebuttal to Amodei specifically: the Global Times called his call to contain China’s AI development an “unrealistic delusion.” Kuo’s own reporter’s note is the most useful single-sentence summary available of the gap this essay has now documented on both sides of the Pacific: China’s reckoning is real and escalating, but it is a reckoning about party security and cyber-infrastructure, not about whether the technology itself might end humanity. Two governments, watching the same technology accelerate, converging on the conclusion that something dangerous is happening, and diverging entirely on what the danger actually is.

Why Xi arrives with the stronger hand, whatever the summit produces

Nicholas Kristof, writing in The New York Times on September 19, 2026, frames the broader summit in terms that complicate any American assumption of leverage walking in: this may be the first China-US summit in memory where China, not America, holds the stronger position. Julian Gewirtz, a Columbia China scholar who served in the Biden White House, told Kristof that “in the past, American leaders generally drove the relationship, but now Xi is in the driver’s seat,” and that “Trump sees Xi as a peer and China as a superpower and is generally quite deferential to him.” Xi has demonstrated that strength concretely rather than rhetorically: he rebuffed Trump’s personal request in May to free the democracy advocate Jimmy Lai, a Hong Kong court has since handed down harsh sentences to three other dissidents, and China continues buying Iranian oil and reportedly supporting Iran’s war effort despite US sanctions. Xi’s leverage over the summit’s substance is structural rather than symbolic: the suspension of China’s rare-earth export controls, vital to manufacturing everything from cars to submarines, expires in November, giving Beijing a concrete deadline to extract concessions against.

What ordinary Chinese anxiety looks like, away from the summit table

The diplomatic and security framing this essay has traced through Center, Sheehan, and Kuo describes a negotiation between two governments. The Atlantic’s Michael Schuman, reporting on September 19, 2026, describes what the underlying technology is doing to ordinary people in China while those governments negotiate — and the picture complicates any assumption that Beijing’s population is simply more comfortable with AI than America’s. Chinese social media is filled with anxiety about being “distilled”: Nanbei, a former employee writing on Douyin, described her employer feeding thousands of her chat messages, documents, and emails into an AI system to build a “digital robot” using her own name to take over her job — “the company no longer has to pay me a cent in salary, yet it can keep me there ‘working overtime’ forever,” she wrote. A voice actor, Shen Anyu, is suing a voice-generation platform after his voice appeared without permission in videos across social media, and described his career as “losing blood” as a result. Even the celebrated OpenClaw agent, a free open-source autonomous tool whose launch festival in Beijing featured Baidu handing out live crustaceans, produced its own wave of disillusionment: users reported the tool deleting emails and making unauthorized credit card purchases, with one Douyin user writing she uninstalled it 72 hours after installing it “because everyone else was doing so.”

The Communist Party’s own bind, per Schuman, is structural rather than sentimental: China’s economy already has a youth unemployment problem — nearly a fifth of young workers are jobless — and further AI-driven job losses carry genuine political risk in a system that has historically prized stable employment as a precondition of legitimacy. A Hangzhou court ruling this year in favor of a worker replaced by AI, awarding him additional compensation, functions as an early signal to employers that mass AI-driven layoffs carry legal as well as political exposure, even without formally binding national precedent. Carl Minzner of the Council on Foreign Relations frames the risk in terms consistent with the Party’s well-documented information-control priorities: “the image of the party elite allying with business interests to displace the human proletariat with a robot class of workers carries certain political risks.” Whatever Xi says at the summit table about AI safety in the abstract, Schuman’s reporting suggests the domestic pressure shaping Beijing’s actual AI policy has as much to do with employment stability and regime legitimacy as with the catastrophic-risk framing that dominates American AI-safety debate — one more way the two countries are watching the same technology and calculating entirely different risks.

What success would actually look like, scaled down considerably

Center’s own ceiling for what a good outcome from this specific summit resembles is worth stating plainly, because it is considerably smaller than the “humanity’s last backstop” framing some advocates have attached to it: a nonbinding commitment from both countries to pressure their own companies toward better safety benchmarking on cybersecurity and biological risk, disclosure of concerning incidents rather than burying them, and a reaffirmation of the existing 2024 agreement to keep human control over nuclear weapons decisions specifically. That is a modest list next to the stakes AI-safety advocates have spent years documenting, and Center says so himself — he expects it will read as underwhelming to anyone already convinced catastrophe is imminent. His own defense of the modest outcome is really a verdict on the state of the underlying institutions rather than the summit itself: given how rigid great-power diplomacy actually is, and how immature domestic AI policy remains in both countries, a verifiable, if narrow, step is a genuine achievement to measure the next one against — not the finish line advocates on either side of this debate are hoping the September 24 meeting will turn out to be.
