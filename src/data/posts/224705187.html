On the morning of Tuesday, November 3, 2026, just as the polls opened on America’s East Coast, the President of the United States went on Fox News and ended an era in two sentences. “We were giving them 4 billion,” he told Brian Kilmeade. “We are. But it gets cut off at the end of the year.”

He meant Israel. As David E. Sanger reported for The New York Times, the President said the bulk of American aid would end under an agreement reached with Benjamin Netanyahu months earlier. In its place Israel would pay for American weapons. The current memorandum of understanding, the ten-year pledge signed in 2016, runs until 2028. The President appeared to be ending it two years early, with Israel’s consent.

Part XIV of this series, written in the summer, argued that American support for Israel had fallen further and faster than anyone in Washington liked to admit. The President has now said so, in his own way, on the morning of an election.

The talking point

What made the announcement remarkable was not only the substance but the reason he gave. He described it chiefly as a political move, a way to take a “bad talking point” away from the left wing of the Democratic Party. “They don’t need it,” he said of the Israelis, “and because why should we suffer the slings from lunatics like an El-Sayed when they don’t need it?”

Abdul El-Sayed, the Democratic Senate candidate in Michigan and one of the most openly critical of Israel among the party’s nominees, won that evening. “It’s a bad talking point,” the President repeated. Israel, he added, was “our No. 1,” but a “thriving country.”

Sanger noticed what the President left out. He never mentioned Gaza, nor the erosion of American support for Israel in the three years since the attacks of October 7, 2023. He seemed to recognise that new aid packages would be extraordinarily hard to pass through Congress, and perhaps impossible after that day’s vote. He did not say why.

For decades, aid to Israel was the closest thing American foreign policy had to a fixed point. Presidents argued with Israeli prime ministers about settlements, about peace plans, about Iran, and the money kept flowing. That a Republican president walked away from it on Election Day, and called it a favour to Israel, tells you how far the ground has moved. The pledge did not end because Israel’s critics won the argument. It ended because the President decided the argument was no longer worth having.

Netanyahu’s reasons

Netanyahu had his own reasons to want out, and they are not hard to find. He has spoken in recent months about weaning Israel off American aid, though he never set a timetable. Control of the money had become a lever in Congress, used by members trying to force changes in Israeli conduct, and each annual vote turned into a ritual fight. Weapons bought through ordinary military sales, or delivered under a broader trade and technology agreement, would face less of that. Congress was already considering a more integrated partnership built on legally mandated co-operation between the two countries’ defence industries. Such a partnership could be lucrative for Israel, through joint projects with the Pentagon and American contractors.

Seen from Jerusalem, then, ending the aid was not simply a loss. It was a way out of an annual public fight, and perhaps to escape the oversight that came with the money, though congressional review of arms sales would not disappear.

Learning it from the news

The trouble was the way the change was made, and the moment.

Israel had voted a week earlier, on October 27. Part XX of this series described the result: no bloc with a majority, and the Arab parties holding the balance. Netanyahu was fighting Gadi Eisenkot, leader of the Yashar party, for the chance to form a government. Eisenkot did not wait.

“The fact that the security establishment, along with Israeli citizens, is learning about a change in the strategic relationship framework through the media highlights just how much Benjamin Netanyahu’s conduct poses a genuine danger,” he said. “We can no longer rely on him and must replace him.”

Eisenkot is a former chief of the general staff. When he says the security establishment learned of a change to its most important alliance from a Fox News interview, he is speaking about colleagues he knows. The charge is not that Netanyahu gave up the money. It is that he agreed, months ago, to reshape the relationship and did not tell the people responsible for defending the country.

Four days later, on Saturday, November 7, President Isaac Herzog gave Eisenkot, not Netanyahu, the first chance to form a government, as Ephrat Livni and Aaron Boxerman reported for The New York Times. Lawmakers holding 67 of the Knesset’s 120 seats recommended him, against 46 for Netanyahu, after one of Netanyahu’s own ultra-Orthodox allies declined to back him. Eisenkot has up to six weeks. Netanyahu remains caretaker prime minister until a new government is sworn in, and he has called a government that relies on Arab votes “national suicide.”

The other lever

The aid announcement was not the only American story about Netanyahu that day. The same morning, The New York Times reported that Israel had told the United States that Iran was considering using chemical weapons, and that the claim had divided American officials. Julian E. Barnes and his colleagues found that some officials saw it as part of a pressure campaign by Netanyahu’s government to draw the President back into major combat with Iran. Gulf governments that received Israeli briefings, without evidence, found them “unserious.” Israel’s own Home Front Command ran nationwide drills that week without making chemical weapons a significant part of them.

The reporters added the political context plainly. Netanyahu was fighting for survival after failing to secure a path to a government, and some analysts believed he might use a security emergency as leverage to force opposition leaders into a coalition with him. The Iran Crisis series has followed the military side of that story. For this series, the point is narrower. In a single week, the caretaker prime minister of Israel was reported to have given up the American aid that symbolised the alliance, and to be pressing an alarming intelligence claim that would draw the alliance deeper into war. One step loosens the tie to Washington. The other pulls Washington closer. Both serve a man who needs the country to feel that only he can keep it safe.

The intelligence did not move the President. The strike window that the American military had drawn for the week after the vote passed without an order, and on November 12 the President wrote that he did not need a deal with Iran either. Netanyahu’s levers are losing their purchase in Washington at the same rate as at home.

What compound interest looks like

This series took its title from an idea: that the original injustices of 1948 and 1967 have been compounding ever since, and that each generation pays the interest on decisions it did not make. The ending of American aid is a strange kind of payment. It was not demanded by Palestinians, who were not mentioned. It was not won by Israel’s critics in Congress, who were dismissed as lunatics. It came because the cost of defending the aid in American politics finally exceeded what a transactional president thought it was worth.

That should worry Israelis more than any single vote. An alliance that rests on shared values can survive disagreement. An alliance that rests on a president’s sense of what is a “bad talking point” can end on a Tuesday morning, in a television studio, before the polls close. The next president may want the alliance back. The next Congress may decide Israel should pay for it.

For Palestinians, the change may matter less than it appears. American weapons will still reach Israel; Israel will simply pay for them. The leverage that critics in Congress hoped to use, conditioning aid on conduct in Gaza and the West Bank, will be harder to apply to a commercial sale than to a grant. Sanger notes that congressional oversight would not end. But a lever that the President has just told the world exists to placate “lunatics” is not one many members will be eager to pull.

Eisenkot’s response suggests what Israeli politics will make of it. He did not defend the aid. He did not attack the President. He attacked Netanyahu for keeping the country in the dark. If he forms a government, he will inherit an alliance already being rewritten, by a prime minister and a president who did it without telling anyone.
