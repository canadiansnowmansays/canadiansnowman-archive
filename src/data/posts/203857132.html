The card has been on the stand since 1987. Gerald placed the current one in 2003 when the original wore out. Four hundred and thirty residents use it each season. Bradley Fenn has a slide.

The Elsinore Festival has, since 1987, offered a program called the Community Ticket.

The Community Ticket provides one free admission per household per season to any Festival production, available to Elsinore residents who have lived in the municipality for at least six months. It is not widely advertised. It is mentioned in the Festival’s annual report, in a footnote on page fourteen. It appears on a small laminated card at the front desk of the municipal hall, which Gerald placed there in 2003 and which has been replaced four times as the laminate has worn. The program has never been formally reviewed. It has simply existed, year after year, as one of those things that a place does because it decided to do it and has continued doing it because the decision has never been revisited.

Approximately four hundred and thirty Community Tickets are redeemed each season. Alistair knows this number. He does not discuss it publicly, because he considers it a private matter between the Festival and the people of Elsinore, and because he has always believed that the most important things a theatre does are the things it does without announcement.

Bradley Fenn raised the Community Ticket at the August meeting of the Tourism and Culture Committee. He had a slide.

The slide showed the per-ticket cost of the Community Ticket program — the face value of the tickets redeemed, calculated at full price — which came to approximately sixty-eight thousand dollars per season. Bradley noted that this represented a significant unexamined subsidy from a privately funded arts organisation to a self-selected group of municipal residents, that the eligibility criteria had never been formally codified in municipal policy, and that the six-month residency requirement raised questions about what the program was intended to reward and whether the reward was being distributed equitably.

He said he was not suggesting the program be eliminated. He was suggesting it be reviewed.

He said “review” in the tone he uses for things he has already reviewed and found wanting.

Gerald noted the slide number. He wrote something in his personal notes. He had been at the Tourism and Culture Committee meeting in 1987 at which the Community Ticket was first proposed. He had filed the minutes from that meeting. He had also, in 2019, filed a note when the program reached its thousandth redemption. He filed things when they seemed worth filing, which was, in his experience, more often than anyone thought necessary and less often than things actually required.

Alistair heard about the slide on a Tuesday.

He was in his office on the south side of the Festival Theatre, the one with the window that looks onto the Avon, the one from which he has watched the morning light on the water for nineteen years. He was reading through the notes for the season’s final production meeting when Wayne called to mention, in the tone Wayne uses when he is mentioning something he considers a threat to the cultural economy of Elsinore, that Bradley had raised the Community Ticket question at committee.

Alistair set his notes down. He looked at the river for a moment.

He said: “The Community Ticket is not a subsidy. It is a practice.”

Wayne said he understood the distinction but that Bradley had framed it as a financial liability.

Alistair said the distinction between a subsidy and a practice was not a matter of framing. A subsidy was a transfer designed to produce a market outcome. A practice was what a community did because it understood something about itself that could not be expressed as a financial liability. The Community Ticket was the Festival’s acknowledgment that the people of Elsinore and the Festival were not in a transactional relationship. They were in the same place together, which was a different thing entirely.

Wayne said he thought this was strong messaging and asked if he could use it in the Presidential Package materials.

Alistair said no.

Constance used her Community Ticket for a production she had not intended to see. She found it extraordinary. She sat with it for three days, then wrote one sentence. This is what the Community Ticket is for.

Constance Hay redeemed her Community Ticket every year. She had done so since 1987, which was the first year they were available, which was also the year she had served on the committee that proposed them.

She used it for a production she had not intended to see — something in the smaller studio theatre, a new Canadian work, the kind of thing she might not have chosen if she had been paying full price and weighing her options with the particular care of a woman on a fixed income who has attended the Festival for fifty years and has strong opinions about where to spend an evening.

She went. She found it extraordinary. She did not write about it in her notebook immediately. She sat with it for three days.

On the third day she wrote one sentence. Then she went to the next thing.

This is, in Constance’s view, what the Community Ticket is for: not reducing the price of something she would have attended anyway, but making possible the thing she would not have chosen but needed to see. The decision has been made for her, gracefully, by the availability of the ticket and the particular evening she chose to use it.

She did not attend the August committee meeting. She heard about Bradley’s slide from Norman, who had heard about it from Carly, who had been there and had filled three pages of notes and was already considering what kind of piece it was.

Constance listened to Norman’s account. She said: “He hasn’t used one.”

Norman said: “No.”

She said: “He’s been here seven years.”

Norman said nothing. He had the look he gets when he is deciding what percentage of what he knows this situation requires.

He decided: forty percent. He would write something. He would not say everything.

The farmer’s wife had used the Community Ticket twice in thirty years. The farmer had used it once, in 2009, for a production of King Lear that Maggie had mentioned at Sunday dinner and that he had thought about for six months afterward without being able to say precisely why.

He drove past the Festival box office on his way to the feed store the week after the committee meeting. The Something Rotten! poster was still in the window. He looked at it.

He was asked, at the feed store, what he thought about the Community Ticket question.

He thought about the 2009 King Lear for a moment.

“Earned it,” he said.

He paid for his feed. He drove home.

Carly wrote the piece for the Beacon. Norman gave her nine hundred words and she used eight hundred and forty, which was, for Carly, restraint of a kind she had not previously practised and which Norman noted without comment because noting it would have been the wrong kind of acknowledgment.

The piece did not say that Bradley Fenn was wrong. The piece noted that the Community Ticket program had been operating for thirty-eight years without a formal review, that approximately four hundred and thirty residents used it each season, and that its founding committee, in 1987, had included two people still resident in Elsinore, one of whom — she did not name Constance — had redeemed hers every year since.

She quoted Alistair: a theatre and its city were not in a transactional relationship. They were in the same place together.

She did not quote Bradley. He had provided a statement. She read it carefully. She summarised it in one sentence: the committee had agreed the program should be reviewed before the next season.

She filed the piece. Norman read it. He did not edit it.

He texted her: Good.

Then, after a pause, he texted again: Very good.

This was, in Norman’s vocabulary, something he had not previously sent to anyone.

Gerald retrieved the 1987 committee minutes from his filing system on a Wednesday afternoon, not because anyone had asked, but because he had decided they were likely to become relevant and he preferred to know where things were before they were needed rather than after.

The minutes showed that the Community Ticket proposal had passed unanimously. The stated purpose, in the language of 1987, was “to ensure that the Festival remains an institution of the whole community and not only of those for whom the price of a ticket presents no difficulty.”

Gerald read this. He put his reading glasses in his shirt pocket, which is what he does when he has read something and is thinking about what it means.

Then he put them back on and continued his work, because thinking and working were not, in Gerald’s experience, mutually exclusive activities, and because the filing system does not pause for reflection.

He made a copy of the relevant page. He filed the copy under Community Ticket — Founding Committee — 1987 — Original Purpose. He filed the original back in its folder.

He did not send the copy to anyone. He left it where it was, available, complete, in the system, in case someone needed to know what had been decided in 1987 and why.

A Note for the Record

The community ticket is an old idea. It appears in various forms in theatres, concert halls, libraries, and public institutions across the English-speaking world: the practice of setting aside a portion of what an institution produces for the people in whose place it sits, at a price — or at no price — that removes the question of whether they can afford it.

The idea rests on a premise that is either obvious or contested, depending on where you are standing: that certain things belong to everyone in a community, not because they are free in the market sense, but because the community and the institution are not in a market relationship. They are in a place together. The institution exists because the community decided it should exist. The community is, in some sense that is not captured by the transaction, already in the room.

To the south, the question of who belongs in the room has taken on a different quality. The principle that certain rights attach to birth on American soil — a principle enshrined in the Fourteenth Amendment since 1868 — has been challenged by executive order. The argument is that birthright citizenship is a policy choice rather than a constitutional guarantee, and that the choice can be unmade by the executive branch without amendment. The courts have thus far disagreed. The argument continues.

In Elsinore, the Community Ticket attaches to six months of residency. It does not attach to birth. It does not attach to ownership, to income, to length of tenure, or to any test of whether the recipient has sufficiently demonstrated their connection to the place. It attaches to being here, in this municipality, on the Avon River, for six months.

Gerald has the 1987 minutes. The purpose was clear then. It is clear now. The question is whether the committee will read them before the review is completed, and whether reading them will change the outcome, and whether the outcome matters less than the fact that for thirty-eight years four hundred and thirty people a season have gone to the theatre because someone in 1987 decided that they should be able to.

Constance Hay went in 1987. She is going this year. She will use her ticket for something she has not yet decided, and she will sit with it for however many days it requires, and she will write one sentence when she is ready.

She has not been asked what the sentence says.
