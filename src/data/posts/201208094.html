There is a thought experiment that runs through the literature on Korean reunification, and it goes like this: if the two Koreas were unified tomorrow — if the DMZ dissolved, if the armies stood down, if the Kim dynasty ended and the two halves of the peninsula were merged into a single state — what would the result actually look like?

The honest answer is alarming. South Korea would suddenly incorporate twenty-six million people whose average income is approximately one twenty-fifth of a South Korean’s, who have been raised in a closed ideological system for three generations, who have no experience of markets or democracy or the basic information environment that South Koreans take for granted, and who would require decades of investment, education, and social integration before they could participate in the economy of the reunified state on anything like equal terms. The cost estimates vary but tend toward the staggering: some analyses suggest that rapid reunification could cost the South Korean economy upward of a trillion dollars over the first decade. Others go higher.

West Germany absorbed East Germany in 1990, and the costs of that integration — economically, socially, psychologically — were still being felt thirty years later in voting patterns, income disparities, and generational resentments. North Korea is not East Germany. East Germany had been a functioning, if dysfunctional, state for forty-five years. North Korea has been a dynastic political religion for seventy-five. The distance between Seoul and Pyongyang — geographically small, culturally enormous — is the product of seven decades of deliberate manufacture, of two populations raised to understand their shared peninsula in fundamentally incompatible ways.

This is why reunification has quietly disappeared from serious policy discourse even in South Korea, where the constitution still formally commits to it as a national goal. The goal remains on paper. The appetite for the reality has largely evaporated. Young South Koreans, in survey after survey, express diminishing interest in reunification and increasing concern about what it would cost them. The satellite image — the blazing South, the dark North — is not only a political symbol. It is an economic description of an integration problem that has no good solution at any foreseeable speed.

The Denuclearization Fiction

The word “denuclearization” appears in roughly forty years of North Korea policy documents, diplomatic communiqués, UN resolutions, and summit statements. It is, at this point, a fiction maintained by states that cannot afford to publicly acknowledge what they know privately: that North Korea will not give up its nuclear weapons, that the conditions under which it might do so do not exist and cannot be created, and that the entire framework of pressure-and-negotiation that has structured Western policy since the 1990s has produced a North Korea with a larger and more sophisticated nuclear arsenal than it had when that framework was introduced.

This is not an assessment that any government is prepared to make publicly, because making it publicly would require answering the next question: if denuclearization is off the table, what is on it? The answer is uncomfortable. What is on the table is deterrence — the management of a nuclear-armed North Korea rather than its elimination, the acceptance of a permanent two-state Korean peninsula in which one state has nuclear weapons and the other is protected by an American nuclear umbrella, and the indefinite maintenance of that arrangement while hoping that change, internal or external, eventually produces a different situation.

This is the position that the United States, South Korea, and Japan have effectively occupied for the last decade, even while formally maintaining denuclearization as their stated objective. The gap between the stated objective and the operational reality is the diplomatic equivalent of the gap the jangmadang generation identified in North Korean propaganda: everyone can see it, no one says it, and the performance continues because the alternative — acknowledging the reality — would require choices that no one wants to make.

He had already signalled as much weeks earlier, at February’s Ninth Party Congress, where he called South Korea the North’s “most hostile enemy” and said its “complete collapse cannot be ruled out,” while unveiling a five-year plan to expand the arsenal further. Then, in his March 2026 parliamentary address, he made it doctrine. He declared North Korea’s nuclear status irreversible. He removed reunification from the constitutional framework. He described South Korea as the “most hostile” state rather than as an estranged partner awaiting reconciliation. He was, with his characteristic directness about things that serve his purposes, naming the reality that everyone else is performing blindness to. North Korea is a nuclear state. It will remain a nuclear state. The question is what that means.

The moment when the diplomatic fiction most visibly cracked was not in Pyongyang but in Beijing. As the New York Times reported on June 9, 2026, statements about working toward ending North Korea’s nuclear programme “used to be standard for the two sides during diplomatic engagements, but they have been omitted since last September, when Mr. Kim visited Beijing to attend a military parade.” China — the country that has officially opposed North Korean nuclear development since the programme began — stopped saying so out loud. The omission is not accidental. It is the diplomatic equivalent of a concession, made without acknowledgment, in the hope that no one notices that the argument has been quietly surrendered.

The clearest sign of where this fiction is heading, though, came in August 2026, and it came from Washington, not Pyongyang. Donald Trump confirmed to reporters that he intended to meet Kim Jong-un again before the year was out — the first sitting American president to seek out such a meeting since his own 2019 detour into North Korea at the DMZ. In the same round of remarks, Trump offered his own casual tally of the arsenal in question, putting the number at fifty-seven “very powerful nuclear weapons,” a figure notably above the roughly fifty warheads outside estimates had settled on the year before. The precision of the number matters less than the register in which it was delivered: an American president reciting his rival’s warhead count the way one might mention a neighbour’s new car, evidence in itself that “denuclearization” has become a word retained for form’s sake rather than a policy anyone is still pursuing.

More consequential still, Trump tied his outreach to Kim to a public rebuke of Seoul, citing South Korea’s reluctance to assist in the war with Iran as grounds to scale back the joint military exercises that have underwritten deterrence on the peninsula for seventy years. The alliance that the entire manage-don’t-eliminate framework depends on is being renegotiated by the American president personally, on terms that route around Seoul rather than through it. If Kim Jong-un has spent this series proving that time and demography are not on his side, Washington has just demonstrated that patience with the alliance structure protecting South Korea cannot be taken for granted either.

The Generational Reckoning

Kim Jong-un controls many things. He does not control time.

The jangmadang generation — the North Koreans born in the 1980s and 1990s who grew up in the post-famine informal economy, who watched South Korean dramas under blankets, who learned that the state’s narrative about the world was systematically false — is now between thirty and forty-five years old. They are the generation that the pandemic crackdown was designed to reach: to break, or at least to discipline, their accumulated distance from the official ideology before it metastasized into something more dangerous than ideological indifference.

Whether the crackdown worked, in the sense of restoring genuine belief rather than performed belief, is a question that no external observer can answer with confidence. What defector testimony consistently suggests is that it did not work in the deepest sense — that the distance between what North Koreans are required to perform and what they privately think has not closed, only become more dangerous to express. The terror functions. The belief does not return.

Beneath the jangmadang generation is an even younger cohort — North Koreans now in their teens and early twenties, raised after the famine, who have grown up in a world in which smartphones exist (North Korea has its own domestic intranet, the Kwangmyong, through which citizens access state-approved content), in which information about the outside world leaks through despite all efforts to stop it, and in which the gap between the official narrative and the observable reality is a daily lived experience rather than a discovery made by watching a smuggled drama.

Kim Jong-un cannot stop time. The generation that is most ideologically pliable — the generation raised on rations from the state before the famine broke the compact — is dying. The generation that replaced it has different memories, different reference points, different understanding of the relationship between the state and the individual. Managing that transition without the kind of systemic fracture the regime fears most is the internal challenge of the next twenty years — one that no amount of nuclear capability or Russian patronage can resolve.

The Information War

The information war — the contest between the regime’s ideological monopoly and the USB drives, radio broadcasts, and border-crossing communications that have been chipping at it for thirty years — is the one front on which Kim has not yet won, despite spending the pandemic years fighting harder than any previous North Korean leader.

The crackdown has been severe. The executions for K-pop are real. The random phone inspections are real. The arrests for South Korean speech patterns are real. The defector numbers have collapsed. By these measures, the crackdown has achieved its tactical objectives.

But the information itself has not been destroyed. Every North Korean who watches a South Korean drama and thinks “why can’t we live like that?” carries a seed of doubt that the regime’s most brutal enforcement cannot fully extirpate. The Kwangmyong intranet, designed to give citizens the experience of networked information without the content the regime fears, has paradoxically taught a generation that networked information exists and that there is a reason they are not allowed to access it. The radio broadcasts that Voice of America, Radio Free Asia, and defector-run stations transmit into North Korea — recently reduced by American budget cuts and South Korean government decisions, a development that should be recognized as the strategic error it is — continue to reach audiences who have learned to listen in secret.

The information war is long. Kim Jong-un has bought time with the crackdown, and perhaps a generation. He has not won the war. The question is whether the regime’s demographic problem — the aging and death of the most ideologically committed cohort — will produce a crisis before the crackdown’s effects can be consolidated. There is no answer to that question that does not depend on factors that are genuinely unknowable from the outside.

What Would Good Look Like?

This series began with a question that was implicit in the opening image: how did this happen? How did a country that was widely predicted to collapse in the 1990s, that endured a famine that killed perhaps a tenth of its population, that was isolated from the international financial system, end up with a leader standing on a Beijing balcony as the peer of Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin?

The answer this series has tried to provide is historical: the peninsula’s geography and its repeated subjugation built a nationalism that the Kim dynasty weaponized. The colonial wound created the furnace in which the ideology was forged. The Korean War built the survival myth that gave the dynasty its foundational legitimacy. The famine created the fracture that Kim Jong-un spent fifteen years trying to close. And the war in Ukraine delivered, with the improbable generosity of historical accident, the external alignment that broke the pressure framework and created the conditions for Kim’s ascent.

Understanding how we got here does not answer the harder question: where can we go from here, and is any destination good?

The most honest answer is that good outcomes are few and difficult to reach. The status quo — a nuclear North Korea, a fortified DMZ, a divided peninsula, a perpetual state of frozen war — is stable in the short term and unsustainable in the long one. It rests on the maintenance of an American nuclear guarantee in Northeast Asia that has become politically contested in Washington, on a Chinese willingness to tolerate a destabilizing neighbour in exchange for the buffer it provides, and on a North Korean regime whose internal contradictions have not yet produced a rupture but whose demographic trajectory suggests that they eventually will.

The reunification scenario is, as discussed, economically and socially catastrophic in any rapid form. It is also not on offer: Kim Jong-un has constitutionally abolished it as a goal.

What remains is a set of less dramatic possibilities. A managed coexistence — two Koreas, permanently separated, gradually developing limited communication and exchange that stops well short of integration — is conceivable but requires a North Korean leadership willing to contemplate it, and Kim Jong-un has given no indication that he is. A negotiated freeze on North Korean nuclear expansion, exchanging some combination of sanctions relief and security guarantees for a halt to production and testing, was attempted and failed under Obama, Trump, and Biden; it might be attempted again, but would require both sides to make concessions that their domestic politics make difficult. A Chinese-managed process of gradual North Korean opening — China leveraging its renewed primacy in Pyongyang to nudge Kim toward economic reform without regime change — is the scenario that Chinese analysts privately prefer, but it requires Beijing to accept the risks of a more open North Korea and Kim Jong-un to accept limitations on his domestic authority that he has never accepted from anyone.

There is also a fifth possibility that isn’t a policy so much as a diagnosis of what has already happened: a Trump-Kim summit, if it occurs, would not be a new scenario for the peninsula so much as the moment the operative one becomes impossible to deny. Deterrence-and-normalization, not denuclearization, has been the working American position for years; a televised handshake would simply be Washington admitting, the way Kim admitted in March, what the last decade of policy has actually amounted to.

None of these is satisfying. None resolves the fundamental problem: a nuclear-armed state with a dynastic government, a captive population, and a foundational ideology that treats compromise as defeat is not a problem that has clean solutions.

The Bear That Endured

Part I of this series ended with an image from the Dangun myth: the bear that chose to endure, remaining in the cave through a hundred days of hardship, emerging transformed into the woman who would become the mother of the Korean nation. The tiger ran away. The bear stayed.

The myth was about endurance as a form of strength — the capacity to survive conditions that would break a lesser creature, to emerge from suffering not defeated but changed. It is the oldest story on the peninsula, and it is the story that the Kim dynasty has built its entire legitimacy upon: Korea has endured. Korea has survived the Mongols and the Japanese and the American bombing. Korea — this Korea, the northern one, the authentic one in the regime’s telling — is the bear, and it has stayed in the cave.

The myth leaves something out, as founding myths tend to do. The bear did not emerge from the cave free. It emerged transformed into a woman who was then impregnated by the god who had set the terms of her ordeal. The endurance was real. The liberation was not. What came after the cave was a different kind of constraint, with different managers.

Twenty-six million North Koreans are still in the cave. The cave is managed now by a man who is forty-two years old, who grew up in Switzerland learning to love basketball, who executed his uncle and sent a young American home to die, who stood on a balcony in Beijing and clapped alongside two of the world’s most powerful authoritarians, and who told his parliament in March 2026 that his country’s nuclear status was irreversible and its dignity intact.

He is not wrong about the endurance. The peninsula’s people have endured extraordinary things. They are enduring still.

Whether endurance, in the end, is enough — whether the bear in the cave eventually emerges into something other than a different kind of captivity — is the question that the history of this peninsula, across four thousand years, has not yet answered.

Postscript

The gap this essay describes did not wait long to demonstrate itself again.

On August 17, 2026, hours before the annual Ulchi Freedom Shield exercise was set to begin, President Trump ordered the Pentagon to “substantially reduce” it — cutting the drill from eleven days to five, calling it “costly” and an “inappropriate and hostile” signal to Pyongyang, and explicitly linking the decision to Seoul’s refusal to join American action against Iran. The alliance renegotiation this essay describes as routing around Seoul rather than through it was, for once, not a matter of interpretation. As the Korea JoongAng Daily reported, South Korean officials said the announcement came without prior notice, leaving Seoul’s own defence establishment to learn of the change from the same Truth Social post as everyone else.

North Korea’s answer arrived within days. As the shortened drills wound down on August 21, more than ten KN-25 ballistic missiles were fired into the East Sea. Kim Yo-jong, Kim Jong-un’s sister and chief spokesperson, dismissed the reduction as changing nothing about what Pyongyang considers the drills’ inherently hostile character — and went further, denying any knowledge of a planned Trump-Kim meeting at all, even as the American president kept telling reporters a fourth summit was coming before the year was out. Seoul’s Unification Ministry has since floated a possible sidebar meeting at the APEC summit in Shenzhen this November, contingent, in the minister’s own careful phrasing, on “if Chairman Kim Jong-un wants it.” Kim Yo-jong did not stop at the drills. She accused Seoul of “double-dealing behaviour” for supporting the American reduction while trying to preserve its own alliance posture — placing the South Korean government, in her words, “in a poor plight,” neither credible to the North nor fully in control of its own alliance with Washington.

Even the number at the centre of this essay’s argument about the diplomatic fiction did not survive the month intact. South Korea’s Defence Ministry, briefing lawmakers after the missile launches, put the size of North Korea’s arsenal at somewhere between eighty and one hundred and twenty warheads. Donald Trump’s fifty-seven — the neighbour’s-new-car number this essay used to illustrate how casually the fiction is now recited — was already out of date by the time he offered it.

What this two-week sequence confirms is the essay’s argument in miniature: a concession extended without being requested, accepted in substance and refused in meaning, an ally left to manage the consequences, and a regime that has learned it can absorb an American goodwill gesture, fire a volley of missiles in response, and lose nothing by it. The bear is still in the cave. The men outside are still arguing, mostly with each other, about the terms of an ordeal none of them controls.
