On election night, 3 November 2026, Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith of Mississippi did not wait for the count. She declared victory, and she did it with a line she had clearly been saving: “I blocked Scott Colom from being a federal judge, and you blocked him from being a U.S. Senator.”

It was a good line. It had the ring of a woman settling an old account in public, which is what it was. When the Biden White House put Colom, a district attorney from Columbus, forward for the federal bench in northern Mississippi, Hyde-Smith declined to return her blue slip, the little piece of Senate courtesy that lets a home-state senator quietly kill a judicial nomination without ever having to explain herself on the floor. Colom never got his hearing. The appointment died the way such nominations usually die, in a drawer.

Hyde-Smith did offer reasons, just never on the Senate floor. As Michael Kruse and Emily Cochrane reported for The New York Times on 13 November, she cited campaign money Colom had received from George and Alex Soros, and a 2021 statement he had signed with more than 70 other progressive prosecutors saying they would not support criminalising doctors or parents who help a transgender child obtain gender-affirming care. Colom had come to her with bipartisan backing, from Representative Bennie Thompson, his mentor, and from two former Republican governors. It made no difference. His interview with the senator was a video call of no more than 15 minutes, and he left it feeling he had been talking to someone whose mind was already made up.

The snub, he says, did not make him want revenge. It made him read her record. She had voted against the 2021 bipartisan infrastructure law, which Mississippi’s other Republican senator, Roger Wicker, had helped pass, and which paid for projects across the state, including the restoration of passenger rail along the Gulf Coast. Colom announced his candidacy in September 2025. “As a Christian, I forgive Cindy Hyde-Smith,” he said in his first interview. “But as a Mississippian, I’m frustrated about the way she votes.”

So on election night she reminded her supporters who had won the first round, and thanked them in advance for winning the second.

The trouble was the count. On the morning of 4 November, The New York Times had the Mississippi race separated by a few dozen votes, with provisional ballots still outstanding. It took another week to finish. On 11 November, the race was called for Colom. He will be the first Black senator from Mississippi since Reconstruction, and he will take the seat of the woman who once made sure he would never sit in judgement of anyone.

There is a word for the moment when a premature victory speech meets a finished count. In the American South, the polite form of it is “bless her heart.”

What the terror was for

Adam Serwer, writing for The Atlantic on 12 November, opens his account of Colom’s win not in 2026 but in Liberty, Mississippi, in the autumn of 1961, when a white former congressman and state legislator named E. H. Hurst shot Herbert Lee in the head in broad daylight. Lee was a Black farmer who had been helping his neighbours register to vote. An all-white coroner’s jury accepted Hurst’s claim of self-defence: Lee, he said, had come at him with a tire iron.

Serwer’s point is that none of the machinery of Jim Crow Mississippi, the shootings, the jailings, the law that once made it a crime to “disturb relations between the races,” was random. It had a purpose, and the purpose was to prevent exactly what happened on 11 November 2026. “The point of all this terror,” he writes, was to stop a Black Mississippian from being elected to the United States Senate.

It worked for a very long time.

Lee’s story did not end with Lee. Louis Allen, a logger who saw the shooting, was pressured into backing Hurst’s account at the inquest. He later told federal authorities the truth, asked for protection, and did not get it. In January 1964 he was shot dead in his own driveway. Allen had also tried, at the Amite County Courthouse in Liberty, to do the thing that seemed to provoke everything else: register to vote. No Black resident of the county had voted since the 1890s.

The 1890s are the hinge. That was when Mississippi wrote a new constitution with a poll tax, a literacy test and an “understanding clause” that let a white registrar decide whether a Black applicant had understood a passage of the state constitution to the registrar’s satisfaction. Almost none of it mentioned race. It didn’t need to. The words were neutral; the hands that applied them were not. The historian Neil McMillen, whom Serwer quotes, described the system plainly: much of the exclusion was “left to the discriminatory application of otherwise racially neutral law.”

Hold on to that phrase. It is about to become relevant again.

The last one before him

The last Black man to represent Mississippi in the Senate was Blanche Bruce, born enslaved in 1841, chosen by the state legislature in 1874 and seated in 1875, when senators were still picked by state lawmakers rather than voters. He was the second Black senator in American history; the first, Hiram Revels, was also from Mississippi, chosen in 1870 for what remained of a vacant term. For a few years during Reconstruction, the most violently segregationist state in the union sent the country its first Black senators.

Then Reconstruction ended, and Mississippi made sure it would not happen again. It took 145 years from the end of Bruce's term in 1881 to Colom's election, a gap longer than most of the world's countries have existed.

Serwer notes one of Bruce’s votes in the Senate: against Chinese exclusion, a measure his own Republican Party supported. Bruce explained it as solidarity, saying he represented “a people who are considered essentially disqualified from the privileges and immunities of American citizenship.” It is a sentence worth reading slowly, because it describes Black Mississippians for most of the century that followed, and because the man who said it was a senator.

Serwer suspects Bruce would be proud of Colom, “albeit initially confused as to why he was running as a Democrat.” The last Democrat to represent Mississippi in the Senate was John Stennis, an avowed segregationist who, as a young prosecutor in the 1930s, led the prosecution of three Black men who had been tortured into confessing. The Supreme Court threw out those convictions in 1936, in the case that made coerced confessions inadmissible. Parties change. The question of who gets to count does not.

Colom's own family is a short course in how much they change. His father, Wilbur Colom, was a civil rights activist in the 1960s and a Republican in the 1970s and 1980s. He worked on Ronald Reagan's transition team and on the Senate campaigns of Haley Barbour and Thad Cochran, and in 1987, running for state treasurer, he told the Clarion Ledger that Black Democrats should be ashamed of themselves for simply handing the party their votes. His mother, Dorothy Colom, always a Democrat, lost two races for the bench before becoming the district's first Black chancery judge on her third try. Their son turned down a big Chicago law firm to come home, and became the first Black judge on the Lowndes County justice court, the first Black city prosecutor in Columbus and, in 2015, the district attorney of a majority-white district, unseating an incumbent who had held the job since 1989.

The campaign kept crossing that history. Last summer Colom stumped at the Neshoba County Fair, where Ronald Reagan spoke about states' rights in 1980, not far from where James Chaney, Andrew Goodman and Michael Schwerner were murdered by Klansmen and lawmen during Freedom Summer in 1964. The day before his victory speech at Tougaloo College, he held a press conference in a hoodie printed with a line from Bob Moses, who directed Freedom Summer, to the effect that from inside Mississippi the rest of America does not seem real, and from outside it Mississippi does not.

Callais and the neutral word

This series has spent most of its life on a single thesis: that the most effective way to suppress a vote in the 2020s is not to forbid it but to make it more expensive. Friction, not prohibition. Portals that aren’t ready, investigations that never end, maps that change three times in an afternoon, monitors at the door. Nobody has to say why. The friction does the work.

The Supreme Court gave that strategy its constitutional blessing this year in Louisiana v. Callais. As Serwer describes it, the Court held that voting restrictions are constitutional if they are passed with partisan rather than racial intent. If lawmakers want to disenfranchise Democrats, and those Democrats happen to be Black, the Constitution has no objection.

Read that beside McMillen’s description of the 1890 constitution and the resemblance is hard to miss. Callais does not revive the poll tax or the understanding clause. It revives their method: the neutral word and the selective hand. A legislature need only say “Democrats” where its predecessors would once have meant something else.

Colom's own district shows how long that method has been at work, and how flexibly. The first time the Justice Department used the Voting Rights Act to protect white voters was in Noxubee County, in 2005, and Wilbur Colom represented the defendant. A decade later a lawyer from that case brought a new suit over Noxubee's voter rolls, and the county, rather than fight an expensive battle, accepted a consent decree that put its roll maintenance under scrutiny. In the months before the 2026 midterms, Bragg notes, Noxubee and neighbouring Lowndes, Colom's home county, both saw voter inactivations surge.

Southern states took the hint. Several Republican-controlled legislatures redrew their congressional maps to eliminate Black-majority districts, which this series tracked on its own gerrymandering map in Part LX and through the long Missouri saga. Brad Parscale, Donald Trump’s former campaign manager, said out loud what the strategy was for: “if states are aggressive, we could see a healthy majority in the House perpetually.” Perpetually is a big word. It is the word every architect of every exclusionary system has believed in, right up to the day it failed.

The turnout nobody planned for

A Senate race is statewide; no map can carve it up. What the new maps could do was send a message about whose votes were wanted. Black voters across the South received it and answered. Serwer reports that Black turnout in the South appears to have surged in November to a hair below the levels usually seen in presidential years, which in a midterm is extraordinary. Combined with a measurable increase in white support for Democrats, it put states like Mississippi, long assumed to be beyond reach, into play.

Colom could not have won on Black votes alone. Mississippi still has the highest proportion of Black residents of any state, but it is not majority-Black, and decades of racially polarised voting placed a hard ceiling over every Black candidate who ran statewide. To win, Colom needed a turnout surge and a slice of white voters at the same time. He got both. In his victory speech he gave the credit away: “It’s not something that I did, y’all. It’s something that we did. All I did was have the courage to imagine a night like this.”

Imagining it was the hard part for most of a year. Colom spent his lunch breaks as district attorney phoning donors from his parents' kitchen table while his mother took notes, and the usual answer, as she remembers it, was that he sounded impressive and could not win. Months in, Bennie Thompson told him on a video call that no help was coming from Washington, and that his job was to make people believe a win was possible. So he drove the state, from the coast to the Memphis suburbs, and a year after announcing he went to Mound Bayou, a town of fewer than 1,500 founded by formerly enslaved people, whose activists had helped force the country to look at the murder of Emmett Till in 1955. There he dwelt longer than usual on Mississippi's history of suppressing the vote. Only in the final weeks did the reinforcements arrive: Bill Clinton, Kamala Harris, James Clyburn and Pete Buttigieg in person, Barack Obama in an ad, and a viral spot built on Hyde-Smith's suggestion that people priced out of beef could choose another protein. Choose another senator instead, it said. Even then, in Thompson's phrase at the victory party, the campaign had "a rich message but a poor treasury." Colom outraised Hyde-Smith in the final quarter, about $1.45 million to $864,000, a sliver of what poured into other Senate races in the most expensive cycle in American history.

The provisional ballots matter here too. A race decided by a margin that thin is a race decided by the slowest, dullest parts of election administration: the ballots set aside because a registration couldn’t be confirmed on the spot, the voter who had to come back with identification, the clerk who counted carefully rather than quickly. Every friction mechanism this series has documented is aimed at exactly that margin. In Mississippi this year, it held.

It held against real pressure. In the summer of 2025 Mississippi moved tens of thousands of voters to “inactive” status using data from the credit agency Experian, as Ko Bragg recounted in The Atlantic on 14 November. DeSoto County, the white-flight suburbs of Memphis, inactivated more than 15,000, more than anywhere else in the state. On Election Day its lines lagged and its polling places ran short of affidavit ballots. Colom carried it anyway, with about 53 per cent, in a county Hyde-Smith had won in both her previous races. Organisers spent the following week helping voters go back to cure their provisional ballots.

The aftermath held too. During the week of counting, asked about fraud, Hyde-Smith offered only that “there’s always irregularities,” and Part LXIV watched Mississippi Republicans demand, on 5 November, that officials “segregate and preserve” the affidavit ballots, the usual first step toward a challenge. It came to very little. When Colom was declared the winner, Emily Bazelon noted in The New York Times Magazine on 14 November, a historically Republican state produced no challenges and no protests. Hyde-Smith conceded and congratulated him. The count simply finished, and the result stood.

Firsts, and what they don’t settle

When the new Congress convenes on 3 January 2027, it will hold seven Black senators, Colom, Raphael Warnock, Tim Scott, Juliana Stratton, Lisa Blunt Rochester, Angela Alsobrooks and Cory Booker, the most in American history. There will be five Black governors serving at once: Wes Moore, Keisha Lance Bottoms, Byron Donalds, David Crowley and Aaron Ford. Before this election, the country had elected three Black governors in its entire history. The first Black governor of any state, P. B. S. Pinchback of Louisiana, inherited the office in 1872, needed the Grant administration’s help to fend off a white-supremacist uprising, and lasted 39 days. The next one took more than a century.

It would be easy to end there, with the firsts lined up like trophies. It would also be wrong.

The fact that Black Mississippians overcame the barriers put in front of them does not make the barriers acceptable. A vote that has to survive a redrawn map, a purged roll, a provisional envelope and a week of counting is not a vote exercised freely; it is a vote that won a fight it should never have had to enter. The surge happened because people were angry enough to carry the extra weight. Anger is not a sustainable basis for a democracy, and nobody should design an electoral system on the assumption that the people it burdens will always be furious enough to overcome it.

Nor does one election reverse Callais. The ruling stands. The maps stand. Mississippi’s legislature still controls how Mississippi votes, and there will be people in Jackson this winter studying how 2026 happened so that 2028 does not happen the same way. Victories of this kind tend to produce counter-measures, not surrenders.

That is also where the new Congress comes in. Congress has power under the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments to enforce the right to vote, and it has used that power before, in 1965. Legislation to restore what the Court has dismantled would almost certainly die on the president’s desk. It would still set the terms of the next fight, and it would put every senator, including the newest one from Mississippi, on the record.

Hyde-Smith’s line, revisited

Go back to election night. “I blocked Scott Colom from being a federal judge, and you blocked him from being a U.S. Senator.”

The first half was true. The blue slip is exactly the kind of quiet, procedurally neutral veto that this series has been describing for sixty-odd parts: a mechanism that requires no public reason and leaves few fingerprints. Hyde-Smith had her reasons; she simply never had to defend them in the open. One senator, one slip of paper, one career redirected.

The second half assumed the voters would behave like the blue slip: quietly, predictably, on her side. They didn’t. The blocked judge became the elected senator, and the senator who blocked him will have to clear out her office for him.

Serwer ends his piece with a promise that sounds like a prophecy: the project of snuffing out Black aspirations to equal citizenship “failed in 1875. It failed in 1965. And it failed in 2026, with Callais. It will always fail, even if it takes a hundred years. Or a hundred and fifty.”

He may be right. But it is worth remembering how much of that hundred and fifty years was paid for by people like Herbert Lee and Louis Allen, who did not live to see the failure. The arc bends. It does not bend by itself, and it has never bent cheaply.
