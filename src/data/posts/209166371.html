In 1988, the playwright and activist Larry Kramer published an open letter addressed directly to Anthony Fauci, in the gay press, that called him a murderer. Two years later, in the spring of 1990, members of the activist group ACT UP marched onto the campus of the National Institutes of Health carrying a mock casket with his name on it and a message that did not require footnoting: Fuck You, Fauci. By any ordinary measure of institutional relations, this should have ended things. A federal official spends four decades cultivating a reputation as the nation’s most trusted nonpartisan scientist, and a movement of the dying decides, not without reason, that he is the man standing between them and the drugs that might save their lives. That is usually where the story stops — with two sides entrenched, one of them dead within a few years, the other issuing a statement of regret at a memorial service. It is not what happened here, and the reason it is not what happened here is the single most instructive episode in Fauci’s long career, more instructive, I would argue, than anything that happened to him during Covid.

ACT UP itself had formed only a year before Kramer’s letter, in March 1987, after Kramer delivered a speech at New York’s Lesbian and Gay Community Services Center arguing that the gay community’s own passivity was compounding the government’s negligence. The group’s tactics were built for maximum visibility rather than polite lobbying: a 1988 shutdown of the FDA’s headquarters in Rockville, Maryland, drew more than a thousand demonstrators and resulted in over 170 arrests; die-ins staged outside churches and government buildings put activists’ bodies, quite literally, in the path of officials who might otherwise never have had to look at what the epidemic was doing to the people it was killing. Fauci’s NIAID was a frequent, specific target of these actions, not because Fauci himself had invented the bureaucratic slowness activists were protesting, but because he ran the institute where that slowness lived, and because — unlike most of his counterparts across the federal health establishment — he was reachable enough to make an effective target.

By the time Kramer’s letter appeared, AIDS had been killing gay men in the United States for the better part of a decade with almost no federal urgency to match it. The Reagan administration, for years, treated the epidemic as a subject barely worth a press briefing; Reagan himself did not deliver a major address on AIDS until 1987, six years after the first cases were identified, by which point tens of thousands of Americans had already died. Fauci, who had become director of the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases in 1984, was the government’s senior scientist most directly responsible for the research response, which made him, fairly or not, the face activists could actually reach. You cannot picket a president who will not meet with you. You can picket the man who runs the institute deciding which drugs get tested, on what timeline, under what rules.

And the rules, as ACT UP and the broader AIDS activist movement saw them, were killing people through sheer bureaucratic patience. Clinical trials at the time typically excluded anyone already taking other medications, which meant that dying patients — nearly all of whom were, by necessity, taking something to manage opportunistic infections — were routinely barred from the very trials meant to help people in their position. Placebo-controlled trials meant that a meaningful fraction of the sickest, most desperate participants in any given study were, by design, receiving nothing. The FDA’s ordinary approval timeline, built for a world where most conditions did not kill within a year or two of diagnosis, treated the years-long review process as an acceptable cost of scientific rigor. To a dying twenty-eight-year-old in 1988, none of that distinction between due diligence and denial meant anything. It looked, and in important respects it was, like an entire scientific establishment more committed to its own procedures than to the people those procedures were supposed to serve.

What made Fauci different from the officials ACT UP was also fighting in Washington and at the FDA was that he actually showed up. In the fall of 1988 — after the effigy-burning protests had already begun, not before — Fauci agreed to meet with activists from ACT UP, including Kramer himself, in a sit-down that by most accounts neither side expected to go well. It did not, at first. Activists have described Fauci as defensive in those early meetings, inclined to explain the reasoning behind the existing trial structure rather than to hear what the structure was costing the people sitting across from him. But he kept meeting. Over the following two years, Fauci did something almost no senior federal science official had done before him with an activist movement that had spent years calling him a murderer in print: he began, gradually and then substantially, to change the system these same activists said was killing them.

The mechanisms that emerged from that period are, decades later, simply how American drug approval works for serious diseases, which is easy to forget precisely because it succeeded so completely. Fauci’s institute helped develop what became known as the parallel track program, allowing patients who did not qualify for a formal clinical trial — because they were too sick, or already on other medications, or living too far from a trial site — to receive experimental AIDS drugs anyway, under expanded access rules, while the formal trials continued on their separate track. NIAID also began building community advisory boards directly into its AIDS research structure, seating activists, patients, and community representatives alongside scientists in decisions about trial design — a genuinely radical inversion of how clinical research had traditionally treated the people it studied, less as subjects to be protected from their own judgment than as stakeholders with standing to shape the questions being asked. The FDA, working alongside NIAID’s growing openness, developed accelerated approval pathways for drugs treating serious and life-threatening conditions, allowing promising treatments onto the market on the basis of surrogate endpoints rather than the full multi-year trials the agency had previously required.

None of this happened because Fauci woke up one morning newly convinced by an abstract argument about patient autonomy. It happened because a movement of people who called him a murderer refused to stop making their case to his face, meeting after meeting, and because Fauci — whatever his flaws, and this series has not been shy about naming a few of them — was capable of doing something that turns out to be rarer among powerful people than it should be: he let evidence and sustained, uncomfortable argument actually change his mind, in public, at some cost to his own institutional pride. Peter Staley, one of the activists who fought hardest against NIAID’s early posture and later became a close personal friend of Fauci’s, has described this period as the moment Fauci went from being an obstacle to being, in Staley’s word, an ally — not because the activists softened their demands, but because Fauci eventually concluded they were right about most of them.

The results were not merely procedural. AZT, the first antiretroviral drug approved for AIDS treatment, had already reached the market in 1987 through an accelerated review, but it was the parallel track system Fauci helped design in the years that followed — formally adopted in 1989 and expanded through the early 1990s — that let dying patients access experimental follow-on therapies like ddI and ddC without waiting for the multi-year trial process to run its ordinary course. By the time protease inhibitors emerged in the mid-1990s and transformed AIDS from a near-certain death sentence into a manageable chronic condition for patients who could access the new combination therapies, the review and access infrastructure built out of the ACT UP confrontations a decade earlier was already in place to get those drugs to patients faster than the pre-1988 system ever could have. It is not an exaggeration to say that some of the very people who marched on the NIH with Fauci’s effigy in 1990 were alive years later, in part, because of the system their own protests had forced him to build.

It is worth being precise about what this episode does and does not prove, because the temptation with an origin story this tidy is to let it do more work than it should. It does not erase the parts of Fauci’s temperament — the need to be the smartest person in the room, the discomfort with being challenged that colleagues would describe throughout his career — that this series has already spent time on and will spend more time on later. Fauci did not emerge from his fights with ACT UP a changed personality; the same man who eventually built what one successor called a personal empire at NIAID is the same man who sat across from Larry Kramer in 1988. What the AIDS years do establish, and establish about as clearly as any episode in a fifty-year career can, is that Fauci’s capacity for scientific consensus was not simply institutional camouflage — a comfortable centrism that let him avoid ever being wrong in public. When a group of people with nothing left to lose proved to him, repeatedly and at volume, that the system he ran was actively costing lives through excessive caution, he rebuilt large parts of that system rather than defend it. That is a different thing than never having enemies. It is the far rarer skill of knowing what to do once your enemies turn out to have a point.

By 1990, the same activists who had carried his effigy through an NIH parking lot were being invited by Fauci onto the advisory structures shaping the government’s AIDS research agenda. Kramer, who never fully forgave the earlier years of the epidemic and never claimed to, nonetheless came to describe Fauci decades later as the only official in Washington who had actually listened. That reversal — genuine, documented, and costly to Fauci’s own comfort at the time — is the piece of his record his current interrogators have the least interest in discussing, because it complicates the caricature considerably more than a diary entry about Barbra Streisand ever could. A man who spent the AIDS crisis absorbing the fury of the people he had failed, and rebuilding federal drug policy in response, is a harder man to dismiss as a self-serving bureaucrat than the diary alone would suggest. It is also, not incidentally, the single clearest precedent for the question this series keeps returning to: what happens to a man who built his entire authority on being trusted, once trust itself becomes the very thing his critics have decided to weaponize against him.

The clearest measure of how completely that relationship transformed came at its very end. Kramer died in 2020, at 84, having lived considerably longer with HIV than anyone expected of a man diagnosed in the epidemic’s earliest and deadliest years — a survival owed in no small part to the antiretroviral therapies Fauci’s post-1988 reforms had helped bring to market. By multiple accounts of his final days, Kramer’s last words to the visitor at his bedside, the same man he had once called a murderer in print, were simply: “I love you, Tony.” Fauci, through tears, said it back. Whatever else this series has to say about the space between Fauci’s public reputation and his private character, that exchange is not something either side of the current argument about him can easily explain away.

The AIDS years gave Fauci something else, too, that would matter three decades later, for better and for worse: proof, in his own mind, that being right eventually vindicates you, however long and however loud the shouting gets in the meantime. That lesson would serve him well through PEPFAR, the subject of the next essay in this series. It would serve him rather less well in 2020, when he assumed, not unreasonably given his own history, that the same pattern would eventually repeat — that the shouting from his new critics would subside once the science settled, the way it eventually had with Kramer and Staley and the rest. It did not. The difference between those two moments, and what it says about the country in between them, is a large part of what the rest of this series is trying to work out.
