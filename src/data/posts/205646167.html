In 1990, China’s share of global manufacturing output was approximately three percent.

By 2010, it was twenty percent — the largest single national share in the world, surpassing the United States for the first time since the early 20th century. By 2020, it was roughly twenty-eight percent. In thirty years, China had gone from a minor player in global manufacturing to the country that made more things than any other country on earth, often more cheaply and in larger volumes than competitors had believed possible.

This transformation was not an accident. It was not simply the product of cheap labour, though labour cost advantages were real and significant. It was the result of a deliberate, patient, state-directed strategy that the West mostly failed to understand until it was too late to reverse easily — and that the 15th Five-Year Plan is designed to consolidate and extend into the technology sectors of the next generation.

Understanding how China became the factory floor of the world is essential to understanding why the 141 pages read the way they do. The rare earth strategy, the semiconductor ambitions, the robotics investment, the quantum networks — all of it follows the logic established between 1990 and 2010, refined over two decades of trial and adjustment, and now being applied to higher-value targets.

The immediate precondition was Deng’s 1992 southern tour, which confirmed that the economic reform project was accelerating despite Tiananmen’s diplomatic aftermath. The specific mechanism was the Special Economic Zone model, extended and deepened: designated areas where foreign investment was not merely permitted but actively courted, where labour regulations were lighter, where tax incentives were substantial, and where the infrastructure — ports, roads, power, telecommunications — was built to international standards that the interior of China had not yet reached.

The zones worked for a reason that took years for Western analysts to fully absorb: China offered not just cheap labour but disciplined, educable labour in quantities that no other low-cost economy could match. Bangladesh was cheap. Vietnam was cheap. Indonesia was cheap. None of them could field a workforce of hundreds of millions with literacy rates approaching ninety percent, work ethics shaped by decades of collective discipline, and a government capable of building a road, a port, and a power plant in the time it took other governments to complete an environmental impact assessment.

Foreign manufacturers arrived in the 1990s to find a country that was simultaneously a massive potential consumer market and an unlimited source of production capacity. The combination was irresistible. Japanese electronics firms came first, then Korean manufacturers, then European and American companies that found the arithmetic of global competition impossible to ignore: a Chinese factory worker in 1995 earned roughly three percent of what an American worker earned, and the quality differential, while real, was narrowing year by year.

The supply chains that formed around Chinese manufacturing during this period were not simply outsourcing arrangements. They were the reconstruction of global industrial geography. A mobile phone assembled in Shenzhen in 2005 might contain components from thirty countries, but the assembly, the tooling, the packaging, the logistics, and increasingly the component design were Chinese. The value that accrued in each of those steps accumulated in China. The companies whose logos appeared on the finished product booked the profit. The communities where the design had previously happened saw the jobs disappear.

The transfer of manufacturing technology that accompanied foreign direct investment is the aspect of this period that the party planned most carefully and the West understood least.

When a foreign company established a joint venture in China — as the regulatory framework typically required — it brought its production processes, its quality control systems, its supply chain management, and its technical knowledge into a partnership with a Chinese counterpart. The Chinese partner learned. The engineers trained on foreign equipment developed expertise. The managers who ran the joint ventures took what they learned into subsequent roles in Chinese companies or started their own. The technology transfer was not hidden — it was often explicitly required by the regulatory framework — but its cumulative effect was systematically underestimated.

The pattern was most visible in electronics. In the early 1990s, China’s electronics industry was capable of assembling components designed and manufactured elsewhere. By the late 1990s, Chinese manufacturers were designing components. By the 2000s, Chinese companies like Huawei and ZTE were competing in global telecommunications markets with technology they had developed internally, built on foundations laid by years of joint venture partnerships with European and American firms. The firms that had established those partnerships did not plan this outcome. They planned to sell into the Chinese market. They succeeded, and in doing so, they helped create the competitors that would challenge them in markets beyond China.

The solar panel industry is the clearest recent example of the pattern at full development. Western governments subsidised the development of solar technology in the 1990s and 2000s as a clean energy priority. Chinese manufacturers entered the market in the late 2000s, undercut Western producers on price through a combination of lower labour costs, state subsidy, and manufacturing scale, and drove most Western producers out of the market within a decade. By 2023, China produced roughly eighty percent of the world’s solar panels. The technology had been developed largely in Western research institutions. The manufacturing had moved to China so completely that rebuilding significant Western capacity would require years and substantial capital investment.

This is the template the 15th Five-Year Plan is applying to AI chips, quantum computing, humanoid robotics, and battery technology. China has done this before. It knows how.

There is one sector where the strategy was applied with such thoroughness that it deserves separate attention: rare earth elements. The seventeen rare earth elements — with names like neodymium, dysprosium, terbium, and europium — are essential components in everything from smartphones to electric vehicle motors to precision-guided munitions. They are not, despite the name, particularly rare in the earth’s crust. They are, however, extremely difficult and environmentally damaging to process. In the 1980s and 1990s, under a strategy attributed to Deng Xiaoping himself — “the Middle East has oil; China has rare earths” — China systematically undercut global rare earth prices, driving Western producers out of the market, and accumulated the processing infrastructure and expertise that other countries had abandoned as uneconomical. By 2010, China controlled approximately ninety-seven percent of global rare earth production. It had not cornered a resource. It had cornered a supply chain, which is considerably more durable. The 141 pages build on this position rather than creating it — they describe the extension of an established strategy into new domains.

The WTO accession of December 2001 completed China’s integration into the global trading system and made the terms of competition fully visible to anyone paying attention.

The negotiations had taken fifteen years. The terms required China to reduce tariffs, open service sectors to foreign competition, and adopt intellectual property protections aligned with international standards. In exchange, China received permanent normal trade relations status with the United States and access to the entire WTO membership’s markets on the same terms as any other member.

The Western assumption embedded in the accession decision — articulated explicitly by the Clinton administration — was that commercial integration would produce political convergence. A China embedded in the rules-based trading order would become more like the countries that had built that order: more transparent, more governed by law, more responsive to its citizens. The assumption was wrong, and it was wrong in ways visible to anyone who examined the available evidence in 2001. The party had never indicated any intention to democratise. Its explicit position was that political stability was the precondition for economic development, not its consequence.

But the assumption was convenient for American businesses that wanted access to Chinese manufacturing and Chinese consumers. It was convenient for American consumers who wanted cheaper goods. It was convenient for the financial sector that profited from managing the capital flows. The analysis that said the assumptions were wrong was available and was largely ignored, because ignoring it was profitable.

The decade from 2001 to 2010 was, in aggregate, the most consequential economic decade in modern history.

China’s WTO accession opened the global market to Chinese manufactured goods at scale. The result was a wave of manufacturing displacement that transformed the industrial geography of the United States, Europe, and other developed economies. American manufacturing employment fell by approximately six million jobs between 2000 and 2010 — a loss the economic literature has linked substantially to Chinese import competition. The communities where those jobs had been concentrated — small and medium-sized cities in the American Midwest and South — experienced the kind of economic and social disruption that does not show up cleanly in GDP statistics but does show up in mortality rates, opioid deaths, and voting patterns.

The political consequences — the rise of economic nationalism, the pushback against globalisation, the trade wars that began under Trump and have continued since — are the downstream effects of decisions made in the decade when China became the factory floor of the world. China did not cause those political consequences. The countries that decided to integrate Chinese manufacturing into their supply chains without thinking through the distributional effects caused them. But China benefited, because the political chaos that economic nationalism has produced in the United States and Europe has consumed attention and institutional capacity that might otherwise have been directed at the strategic competition the 141 pages describe.

The China that emerged from the factory floor decade was qualitatively different from the China that had entered it.

It was richer — GDP per capita had roughly tripled between 2000 and 2010. It was more urban — the urban population had grown by approximately 250 million people, producing the loneliness epidemic that the addendum essay to this series examines. It was more technologically capable — the manufacturing experience of the decade had produced a generation of engineers, managers, and entrepreneurs with knowledge of global production systems that no classroom could have provided. And it was more confident — the 2008 Beijing Olympics, staged with a precision and scale that genuinely impressed most observers, was the party’s announcement that China had returned.

The opening ceremony — directed by Zhang Yimou, who had spent his career making films alternately celebrated internationally and suppressed domestically — deployed fifteen thousand performers in sequences of choreographic complexity that television cameras struggled to contain. The drumming sequence that opened the ceremony, featuring two thousand and eight performers in perfect synchronisation, was a statement about what a society organised around collective discipline could achieve that no words could have made as effectively. The world watched. The party intended for the world to watch. China had hosted the Olympics before — never. China had hosted an event of this scale and international attention — never. The ceremony was not merely sport. It was the opening statement of the Long Return made visible, in real time, to four billion television viewers.

The 2008 global financial crisis reinforced that confidence in ways the party found politically useful. The crisis originated in American financial institutions, spread through the global banking system that American and European firms had built, and was addressed by Western governments through the same kind of large-scale state intervention that Chinese economists had been told for years was incompatible with market efficiency. China’s response — a four trillion yuan stimulus package that maintained growth while the United States and Europe contracted — looked, from Beijing, like confirmation that the Chinese model worked and the Washington Consensus did not.

The lesson the party drew was clear and consequential: we survived Tiananmen, the Soviet collapse, the Asian financial crisis of 1997, and the global financial crisis, and emerged from each stronger than the system that had been lecturing us about how to run an economy. The Long Return was not merely possible. It was, in the party’s reading, already substantially accomplished.

What remained was to make it permanent. That project — the consolidation of China’s gains into strategic dominance, under a leader who understood the stakes and was prepared to act accordingly — is the subject of Part XII.
