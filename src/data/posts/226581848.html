On Wednesday, November 25, 2026, the day before Thanksgiving, Washington released two numbers within the same hour, and they seemed to describe two different countries.

The first was gross domestic product. The economy grew at a 4.3 per cent annual rate in the third quarter, faster than first reported and the strongest pace in three years. The second was the Personal Consumption Expenditures price index, the Federal Reserve’s preferred measure of inflation. Prices rose 3.8 per cent in October from a year earlier, up from 3.6 per cent in September.

Economists will tell you the first number means the United States is nowhere near a recession. Most Americans will tell you it is already in one. Part XIII of this series, “The Good Report Nobody Felt,” argued that a strong jobs report and an angry electorate could both be right at the same time. This part asks a narrower question. When Americans say “recession” in the face of a 4.3 per cent growth rate, what do they actually mean? The answer turns out to be more precise than the economists give them credit for.

The number that got the headline

Start with the case for good news, because it is real. As Colby Smith reported for The New York Times, the revised figures showed the economy growing faster from July through September than the government had first estimated. Consumer spending has held up despite rising costs. Unemployment stands at 4.1 per cent. By the definition economists use, two consecutive quarters of shrinking real output, the country is far from a recession.

Smith also identified one of the main engines of that spending: the sharp rise in household wealth from stock market gains. When portfolios swell, the people who own them spend more, and their spending shows up in the national accounts as growth.

That detail is the hinge of this whole instalment. Growth driven by wealth is growth that flows first to the people who hold the wealth.

The number households read

The inflation figures told the other story. At 3.8 per cent, headline inflation is nearly double the Fed’s 2 per cent target, and it is moving the wrong way. Core prices, which strip out volatile food and energy, rose 3.3 per cent over the year, the fastest pace since 2023. Measured over the past three months and annualised, core prices are rising at 3.6 per cent, which suggests the trend is steepening rather than levelling off.

There was one modestly better figure. On a monthly basis, prices rose 0.3 per cent in October, a little slower than in September. Smith credited that to falling energy and commodity prices in recent weeks, and to the fading of the first round of President Trump’s tariffs. But the Fed’s main worry, he reported, is that price increases are spreading across a widening range of goods and services. Inflation that is concentrated in gasoline can fall when gasoline does. Inflation that is everywhere takes longer to dislodge.

The Fed has now missed its 2 per cent target for nearly six years. A child who started kindergarten the last time prices were rising at the target rate is now in Grade 6. For a household, six years of above-target inflation is not a statistic. It is the permanent gap between what things cost in 2020 and what they cost now, a gap Part X, “The Steak You Remember,” measured at the meat counter.

The word they reach for

As Audra D. S. Burch reported for The New York Times the same day, about 61 per cent of Americans, across political parties, believe the United States is in a recession, according to a Harris Poll conducted for the Guardian in October.

It would be easy to call that a misunderstanding. Burch’s reporting suggests something different. Consider Austin George, 41, of Fairfield, California. His sense that something was wrong began when his girlfriend, Jessica Marmolejo, spent months looking for work this spring and finally took a pay cut, leaving a dentist’s office for a lighting warehouse. The couple postponed buying a house and kept their down payment as a cushion against prices. For George, the relevant number on November 25 was not 4.3 per cent. It was 3.8 per cent. “The warning lights are blinking,” he said.

Don Crafton, 56, a school-district maintenance worker in Belle Vernon, Pennsylvania, is a former Democrat who voted for Trump twice. He and his girlfriend take home about $3,500 a month between them. It covers what they need and nothing more. Whether economists call it a recession, he said, is beside the point: “We live paycheck to paycheck.”

Erin Hatton, a sociologist at the State University of New York at Buffalo who studies the labour market, offered the most useful reading of the word. A family that is struggling, she told Burch, might assume its trouble is personal. Calling it a recession is a way of saying it is not just them: the problem is widespread and structural.

That is a fair description of what this series has documented for fourteen parts. “Recession” is the only word in everyday English for “the economy is failing people like me.” The technical definition measures output. The everyday definition measures whether a paycheque still covers a month. Those are different things, and in 2026 they have come apart.

This is not the first time. Americans described a “vibecession” in 2022 and 2023, under President Biden, when growth was solid and inflation was the dominant fact of household life. The White House at the time made a version of the argument this White House makes now: look at the jobs, look at the growth. Voters were not persuaded then, either.

Two economies, one bill

Burch put the divergence plainly: the economy’s strength is not evenly distributed. Many high-income households are thriving on a rising stock market. Lower-income households are treading water or falling behind.

The data released on November 25 showed the seam. Consumer spending stalled in October once it was adjusted for inflation. Nominal spending rose, but the extra dollars bought roughly the same goods and services as before. That is what it looks like when households run to stand still.

The Pew Research Center recently found that almost half of Americans struggle to pay for at least one basic need. Among lower-income adults, nearly three-quarters reported difficulty covering transportation, housing, groceries or health care. No GDP report can offset that, because GDP adds up spending. It does not ask who is doing it, or whether they could afford to.

The stock market that props up the top of this economy has been unsteady too. Smith noted that equity markets have swung over concerns about whether the artificial intelligence investment boom, which has done so much to sustain growth, can last. Nvidia, the chip-maker at the centre of that boom, fell sharply in mid-November, then rallied on Monday, November 23, after Jensen Huang was reinstated as chief executive following the president’s intervention. Huang had resigned less than two weeks earlier after admitting to an affair with a woman U.S. officials had identified as a Chinese intelligence asset. George read the episode as confirmation of what he already believed. “It all points to an economy working for the rich and selling out the average American,” he said.

One does not have to share his politics to see the arithmetic. If a large share of consumer demand depends on stock-market wealth, and the stock market depends on the fortunes of a handful of A.I. companies and the personal drama of their chief executives, then the “strong economy” rests on a narrower foundation than the headline suggests. The households without portfolios have no stake in the rally and no cushion if it ends.

The budget mind-set

Belief in a recession changes behaviour, whatever the GDP figures say. Burch spoke to Spencer Smith, 39, a company manager for a Broadway show in New York. Watching the effects of tariffs and the war with Iran, he began preparing for what he called a “worst-case” scenario. He has cut his monthly costs and built savings to cover about six months of expenses. He postponed a non-emergency foot operation that could have meant paying for physical therapy out of his own pocket. He cancelled a January trip to Mexico City. He switched from Uber to the subway and cut back on restaurant meals that can easily top $100.

“I am now in a budget mind-set,” he said.

Megan Irving, 51, a psychotherapist on the Oregon coast, sees the same thing from the grocery aisle: shoppers with calculators and coupons, and mothers telling their children to put things back. “It walks and talks like a recession,” she said.

Multiply Spencer Smith’s choices across millions of households and you have one of the ways a recession can begin. Not every downturn starts with a shock. Some start when enough people, convinced that one is coming, stop spending at the margin. That is not yet what the data shows. Spending held up through the summer, and October’s stall is one month. But a population that has spent a year cutting back is a population with less room to absorb the next blow.

The rate that comes next

For households carrying debt, the November 25 inflation figures settle one question. Smith reported that the data supports the view of most Fed officials that borrowing costs must keep rising. The Fed raised rates in September, its first increase in years, and held them in October. It is widely expected to raise them again at its final meeting of the year, on December 8 and 9, to a range of 4 to 4.25 per cent.

The pace after that is in dispute. Many officials have warmed to raising rates at consecutive meetings, which would put another increase on the table at the end of January. The Fed meets again in March. The deciding factor, Smith reported, is whether inflation improves or keeps accelerating, driven by the war with Iran, which shows no sign of ending, and by the surge in A.I. spending.

Part VI of this series, “The Cost of Borrowing to Get By,” followed families using credit cards and personal loans to bridge the gap between their pay and their bills. Each rate increase raises the cost of that bridge. The households most likely to call this a recession are, for the most part, the households most exposed to the Fed’s cure for it.

The tariff that comes next

On the same morning the inflation figures showed the first round of tariffs fading from prices, the White House proposed a new set of levies on imports from dozens of countries, including China, Japan and members of the European Union.

This series has used a simple framework to track the administration’s economic messaging: Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate. November 25 delivered the first step again, on cue. The relief Smith described, slightly slower monthly inflation as the old tariffs worked their way through, was announced in the same news cycle as the tariffs designed to replace them. For a household, the practical meaning is that the pause in tariff-driven price increases may be brief. The receipt Part III described, “The Receipt at the End of the Trade War,” may have a second page.

What January can and cannot do

Many of the voters Burch interviewed registered their frustration earlier this month, on November 3, when Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress. When the new Congress is sworn in in January, many of them expect it to move quickly on prices: ending the Iran war, reversing the tariffs, and undoing much of the president’s signature domestic policy law, including its cuts to Medicaid and food aid and its tax cuts for the rich. Spencer Smith hopes Democrats will also restore the expired health insurance subsidies under the Affordable Care Act. He has no patience for an intraparty argument about who is most right. “It’s time to get some stuff done and try to help people who are suffering,” he said.

That is a fair demand and a difficult one. The levers that matter most to a household’s monthly budget are mostly out of Congress’s direct reach. The Fed sets interest rates. The war’s effect on energy prices depends on the war. And the president still holds the veto. What a Democratic Congress can do quickly is hold hearings, refuse to fund policies it opposes, and pass bills that put the president on the record. Whether that adds up to relief a household can feel by next summer is the open question of 2027.

The verdict, renewed

The United States is not in a recession by any definition an economist would accept. The economy grew at its fastest pace in three years. Unemployment is low. Consumer spending held up through the summer.

And yet 61 per cent of Americans say otherwise, and they are not wrong about what they are describing. They are describing prices rising faster this autumn than last summer, borrowing costs about to rise again, spending power that stalled in October, and a growth rate powered by a stock market most of them do not meaningfully own. They are using the only word available for an economy that grows while they do not.

On Thursday, November 26, American families will sit down to Thanksgiving dinner. The turkey, the trimmings and the gas to drive to the table will cost more than they did a year ago. Two weeks later, the Fed will meet to make borrowing more expensive. Nobody at the table will need the Commerce Department to tell them how the year went. The ledger stays open.
