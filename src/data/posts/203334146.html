Stephen Gordon

On June 12, 2026, the CRTC did the thing I have been promising you, post after post, that it would eventually do.

It opened the consultation.

Broadcasting and Telecom Notice of Consultation CRTC 2026-134 — Harmonizing the consumer protection codes — is now live. The four existing codes (Wireless, Internet, Television, and the Deposit and Disconnection Code) are to be folded into a single unified Consumer Protection Code. Comments are due by August 11, 2026. A public hearing begins November 30, 2026, in Gatineau.

For those of you who have followed this series from the beginning — from the $4.10 I owed Fido, through the $42.38 they billed me, through the $1.22 late fee they charged me for declining to pay the $42.38 I didn’t owe, through two CRTC letters of magnificent non-engagement, through a customer satisfaction survey from an organization that exists to measure whether it has successfully stopped people from being bothered — this is the moment we have been waiting for.

I want to report that the moment is everything I hoped.

I cannot.

I read the whole thing

I did what I suspect very few Canadians will do, which is read the entire notice. All of it. The introduction, the preliminary views, the procedural instructions, the legal framework in Appendix 3, the 24 questions in Appendix 2, and — this is the important part — the full working draft of the proposed unified code in Appendix 1.

A working document, the Commission helpfully explains, is “intended to provide a possible model for the harmonized Code and to stimulate discussion and debate.” It is, in other words, the CRTC showing its hand. This is the version of the rules they are starting from. This is what they think the consumer protection regime should look like, laid out for everyone to comment on.

So I went looking for advance billing.

I found it.

I found it exactly where I told you I would find it, doing exactly what I told you it would be doing.

Provision G.6(ii)

Here is the relevant passage from the Commission’s own working document, in the section on contract cancellation:

“All service providers must provide refunds for communications services following cancellation of such services when some or all of the monthly service fees are billed in advance. The refunds must be pro-rated, based on the number of days left in the last monthly billing cycle after cancellation.”

Read that carefully, because it is the entire argument of this series compressed into two sentences.

The Commission requires a refund when fees are billed in advance. It does not ask whether fees should be billed in advance. It takes advance billing as a fact of nature — like weather, or gravity, or the heat death of the universe — and busies itself with the cleanup.

The phrase “billed in advance” appears in the working document the way a stage direction appears in a play: as scenery, as the given conditions, as the thing that is simply true before the action starts. Nowhere in 24 questions does the Commission ask the one question that would have made this proceeding a genuine first-principles review:

Should telecommunications carriers be permitted to bill consumers for service before providing it?

The definitions section, for what it’s worth, does the same thing twice more. “Postpaid” services are defined as those billed after use. “Prepaid” as those purchased in advance. Advance billing is woven so thoroughly into the architecture that the Commission no longer sees it as a choice. It sees it as a wall.

This is not an oversight. It is the pattern.

Regular readers will recognize what is happening here, because it is the same thing that has happened at every previous stage.

The CCTS told me the systemic question — should advance billing exist — was outside its mandate, and sent me to the CRTC.

The CRTC told me advance billing was a “business decision” outside its usual practice, and sent me to the CCTS. Then, when I pushed, it told me to wait for the formal consultation.

The formal consultation has now opened. And the formal consultation carries advance billing forward, unexamined, into the new unified code — the same way the Wireless Code did in 2013, the same way the Internet Code did in 2019, the same way Telecom Decision 2016-171 did when it mandated prorated refunds without ever asking whether the underlying practice deserved to survive.

Four chances to ask the question. Four times the question went unasked. And now a fifth document — the one explicitly designed to rethink consumer protection from the ground up — inherits the same blind spot.

I take a certain grim satisfaction in this. Not pleasure, exactly. The satisfaction of a man who predicted the weather and then got rained on.

A small word about why this practice exists

It is worth remembering that advance billing was not handed down on stone tablets. Somebody decided it.

The clearest articulation I have found of why comes, oddly, from an American regulator. The Virginia State Corporation Commission explains on its consumer pages that it allowed advance billing, some years ago, “to ensure that utility companies had the required amount of cash for working capital,” and that “without it, local rates would have been higher.” It is a rare thing — a regulator saying the quiet part out loud. The justification for making you pay early was never about protecting you. It was about the carrier’s cash position.

And here is the part that ought to give the Commission pause. In Canada, advance billing is not some modern wrinkle of the cellphone age. It is older than the company. The very first telephone arrangement in this country — the 1877 line connecting Prime Minister Alexander Mackenzie’s office to the Governor General at Rideau Hall — was leased at $42.50 a year, payable in advance.

In 1877, that made sense. A fragile new enterprise was stringing copper across an enormous and mostly empty country, with no way to meter usage and no capital markets to lean on. Collecting the year up front was how you financed a miracle.

It is no longer 1877. The carriers billing you thirty days early today are among the largest corporations in Canada. They have treasury departments. They have real-time billing systems that can prorate to the day — I know they can, because they prorate against you the moment you owe them money. They have access to capital at rates the rest of us can only dream about. Every condition that once justified advance billing has dissolved. The practice has simply outlived its reasons, the way a callus outlives the blister.

Meanwhile your electricity utility — with vastly larger infrastructure than any wireless carrier — manages, somehow, to bill you for power after you have used it. As do water and gas. The entire essential-utility sector figured out arrears billing generations ago. Telecom is the outlier, and the only thing holding the outlier in place is that everybody does it and nobody has asked why.

What this means for you, and why August 11 matters

Here is the good news buried in the bureaucracy.

The consultation is genuinely open, and genuinely public, and you are genuinely allowed to participate. Not as a spectator — as a party. When you file an intervention, you become part of the official record. The Commission is obligated to consider it. And only those who file an intervention earn the right to take part in the later stages, including the November hearing.

The deadline is August 11, 2026, at 8 p.m. Eastern. After that, the door closes.

You do not need to be a lawyer. You do not need to cite paragraph numbers or quote the Telecommunications Act. You need to do three things:

  1. Tell them what happened to you. A time advance billing cost you — money overpaid, a refund that took six weeks, a late fee on an amount you never owed, a customer service line that vanished the moment you cancelled.

  2. State the ask. That the unified code should either prohibit advance billing outright (bill in arrears, like every other utility), or, failing that, require real-time balance updates on cancellation and late fees calculated only on what you actually owe.

  3. Ask the question they didn’t. Put it on the record, in your own words: should carriers be allowed to bill in advance at all? Because the only way a question gets examined is if enough people refuse to let it stay invisible.

You can file through the intervention form on the CRTC’s site, by mail to CRTC, Gatineau, Quebec K1A 0N2, or by fax, if you are the kind of magnificent holdout who still owns a fax machine. I will post a plain-language, step-by-step guide to filing in the next instalment of this series, before the deadline, so no one is left squinting at a government form at 7:55 p.m. on August 11.

If it helps to see what one looks like, my own intervention is posted here — the full thing, with the regulatory analysis, the historical background, and the sources. Borrow from it freely. Adapt the arguments, lift the research, ignore the parts that don't fit your situation. That is what it's there for. The Commission is far more likely to examine a question that arrives in a hundred different voices than one that arrives in mine alone.

The question, one more time

I have now spent the better part of a year on a $1.22 late fee. I am aware of how that sounds. Dior is aware of how that sounds; she has communicated her assessment through the medium of profound, unbroken sleep.

But the $1.22 was never the point. The point is that twenty-three thousand Canadians filed complaints with the CCTS last year, and nearly half were about billing, and the single largest category was “incorrect charges and missing account credits” — which is to say, the precise wreckage that advance billing produces when a cancellation meets a system that won’t update in real time. The point is that every one of those disputes costs the carrier money to process, which means the practice isn’t even good business — just habit dressed up as necessity.

The point is that a regulator has finally opened the one proceeding designed to rethink all of this from scratch, and has managed, on page after page, to rethink everything except the assumption sitting at the centre of it.

There’s also some movement on the media side that I’m not ready to detail yet — more on that soon. The argument is starting to travel beyond this Substack, which is exactly what it needs to do.

The table has been set. The consultation is real. The hearing is booked.

They have simply, once again, forgotten to set a place for the question.

So we will bring our own chair.
