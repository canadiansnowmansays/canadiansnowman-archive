Stephen Gordon, July 15, 2026

There is a particular kind of blindness that only the powerful can afford, and only the comfortable can fail to notice. I have spent a great deal of ink in this space cataloguing what happens when America’s executive branch decides that certain citizens matter less than others. This time the subject is different. This time it’s the institution that is supposed to catch the executive when it does that — and the strange, patterned way it keeps declining to.

I’m talking about the Supreme Court of the United States, and a doctrine it has never named but has practiced with remarkable consistency: if a president’s racial animus can be paired with any other plausible motive, however thin, the animus stops counting. National security. Partisan advantage. Statutory interpretation. Pick your fig leaf — the Court will treat it as though it were the whole tree.

As The Atlantic laid out this week, and as this year’s docket has since confirmed in increasingly stark terms, the throughline runs from 1944 to last month, and once you see it, you can’t unsee it.

The Original Sin

It starts, as so much American constitutional rot does, with Korematsu v. United States — the 1944 ruling that blessed the internment of tens of thousands of Japanese Americans. Justice Hugo Black, writing for the majority, insisted the case had nothing to do with race and everything to do with “military necessity.” A colleague, Justice Frank Murphy, wasn’t fooled; he called the policy what it was, a descent into naked racism.

Here is the useful thing about Korematsu: nearly everyone now agrees it was wrong. Chief Justice John Roberts himself said so in 2018, calling it “gravely wrong the day it was decided.” He wrote that sentence in the majority opinion of Trump v. Hawaii, decided June 26, 2018 — the case that upheld the first Trump administration’s travel ban on several majority-Muslim countries. Roberts disavowed the doctrine and then, in the same breath, applied a version of its logic anyway: because the ban was facially neutral, nationality-based rather than explicitly religious, and tied to an independent national-security rationale, the majority extended the executive the deference it traditionally gets on immigration and foreign affairs, treating the president’s public statements about wanting to keep Muslims out as background noise rather than controlling evidence. I’ll grant this one is genuinely contestable — courts have long given the executive wide latitude in this exact domain, and reasonable lawyers can defend that deference on its own terms, independent of what anyone thinks of Trump personally. But Justice Sotomayor’s dissent still lands a fair hit: she noted the odd position of a Court invoking Korematsu as its cautionary tale in one paragraph while, in the next, accepting a rationale structurally similar to the one Korematsu itself relied on — a facially neutral security justification standing in front of an open record of animus.

That was 2018. I’d like to tell you the Court learned something in the years since. It has not. It has refined the technique.

Three Rulings, One Season

This year gave us an escalating sequence, close enough together to make the pattern impossible to miss.

On April 29, 2026, in Louisiana v. Callais, a 6–3 Court struck down Louisiana’s creation of a second Black-majority congressional district — a district drawn only because a federal court had found the state’s map likely violated the Voting Rights Act in the first place. The majority’s reasoning treated the state’s compliance with federal voting-rights law as itself suspect, on the theory that any deliberate creation of a majority-Black district is presumptively unconstitutional race-consciousness — even when a state is trying to fix a proven discrimination problem.

It did not take long for that ruling to metastasize. On June 2, 2026, in an unsigned, unexplained shadow-docket order, the same six justices let Alabama revert to a congressional map that two separate courts — including a panel with two Trump appointees on it — had already found to be intentionally discriminatory. The order arrived after nine at night, with a special election looming, and for the first time in the Court’s history invoked the word “colorblind” as if it were settled constitutional text rather than a slogan lifted out of context from a 19th-century dissent. Justice Sotomayor’s dissent did not mince words: she accused the majority of rewarding Alabama’s “gamesmanship and outright defiance of court orders.” Legal commentators noted something stranger still — the order retroactively rewrote what Callais had meant just five weeks after the Court decided it, expanding a voting-rights ruling into a shadow-docket order eliminating a sitting Black congressman’s district with no briefing, no oral argument, and no signed opinion anyone could be held to.

Then came the tell — though it deserves to be handled carefully, because the comparison is doctrinally messier than it first appears. Callais and the Alabama order are Fourteenth Amendment cases, governed by strict scrutiny of state racial classifications in law. Immigration stops live under a different body of doctrine entirely — Fourth Amendment “reasonable suspicion,” which has permitted race or ethnicity as one factor among several since United States v. Brignoni-Ponce in 1975, decades before any of this term’s rulings. A defender of the Court can fairly say these aren’t the same test applied two different ways; they’re two different tests, built for two different constitutional questions, and the outcomes don’t have to match just because both involve race.

That’s a real distinction, and it’s worth stating plainly rather than papering over. Here is my argument for why it doesn’t fully dissolve the discomfort, and I want to flag it as argument rather than pretend it’s simply an unveiling of fact: the doctrinal separateness the Court leans on is precedentially real, but it’s also a framing choice the Court itself has the power to revisit, not a fixed law of nature. My claim is that the Court could have treated “the government is using race to sort people” as the operative fact common to both contexts, reasoning across the Fourteenth and Fourth Amendment lines rather than letting each operate in its own sealed room — and that choosing not to is itself worth scrutiny, even though the current doctrinal split has a real precedential pedigree stretching back to Brignoni-Ponce. A defender of the Court is entitled to disagree with that framing choice; I’m naming it as my view, not as the only honest reading of the record. In September 2025’s Noem v. Vasquez Perdomo, Justice Kavanaugh wrote separately to bless ICE’s use of “apparent ethnicity” as a “relevant factor” — alongside things like speaking Spanish or working day-labor jobs — in deciding whom to stop and question about immigration status. Lawyers and commentators started calling these “Kavanaugh stops.” Justice Sotomayor’s dissent in that case observed that the government’s position, now endorsed by a concurrence, came close to declaring that all Latinos — citizens included — were fair game. Kavanaugh tried, months later, to walk the position back in an unrelated case, insisting immigration stops “must not” be based on race or ethnicity — without ever acknowledging the contradiction, or explaining which version of his own rule actually governs.

Sit with that pairing, doctrinal labels and all. Creating a district to remedy proven discrimination against Black voters: presumptively unconstitutional race-consciousness, decided with urgency and a new constitutional slogan. Using “apparent ethnicity” as one factor in stopping Latino day laborers at a car wash: a permissible exercise of law-enforcement judgment, decided on the emergency docket with a concurrence Kavanaugh himself later seemed to regret. Different bodies of law, sure. But the same six-justice bloc chose, across both, which direction gets the benefit of the doubt and which doesn’t — and that choice is not compelled by the doctrinal separation itself. Call it a one-way ratchet if you like the phrase, or call it something more modest: a Court that reaches for skepticism when race-consciousness helps a historically excluded group, and reaches for deference when it doesn’t. Either way, it’s a pattern, not a coincidence of unrelated bodies of law landing in the same place by chance.

The Tell, Continued

The asymmetry runs deeper than immigration versus voting rights. The Court is entirely capable of taking a public official’s ugly words seriously as legal evidence — when it wants to.

In 2018’s Masterpiece Cakeshop decision, the same Court cited a Colorado civil-rights commissioner’s offhand remark about religion as proof of unconstitutional hostility toward a Christian baker. It’s fair to note the comparison isn’t perfectly clean: the commissioner’s remark came from inside the adjudicative proceeding against the baker — a state actor’s bias, caught on the record, while deciding that specific case. Campaign-trail statements offered later as evidence of a policy’s motive sit in a different evidentiary category, and a defender of the Court can reasonably say those shouldn’t carry identical weight. Fair enough. But the underlying question survives the technical distinction: the Court has, in other contexts, found a single remark from a single official sufficient to establish disqualifying animus. It has not extended that same willingness to look for animus to a president’s years-long, repeated, on-the-record record of racial invective against the very groups his policies subsequently targeted. Whatever the formal category of evidence, the threshold for “this looks like bias” moves depending on who’s in the dock. Nor is this only an outsider’s complaint. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson, dissenting in 2023’s Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard — the case that ended race-conscious college admissions and gave Justice Thomas the platform to first argue for a constitutionally colorblind reading — accused the majority of a kind of willful obliviousness to the practical stakes for the students they were ruling against. It is worth remembering, too, that Chief Justice Roberts did not arrive at this position recently; associates and historians of his career have traced his colorblindness advocacy back to his years as a young lawyer in the Reagan Justice Department, decades before he had a robe to write it into law. This isn’t drift. It’s a project that finally found its majority.

There is even a case that seems, on its face, to cut against the argument — and is worth addressing rather than skipping past. In United States v. Donte Carter, decided June 22, 2026, the Court declined to review a D.C. appeals court ruling that had factored a Black defendant’s race into whether he’d been unlawfully seized by police, resulting in his conviction being vacated. Only Alito and Thomas dissented, warning that letting race inform that analysis was itself unconstitutional. On its surface, this looks like the Court simply letting race-consciousness stand. But look closer: it was a bare denial of certiorari, not a ruling on the merits, binding nowhere outside the D.C. circuit, and the majority explained nothing. Contrast that silence with the eagerness on display in Callais and the Alabama order — full-throated majority opinions, invented doctrine, urgent late-night timelines. When race-consciousness helps a Black defendant, the Court can’t be bothered to weigh in. When it helps a Black voter, the Court moves at the speed of an emergency docket.

So Why?

This is the question worth sitting with, and I don’t think there’s one tidy answer. It’s also worth naming the strongest version of the other side before picking through the theories: a defender of the Court would say none of this requires bad faith at all. The Fourteenth Amendment’s core commitment, on this reading, is to individual equality under law, not group-level balancing — and a Court skeptical of race-conscious remedies is applying a coherent, decades-old philosophy (traceable to Harlan’s own colorblindness language in his Plessy dissent), not laundering animus. Immigration law and voting law are different doctrinal houses with different histories, so treating them differently isn’t inconsistency, it’s just doing two different jobs correctly. That’s a genuine position, held by serious people, and I don’t think it can be waved away as obviously cynical.

I don’t fully buy it, for the reasons above — the doctrinal separateness the defense leans on is itself a choice the Court keeps making in one direction, and a philosophy that happens to demand skepticism only of remedies for the historically excluded, while extending deference to enforcement burdening a different set of racial minorities, is a philosophy worth interrogating rather than simply accepting as neutral. But I’d rather name the strongest disagreement than pretend it doesn’t exist. With that said, here are three ways to explain the pattern that don’t require assuming everyone on the bench is lying about their reasoning.

One theory is capture, not deference. For forty years the conservative legal movement has run a farm system — law schools, clerkships, the Federalist Society pipeline — built to produce not neutral umpires but jurists who arrive already sharing the premise that “colorblindness” means treating any remedy for racial harm as itself the harm. If that’s right, the Court isn’t reluctantly finding cover for the administration. It agrees with the administration’s priors and is using procedure to launder that agreement into something that reads as law.

A second theory is institutional self-preservation. A Court that has already tested the limits of its authority against an administration willing to ignore inconvenient rulings may be choosing its battles — finding technical off-ramps rather than triggering a constitutional confrontation it isn’t certain it would win. Under this theory the animus is seen, just not acted on, because acting on it would cost the Court something it isn’t willing to spend.

I’d stop at those two as the load-bearing explanations, because they’re the ones I can point to structural evidence for — a documented pipeline in one case, the Court’s own recent institutional history in the other. There’s a third possibility worth naming honestly for what it is: that the justices simply identify more easily with some litigants than others, and that instinct shapes which evidentiary rules feel natural to apply where. I find it plausible, but I want to be upfront that it’s the weakest of the three on the evidence — it’s closer to speculation about interior motivation than an argument I can back with something outside the outcomes themselves, and I’d rather flag that limitation than dress it up as equally rigorous.

I don’t think these explanations are mutually exclusive, and I’d weight them unevenly: a court captured by a particular judicial philosophy and protecting its own institutional position will produce most of the pattern described above on its own. Whether individual sympathy adds anything on top of that, I can’t prove — only notice.

The View From Up Here

I write from a country that has its own history of failing minorities and telling itself flattering stories about it, so I’ll resist the temptation to sound superior. But there is something particularly striking about watching a judiciary — the branch built, in theory, to be insulated from political incentive by lifetime tenure — turn out to need no incentive at all. It simply already believes what the administration is telling it, or has decided the belief isn’t its problem to adjudicate.

Fred Korematsu spent decades trying to clear his name. He succeeded, eventually, in a courtroom that had the decency to admit the government lied to win the first time. The current Court has the transcript of that admission sitting in its own case law. It cites it approvingly. And then it does the thing anyway.

That’s not ignorance. Regular readers of The American Contradiction will recognize the shape of it — it has been confirmed again.
