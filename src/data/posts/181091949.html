Every debate about American healthcare reform eventually hits the same wall: “But America is different.”

Different size. Different diversity. Different culture. Different Constitution. Different relationship between citizen and state.

All true. And all irrelevant.

Because the countries that solved healthcare aren’t identical to each other either. Switzerland uses competing private insurance companies. Canada largely bars private insurance for core services. Germany combines public and private options. Singapore mandates personal savings accounts. The UK runs a socialized system where most hospitals are publicly owned and hospital doctors are public employees.

They use radically different approaches. But they all achieve three things America doesn’t:

Universal coverage: Everyone gets care

Lower costs: Between roughly half and two-thirds of American per-capita spending

Better outcomes: Longer life expectancy, lower infant mortality, fewer preventable deaths

This is Part 4 of Code Blue, our investigation into America’s 80-year healthcare failure. We’ve documented the broken promises (Parts 1-2) and followed the money (Part 3). Now we examine what actually works — not as theoretical models, but as proven systems delivering care to hundreds of millions of people across wildly different countries and cultures.

(Spending figures below are the OECD’s 2024 per-person figures in U.S. dollars adjusted for purchasing power, from Health at a Glance 2025, so every country is compared in the same year. Life expectancy figures are World Bank 2023.)

The solutions exist. Let’s see how they did it.

Switzerland: Capitalism with a Conscience

If you want to argue America can’t have universal healthcare because “free markets” and “individual choice,” Switzerland destroys your case.

The Swiss Model:

Mandatory private insurance (no government insurance option)

Competing insurance companies (several dozen)

Individual mandate (everyone must buy insurance)

Premium subsidies for lower-income families

Strict price controls and coverage requirements

No denial for pre-existing conditions

No profit on basic coverage (insurers can profit on supplemental plans)

Sound familiar? It should. The Affordable Care Act closely resembles Switzerland’s system — except Switzerland has teeth where the ACA has loopholes.

Results:

Coverage: essentially universal

Per capita spending: $9,963 (vs. $14,885 in the US)

Life expectancy: 84.2 years (vs. 78.4 in the US)

Out-of-pocket costs: Adults choose an annual deductible from CHF 300 to CHF 2,500, then pay 10% of further costs up to a cap of CHF 700 a year

Quality: Consistently rated among the best in international comparisons

How it works: Every Swiss resident must purchase health insurance from a private company. The government defines a standard package that all insurers must offer — covering doctor visits, hospital stays, prescription drugs, and preventive care.

Insurers compete, but not on coverage (which is standardized) or by denying pre-existing conditions (which is illegal). They compete on premium prices, customer service, and supplemental coverage options.

Low-income families receive government subsidies to afford premiums. Tariffs for medical services and drug prices are negotiated and regulated. Hospitals are a mix of public and private, with the cantons owning or funding many of them.

The trade-off: Switzerland has high out-of-pocket costs and expensive premiums (an average of roughly CHF 379 a month in 2025). But the deductible and co-insurance caps limit what any one illness can cost. No one is denied basic coverage. And total costs are still about two-thirds of American spending.

What America could learn: You can have private insurance, market competition, and individual choice while still achieving universal coverage — IF you regulate prices, mandate coverage, subsidize affordability, and prohibit denials for pre-existing conditions.

The ACA took half-measures toward this model. Switzerland proves the full model works.

Germany: The Public-Private Partnership

Germany introduced the world’s first national health insurance law in 1883 under Otto von Bismarck. 142 years later, it still works.

The German Model:

Statutory health insurance covers about 88% of the population

Private insurance option for high earners, civil servants and the self-employed (about 11%)

Employer and employee split contributions 50/50

Contributions based on income, up to an income ceiling

Choice of nearly 100 non-profit “sickness funds” (insurance providers)

Government-regulated fee schedules and drug price negotiation

Coverage compulsory for the whole population since 2009

Results:

Coverage: essentially 100%

Per capita spending: $9,365

Life expectancy: 81.0 years

Wait times: Generally short by international standards

Quality: Excellent infrastructure, high patient satisfaction

How it works: Most Germans are covered by statutory health insurance — non-profit funds that operate more like utilities than insurance companies. Contributions are set as a percentage of income (a general rate of 14.6%, split between employer and employee, plus a supplementary rate that varies by fund).

Employees earning above a threshold (€77,400 a year in 2026) can opt out and buy private insurance, which offers faster access and fancier hospital rooms but costs more.

Fee schedules for doctors and hospitals are negotiated within a government framework, and new drugs’ prices are negotiated after launch. A hip replacement costs what it costs — hospitals can’t charge wildly different prices to different patients.

Most doctors treat both statutory and private patients, and the large majority of their patients are in the statutory system.

The trade-off: Private insurance patients sometimes get faster access to specialists. Public system patients can wait weeks for non-urgent specialist appointments. But urgent care is immediate, and no one is denied treatment.

What America could learn: You don’t have to eliminate private insurance to achieve universal coverage. Germany proves you can have a strong public system alongside private insurance — IF you regulate prices and make coverage compulsory. Statutory insurance is mandatory for most employees; the choice Germans get is which sickness fund to join, not whether to be insured.

This is close to the “public option” model many Democrats proposed. Germany’s version dates back to 1883.

Canada: Single-Payer, Locally Run

Full disclosure: I’m Canadian. I’ve lived with this system for most of my life. So let me give you the unvarnished truth — the good and the bad.

The Canadian Model:

Single-payer insurance run by provinces (not federal government)

Private insurance largely barred for services covered by the public system

Funded by taxes (no premiums, no deductibles, no co-pays for covered services)

Hospitals mostly private non-profits

Doctors private practitioners (mostly paid fee-for-service by government)

Core coverage: hospital care, physician services, diagnostic tests

Supplementary coverage (dental, vision, prescription drugs) varies by province, age, and income level

Results:

Coverage: 100% for core medical services

Per capita spending: $7,301

Life expectancy: 81.6 years

Wait times: Short for emergencies; long, and in many cases much too long, for specialists, scans and elective surgery

Satisfaction: High for accessibility, deep frustration with wait times

How it works: Each province runs its own insurance plan, funded primarily through taxes. You visit any doctor or hospital, show your provincial health card, and receive care. No bills. No claims. No denials.

Doctors are private practitioners who bill the provincial government for services rendered. Fee schedules are negotiated between provincial governments and medical associations. A knee replacement in Ontario costs the same whether you’re rich or poor, old or young.

Beyond the basics: The universal system covers hospital and physician care. For dental, vision, and prescription drugs, coverage is more complex:

Seniors (like myself at 74) receive enhanced coverage through programs like Ontario Drug Benefit — covering most prescriptions, with an annual deductible of around $100 for higher-income seniors. In Ontario, people 65 and over get one major eye exam every 18 months through OHIP. The new Canadian Dental Care Plan, rolled out from 2024 starting with seniors, now covers dental services for eligible Canadians without dental insurance and with family incomes under $90,000.

Low-income residents qualify for provincial drug benefit programs, dental coverage, and vision care regardless of age.

Working-age Canadians with middle to higher incomes typically rely on employer-provided insurance or purchase private plans for dental, vision, and prescription drugs.

This creates a tiered system for supplementary services: comprehensive coverage for those who need it most (seniors, low-income), employer or private insurance for the working population. It’s not perfect, but it means the most vulnerable populations don’t go without care.

The reality: Canadian healthcare works well for emergencies, acute care and chronic disease management. Heart attack? You’re in an ambulance and treated urgently, and you’ll never see a bill. Cancer diagnosis? Once you’ve seen the oncologist, treatment typically starts within about a month.

But almost everything that isn’t urgent involves a wait — and the waits are long. The Fraser Institute’s annual physician survey put the 2025 national medians at:

15.3 weeks from a GP’s referral to seeing a specialist

13.3 more weeks from the specialist to treatment

28.6 weeks in total, referral to treatment — more than six months

18.1 weeks for an MRI, 8.8 weeks for a CT scan

48.6 weeks — nearly a year — from referral to orthopaedic surgery such as a hip or knee replacement

4.7 weeks for medical oncology and 4.2 weeks for radiation oncology after the specialist visit

(Mackenzie Moir and Nadeem Esmail, “Waiting Your Turn: Wait Times for Health Care in Canada, 2025 Report”, Fraser Institute, December 9, 2025.)

Waits vary a great deal by province. Ontario’s 19.2-week total was the shortest in the country; other provinces are far worse. The Fraser Institute is a market-oriented think tank and its figures come from a survey of physicians, but provincial wait-time data tell the same basic story. These are not trivial inconveniences. People wait in pain, some lose the ability to work while they wait, and some conditions worsen.

Let’s be brutally honest about what these trade-offs actually mean:

The trade-off visualized:

Americans love pointing to Canadian wait times while ignoring that:

The “faster” American access only applies if you’re insured AND can afford your deductible AND the procedure is in-network AND you don’t get surprise-billed

About 27 million Americans had no health insurance in 2024 (8% of the population, Census Bureau, “Health Insurance Coverage in the United States: 2024,” September 9, 2025). For many of them the wait never ends — they never get the procedure at all

Americans with insurance but high deductibles often delay or skip necessary care — that’s also a wait time, just measured differently

Canadian wait times are real, frustrating and, for many procedures, unacceptably long. But they’re triaged by medical necessity, not ability to pay. Heart attack? Immediate care. Cancer? Treatment typically starts within weeks. Hip replacement that can wait? You wait — often close to a year.

Meanwhile, millions of Americans report skipping or rationing prescribed medication because of cost. That’s not “wait times” — that’s denial of care.

The choice isn’t between “instant American access” and “long Canadian waits.” The choice is between:

Canadian system: Everyone gets care, prioritized by medical need, with no bills for covered care — but often after long waits

American system: Fast access if you can pay, delayed care if you’re underinsured, no care if you’re uninsured, bankruptcy if you’re unlucky

When Americans criticize Canadian wait times, they’re comparing the best-case American experience (fully insured, low deductible, in-network, no surprise bills) against the worst-case Canadian experience (non-urgent elective procedure).

Compare apples to apples: the average American experience against the average Canadian experience. The trade-off looks very different. That doesn’t excuse Canada’s waits — Canadians are right to be angry about them, and provinces need more capacity. But the cure for long waits is more surgeons, scanners and operating rooms, not leaving 27 million people uninsured.

What America could learn: Single-payer works. For more than 40 million people spread across the second-largest country on earth, including remote northern communities, Canada delivers care to everyone at roughly half the cost of the American system.

The system also demonstrates how to layer coverage: universal access to essential services (hospital, physician care) supplemented with targeted programs for vulnerable populations (seniors, low-income) while allowing private insurance to fill gaps for others.

It also offers a warning: a single-payer system that is starved of capacity will ration by queue. America should copy the coverage and the pricing, and fund the capacity.

The wait times are real. But Americans already have wait times — plus medical bankruptcy, insurance denials, and 27 million uninsured people. Given a choice between “wait for elective hip replacement” and “go bankrupt or go without,” many people would still take Canada’s trade-off — while demanding that Canada shorten the wait.

And for seniors and lower-income Canadians, the system delivers substantial coverage — medical care, prescription drugs and, increasingly, dental and vision — at little or no direct cost.

Singapore: The Mandatory Savings Model

Singapore took a completely different path — and it’s widely regarded as one of the most cost-efficient systems in the world.

The Singapore Model:

Mandatory health savings accounts (MediSave) — 8-10.5% of wages, depending on age

Basic government-run insurance (MediShield Life) for large hospital bills

Government endowment fund (MediFund) for those who can’t afford care

Government publication of hospital bill sizes and fee benchmarks

Heavy government involvement in hospital construction and management

Strong emphasis on preventive care and personal responsibility

Results:

Coverage: universal for citizens and permanent residents

Per capita spending: Singapore isn’t in the OECD data; the World Health Organization put it at about $6,660 per person in 2022 (purchasing-power adjusted)

Life expectancy: 83.1 years

Quality: Excellent, highly efficient

Satisfaction: High

How it works: For every working Singaporean, 8-10.5% of wages (drawn from combined employer and employee CPF contributions) goes into a personal MediSave account, which can only be used for healthcare. These accounts cover hospital bills, some outpatient care, and insurance premiums.

For major expenses (surgeries, cancer treatment, extended hospital stays), MediShield Life insurance kicks in — a government-run insurance plan with premiums payable from MediSave accounts.

For those too poor to afford care even with these systems, MediFund provides a government safety net.

The government heavily regulates the market, publishes what hospital treatments typically cost, and directly operates the public hospitals. The result: excellent care at well under half of American per-person spending.

The trade-off: This system requires high personal savings rates and strong government management. It works in Singapore partly because Singapore has a small, highly-organized population with cultural emphasis on saving and personal responsibility.

What America could learn: Health savings accounts don’t have to be libertarian pipe dreams. Singapore proves they can work as part of a comprehensive system with strong government backup for catastrophic costs and the truly poor.

Republicans love HSAs. Democrats love government safety nets. Singapore combines both.

United Kingdom: The Fully Socialized Model

The National Health Service is what Americans think of when they hear “socialized medicine.” Time to separate myth from reality.

The UK Model:

Government-run system

Most hospitals owned and operated by the NHS

Hospital doctors and nurses are NHS employees; most GPs are independent contractors paid by the NHS

Funded almost entirely through taxes (no premiums, no deductibles)

Private insurance legal (held by a minority of Britons) but parallel to the NHS

Prescription drugs in England: £9.90 per item — that’s it (free in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland)

Results:

Coverage: 100%

Per capita spending: $6,747

Life expectancy: 81.2 years

Wait times: Variable, longer for elective procedures

Quality: Good but strained by underfunding

How it works: You get sick, you go to an NHS hospital or GP clinic, you receive care, you go home. No bills, no insurance cards, no claims. If you need a prescription in England, you pay £9.90 regardless of what the drug is — unless you’re a child, pregnant, 60 or over, or have certain medical conditions (in which case it’s free).

Most hospitals are publicly owned. Hospital doctors and nurses are NHS employees with salaries and pensions. The entire system is funded through taxes.

The reality: The NHS is beloved in the UK — but it’s also chronically underfunded. Wait times for non-urgent procedures can stretch to months or over a year. The system works well for emergency care and general practice but struggles with capacity for elective procedures.

A minority of Britons buy private insurance for faster access to specialists and elective procedures. The private system exists parallel to the NHS — you can use both.

The trade-off: The NHS delivers care to nearly 70 million people at about 45% of American per-capita costs. But chronic underfunding creates wait times and capacity constraints. Still, Brits consistently rank the NHS as one of their most treasured institutions.

What America could learn: Socialized medicine works. The NHS proves you can deliver universal healthcare through publicly owned hospitals and publicly paid doctors. Whether Americans would accept this level of government involvement is a political question, not a practical one.

The Common Elements

Look across these five radically different systems and three elements appear in all of them:

  1. Universal coverage is mandatory: No country achieved universal healthcare by making coverage optional. Switzerland mandates private insurance purchase. Canada and the UK make coverage automatic; Germany makes it compulsory. Singapore mandates savings accounts. Optional systems leave people uninsured.

  2. Government controls prices: Every successful system has government price regulation — either through direct price-setting (Canada, UK), negotiated fee schedules (Germany, Switzerland), or strong government involvement in markets (Singapore). No successful system lets providers charge whatever they want.

  3. Administrative simplicity: Whether single-payer (Canada), multi-payer (Germany, Switzerland), or hybrid (Singapore), all successful systems have far less administrative complexity than the US. Doctors spend time on medicine, not insurance battles.

These aren’t liberal or conservative principles. They’re practical requirements proven across capitalist Switzerland, socialist UK, and everything in between.

What About Innovation?

The standard American objection: “But we lead the world in medical innovation! Universal healthcare would kill that!”

The evidence says it wouldn’t:

America does lead in total medical research spending and drug development. But universal coverage hasn’t stopped other countries from innovating. Switzerland, home of Roche and Novartis, has the most expensive universal system in the OECD and is one of the world’s great centres of pharmaceutical research. The UK, with the most fully socialized system on this list, is home to AstraZeneca and GSK, and the NHS runs major research hospitals that produce breakthrough discoveries. Germany is home to Bayer and a deep medical-device industry.

America produces significant medical innovation — but so do countries with universal healthcare.

Innovation happens where research funding, universities, and pharmaceutical companies invest — not where people go bankrupt from medical bills.

The Path Forward

Five countries. Five different approaches. All successful. All delivering universal coverage at roughly half to two-thirds of the American per-capita cost. All achieving better health outcomes.

Which model should America adopt? That’s the question for Part 5.

Spoiler: The answer isn’t “pick one and copy it.” The answer is: “Combine the best elements and craft a uniquely American solution.”

Switzerland proves you can have private insurance and market competition with universal coverage. Germany proves you can have public and private options coexisting. Singapore proves you can emphasize personal responsibility while maintaining a government safety net. Canada proves single-payer can work at scale across a vast, diverse country — and warns what happens when capacity doesn’t keep up. The UK proves socialized medicine can deliver care at well under half the American cost.

In Part 5, we’ll examine the bipartisan compromise nobody wants to talk about: a regulated multi-payer system that combines Swiss-style competition among private insurers with German-style regulated prices. It would give Republicans the market competition they want and Democrats the universal coverage they demand, and both parties still resist it.

The solutions exist. The only question is whether America has the political will to implement them.
