Part XI closed with a dynasty whose legitimacy rested on a bloodline imported wholesale from ancient Israel, a claim so effective it survived nearly three thousand years before a committee of junior officers finally decided it no longer mattered. This part turns to three West and Southern African kingdoms that built their own, entirely different legitimating traditions, none of them borrowed from anywhere else, each centered not on descent from a foreign king but on a physical object or demonstrated capability believed to hold the actual spirit and strength of the nation itself.

The Ashanti Empire, rising in what is now Ghana in the late seventeenth century under a king named Osei Tutu and his priest and chief advisor Okomfo Anokye, is built around the most literal version of this idea examined anywhere in this series. According to Ashanti tradition, Okomfo Anokye summoned from the sky, in front of the assembled chiefs of the Ashanti clans gathered near Kumasi, a wooden stool covered entirely in gold, which descended slowly through a cloud of white dust and settled gently onto Osei Tutu’s knees. This Golden Stool, the Sika Dwa Kofi, was proclaimed to contain the sunsum, the soul, of the entire Ashanti nation, not the personal property of any individual king, and explicitly never to be sat upon by anyone, including the king himself, who was merely permitted to rest his weight beside it on a lower ceremonial stool during state occasions.

Individual Ashanti chiefs retained their own personal stools, blackened with soot and libations upon their deaths as ancestral shrines in a tradition of stool veneration considerably older than the Golden Stool itself, and this existing cultural vocabulary is precisely what made Anokye’s innovation so immediately legible and persuasive to the assembled chiefs: he was not asking them to accept an entirely foreign concept of sacred kingship, but to recognize a single supreme stool sitting atop a hierarchy of smaller sacred stools they already venerated, uniting dozens of previously independent Akan chiefdoms under one confederation almost overnight by giving their existing religious framework a single capstone they could all defer to without any one clan having to submit to another clan’s own ancestral stool specifically. The Golden Stool stood apart from all of them, understood not as a royal possession passed down through inheritance in the way a European crown might be, but as the fixed, unmovable center of the entire Ashanti political and spiritual order, older and more permanent than any single occupant of the Ashanti throne.

The political genius of this arrangement is worth naming directly, because it solves the same problem this series has watched nearly every other monarchy wrestle with in its own way: by locating the nation’s sacred legitimacy in an object entirely separate from the king’s own body, Ashanti tradition made the monarchy answerable to something no individual ruler could personally claim to embody, control, or carry away into exile. This produces a check functionally similar to Zhou China’s Mandate of Heaven examined in Part III, in that legitimacy is understood as something the king holds in trust rather than something he simply is, but rendered here as a physical, guardable artifact rather than an invisible cosmic judgment read into floods and comets. A king who lost the confidence of the Ashanti chiefs’ council, the state’s traditional deliberative body, could in principle be destooled, a formal removal from office that left the Golden Stool itself entirely untouched and available to legitimate whichever successor the chiefs chose next, in much the same way Zhou China’s Mandate simply transferred to a new dynasty rather than disappearing along with a failed one.

The stool’s symbolic weight was tested directly, and in the most literal way imaginable, in 1900, when the British colonial governor Frederick Hodgson, having already exiled the reigning Ashanti king to the Seychelles following an earlier conflict, addressed an assembly of Ashanti chiefs and demanded that the Golden Stool itself be brought to him so that he might sit upon it personally, as a deliberate and explicit demonstration of British supremacy over Ashanti sovereignty. The demand fundamentally misread what the stool actually represented, treating it as though it were simply a throne in the European sense this series has documented across a dozen prior monarchies, rather than the literal repository of the nation’s soul. The demand triggered the War of the Golden Stool, led remarkably not by any male claimant to the throne but by the Queen Mother Yaa Asantewaa, who rallied Ashanti forces with a speech reportedly declaring that if the men of Ashanti would not go forward and fight, the women would take up the guns themselves and fight the white men until the last of them fell in the battlefield.

The war that followed, lasting several months and involving a prolonged siege of the British fort at Kumasi, ultimately ended in British military victory and the formal annexation of Ashanti territory into the British Gold Coast colony later that year. The stool itself, however, was never surrendered, having been hidden by its guardians throughout the entire conflict at a location kept deliberately secret even from most of the Ashanti court, and it remains today in Ashanti hands, cared for as a still-active symbol of the reconstituted Ashanti kingship recognized within modern Ghana’s constitutional structure, a rare case in this series of a legitimating symbol that genuinely outlasted the very colonial power that went to war specifically to seize it.

The Zulu kingdom, consolidated under Shaka in the early nineteenth century in what is now KwaZulu-Natal in South Africa, built its legitimacy through an entirely different mechanism and a considerably more openly martial theory of kingship than the Ashanti’s sacred stool. Shaka’s authority rested less on any inherited sacred object and more on his own systematic transformation of Zulu military organization, a set of reforms this series can trace with unusual precision because their tactical logic is still studied by military historians today. He replaced the traditional long throwing spear, which committed a warrior’s single weapon to a single throw at range, with a shorter stabbing spear, the iklwa, designed for close combat that forced engagements to a decisive and immediate conclusion rather than a prolonged exchange of thrown weapons. He organized young men into age-based regiments, the ibutho system, that trained, lived, and fought together as cohesive units bound directly to the king’s own authority rather than to their individual clan chiefs, deliberately weakening the older clan-based loyalties that had previously fragmented Zulu-speaking peoples into dozens of competing small chiefdoms. And he enforced a period of compulsory unmarried service within these regiments, delaying marriage until a warrior had proven himself in battle and been formally released by the king, which gave Shaka direct personal leverage over one of the most fundamental life decisions available to any young Zulu man.

Shaka’s kingship, built through this reorganization, was legitimacy through demonstrated and continuously renewed battlefield success on a scale that rapidly absorbed or subdued dozens of neighboring chiefdoms during a period of extraordinary regional upheaval and displacement remembered as the Mfecane, or the crushing, a decades-long cascade of warfare and forced migration across large stretches of southern Africa that some historians estimate displaced or killed well over a million people as smaller chiefdoms were absorbed, scattered, or destroyed by Shaka’s expanding Zulu state and by other rising powers reacting to the same regional pressures. This is a legitimacy model closer to Sumer’s original lugal, this series’ earliest case examined in Part I, than to any divinely sanctioned bloodline or ancient sacred artifact: authority earned and continuously renewed through demonstrated martial capability rather than inherited through any fixed symbol or covenant. It is a model that produces extraordinary short-term territorial expansion, precisely because subject peoples had every practical incentive to fall in line behind a demonstrably winning power, but it offers considerably less protection to the king himself once that capability is perceived by his own inner circle to be faltering, a vulnerability Shaka’s own violent end demonstrates with unusual clarity. In 1828, increasingly erratic and harsh in his rule following the death of his mother Nandi, Shaka was assassinated by his own half-brothers, Dingane and Mhlangana, who moved quickly to seize the throne for themselves, a demonstration that a legitimacy grounded purely in continuously demonstrated strength offers no built-in mechanism for an orderly succession the moment that strength is judged, by those closest to the king, to have curdled into instability.

Benin, in what is now southern Nigeria, developed perhaps the most sophisticated tradition of physical legitimacy examined anywhere in this trio, through an extraordinary and continuously updated body of bronze and brass plaques and sculptures commissioned by its Oba, or kings, over some five centuries of documented royal patronage. These works, created using a lost-wax casting technique of remarkable technical sophistication that European visitors, upon first encountering Benin City in the late fifteenth century, found genuinely difficult to reconcile with their own assumptions about African metalworking capability, served as a continuously updated visual chronicle of the kingdom’s own royal history, depicting successive Obas, their military campaigns, their elaborate court ceremonies, and their claims to divine sanction descending from Oduduwa, the legendary founding ancestor figure shared across several Yoruba and related royal traditions in the region. The plaques were mounted on pillars within the royal palace itself, functioning simultaneously as historical record, religious statement, and a continuously visible reminder to every visitor to the court of precisely how deep and how documented the Oba’s own claim to rule actually was, a durable medium meant to outlast any individual reign in a manner directly comparable to the function this series traced through Zhou China’s carefully kept court records of omens and portents in Part III.

When British forces launched a punitive expedition against Benin in February of 1897, following an earlier ambush and killing of a British trade delegation that had attempted to enter the city during a sacred religious festival against explicit local prohibition, the resulting conflict ended with British troops burning much of Benin City and looting several thousand of these bronzes and ivories from the royal palace, along with the Oba himself, Ovonramwen, being captured and eventually exiled to Calabar, where he died in 1914 having never been permitted to return to his own kingdom. The looted bronzes were subsequently sold at auction in London to help offset the cost of the expedition itself and were dispersed into museum and private collections across Europe and North America, where the substantial majority remain today despite a decades-long international restitution campaign, led in recent years by Nigeria’s own government and by museums including Germany’s own state collections, which have begun returning individual pieces, a process this series notes as still very much unresolved at the time of writing.

It is worth noting one further contrast among these three cases before closing, because it sharpens exactly what kind of durability each legitimating strategy actually bought its kingdom. The Ashanti stool survived because it was portable enough to hide and sacred enough that no colonial administrator, once corrected, dared force the issue further without risking a war that had already proven costly. The Zulu model, resting entirely on the person of the reigning king rather than any separable object, proved considerably more brittle, and the kingdom’s independence was extinguished within a few decades of Shaka’s death, first through the fracturing of his own successors’ rule and then through direct British and Boer military conquest culminating in the Anglo-Zulu War of 1879, in which Zulu forces achieved one dramatic early victory at Isandlwana before ultimately succumbing to superior firepower at Ulundi later that year. Benin’s bronzes, unlike the Ashanti stool, could not be hidden — they were fixed, numerous, and precisely the kind of valuable, portable colonial trophy an expedition specifically outfitted to fund itself through looting would prioritize, which is exactly why they ended up scattered across foreign museums rather than surviving intact in Benin itself the way the Golden Stool survived in Kumasi. Three different physical or reputational anchors for legitimacy, in other words, produced three measurably different outcomes once each kingdom actually collided with a colonial power determined to test them.

What unites all three kingdoms examined in this part, despite their considerable differences in legitimating mechanism, is a lesson worth carrying forward as this series turns next to Europe’s most dramatic self-made monarch: legitimacy anchored in a tangible object, whether a golden stool, a bronze chronicle, or a continuously renewed battlefield reputation, proved durable enough in at least two of these three cases to survive colonial conquest itself, even when the throne’s actual political sovereignty did not, a durability considerably harder to achieve for legitimacy resting on nothing sturdier than one man’s personal ambition and a crown he had to place on his own head because no one else’s tradition was prepared to hand it to him.

One further dimension of the Ashanti case deserves inclusion here, because it shows the Golden Stool’s legitimating function surviving not only the 1900 war but the far more disruptive experience of full colonial administration in the decades that followed. Britain, having annexed Ashanti territory outright after the War of the Golden Stool, formally exiled the reigning Asantehene, Prempeh I, to the Seychelles for a quarter century, and administered the former kingdom directly through British district commissioners with no reigning Ashanti monarch at all. Yet when Prempeh I was finally permitted to return in 1924, and especially when the Ashanti Confederacy Council was formally reconstituted under British colonial law in 1935 with his nephew Prempeh II recognized as Asantehene, the restoration proceeded with remarkably little friction precisely because the Golden Stool itself, never surrendered, never displayed, never captured, had continued functioning as the nation’s undisputed spiritual anchor throughout the entire quarter-century interregnum during which no one actually held the throne associated with it. Modern Ghana, independent since 1957, has preserved this same arrangement in its own constitutional structure, recognizing the Asantehene and the broader institution of Ashanti chieftaincy as a legally protected traditional authority operating alongside the national republican government, a rare case in this series of a monarchy’s core legitimating institution outliving not only colonial conquest but the colonial power’s eventual departure, and continuing to function within a fully sovereign republican state that has chosen to preserve rather than abolish it.
