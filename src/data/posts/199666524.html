This week, the President of the United States bombed Iran twice, threatened to bomb Oman, was fired upon by a ballistic missile aimed at Kuwait, watched Israel strike Beirut for the first time in a month, presided over inflation at a three-year high, demanded that Saudi Arabia recognise Israel as the price of peace, rejected every available mechanism for disposing of Iran’s uranium, and declared that he does not care about the midterm elections.

It was, by the standards of this war, a normal week.

And through all of it — the strikes, the counterstrikes, the missile over Kuwait, the cabinet-room threat to blow up an ally — the price of oil barely moved. Brent crude sat at about $94 a barrel, roughly where it was in early March. The strait has been closed for three months. Experts warned at the war’s outset that a closure of more than a few weeks would send oil to $150 or $200. Instead: $94, and falling on Thursday.

“The math just doesn’t add up,” oil analyst Rory Johnston told The Atlantic (The Atlantic). “For people like myself who spend all day analyzing this stuff, we’re looking at prices wondering: Am I going insane? What is happening?”

What is happening is the TACO equilibrium.

The acronym — Trump Always Chickens Out — was coined during the 2025 tariff wars, when the president announced sweeping levies, watched the markets convulse, and reversed course thirteen hours later, citing markets that had gotten “yippy.” Investors learned to “buy the dip” and profit when he backed down. The pattern was so reliable it got a name: the TACO trade.

Rogé Karma, writing in The Atlantic, explains how that pattern has now trapped the Iran war in a self-perpetuating loop. Oil prices stay low because traders assume Trump will end the war before prices get too high. But because prices stay low, Trump faces no economic pressure to end the war. The expectation of capitulation removes the need to capitulate.

And the president appears to have figured this out. Every time oil creeps up, he gestures at a deal — “very good and productive conversations,” “largely negotiated,” “pertaining to PEACE” — and the markets cool. When they cool, he no longer needs the deal. “Prices rise, Trump talks about a deal, prices fall, and then Trump suddenly feels like he doesn’t actually need to make the deal,” Johnston said. An endless merry-go-round.

This is the same mechanism documented in Part 41 of the Trump Accountability Project — Truth Social as a market instrument. There it was meme coins and trading desks. Here it is the price of crude and the lives of the people beneath the bombs. The instrument is identical. Only the stakes have changed.

The loop has a clock on it, though. Countries are burning through their strategic oil reserves — roughly 13 million barrels a day, by one estimate — and when those run dry, the law of supply and demand reasserts itself. "It's a ticking clock," the Eurasia Group's Gregory Brew told Karma. "Eventually that reality is going to set in. And when it does, prices are going to rise very, very fast."

The bond market is less patient than the oil market.

The April inflation report landed this week with the force of a verdict. The Personal Consumption Expenditures index — the Fed’s preferred gauge — rose 3.8 percent year-on-year, the fastest since May 2023 (New York Times). Core inflation hit 3.3 percent. GDP growth was revised down from 2 percent to 1.6 percent. The savings rate fell to its lowest since June 2022. Incomes flatlined. For the first time since 2022, prices are rising faster than wages.

Vincent Alban/The New York Times

The Fed is now openly discussing rate hikes. Governor Lisa Cook: “I am prepared to raise rates, if the expected disinflation does not appear in a timely manner.” Governor Christopher Waller: he “could no longer rule out rate hikes.” This is the war arriving in the cost of every mortgage, every car loan, every credit card balance in America — the fourth inflationary shock in five years, after the pandemic, the Ukraine invasion, and Trump’s own trade war.

As Jared Bernstein, Biden’s former chief economist, put it: “Equity investors have been ingesting large amounts of hopium that any day now Trump will come to his senses and call the war off. But bond traders generally don’t have patience for hopium.”

“I don’t care about the midterms,” the president said at Wednesday’s cabinet meeting. He may not. But the bond market does not care whether he cares.

His own Treasury secretary, Scott Bessent, predicted oil and gas prices could “come down very quickly” once the war ends, because “the oil market is going to be very well supplied on the other side of this.” Which is precisely the expectation that keeps prices low, keeps the pressure off, and keeps the war going. The administration is, in effect, narrating the very equilibrium that traps it.

The deal, meanwhile, remains a document that two governments describe in incompatible terms.

A ship anchored in the Strait of Hormuz, near Larak Island, Iran, this month.Credit...Majid Saeedi/Getty Images

By Thursday, U.S. officials said they were “closing in” on a framework, though Trump had not signed off (New York Times). Under the American understanding: the strait reopens immediately, but the blockade stays — reduced in stages tied to how much prewar traffic Iran restores, an incentive to demine quickly. Nuclear talks deferred to a second phase. And — the surprising new addition — a $300 billion “investment fund” for Iranian reconstruction, an idea reportedly floated by Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, both real estate investors, who suggested promoting real estate projects in Tehran.

Read that again. The war’s endgame now includes a Kushner-Witkoff real estate fund for the country the United States spent three months bombing.

By Friday morning, with the framework in front of him, Trump had still not signed. Mediators had told the administration Wednesday night that Iran was comfortable with the latest memorandum. The president's reaction, one U.S. official said, was that he wanted to take a few days to consider it. Iran finally said yes. Trump said wait. Vice President JD Vance, asked Thursday whether a deal would actually happen, told reporters: "It's hard to say exactly when or if the president is going to sign... I can't guarantee that we're going to get there." The vice president of the United States, on the record, hedging the signature of his own president on the war his own president launched.

The Iranian version of the document is different. Iran says it includes a “declaration of the end of war” on all fronts including Lebanon, the lifting of the blockade within 30 days, the suspension of its nuclear programme only in exchange for no new sanctions, and the release of up to $20 billion in frozen assets. The nuclear stockpile — the casus belli, the reason thousands died — would be negotiated later. Always later.

And the mechanism for disposing of that stockpile? The Times lays out the four technical options on the table. Ship it out — most logically to Russia or China, who took 97 percent of the stockpile under Obama's deal — but Trump has said he is not comfortable with either. Render it unrecoverable in place by vitrifying it into glass or burying it deep — but Iran will not accept the inspections required. Convert it into reactor fuel rods — costly, impractical, time-consuming. Down-blend it to reactor-grade levels — easily reversible if Iran keeps its centrifuges, which it will. Four options. Each fatal in its own way. The 970 pounds of near-bomb-grade uranium remain exactly where they were on February 27th, plus another ten tons of lower-enriched material the negotiators have barely mentioned. The most painful detail in Sanger's reporting: the U.S. acknowledges that Iran's promise not to pursue a nuclear weapon "is what Iran promised in the opening paragraphs of the 2015 agreement it made with the Obama administration." The deal Trump withdrew from. The deal he ridiculed for a decade. The deal whose central commitment he is now trying to extract by force, and may yet fail to obtain.

Obama’s “Pallets of Cash”

On the frozen funds, Trump faces a trap of his own construction. He spent years attacking Obama for the “Pallets of Cash” — $1.7 billion returned to Iran in 2016. Now his own deal contemplates releasing many times that, so his team is engineering workarounds: Qatar releasing funds, Gulf states underwriting reconstruction, anything to avoid the image of American cash on Iranian tarmac. The man who built a brand on attacking a $1.7 billion payment is negotiating a $300 billion fund.

Consider how the negotiation is actually being conducted. Senior Iranian officials communicate with Tehran via couriers because the supreme leader is in hiding to avoid assassination. One U.S. official conceded to The Times that this slow pace was understandable — given that "the Americans and Israelis have interrupted both previous rounds of negotiations with bombing campaigns." Pause on that sentence. The administration is admitting, on background, that the United States is now negotiating with a regime it has twice bombed mid-negotiation, and that the slow pace of talks is the natural consequence. This is what passes for diplomacy in May 2026: messages carried by hand to a man in a bunker, by a government that twice tried to kill the negotiators.

We have seen this structure before — and recently. It is the Gaza playbook: agree a ceasefire, defer the hard problems to a “Phase 2” that never arrives. In Gaza, the first phase secured hostages and aid; the second phase — Hamas disarmament, reconstruction, a new administration — simply never happened. Hamas consolidated. The rubble stayed rubble. Michael Koplow of the Israel Policy Forum described the pattern to the Times as “a series of flashy things that then were never implemented.” His warning about Iran is the one to remember: “Iran is going to be far tougher, because it’s a much bigger conflict, and the issues are harder and far more complicated.” A phased deal is a way to claim victory while leaving the core issue — the nuclear programme — exactly where the war found it.

The Abraham Accords demand was the week’s purest absurdity.

Trump declared that Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Pakistan, and others “should” — “mandatory,” he said — normalise relations with Israel as part of the Iran deal (New York Times). Half the countries he named already recognise Israel. The other half have no intention of doing so. Qatar lobbied desperately to prevent this war; the notion that ending it would induce Doha to recognise Israel left regional analysts baffled.

Trump with leaders from Israel, Bahrain and the United Arab Emirates at the signing of the Abraham Accords in Washington in 2020.Credit...Doug Mills/The New York Times

“It’s just bizarre,” said Yoel Guzansky of Tel Aviv University. “What’s the connection between a deal with Iran and that? I’m honestly puzzled.” Pakistan’s defence minister said joining would clash with the country’s “fundamental ideologies.” A Saudi analyst: normalisation “is not a slogan, and it is not a bargaining chip.” Two Western diplomats said no one was taking the idea seriously.

Trump even mused that Iran itself might join. “Wow, now that would be something special!” Lindsey Graham — who days earlier called the deal a potential “nightmare for Israel” — pronounced the Accords idea “simply brilliant.” The hawk was placated by a fantasy.

And while the diplomats haggled over real estate funds and recognition fantasies, the war kept expanding into countries that never asked to be in it.

Iran fired a ballistic missile at Kuwait — intercepted, condemned by Kuwait and the UAE, a sovereign nation attacked because U.S. strikes had launched from somewhere the IRGC wished to punish. Israel struck Beirut for the first time in three weeks, hitting an apartment building in the southern suburbs, and pushed ground forces beyond the “yellow line” deep into southern Lebanon. A strike near Sidon killed a mother and her two children. More than 3,200 dead in Lebanon since March. Netanyahu: “deepening its operation.” He told an interviewer he speaks with Trump “every day, almost every day,” and that in Iran “the task needs to be completed.”

The deal says the fighting stops on all fronts. Israel struck Beirut the same day the framework was reported. Those two facts cannot coexist, and everyone negotiating knows it.

There is one set of numbers the administration would prefer you not see.

Eleven weeks ago, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth told Congress that the 38-day campaign had “obliterated” Iran’s defences. Trump said this week that Iran was “negotiating on fumes” — “their navy is gone, their air force is gone, everything’s gone.” But his own intelligence agencies tell a different story (New York Times). Confidential assessments from early this month show Iran has restored operational access to 30 of the 33 missile sites it maintains along the Strait of Hormuz. It retains roughly 70 percent of its prewar missile stockpile and 70 percent of its mobile launchers. The Pentagon, it turns out, opted for lighter munitions to seal the entrances of hardened sites rather than destroy them — and the Iranians dug them out faster than American planners anticipated. Meanwhile, U.S. stockpiles of the long-range missiles needed to actually destroy those sites are, officials concede, “critically low.”

This is why the strikes keep happening. The “self-defense strikes” near Bandar Abbas this week were aimed at exactly the missile batteries and mine-laying boats the war was supposed to have eliminated. And here is the detail that captures the entire enterprise: the IRGC navy was caught laying mines in the strait at the same moment Iranian diplomats in Doha were negotiating to demine it. Dana Stroul, a former senior Pentagon official for the Middle East, put it precisely: “The regime is still attempting to portray itself as a good-faith negotiator, but the I.R.G.C. navy was caught mining the strait at the same time Tehran was apparently negotiating to demine the strait.”

The strait is not merely “closed.” It is a live combat zone. South Korea confirmed this week that an explosion that tore a 16-by-23-foot gash in the hull of an HMM container ship was caused by an Iranian Noor anti-ship missile. Iran denied it. South Korea’s investigators did not.

A war launched to destroy Iran’s missiles has ended with Iran holding 70 percent of them and firing one at Kuwait.

For 88 days, ordinary Iranians lived inside the war’s other weapon: silence.

The internet blackout imposed on February 28th became, by the count of monitoring groups, the longest nationwide shutdown in modern history — 90 million people sealed inside a censored “domestic internet,” cut off from family abroad, from independent news, from the websites their businesses ran on. The economic cost was estimated at up to $80 million a day. This week, haltingly, it began to lift.

“I have mixed feelings,” a 29-year-old Tehran tech worker named Hamid said in a voice message sent minutes after he reconnected. “I am happy, but at the same time, I feel kind of stupid that I am happy about such a simple thing.” A 39-year-old advertising worker, Maryam, described the return of the world: “I feel like I’ve just come out of prison and I’m in shock. I have nothing to say — I’m just listening to the sounds outside.” And then, the sentence that should haunt anyone who launched this war in the name of the Iranian people: “That sense of humiliation is what really bothers people. They feel like hostages in this country. The sad part is that we were starting to get used to it.”

The war was sold, in part, as liberation. Its actual effect on the Iranian street was three months of darkness, imposed by the regime, endured by the people, and noticed by almost no one in Washington.

Anna Moneymaker / Getty

Eliot Cohen, writing in The Atlantic, offered a caution worth heeding even here (The Atlantic). The words we use — war, victory, defeat, quagmire — are mostly too lazy for what is actually happening. The war did not begin on February 28th; it began, in some sense, in 1979, and Americans have died at Iranian hands for nearly five decades. Victory and defeat are rarely clean. Both sides can lose. Both can claim to win.

Cohen is right that the catchphrases obscure more than they reveal. But he is also right about something his fellow hawks will like less: the administration’s entry into this war was blundering and incompetent. It did not secure the islands around the strait. It did not pre-position minehunters. It did not arrange war insurance. It did not court allies — it spewed venom at them, and is now threatening to bomb one of them over the very waterway it failed to keep open.

That is the war in a sentence. Not a quagmire, not a clean defeat, not unconditional surrender. Something stranger: a war that achieved none of its aims, expanded to nations that never chose it, and is being wound down through a framework that restores the world to February 27th — at the cost of thousands of lives, a Kushner real estate fund, and an inflation rate the Federal Reserve may have to break the economy to tame.

Arash Azizi, also in The Atlantic, put the Iranian calculation plainly: the regime believes it has won, or at least not lost. It survived the onslaught. Its lead negotiator was just reelected speaker of parliament. The hardliner who opposed diplomacy has been sidelined. And it is emerging with terms better than anything on offer before the war.

“The Islamic Republic’s leaders may soon learn,” Azizi wrote, “that the problems of peace are the hardest to solve.”

So, perhaps, will the president who promised unconditional surrender and is negotiating a real estate fund.
