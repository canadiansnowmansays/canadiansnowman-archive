This series began, twenty-four parts and roughly six months of research ago, with a comparison, made in an earlier essay of mine written on the anniversary of the Magna Carta, between King John’s temperament and that of a rather more contemporary figure who treats every institutional check on his authority as an illegitimate imposition to be escaped through whatever leverage happens to be available, whether a friendly judge, a friendly legislature, or a friendly election result. That comparison is the reason this entire project exists, and it seems right, having now traced kingship from Uruk’s granary ledgers through Bhutan’s voluntary abdication and the Ottoman caliphate’s careful dismantling, to return to it directly rather than leave it as a passing aside in an introduction written months before this series’ actual research began.

The pattern this series has been tracking under the name wannabe kings is, by this point, a familiar shape: a man who wants a throne’s substance, the unaccountable authority, the personal loyalty, the freedom from institutional constraint, without ever having received a throne’s actual legitimating credentials, whether bloodline, covenant, or the kind of accumulated moral authority this series traced through Bhumibol Adulyadej in Part XXII. Napoleon, examined at length in Part XIII, is this series’ clearest historical case study in what happens when a man tries to manufacture that legitimacy from nothing but demonstrated competence and stage-managed popular acclaim, crowning himself because no existing tradition was prepared to hand him a crown he had not been born to. Julius Caesar, examined in Part IV, is the case that ended in assassination rather than empire, killed specifically because his contemporaries recognized the substance of kingship in his conduct even though he never claimed the title. What unites every figure this series has examined under this heading is not the specific mechanism of their ambition, but a shared refusal to accept that legitimate authority comes with constraints attached, a refusal this series has now traced destroying its holder in some cases and, in Napoleon’s own case, simply proving less durable than the institutions it tried to bypass.

It is worth pausing on one further historical figure before turning to Putin directly, because he shows the wannabe-king pattern pushed to a genuinely theatrical extreme this series has not yet examined, and because the sheer scale of the gap between his self-image and his actual circumstances makes the underlying psychology unusually easy to see clearly. Jean-Bédel Bokassa, a former French colonial army officer who seized power in the Central African Republic through a coup in 1966, spent over a decade consolidating personal control before declaring himself Emperor Bokassa I in 1976, staging a coronation modeled explicitly and in considerable visual detail on Napoleon’s own 1804 ceremony examined in Part XIII, complete with an imported gold-encrusted throne shaped like an eagle and a crown reportedly costing a substantial share of one of the poorest nation’s entire annual budget. Bokassa’s own self-coronation, an almost too on-the-nose echo of Napoleon’s crown-taken-from-the-Pope’s-hands gesture, produced neither the durable dynasty nor even the temporary continental dominance Napoleon briefly achieved; he was overthrown in a French-backed coup within three years, and his reign is remembered today largely as a byword for the gap between a wannabe king’s own self-image and the actual substance available to support it, a gap this series has now seen, in varying degrees of severity, in nearly every case examined under this heading.

Vladimir Putin’s own path to power in Russia offers this series its clearest contemporary case study in constructing exactly this kind of manufactured legitimacy from the ground up, and it is worth tracing the specific mechanisms rather than treating the comparison as merely rhetorical. Putin’s rise began through entirely conventional, constitutional means, appointed acting president by Boris Yeltsin at the end of 1999 and then winning election in his own right in 2000, a process this series does not dispute was procedurally legitimate at its outset. What this series is interested in is the machinery constructed over the following decades to ensure that legitimacy, once secured, would never again face genuine challenge through the same democratic mechanism that had originally granted it. Russia’s Constitutional Court in 2020 approved a set of amendments, subsequently ratified through a national referendum whose independent international monitoring was severely restricted, resetting Putin’s own prior term count to zero and permitting him to remain in office until 2036, a legal maneuver this series recognizes as directly comparable in function, though considerably more procedurally elaborate, to Julius Caesar’s own conversion of a temporary emergency dictatorship into a permanent one examined in Part IV.

The mechanics of Russia’s post-2000 political consolidation are worth tracing in a bit more specific detail, because they show the erosion of institutional constraint happening across several distinct and mutually reinforcing channels rather than through any single decisive stroke. Independent television networks that had provided genuinely critical coverage of the Kremlin during the 1990s were brought under state or state-friendly ownership within Putin’s first term, most notably the NTV network, seized from media magnate Vladimir Gusinsky through a combination of legal pressure and manufactured debt claims in 2001, establishing a pattern of using nominally legal commercial and tax mechanisms to achieve what a more overt act of political censorship might have made too visible to ignore. Regional governors, previously directly elected, were converted to presidential appointees following a 2004 terrorist attack on a school in Beslan that Putin’s government cited as justification for greater central control over regional security, a genuine crisis deployed to justify a permanent institutional change considerably broader than the crisis itself required. And prominent political opponents have faced prosecution, exile, or worse: Alexei Navalny, the most nationally prominent opposition figure to emerge during Putin’s tenure, survived a poisoning attack in 2020 that Western governments and independent laboratories attributed to a Soviet-era nerve agent, returned to Russia regardless the following year, was immediately imprisoned on charges widely regarded internationally as politically motivated, and died in a remote Arctic penal colony in February of 2024 under circumstances Russian authorities described as natural but that drew immediate and widespread international skepticism, an outcome this series notes without further comment beyond observing that it closes out, with grim finality, the specific channel of domestic electoral challenge that Navalny himself had spent over a decade trying to keep open.

The theological and cultural dimension of this legitimacy-construction deserves equal attention, because it shows Putin drawing, whether consciously or not, on precisely the toolkit this series has traced across two dozen prior monarchies. The Russian Orthodox Church under Patriarch Kirill has provided consistent public blessing for Putin’s own rule and for Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine beginning in 2022, framing the conflict in terms explicitly invoking Russia’s own claimed spiritual mission and historical destiny, a pairing of political authority with religious sanction this series recognizes immediately from the Saudi state’s own alliance with Wahhabist religious authority examined in Part XVII, from the divine-right theology Charles I invoked and died defending in Part VIII, and from the Third Rome theology this series traced through Ivan the Terrible’s coronation in Part XV. Putin’s own public rhetoric has increasingly framed Russia’s war and his own continued rule in terms of restoring a historical Russian greatness supposedly stolen by the Soviet Union’s own collapse and the West’s subsequent expansion, a theme he articulated at considerable length in a lengthy essay published under his own name in July of 2021, titled On the Historical Unity of Russians and Ukrainians, which argued that Ukraine’s own independent statehood was, in effect, an artificial historical accident rather than a legitimate national reality, a legitimating narrative this series recognizes as structurally similar to the restoration rhetoric this series traced through Zhu Yuanzhang founding the Ming dynasty on the claimed collapse of a corrupted predecessor in Part III, and to Xi Jinping’s own rhetoric of national rejuvenation noted briefly in that same part.

It is worth adding one further mechanism to this account, since it bears directly on the resource-based legitimacy this series traced through the Gulf monarchies in Part XVII: Russia’s own vast oil and natural gas wealth has played a comparable, if less formally institutionalized, role in cementing Putin’s own domestic political durability, funding both the state services and periodic targeted spending increases that have helped maintain broad public acquiescence during periods of otherwise severe economic strain, including the extensive Western sanctions regime imposed following the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, and the accumulated personal wealth of a small circle of oligarchs whose own fortunes have depended, since the early 2000s, on maintaining good personal standing with the Kremlin rather than on any independent commercial base immune to state pressure, a concentration of resource-dependent loyalty this series recognizes as structurally similar, though achieved through considerably more coercive means, to the Gulf states’ own oil-funded political bargain.

What makes Putin’s case a genuinely distinct variant within this broader wannabe-king category, rather than a simple repetition of Napoleon’s own strategy, is the absence of any comparable moment of self-coronation, any single dramatic gesture equivalent to Napoleon taking the crown from the Pope’s own hands. Putin’s authority has instead been constructed gradually, through the patient, cumulative erosion of the very electoral and judicial mechanisms that originally granted it, a strategy this series recognizes as considerably closer in method to the slow constitutional erosion this series traced destroying the Roman Republic in Part IV than to Napoleon’s own single dramatic seizure. Marius’s military reforms, Sulla’s march on Rome, and Caesar’s own dictatorship perpetua each represented a further step in a decades-long process by which Republican institutions were hollowed out from within while their outward forms remained nominally intact, precisely the pattern this series has now watched Russia’s own constitutional court, electoral commission, and legislature undergo across the more than two decades of Putin’s own rule.

Donald Trump’s own documented conduct, examined with the same insistence on specific mechanism rather than characterization this series has applied to every other case in this part, belongs directly alongside Putin’s, though the two cases differ in a way this series regards as genuinely important rather than incidental: Trump’s efforts have played out entirely within, and been repeatedly checked by, the very institutions of a functioning constitutional democracy, rather than against a state apparatus he had already spent two decades hollowing out. Following his defeat in the 2020 presidential election, Trump and a number of his allies pursued a coordinated effort to organize slates of alternate electors in several states he had lost, submitting documents to Congress and the National Archives falsely certifying those electors as the states’ legitimate representatives, a scheme that produced criminal charges against Trump himself and several co-defendants in Georgia and in a federal case brought by special counsel Jack Smith, though neither case reached trial: the federal case was dismissed after Trump won the 2024 election, in line with the Justice Department’s long-standing policy against prosecuting a sitting president, and the Georgia case was dropped in November of 2025 after the original prosecutor was disqualified. In a recorded telephone call on the second of January, 2021, Trump asked Georgia’s Secretary of State, Brad Raffensperger, to “find” the exact number of votes needed to reverse his loss in that state, a request Raffensperger declined, and one this series notes as directly comparable in structure, though not in ultimate consequence, to King John’s own appeal to a friendly external authority to undo a result he found unacceptable.

The events of the following days show the same underlying instinct escalating from legal and administrative maneuvering into something considerably more direct. On the sixth of January, 2021, following a rally at which Trump repeated unsubstantiated claims that the election had been stolen from him, a crowd of his supporters breached the United States Capitol during the formal certification of Joe Biden’s victory, an event a bipartisan Senate investigation and a subsequent House select committee both concluded Trump had substantially encouraged through his own rhetoric and had been slow to act to stop once it began, though Trump himself has consistently denied wrongdoing and was acquitted in a subsequent Senate impeachment trial that fell short of the two-thirds majority required for conviction.

It is this specific and recurring pattern, an unwillingness to accept an adverse result from a court, an election, or a certifying body as final, combined with an active search for some friendlier authority willing to overturn it, that this series regards as the genuine throughline connecting Trump’s conduct to King John’s own behavior in the year following Runnymede, examined at length in Part VI, rather than any claim that the two men’s ultimate power, methods, or historical circumstances are otherwise comparable. Both cases matter to this series for the same underlying reason: they demonstrate that the temperament this series has traced through Caesar, Napoleon, Bokassa, and Putin alike, a refusal to treat a negotiated or adjudicated limit on one’s own power as genuinely final, is not confined to ancient absolute monarchs or foreign strongmen governing without meaningful institutional constraint, but can appear, and has appeared, inside a modern constitutional democracy whose courts, election officials, and legislature ultimately held against it, a considerably more reassuring outcome than this series has been able to report for John, Caesar, or the Roman Republic’s own gradual collapse, though this series would note that the outcome held specifically because those institutions functioned as designed, not because the underlying impulse being tested was in any way milder.

This is the same King John comparison this series first raised in Part VI, made explicit here rather than left as a passing aside for readers to draw forward on their own.

It is worth being precise, in closing this section, about what this series is and is not claiming in drawing King John and Trump together. This series is not asserting equivalence of outcome, since John’s own defiance produced a civil war fought with swords on English soil while Trump’s own conduct has been contested through courts, elections, and congressional proceedings rather than armed conflict, a difference this series regards as genuinely significant rather than incidental. What this series is asserting is a narrower and, it believes, defensible claim about temperament and method: that a leader’s instinct, faced with a negotiated or judicially imposed limit on his own power, to treat that limit as illegitimate and to seek its reversal through whatever friendly authority is available, rather than to accept it as the settled price of continued legitimate rule, is a recognizable and recurring pattern this series has now traced across eight centuries, from a thirteenth-century English king to the present day, and that recognizing the pattern does not require this series to render any verdict on the specific legal or political disputes involved in its most recent iteration.

What this series would rather close with, having traced this pattern across Caesar, Napoleon, and Putin alike, is the observation that every wannabe king examined in this entire project has shared one further trait beyond the refusal of constraint itself: an evident belief that the specific mechanism of legitimacy, whether bloodline, covenant, mandate, or accumulated moral authority, is a formality to be arranged after the fact rather than a genuine constraint that must be earned before the fact. Caesar assumed his own competence and popularity entitled him to permanent, unaccountable power regardless of what four centuries of Republican tradition held about the office he was reshaping. Napoleon assumed his own military and administrative genius entitled him to found a hereditary dynasty from nothing within a single generation, a project this series documented collapsing within barely more than a decade of his own final defeat. And every wannabe king this series has examined has, in the moment of asserting this entitlement, revealed the same underlying error: mistaking the story a legitimate throne tells for a decoration that can simply be applied to naked personal power after the power has already been seized, rather than recognizing, as this series’ opening parts on ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt established at the very outset, that the story was never decoration at all. It was, from the very beginning of this entire survey, the actual load-bearing structure, the thing that convinced enough people, across enough generations, that the compliance being asked of them was owed to something larger and more durable than the specific man currently collecting it. Every ruler in this series who forgot that distinction, from Caesar in the Senate to Napoleon on Saint Helena, eventually discovered its absence the hard way.

Part XXVI pulls back from individual case studies entirely, for a single survey of every monarchy still standing in the world today, twenty-six distinct reigning royal houses, ranging from ceremonial figureheads to genuinely absolute rulers, a final accounting of exactly how much of this ancient institution actually survives into 2026.
