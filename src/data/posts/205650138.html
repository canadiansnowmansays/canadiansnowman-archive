Xi Jinping’s first public act as General Secretary of the Chinese Communist Party was a visit to a museum.

On November 29, 2012, two weeks after his accession to the top party position, Xi led the six other members of the new Politburo Standing Committee to the National Museum of China on the east side of Tiananmen Square, to view an exhibition titled “The Road to Rejuvenation.” The exhibition traced China’s history from the humiliations of the 19th century — the Opium Wars, the unequal treaties, the foreign concessions — through the revolution that ended the Century of Humiliation and the decades of construction that followed, to the present moment of rising power and national confidence. Standing before it, Xi gave a speech that introduced the phrase he would make the organising principle of his leadership: the Chinese Dream.

“We are now closer than at any time in history to the goal of the rejuvenation of the Chinese nation,” he said. “And we are more confident and capable of achieving this goal than at any time in history.”

The choice of venue was not accidental. The museum, the exhibition, and the phrase were all making the same argument: that Xi’s accession was not the beginning of something new but the culmination of something very old. The Long Return — the restoration of China’s rightful centrality after two centuries of humiliation and reconstruction — was entering its final phase. And the man who would lead it had chosen, as his first public statement, to frame himself not as an innovator but as an inheritor.

Thirteen years later, in August 2026, the same framing was on display at a funeral. Zhu Rongji, the last of the reform-era premiers who had guided China’s market opening in the 1990s, died at 97, and his state funeral fell one day after Beijing marked the centenary of Jiang Zemin’s birth. The pairing was not lost on China scholars. Two commemorations of the reform generation, staged within twenty-four hours of each other, invited a comparison the party had reason to want managed: between an era that felt purposeful and optimistic and a present one shadowed by a property crisis, shrinking job prospects for the young, and a tightened grip on the private sector. Xi used his commemoration speech for Jiang to close off that comparison before it could take hold, stressing continuity in their shared belief in Marxism and party strength and casting himself as a modernizer adapting to circumstance exactly as Jiang had done. He invoked Jiang’s decisiveness during the 1997 Asian financial crisis and called for the same “tenacious fighting spirit” against today’s “high winds and turbulent waves” — language widely read as a coded reference to the confrontation with the United States that has intensified on his watch. The instinct was the same one that sent him to the museum in 2012: claim the reform mandate as continuous, so that his own departures from it read as its fulfilment rather than its betrayal.

What he inherited, along with the authority and the mandate, was a set of structural economic vulnerabilities that the patient decades had produced but not resolved. The property sector, which by some estimates accounted for as much as a quarter of GDP when construction, related industries, and land revenues were combined, had inflated to levels that made housing in major cities unaffordable for the workers who built them. Local government financing vehicles — off-balance-sheet debt structures created to circumvent formal borrowing limits — had accumulated obligations whose full scale was not transparent even to central government auditors. The export-led growth model that had powered the patient decades was encountering diminishing returns as Chinese wages rose and as Western governments began noticing the trade imbalances that Chinese exports had produced. Xi’s China was not simply inheriting a rising power’s momentum. It was inheriting a set of compounding structural risks that would require management alongside the strategic ambitions the 141 pages eventually describe.

The anti-corruption campaign that Xi launched within months of taking power was the most consequential domestic political action of his first term, and the most misread by Western observers.

The campaign was real. The scale of corruption in the Chinese party-state was real — an estimated $3.5 trillion in illicit assets had been moved offshore between 2000 and 2011, according to the Global Financial Integrity organization, and senior officials at every level of government had developed personal business interests, through relatives and associates, that created conflicts between their public duties and their private enrichment. The country that Xi was inheriting had a corruption problem that was simultaneously economically damaging and politically delegitimising — the public anger at officials who had accumulated private wealth through their positions was a genuine threat to party authority.

The campaign addressed the problem. Between 2012 and 2017, more than 1.5 million officials were investigated and punished, including dozens of senior military officers and several members of the Politburo itself. Zhou Yongkang, formerly one of the nine members of the Politburo Standing Committee and the former head of China’s internal security apparatus, was sentenced to life imprisonment — a level of prosecution that would have been unthinkable under previous leaders. Bo Xilai, the charismatic Chongqing party secretary who had been the most visible potential rival to Xi’s succession, was already in prison after his wife’s murder of a British businessman had exposed his corruption.

The Western misreading was to see the anti-corruption campaign primarily as a governance reform. It was a governance reform — the corruption was real, and addressing it was genuinely valuable. But it was simultaneously a political purge. The officials who were investigated and punished were disproportionately concentrated in the networks of Xi’s rivals and predecessors. The campaign eliminated potential challenges to Xi’s authority under the morally unassailable cover of fighting corruption. Finding evidence of corruption in the Chinese party-state, where virtually every senior official has acquired personal benefit from their position, is primarily a question of where investigators are directed to look. The campaign directed them, with notable consistency, at Xi’s opponents.

The military was purged in parallel. The two most senior People’s Liberation Army officers below the Central Military Commission were charged with corruption. The PLA’s command structure was reorganised, its traditional military regions replaced with new theater commands oriented toward joint operations rather than regional defence — a reform that simultaneously modernised the military and broke up the power bases that regional commanders had accumulated. By 2017, Xi had consolidated control over the military in a way that no Chinese leader since Mao had achieved.

The abolition of presidential term limits in March 2018 removed any ambiguity about what Xi was building.

Since Deng’s post-Mao reforms, the Chinese system had maintained informal norms limiting leaders to two five-year terms. The norms were not constitutional — the party’s leading position meant that a General Secretary who controlled the party controlled the state regardless of formal term limits — but they had functioned as a genuine constraint, providing for the regular transfer of power that prevented the recurrence of one-man rule. Jiang Zemin had served two terms and stepped back. Hu Jintao had served two terms and stepped back. The system appeared to have institutionalised succession.

Xi Jinping served two terms and removed the term limits.

The National People’s Congress amended the constitution in March 2018 by a vote of 2,958 to 2, with three abstentions. The two negative votes were the most public dissent that the Chinese political system had produced on a major constitutional question in decades. Their identity has never been disclosed. The amendment made Xi eligible to serve as President indefinitely — a position from which, combined with his retention of the General Secretary and Central Military Commission chairmanship, he could govern China without a defined endpoint.

The significance was understood immediately. Deng’s informal norms had been designed precisely to prevent this — to ensure that the party would not be captured by a single individual the way it had been captured by Mao. Xi had used the party’s own mechanisms to dismantle those norms, operating entirely within the system’s legal framework. The constitutional amendment was not a coup. It was a demonstration of what the system Deng built looked like when a sufficiently determined leader decided that its informal constraints did not apply to him.

The ideological dimensions of Xi’s consolidation deserve attention, because they are more substantive than Western accounts often acknowledge.

“Xi Jinping Thought on Socialism with Chinese Characteristics for a New Era” — the formal title of the doctrine written into both the party constitution and the state constitution — is not simply a personality cult in the style of Mao’s Little Red Book. It is an attempt to provide a coherent theoretical framework for what China is trying to do: to articulate why the Chinese model — party-led development, political stability as the precondition for economic growth, a strong state as the guardian of national sovereignty — is not a deviation from the universal path to modernity but an alternative path that may be superior for countries with China’s history and circumstances.

The argument has genuine intellectual content. The liberal democratic model that the West proposed as universal in the 1990s has had a difficult quarter-century: the 2008 financial crisis, the rise of populist nationalism, the failures of democratic governance in managing COVID, the institutional dysfunction visible in multiple Western democracies. Xi’s argument — that China’s model has delivered more for more people more efficiently — is not absurd. The question is whether it is sustainable: whether a model that depends on a single party’s competence and a single leader’s judgment can manage the complexity of a 1.4 billion-person economy over the long run, through the succession problem, through the demographic headwinds, through the technological competition that the 141 pages describe.

The surveillance architecture built under Xi is the physical infrastructure of ideological control. China’s social credit system — more accurately described as an array of overlapping governmental and commercial rating systems than the single unified score that Western media often portrays — combines financial data, legal records, business compliance information, and social behaviour monitoring into assessments that can affect access to transportation, financial services, educational opportunities, and government contracts. The system is uneven in its implementation and less comprehensive than either its Chinese proponents or its Western critics claim. But it represents a serious attempt to use the data capabilities of a digital economy to enforce social and political compliance at a scale that previous authoritarian systems could only approximate.

The Xinjiang programme is the most extreme expression of this ambition. In the western region that is home to the Uyghur Muslim minority, China has constructed what independent researchers describe as one of the most intensive systems of surveillance and coercive re-education in the world. The programme involves mass detention in facilities that China describes as vocational training centres and that critics describe as internment camps — the distinction turns on whether residents can freely leave, and the evidence strongly suggests they cannot. Satellite imagery, leaked government documents, testimony from former detainees, and the accounts of Uyghur diaspora communities have produced a picture of a programme that has detained somewhere between one and two million people, subjected detainees to political indoctrination, restricted religious practice, and monitored the entire population through a network of cameras, checkpoints, and mandatory smartphone surveillance apps that is denser than anything deployed elsewhere in China.

Xi’s government describes this as a counter-terrorism programme responding to genuine security threats — Uyghur militants have conducted attacks in Xinjiang and beyond, and the security concern is not fabricated. The scale of the response, and the collective punishment of an entire ethnic and religious population for the actions of a minority, is what has drawn the characterisation of genocide from the United States government and several parliaments. China denies the characterisation and has not allowed independent international inspection of the facilities. The programme is both the logical extension of Xi’s consolidation — the application of the surveillance state’s maximum capacity to a population perceived as resistant — and the clearest statement of where that consolidation leads when it encounters genuine resistance.

The history in this series suggests the sustainability question is genuinely uncertain, in ways that Xi’s confidence does not acknowledge.

The Military-Civil Fusion strategy — the systematic integration of China’s civilian technology sector with its military research and development — was formalised under Xi and represents perhaps the most consequential structural change of his first decade.

Under previous leaders, the People’s Liberation Army and the civilian technology sector had maintained significant separation, partly from institutional habit and partly from the Deng-era doctrine that economic development should take priority over military modernisation. Xi has reversed this. The strategy requires Chinese companies, universities, and research institutions to share technology, data, and personnel with the military, and it requires the military to direct some of its R&D investment through civilian channels to accelerate commercialisation. The result is a system in which the civilian technology sector is simultaneously a commercial enterprise and a component of national military development.

This is the context in which Western governments’ concerns about Chinese technology companies need to be understood. The concerns are not primarily about these companies’ commercial practices. They are about a legal and institutional framework that makes the distinction between civilian and military technology genuinely difficult to maintain.

The economic nationalism of Xi’s tenure is the other dimension that distinguishes his approach from his predecessors. Deng and his successors welcomed foreign investment because they needed the capital and the technology transfer it brought. Xi has progressively tightened the conditions under which foreign companies operate in China: requiring data localisation, imposing cybersecurity regulations that require disclosure of source code and encryption keys, restricting foreign access to certain markets, and expanding the categories of activity defined as threats to national security. The message to foreign companies has been consistent: China wants your investment and your technology, but on Chinese terms, and the terms are getting stricter as Chinese alternatives become available.

This has produced the beginning of a decoupling that neither side fully intended. American semiconductor restrictions, which limit China’s access to the most advanced chips and the equipment needed to manufacture them, are the Western response to the Military-Civil Fusion strategy — an attempt to use export controls to slow a technological development programme that is explicitly designed to undermine Western military advantage. China’s response has been to accelerate indigenous semiconductor development, to reduce dependence on Western software and hardware across critical systems, and to build parallel supply chains that reduce the leverage that technology export controls provide.

The 141 pages are the document of this acceleration. The 15th Five-Year Plan is Xi’s answer to the decoupling — a roadmap for reducing Chinese vulnerability to Western technology controls by building domestic capability in every sector where that vulnerability currently exists. Understanding this is understanding why the plan reads the way it does: not as a development programme for a country catching up, but as a self-sufficiency programme for a country preparing for a prolonged period of strategic competition in which access to Western technology cannot be assumed.

The 141 pages are Xi’s calculation. They are stated clearly, in public, for anyone willing to read them.
