At three o’clock in the morning on Saturday, June 13, 2026, a construction worker reached up to the white marble facade of the John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts in Washington and pulled the letter “A” from the wall.

There was no sound of power tools. The letter came off by hand.

Workers had spent the previous eight hours building scaffolding in front of the section of the facade bearing the president’s name. Then they had hung heavy white tarps from the structure, obscuring the removal from the several hundred people gathered on the plaza below to watch. A gap in the tarps allowed a New York Times photographer to observe the moment. The letter came off. Then the others followed.

The Kennedy Center had requested a twelve-hour extension of the midnight deadline, attributing the delay to a cluster of summer thunderstorms. The extension was granted for operational purposes but the legal position was unchanged — both the district court and the federal appeals court had denied the board’s requests for a stay. The lawyers for Trump and the center had spent all of Friday seeking legal intervention to keep the name on the marble while the appeal proceeded. Both courts said no. By the time the tarps went up and the scaffolding was in place, the legal options had been exhausted. What remained was the physical fact of letters on marble, a court order, and a construction crew working in the early hours of a Saturday morning while a crowd watched from below.

Representative Joyce Beatty, the Ohio Democrat whose lawsuit had produced Judge Cooper’s ruling, stayed on the plaza until the work crew departed around 4 a.m. She had been there for hours. She did not leave until the letters were down.

Allerton Kilborn, seventy-nine years old, had traveled from his home in Chevy Chase, Maryland, and stayed on the grounds for more than twelve hours, moving between the scene outside and the air-conditioned center. He had once met John Kennedy. He had brought a book. “For the adventure of it,” he said. “This is history.” He described Trump’s addition of his name to the memorial as a desecration. “I’m not religious,” he said, “but I see it in religious terms.”

In the crowd, a Trump supporter from Wasco, California named Jeanette Mercado said she saw nothing wrong with the president’s name on the building. Her husband, Bert, who said he was also a Trump supporter, took a different view. “There should be a sense of continuity here,” he said. “Why are you going to interject your name?”

The champagne at the Watergate, which residents had kept on ice for two days through two false alarms, was finally opened.

This is what the architecture of legal resistance looks like when it functions as designed. Not a grand confrontation. Not a political movement. A construction crew at three in the morning, a gap in a tarp, a letter pulled from marble by hand, a seventy-nine-year-old man with a book standing on a plaza until 4 a.m. because he thought it mattered.

It did matter. It matters in ways worth examining carefully, because the Kennedy Center removal is not simply a story about a name on a building. It is a story about the mechanisms by which a democratic republic says no — about which mechanisms work, which ones don’t, and what the distinction between them reveals about the health of the institutions involved.

Judge Christopher R. Cooper did not write ninety-four pages because the law was complicated.

He wrote ninety-four pages because the people who needed to understand his ruling included a president who had demonstrated a willingness to treat legal constraints as obstacles to be routed around rather than limits to be respected, and ninety-four pages of federal jurisprudence is harder to route around than two. He was thorough because thoroughness was the appropriate response to the situation. He was thorough because the historical record he was contributing to deserved the effort. And he was thorough because the law, in this case, actually was as clear as he said it was, and clarity deserved documentation.

“Crystal clear,” he wrote, about the 1964 statute that named the Kennedy Center. “Congress gave the Kennedy Center its name, and only Congress can change it.”

Four words. Only Congress can. In the architecture of American democratic governance, those four words carry the weight of the entire constitutional design — the separation of powers, the distinction between the branches, the principle that what the legislature creates the executive cannot simply unmake by preference and declaration. They are four words that George Washington would have recognized and approved. They are four words that two hundred and fifty years of American institutional practice had embedded so deeply in the structure of governance that a federal judge could invoke them on a dead president’s birthday — the day after, precisely — with the confidence of a man citing arithmetic.

They are four words that the current president treats as a provocation.

The legal proceedings that preceded the 3 a.m. removal deserve examination, because they illustrate both the strength and the limits of the judicial mechanism.

The board’s lawyers filed for a stay with language that called to mind, in the words of the New York Times, “the president’s own cadence, punctuation choices and penchant for self-promotion.” The motion, signed by Brett Shumate of the Justice Department, argued that removing Trump’s name would harm fundraising because donors “were only willing to do so with the name ‘Trump’ on the building.” It added: “Many did it because they loved the concept of two Great Presidents, one Republican, one Democrat, working together as one — In many ways, a bipartisan relationship!”

The exclamation point. The capitalised Great Presidents. The phrase “working together as one.” A legal filing submitted to a federal appeals court, written in the register of a Truth Social post, arguing that the name of a living president should remain on the memorial to a dead one because donors preferred it that way.

Lawyers for Representative Beatty countered that the appeal had been filed “at the eleventh hour, in a transparent effort to jam the court and game the judicial system.”

Both courts denied the stay. The scaffolding went up. The letters came down.

Judge Cooper noted in his Friday ruling declining to suspend his own deadline that the Kennedy Center had already taken steps to comply — staff memos, email signature changes, website updates, parking signs retaped. “These efforts,” he wrote, “undermine the notion that defendants face irreparable harm in complying with the order in full.” The institution that had put eighteen letters on the building in under a day took two weeks and a thunderstorm extension to take them off. The asymmetry is its own commentary.

The Kennedy Center ruling is the most visible legal pushback in the naming project, but it is not the only one, and understanding the full architecture of resistance requires examining the various mechanisms through which the republic has said no.

The courts are the most immediate and, in the Kennedy Center case, the most effective mechanism. The ruling rests on a straightforward application of statutory law — the 1964 Kennedy Center Act is unambiguous about who named the building and who has the authority to rename it. No Constitutional doctrine of unusual complexity is required. No balancing of competing rights. The law says what it says, and what it says is that Congress did this and only Congress can undo it.

On the same day the Kennedy Center letters were coming down, a second federal judge issued a ruling in a different but related dimension of the same project. Judge Angel Kelley of the United States District Court for the District of Massachusetts temporarily blocked the National Park Service from removing or revising signs, films, and other materials at national parks across the country under a Trump executive order calling for the removal of materials that “inappropriately disparage Americans” or cast the United States “in a negative light.”

To comply with that order, the Park Service had removed plaques about slavery at Independence National Historical Park in Philadelphia, a sign about climate change at Fort Sumter in South Carolina, and a sign about Indigenous peoples at Acadia National Park in Maine. Judge Kelley ordered those materials restored within three weeks. “Not only does this undermine the integrity of the national parks,” she wrote. “It sets a dangerous precedent of censorship and sanitization.”

This ruling illuminates something the Kennedy Center case alone does not fully reveal. The naming project — the inscription of Trump’s name on institutions, buildings, currency, monuments — operates in one direction. The parks sign removal operates in the opposite direction. One writes a specific name onto the republic’s surfaces. The other erases inconvenient names and histories from them. Both are the same impulse: controlling what the republic’s surfaces say. Both have now encountered federal judges who found the law clear and the overreach documented.

The Interior Department spokeswoman’s response to Judge Kelley’s ruling is worth preserving in full, because it captures the administration’s register with unusual precision. She called it a ruling from a “liberal activist judge” and added that the department “will look at our appeal options while we celebrate UFC Freedom 250 on the South Lawn of the White House this weekend in honor of our nation’s 250th with the greatest president in the history of our country — President Donald J. Trump.”

The parks signs about slavery and climate change and Indigenous peoples are being restored by court order. The UFC event on the South Lawn has been confirmed. The greatest president in the history of the country, in the administration’s assessment, is the current one. The courts, apparently, are the ones with the wrong perspective.

The Congressional authority principle that underlies the Kennedy Center ruling extends, in theory, to the entire landscape of federal institutions. Congress created the Institute of Peace. Congress named the Kennedy Center. Congress controls federal buildings, federal monuments, federal currency, the naming of military vessels. The executive branch operates within a framework that the legislature constructed, and that framework contains, built into it as a structural feature rather than an afterthought, the principle that the president cannot simply rename what Congress has named.

The problem is that the Congressional authority principle requires Congress to exercise the authority it possesses. A principle without enforcement is a norm, and norms are not the same as laws.

Congress has, with respect to the naming project, been largely silent. Individual members have objected — Congressman Cohen asked whether the ego would stop staining the city, Representative Beatty filed the lawsuit that produced the ruling, floor speeches have been delivered. But the Republican majority in both chambers has not, as a body, moved to assert the Congressional prerogative. The legislation that would formally protect the Kennedy Center’s name, restore the Institute of Peace’s independence, and prevent the executive branch from unilaterally renaming federal institutions has not been introduced, debated, or advanced.

The Congressional Republicans who might otherwise defend institutional norms have calculated that the political cost of confronting a popular president within their own party exceeds the institutional cost of acquiescing to the naming project. This is the calculation that makes democratic norms fragile — not that people don’t believe in them, but that the incentive structure makes enforcing them more costly, in the short term, than not enforcing them.

So the courts carry the weight that the legislature declines to lift. And the courts have been consistent. The Institute of Peace takeover found illegal. The Kennedy Center renaming found unlawful. The parks sign removal found censorship and sanitization. The pattern across different judges, different jurisdictions, different legal frameworks is the same: the naming project systematically exceeds the legal authority of the executive branch.

The republic’s legal architecture was built to handle exactly this kind of executive overreach. Whether it was built to handle this volume of it simultaneously is a question the current judicial docket is answering in real time — and answering, so far, with a consistency that the administration has been unable to reverse on appeal.

There are historical precedents for what happens when a leader’s name is removed from public institutions, and they are not, on the whole, comforting analogies for the namer.

The Roman practice of damnatio memoriae — the formal condemnation of a disgraced emperor’s memory, the removal of his name from public inscriptions, the destruction of his statues — was applied to emperors who had overreached badly enough to alienate the Senate after their deaths. Caligula received it. Domitian received it. Commodus received it. The practice was not always complete — too many inscriptions, too many statues, too many coins for the erasure to be total — but it expressed the republic’s judgment, retroactively delivered, on those who had confused themselves with the state.

The United States does not practice damnatio memoriae. The American tradition of historical reckoning is more complicated and more ambivalent — decades of argument rather than the clean surgical erasure of the Roman practice.

But the question of what happens to Trump’s name on public institutions after Trump is not abstract. It is a practical administrative question that will be answered by whoever controls the executive branch when the current term ends, and by whatever Congress exists at that point, and by the courts asked to adjudicate the inevitable disputes.

Judge Cooper’s ruling establishes something useful for that future reckoning: the principle that Congressional authority over naming is real, enforceable, and not subject to executive override. A future Congress that chose to restore the Kennedy Center’s original name would be on solid legal ground. A future executive branch that chose not to defend the Institute of Peace renaming would be making a decision the legal record supports.

The name may be on the marble. The marble has been there longer than the name, and will be there after.

The Kennedy Center was named by Congress for a dead president on the occasion of his assassination, as a living memorial to a man who had championed the performing arts and been taken before his time. The act of naming was an act of grief and tribute — the legislature speaking on behalf of the republic about what it valued and who it chose to remember.

Judge Cooper noted, in his ruling, that adding Trump’s name had “relegated President Kennedy’s name to second place.” That phrase — relegated to second place — is worth sitting with. Kennedy’s name on the building he inspired, the building created in his memory two months after his death, the sole national memorial to him in Washington — relegated, by board vote, to second place.

At 3 a.m. on Saturday morning, a construction worker pulled the letter “A” from the wall.

There was no sound of power tools.

The letter came off by hand.

The republic, in that moment, was doing what the republic does when it is functioning as designed — slowly, imperfectly, in the small hours of a Saturday morning, in the presence of a seventy-nine-year-old man who had once met Kennedy and thought it mattered enough to stay until 4 a.m.

It did matter.

Only Congress can change it.

The courts, when Congress won’t act, can change it too.

Donald Trump has spent a lifetime writing his name on things. Towers. Golf courses. Hotels. A university. A Bible. A perfume called Success. A peace institute he gutted before the paint on the letters dried. A performing arts centre dedicated to a murdered president he has spent his entire career trying to impress.

The name is always the same: gold where possible, large where mandatory, permanent where permitted, and imposed where neither permission nor permanence was ever his to grant.

This series is about what that compulsion reveals — psychologically, politically, historically, and occasionally comically. It is not a flattering portrait.

Then again, neither is a loading dock on Bay Street.
