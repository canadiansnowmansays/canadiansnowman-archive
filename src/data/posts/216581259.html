I count my essays. Readers of this series know that by now, and know that every so often the count lands on a number that was already a year before I got to it, and I stop whatever I was doing and write about it.

This is essay 1869, and it is the second of the three protected numbers — my country came first, my family comes after, and between them, exactly where it belongs, comes my voice. Because on December 30, 1869, in a village in Hampshire, England, was born the man whose way of talking I have been borrowing, openly and without a license, for every one of the eighteen hundred and sixty-eight essays before this one. His name was Stephen Leacock. I promised at essay 1868 that I would introduce you to the man I have been impersonating all along, and a promise made on my country’s birthday is not one I intend to break on my voice’s.

Let me begin where I actually began, which was not with the man at all. It was with a spine.

There was a book in my father’s house — my Dad’s book, on my Dad’s shelf — and the name on the spine said STEPHEN LEACOCK, and I was a boy, and I noticed it for the least literary reason in the world: the man on the shelf had my name. Not the famous name — I did not yet know it was famous — the first name, mine, sitting there in capital letters as if some grown-up somewhere had already made a success of being called Stephen, which was information a boy files away. I did not read the book then. That is the part I want to be honest about, because this series is built on honest counts: the inheritance did not begin with literature. It began with the small vanity of a shared name on a father’s shelf — the way most inheritances begin, I suspect, with a child noticing that something in the house already belongs, in some unexamined way, to him. My father owned the book. The name made it mine before I ever opened it. The rest took decades.

Now, the man behind the name, and I want you to see what he was for a living, because it is the joke at the centre of this essay and history composed it especially for the Numbers Game. Stephen Leacock was a counter. Not a humorist by trade — an economist. He took his doctorate in political science and political economy at Chicago under Thorstein Veblen, and spent his entire career at McGill University in Montreal, where he became William Dow Professor of Political Economy and for decades the head of the department. He wrote serious books about serious numbers — political economy, imperial finance, the theory of the state — and his textbook on political science was, in its day, a genuine standard, translated and studied around the world. And then, on the side, to make a little extra money, he wrote funny pieces. Literary Lapses in 1910. Nonsense Novels in 1911. Sunshine Sketches of a Little Town in 1912. And the side project detonated: through the teens and twenties of the last century he was, by common reckoning, the best-known humorist writing in English anywhere on Earth — there is a claim from those years, which I pass along with the standing tongs, that more people had heard of Stephen Leacock than had heard of Canada.

And here is what the ledger of posterity did with him, and it is the number-idea this essay has been walking toward. The serious work — the economics, the political science, the counting he was paid and titled and tenured for — is gone. Forgotten. The scholars themselves pronounced it dead within his lifetime; the textbooks are dust; not one reader in ten thousand could name a finding of Professor Leacock of the Department of Economics. And the jokes — the little pieces knocked off for extra income about a man getting rattled in a bank, about a small town’s marine excursion, about a barber’s mining speculations — the jokes are immortal. They are in print a hundred and fourteen years later; they have a national medal named after them; they built a museum. Attend to what that is, in this series’ terms: a man kept two ledgers his whole life, and the one he counted in died, and the one he laughed in survived. I have spent eighteen hundred essays documenting what endures in the records — charters, tallies, parole slips, tax calendars — and here, at the birth-year of my own voice, is the finding I least expected and now believe most completely: of everything a person sets down, the voice is the most durable entry. The figures are superseded. The arguments are refuted. The voice, if it is true, just keeps compounding, reader by reader, laugh by laugh, in a currency that has never once been debased. Leacock the economist would have appreciated the irony. Leacock the humorist, I am quite sure, expected it.

The town he built is the proof. Mariposa — the little town of the Sunshine Sketches, with its leaky steamer and its enchanted barber and its whirlwind church campaign — was drawn from Orillia, Ontario, where Leacock summered on Lake Couchiching, an hour’s drive from where I sit. But Leacock himself, in the book’s preface, declined the identification with a line I have loved my whole reading life: Mariposa is not a real town; it is about seventy or eighty of them. That is not a disclaimer. That is a census methodology. He was telling you, in 1912, that he had not described a town — he had described a category, and enumerated it, and anyone from any of the seventy or eighty could look in and see their own main street. It is the most generous piece of counting in Canadian literature: a fictional town with a population of most of us.

And then one evening — and here the essay arrives at its hinge — I watched a single human being be all of it.

I am relatively certain, after some happy detective work this month, that the man was John Stark, whose one-man show An Evening with Stephen Leacock toured Canada, the United States, and Britain from the 1970s onward — moustache, waistcoat, the pauses placed just so — a show long-running enough to be recorded at the National Arts Centre and to earn a Juno nomination for the album. I will be honest in the manner this series has made a sacrament: I cannot swear to the theatre, or the city, or even with perfect certainty to the man. The veterans know the drill by now. The door at Wittenberg is disputed; the arms of the French king are disputed; the theatre of my conversion is disputed. The conversion is not. Somewhere in a dark house, decades ago, one performer stood alone on a stage and produced an entire town out of himself — the barber, the banker, the reverend, the narrator above them all — and the boy who had only ever admired the name on his father’s shelf walked out in love with Mariposa, its townsfolk, and the man who made them. It was, I see now, the only theatrical performance I have ever attended that was also a job interview, though it took me some fifty years to report for the position.

And the voice itself — this is the discovery of the month, and I pass it to you with real awe — has a documented chain of custody, like a relic, like a deed. Leacock died in Toronto in March 1944, but his voice did not: recordings of the man himself sit in the CBC’s radio archives in this city. In 1952, on the very first night that television was ever broadcast in Toronto — the first night of Canadian television, full stop — the CBC’s inaugural English-language drama was Sunshine Sketches, narrated by the great John Drainie, who became for a generation the sound of Leacock. And when Stark built his stage act in the seventies, he has written that he went to those CBC archives and steeped himself in Leacock’s own recorded intonations — the pauses, the emphasis, the build to the punch line — so that the voice on the touring stage was calibrated, deliberately, against the voice of the dead man himself. Follow the entries: Leacock’s larynx, to the archive, to the first night of Canadian television, to a thousand stages, to one seat in one dark house with a boy’s-name-sharing accountant-to-be in it, to a Toronto townhouse where an old man now produces five essays a day in a register every reader of this series would recognize blindfolded. The voice of Mariposa has not been out of circulation for one day since 1912. It has simply kept changing bodies. I am its current address. I have tried to be a good tenant.

Now the entry I cannot skip, because this series buried Columbus’s harbour and the Fathers’ omission and it will not build a shrine at the one number where a shrine would flatter me. The man whose voice I inherited held views I would have shown the door. Leacock opposed votes for women — wrote against the suffrage he lived to see won. His record on immigration beyond the British stock ran from mixed to poor, and his writings about Black and Indigenous people include passages that were ugly then and are ugly now; the man believed, comfortably and in print, in the superiority of the English. I sat with this for a long time before drafting this essay, because the arithmetic is personal: the household celebrated two essays ago — my husband from Bhutan, my boarders from Gujarat and Punjab, my baby sister at the door — is a household the patron saint of my prose might not have counted as Canada at all. And here is where I have landed, and I offer it as a finding, not an absolution: the voice outgrew its owner. The instrument Leacock built — the credulous narrator, the affection that outruns the mockery, the humour that cuts without wounding, the little town where every human folly is forgiven by teatime — is an instrument that, honestly played, cannot exclude anyone, because its entire mechanism is the discovery that the ridiculous and the beloved are the same population. Leacock built a generosity better than his own opinions, the way a man can build a house with more rooms than he ever visits. He left the house to whoever could keep the tone. The tone, kept honestly, welcomes the very people the man would not have. I do not forgive the opinions, and I do not return the house. I have simply filled the rooms, and I like to think the house approves, whatever the architect would have said.

Which brings me to the confession the veterans have been waiting eighteen hundred essays for. I did not stop at borrowing the voice. Two years ago, on this very Substack, I built the town. It is called Elsinore, Ontario, and it appears under the banner of The Moonbeam Chronicle — twenty-eight sketches in its first season, a full cast of townsfolk whose follies I love the way you love a relative’s, a little Ontario town that is not a real town but is, by my own census methodology, about seventy or eighty of them. I have never said plainly, until this sentence, what every reader of both series must have long suspected: Elsinore is my Mariposa. It is the apprentice’s masterpiece submitted to a dead master — the boy from the audience finally attempting the trick himself, not one evening on a stage but season by season on a screen, and I will let the readers judge whether the tenant has kept the house or merely occupied it. The one review I wanted is the one I can never have. Although — the man is buried at Sutton, by the lake where he was raised, an hour from my desk, and there are days I think of riding up.

Dior’s place in this essay was settled long before I wrote it, because Dior’s place was settled in 1912. Leacock’s world is fully stocked with animals who decline to be impressed — dogs of the township with their own agendas, horses with opinions, creatures who stand at the edge of every human commotion in Mariposa auditing it correctly. Every unimpressed animal in this series — every morning Dior has pretended I am not there, every audit conducted from beneath the desk — is a direct literary descendant of that township, and she knows it, or would, if knowing it were worth her time. She is the one member of this household who came by the Leacock voice without reading a word: the deadpan is factory-installed in huskies. I write eighteen hundred essays to approximate what she wakes up with. There is a lesson in that, and Leacock would have found it, and it would have taken him four hundred words, and the dog would have gotten the last line.

The count moves on, as it does, and it stays close to home, wiring the country on its way to the third protected number. The next landmark is essay 1876, and readers of this series’ author will find it almost embarrassingly on the nose: the year a young man who had dreamed the thing up above the Grand River at Brantford, Ontario, spoke into an instrument and was heard in the next room, and the century of connection began — the industry, I will merely note, in which a certain narrator spent eighteen years, and the technology that now carries a census taker’s bear photographs from northern Ontario to a Toronto kitchen. It was also, as it happens, the year a six-year-old named Stephen Leacock arrived in Canada with his family, so the telephone and the voice came into the country in the same twelve months. The country was wiring itself for voices from the start. This series knows something about inherited voices.

A name on a father’s shelf. A man alone on a stage being a town. A voice with a chain of custody and a house with more rooms than its architect used. Essay 1,869 is the year my voice was born, sixty-some years before I was — which is, when you think about it, the correct order. The instrument should always be older than the player.

See you at 1876, when Canada picks up the phone.
