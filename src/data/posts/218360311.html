On September 30, 2026, Pete Hegseth stood at a lectern at Marine Corps Base Quantico and told 600 junior officers and sergeants how many of their future bosses he had removed. He did not read it as a confession. He read it as a scorecard.

Every cabinet secretary inherits people. Most of them spend their first year learning which ones are good. Hegseth spent his first year and a half deciding which ones were gone, and on Wednesday he let the room know he regards the job as half done. This instalment looks at that number: where it came from, who it describes, and what it costs.

The Performance

“The number crunchers say we fired 10 percent of the generals and admirals across the force,” he said, “and that number sounds about right.”

There is a peculiar pleasure in a man citing a newspaper’s tally of his own dismissals as though it were a quarterly earnings report. The figure he was quoting came from the New York Times, which has kept a running count. As Greg Jaffe noted in his Times analysis on September 30, 2026, “Hegseth Talks Tough as Republican Doubts About His Leadership Grow,” Hegseth has now fired or blocked the promotions of more than 80 generals and admirals since taking office. The Secretary didn’t dispute the arithmetic. He embraced it.

Then he added to it. As Joe Gould and Hamed Ahmadi reported for the Washington Sun (formerly NOTUS) in “Hegseth Is Cutting the Number of Generals and Admirals by 20%,” he announced that the Pentagon had separately eliminated another 10 percent of general-officer positions. “So, yes, 20% reduction across the board,” he said. “Now the media calls that a purge. I call it accountability and long overdue, and frankly, the bare minimum.”

He explained the philosophy in a sentence that may outlive him: “You can’t change a culture with the same people who enabled it. Personnel is policy at every single level.” And he explained what had happened to the people in question. “Those generals who did not, who clung to the woke department or seemed to think civilian control of the military is simply a suggestion — well, they no longer work here.”

To finish, he promised a new way to pick generals. Colonels seeking a star will now face what he called a “performance-based joint warfighter evaluation,” which he contrasted with earlier assessments he said were built to produce “politically correct quotas for race and gender.” In the same speech he described the War Department as “colorblind, gender-neutral, and merit-based.”

The Paperwork

The record behind the scorecard is long, and it has a shape.

The cut Congress already made. Begin with what is not new. Trimming the top of the military is an old idea, and a reasonable one. As Tom Nichols pointed out in The Atlantic on October 1, 2026, in “Pete Hegseth, Idea Guy,” Congress capped the number of generals and admirals at 962 for years, then used the 2017 National Defense Authorization Act, passed in Barack Obama’s last year in office, to order the total cut by 11 percent by the end of 2022. That reduction went through the normal process: written into law, debated, phased in, applied to positions rather than to named individuals.

That is the useful distinction, and it is why this essay keeps the two halves of Hegseth’s “20 percent” apart. Cutting general-officer positions is policy, and Congress has done it before. Firing the people in them, without reasons, is something else. Nichols put the difference plainly: removing an admiral whose office has outlived its purpose is one thing, and removing one because the Secretary thinks they are disloyal is another. Hegseth’s speech blurred the two on purpose.

The marquee firings. The pattern began early. In February 2025, within a month of his swearing-in, Hegseth’s department removed Gen. Charles Q. “CQ” Brown Jr., the Black fighter pilot who was chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, and Adm. Lisa Franchetti, the first woman to serve as Chief of Naval Operations and to sit on the Joint Chiefs. Gen. Jennifer Short, the senior military assistant to the secretary of defense, followed. None of them was given a public reason.

The pattern continued into wartime. As Drew F. Lawrence reported for DefenseScoop in “Hegseth ousts Gen. George as Army chief of staff,” Hegseth removed Gen. Randy George, the Army’s top officer, on April 2, 2026, effective immediately and more than a year before his term was due to end. The Iran war was five weeks old. No reason was given. The Pentagon’s statement thanked George for his decades of service, which is the military equivalent of a firm handshake at the door.

The promotion lists. Firing is the visible half. The quieter half is the promotion list, and here the Secretary has done something few of his predecessors attempted: he has reached into lists drawn up by boards of senior officers and struck names off by hand.

In March 2026, NPR confirmed that Hegseth had stopped the promotions of four Army officers on track to become one-star generals: two Black men and two women. As NBC News reported in “Hegseth has intervened in military promotions for more than a dozen senior officers,” officials said the attributes he had cited for removals included past support for Covid vaccine or mask mandates and affiliation with diversity programmes. Five officials told the network they believed race and gender had played a role.

The Army’s own civilian secretary objected. Dan Driscoll refused Hegseth’s demands to drop the four officers, pointing to their long records of exemplary service. Hegseth struck the names himself. Driscoll resigned on August 31, 2026. Part III of this series follows what that did to the Army’s chain of command.

The Navy list told the same story. In the spring of 2026, Hegseth removed at least seven officers chosen by a board of senior admirals for promotion to one-star rank. Later counts put the figure at nine, including every woman on the list (three) and two Black men. The Times reported that the share of women among candidates for promotion to general and admiral had fallen to its lowest level in 25 years.

The ratio. Here is the number Hegseth did not read aloud at Quantico. Senator Jack Reed told the Senate, as the Spokesman-Review reported in “Hegseth strikes female and black Navy officers from promotion list,” that nearly 60 percent of the senior officers Hegseth has fired are women or Black. Women and minorities make up fewer than 20 percent of generals and admirals. The Times’s broader count of more than 80 fired or blocked officers puts the share of women and minority officers at almost half. Either way, a group that holds one star in five has absorbed something like half to three-fifths of the removals.

Republicans have noticed. As Burgess Everett and Shelby Talcott reported for Semafor on September 28, 2026, in “Republicans fuming at Hegseth as war costs build,” Senator Susan Collins of Maine said Hegseth had blocked promotions for some of the military’s most capable officers. “The number of women and Black people that had been struck from the list was awful,” she said.

How firing generals used to work. None of this would be remarkable if the point were simply that a defence secretary fired generals. Secretaries fire generals; it is one of the reasons civilian control exists. What is unusual is the silence.

The modern precedents all came with reasons. In June 2008, Robert Gates forced out both the Air Force’s chief of staff, Gen. T. Michael Moseley, and its civilian secretary, Michael Wynne, after a string of failures in the handling of nuclear weapons and components. He said so publicly, citing an investigation. In May 2009, Gates removed Gen. David McKiernan as commander in Afghanistan and explained that he wanted fresh thinking for a new strategy. In June 2010, President Obama accepted Gen. Stanley McChrystal’s resignation after a magazine article in which the general’s staff mocked civilian officials, and he explained that too, in a statement from the Rose Garden.

One can argue with any of those decisions. But the officers involved, the Congress and the public knew why they had happened. The reason was part of the decision, because the reason is what tells everyone still serving what the standard is. A firing without a reason sets a standard as well: that the standard is whatever the Secretary says it is on the day.

The motive question. Does the pattern prove intent? Strictly, no. Hegseth has never explained an individual firing or deletion. When pressed in hearings, he has said that he does not discuss such decisions “out of respect for those officers.” That silence makes the motive impossible to prove. It also leaves the reader with only three pieces of evidence to weigh.

The first is the numbers above. The second is his own published record. In The War on Warriors, published June 4, 2024, he argued that diversity programmes had weakened the force, and in the years before his appointment he suggested that some Black and female senior officers had been promoted for reasons other than merit. The third is the explanation he offered at Quantico: that the purged generals “clung to the woke department.” He did not say what clinging looked like, or how a promotion board’s choice of a female admiral counted as an example.

Senator Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut, speaking to the Washington Sun on the day of the speech, conceded there could be a case for fewer senior officers. His objection was to the method. He said there was no evidence of any systematic or strategic review, either of the numbers or of the individuals. “If you’re a woman or minority,” he said, “there’s really no future for you here.”

The responsible conclusion is narrower than the angriest commentary and wider than the Secretary would like. The removals fell disproportionately on women and Black officers. The man doing the removing has a public record of doubting their credentials. He refuses to give reasons. Whatever he intended, that is the record he has chosen to leave.

The Bill

The bench. A general is the product of about twenty-five years of selection. Every officer removed early takes with them experience the Army or Navy cannot replace in a single promotion cycle, and in wartime the gap matters more. Reed told Hegseth at a hearing that he was hollowing out the military’s bench of its most experienced and highest-performing senior officers. The phrase “bench strength” is a cliché. It is also, for a force fighting a seven-month war against Iran while trying to deter China, simply true.

The pipeline. The audience at Quantico was chosen deliberately. Hegseth told the troops he had “a sweet spot, a special thing for E6s and O3s,” the staff sergeants and captains from whom the next generation of generals and senior enlisted leaders will come. Some of the captains in that room were women. Some were Black. They have now watched the military’s most senior woman, its most senior Black officer, and a measurable share of every promotion list since be removed without explanation. A retired four-star general told Federal News Network that people will leave early because they see no opportunity. The cost of that does not show up in 2026. It shows up in 2040, when the colonels who would have been generals have quietly become consultants.

The process. Promotion boards exist so that no single official chooses who rises. The officers on them are themselves selected for judgement, and the system’s whole value is that it is hard to bend. A secretary who strikes names from the board’s list by hand, overrules his own service secretary to do it, and declines to explain why has converted a merit process into a personal one. His new “joint warfighter evaluation” may well be rigorous. But whatever it measures, the final list now passes through one man’s hands.

The precedent. There is a cost that will not show up until 2029. David French, the New York Times columnist and a former Army lawyer who served in Iraq, said on the Times podcast “The Opinions” on October 7, 2026, in an episode titled “The Problem With Hegseth’s Pentagon,” that one of the fired generals had told him, in effect, that they had been fired for carrying out lawful orders. French’s guest, Mike Nelson, a retired Special Forces officer, sorted the charges against the purged officers into three piles: diversity programmes, the Covid vaccine mandate and the withdrawal from Afghanistan. Each can be argued against. Each was a lawful order from an elected president, passed down through civilian leadership. Nelson then asked the obvious question: would Hegseth want the generals he is promoting to be fired in January 2029 for carrying out his orders?

That is the real danger of punishing officers for their predecessors’ policies. It turns every change of administration into a purge, and teaches every general that the safest career strategy is not competence but correctly guessing who will win the next election. A military that learns that lesson has stopped being nonpartisan, whichever party is in power.

The norm. In September 2025 the generals at Quantico sat in silence through a speech that insulted them, because silence is how a nonpartisan officer corps says we serve whoever wins. A year on, the most senior of those officers are disproportionately gone, and the man who removed them has redefined their silence as “clinging.” Every officer still serving has drawn the lesson. Some will draw it in the right direction: competence matters, keep your head down, do the job. Others will draw a different one: loyalty matters more than the board’s opinion of you.

A defence minister who boasts of firing a tenth of his generals during a war invites one obvious question: which ones, and why? Hegseth has been asked it many times. He has answered it once, at Quantico, with a number.
