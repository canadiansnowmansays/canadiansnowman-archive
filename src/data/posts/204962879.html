by Stephen Gordon

I count my essays. The number climbs, it lands on a year that already means something, and I stop and tell you what. This is essay 1,453, and I have been waiting for this number, because 1453 is one of the genuine hinges of human history — one of the small handful of years where you can stand and say, with very little exaggeration, that the world was one thing before it and another thing after.

On the twenty-ninth of May, 1453, the city of Constantinople fell to the army of the Ottoman sultan Mehmed II, and the Roman Empire ended.

I want you to feel the weight of that second clause, because it is easy to skate past. Not the Byzantine Empire, that tidy textbook label historians invented later to keep their eras straight. The people inside those walls did not call themselves Byzantines. They called themselves Romans. They were the direct, unbroken, legal continuation of the empire Augustus founded — the same state, moved east to Constantine’s city eleven hundred years before, having outlived the fall of Rome itself in the west by nearly a thousand years. When those walls were breached in 1453, an institution that had begun before the birth of Christ finally, after roughly fifteen centuries, stopped existing. The last Roman emperor, Constantine XI, died somewhere in the fighting, his body never certainly identified — the Roman Empire ending not with a treaty or a ceremony but with a middle-aged man vanishing into a melee at a broken gate.

Fifteen hundred years. That is the number under this essay. Rome, in one form or another, lasted fifteen hundred years, and it ended on a Tuesday in May because a twenty-one-year-old sultan had better cannons than the walls were built to withstand.

Let me stay on the cannons for a moment, because they are the hinge inside the hinge.

The walls of Constantinople were the most formidable fortifications in the world and had been for a thousand years. They had turned away army after army for a millennium. What finally beat them was not courage or numbers — it was artillery, gunpowder cannon of a size the world had not seen, one monster bombard so large it had to be dragged to the walls by sixty oxen and could be fired only a few times a day. The age of the high stone wall, the age of the castle, the age that had defined European warfare and European power for centuries, ended at those walls in 1453, because gunpowder had arrived and stone was suddenly obsolete.

So 1453 is not one turning point. It is several, stacked. The end of the Roman Empire. The end of the Middle Ages — many historians mark the medieval period as closing precisely here. The end of the age of walls. And the rise of the Ottoman Empire as the dominant power of the eastern Mediterranean, which it would remain for four hundred years. Whole eras hinge on this one year. Careers of civilizations turn.

And there is one more consequence, quieter, that matters more to this series than all the rest — because with Constantinople closed to them, European traders lost their overland routes east, and began, urgently, looking for another way to the riches of Asia. A way by sea. Around Africa, perhaps. Or — a wilder idea, dismissed by serious people — straight west, across the open Atlantic, on the theory that the world was round and you’d get there eventually. Hold that thought. It becomes essay 1,492, and it starts here, in the rubble of a fallen city, with merchants who suddenly needed a new road.

Now I have to address the phrase, because I cannot write an essay called turning point in this particular decade and pretend the phrase hasn’t been claimed.

There is, in the country I spend a great deal of these essays observing, an organization called Turning Point. You have heard of it. It is a political operation, energetic and well-funded, that has spent years and a great deal of money positioning itself as the pivot of history, the hinge on which the future swings, the moment everything changes. It is very confident about being a turning point. It says so, often, in its name and its branding and its considerable noise.

So let me offer, in the driest spirit of public service, a small comparison for scale.

This is a turning point: an empire fifteen centuries old, the last living thread connecting the modern world to classical antiquity, extinguished in a single morning; the technology of a thousand years of warfare rendered obsolete between sunrise and sunset; the medieval world ending and the modern world beginning; and, as a footnote, the entire course of global exploration redirected westward in a way that would, within forty years, put Europeans on a continent they did not know existed. All of it, one Tuesday, one year, one number: 1453.

And that is a conference with good lighting.

I do not say this to be cruel, or only a little. I say it because this series is, at its foundation, about the difference between a number that means something and a number that has been told to mean something — between weight that is earned and weight that is merely asserted, loudly, by people with a logo. 1453 did not have to announce that it was a turning point. It did not brand itself. There was no press release from the walls of Constantinople. It simply was one, so completely that we still organize the textbooks around it six centuries later. That is what an actual turning point looks like: it doesn’t tell you. It just turns, and everything after is different, and no one needs convincing.

Here is the part that stays with me, underneath the easy shot.

When Constantinople fell, a great many Greek scholars — men who had spent their lives preserving the manuscripts of the ancient world, the philosophy and mathematics and literature of Greece and Rome — fled west, carrying what they could. They arrived in Italy with their arms full of books, the accumulated memory of the classical world, and they arrived at exactly the moment that a goldsmith in Mainz had just worked out how to make books multiply.

Do you see it? I only saw it myself while writing this. Essay 1,440 and essay 1,453 are the same story told from two ends. Gutenberg builds the machine that can copy any text endlessly — and thirteen years later, Constantinople falls and floods Italy with the very texts most worth copying, the salvaged brain of antiquity, delivered into the one civilization that had just acquired the power to reproduce it without limit. The Renaissance is, in large part, what happened when those two events met: the rescued knowledge of the ancient world, arriving precisely when a machine existed to make it common.

The city died and its memory scattered west into a machine that could keep it alive forever. That is not a turning point you can put on a lanyard. That is history doing the thing it does only rarely, where two unrelated events on the count turn out to have been waiting for each other all along.

So this is essay 1,453, and it is the hinge of the whole window I have been walking through — the year the ancient world finally ended and the modern one cracked open, the year the walls fell and the scholars fled and the road west opened and the count, my own strange count, happened to land right on the seam.

Fifteen hundred years of Rome, ended on a Tuesday. The Middle Ages closed. The age of walls finished. And the memory of antiquity, carried west in the arms of frightened scholars, landing in Gutenberg’s world just in time to be saved.

That is a turning point. It did not need to say so. The ones that matter never do.

I will see you at the next one — which is lighter, and involves a king, a battlefield, and, five centuries later, a parking lot. History is not always this solemn. Sometimes it buries a monarch under a municipal car park and waits for someone to notice.

Fifteen hundred years. Count it sometime, and then look at whatever is currently calling itself the turning point of history, and judge for yourself which one turned.
