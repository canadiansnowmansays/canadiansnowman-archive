II. THE VIETNAM LESSON: WHY THE WAR POWERS ACT EXISTS

Helicopters over troops in Vietnam.

The Problem (1964-1973)

To understand how we got here, you have to go back to Vietnam.

The Problem (1964-1973)

August 1964: Two U.S. Navy destroyers allegedly come under attack in the Gulf of Tonkin off the coast of North Vietnam. (It later emerges the second attack probably never happened—it was radar phantoms and panicked reporting.)

President Lyndon Johnson uses the incident to secure the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution from Congress, authorizing him to use military force in Southeast Asia. It’s not a declaration of war. It’s authorization to respond to a specific attack.

Johnson treats it as a blank check.

Over the next nine years, the United States escalates from military advisors to full-scale war. At peak involvement: 543,000 American troops in Vietnam. 58,220 Americans killed. Somewhere between 1 and 3 million Vietnamese, Cambodian, and Laotian civilians dead.

Congress never voted to declare war.

Iconic Vietnam-era image — “Flower Power” protest photo juxtaposed with soldiers.

The public discovered in 1969 that President Richard Nixon had been conducting a secret bombing campaign in Cambodia—a neutral country—without informing Congress. Operation Menu: 3,875 bombing sorties. 2,756,941 tons of ordnance dropped. All without congressional knowledge, let alone authorization.

The lesson was stark: A president could wage a decade-long war killing tens of thousands of Americans and millions of Southeast Asians, and Congress would be powerless to stop it because Congress had never formally declared war and couldn’t agree on how to end something it never officially started.

The Response: War Powers Resolution (1973)

Congress in session (House or Senate chamber) during the 1973 override of Nixon’s veto.

Congress finally acted.

On November 7, 1973, over President Nixon’s veto, Congress passed the War Powers Resolution. The vote to override Nixon’s veto: 284-135 in the House, 75-18 in the Senate.

The Resolution’s Key Provisions:

  • Consultation: The President must consult with Congress “in every possible instance” before introducing U.S. forces into hostilities

  • Notification: The President must notify Congress within 48 hours of committing armed forces to military action

  • Termination: The President must terminate military action within 60 days (with possible 30-day extension) unless Congress:

    • Declares war

    • Enacts specific authorization

    • Extends the 60-day period

    • Is physically unable to meet due to armed attack on the United States

  • Fast-Track Removal: Congress can force withdrawal of forces at any time via concurrent resolution (later amended to joint resolution after INS v. Chadha)

The Promise:

Never again would an American president be able to wage unauthorized war. Never again would Congress sit passively while presidents committed American forces to years-long conflicts without democratic debate and formal authorization.

The Reality:

Every president since Nixon has violated it.

And Trump has done so more systematically, more brazenly, and with more deadly consequences than any of them.

III. FIFTY YEARS OF VIOLATIONS: THE BIPARTISAN PATTERN

Here is what 50 years of “never again” looks like in practice:

GERALD FORD (1974-1977)

Mayaguez Incident (1975): Cambodian forces seized a U.S. merchant ship. Ford ordered a military rescue operation that killed 18 Marines—more Americans than were being held on the ship.

War Powers compliance: Ford notified Congress after the operation began, technically within 48 hours. But consultation beforehand? None.

Congressional response: None. The operation was over before Congress could react.

RONALD REAGAN (1981-1989)

Lebanon (1982-1984): Reagan deployed Marines as peacekeepers. When a truck bomb killed 241 American servicemen in October 1983, Congress retroactively invoked the War Powers Resolution, authorizing an 18-month mission. Reagan signed it but issued a statement saying he didn’t believe the War Powers Resolution was constitutional.

Grenada (1983): Reagan ordered invasion of Grenada two days after the Beirut bombing. Notified congressional leaders the morning of the invasion—consultation happened after the decision was made.

Libya (1986): Ordered bombing of Tripoli and Benghazi in retaliation for Libyan-linked terrorist attack on U.S. servicemen in Germany. Claimed self-defense. No congressional authorization.

Iran-Contra: Reagan’s administration funded and supplied Nicaraguan Contras in direct violation of the Boland Amendment, which specifically prohibited military aid to the Contras. When exposed, 14 officials were indicted. Reagan claimed ignorance. Nobody went to jail—most were pardoned by George H.W. Bush.

Pattern established: Claim self-defense, notify Congress perfunctorily, ignore calls for authorization, face no consequences.

GEORGE H.W. BUSH (1989-1993)

Panama (1989): Ordered invasion to capture Manuel Noriega. 23 American troops killed, hundreds of Panamanian casualties. Notified Congress after the invasion began.

Congressional response: None.

Gulf War (1991): Bush deployed 500,000 troops to Saudi Arabia after Iraq invaded Kuwait, then sought congressional authorization. Congress granted it—but only after the deployment was complete, making the vote less an authorization than a ratification of a fait accompli.

Precedent set: Deploy first, ask permission later—if at all.

BILL CLINTON (1993-2001)

Somalia (1993): Expanded mission from humanitarian relief to nation-building without congressional authorization. After 18 American soldiers killed in Mogadishu, Congress forced withdrawal.

Haiti (1994): Threatened invasion, secured last-minute diplomatic agreement. Would have proceeded without authorization.

Bosnia (1995): Deployed 20,000 troops as peacekeepers. Promised one-year mission. Troops remained for years.

Kosovo (1999): The defining Clinton violation.

73-day bombing campaign against Serbia. No congressional authorization. No UN Security Council approval. No imminent threat to the United States.

When the 60-day War Powers deadline approached, Clinton simply ignored it. Congress held votes but couldn’t agree on whether to authorize or end the campaign. Clinton continued bombing.

After 73 days, Serbia capitulated. Clinton claimed victory. Congress did nothing.

Lesson learned: If you ignore the 60-day deadline and Congress is divided, you can wage war indefinitely without authorization.

GEORGE W. BUSH (2001-2009)

Afghanistan (2001): Congress passed the Authorization for Use of Military Force (2001 AUMF) three days after 9/11, authorizing force against those responsible for the attacks.

Iraq (2003): Bush claimed Iraq had weapons of mass destruction and links to al-Qaeda. Both claims were false. But Congress authorized force anyway in the 2002 Iraq AUMF.

The WMD lie: Iraq had no weapons of mass destruction. Bush received authorization through false pretenses. But because Congress technically voted, this isn’t counted as a War Powers violation—just a catastrophic intelligence failure and presidential deception.

The 2001 AUMF precedent: The authorization to use force against “those who planned, authorized, committed, or aided” the 9/11 attacks has been stretched to justify military action in at least 19 countries against groups that didn’t exist in 2001.

It became a blank check for permanent global war.

BARACK OBAMA (2009-2017)

Libya (2011): The clearest War Powers violation before Trump.

The operation: NATO bombing campaign to support rebels against Muammar Gaddafi. Started as humanitarian intervention to prevent massacre in Benghazi. Became seven-month campaign to overthrow Gaddafi’s regime.

War Powers compliance: Obama notified Congress but never sought authorization. When the 60-day deadline approached, the administration claimed it wasn’t engaged in “hostilities” under the War Powers Resolution because:

  • No U.S. ground troops

  • No American casualties

  • Only air strikes

The absurdity: You can bomb a country for seven months, help overthrow its government, and claim you’re not engaged in hostilities.

Congressional response: Some members introduced resolutions. None passed. Obama continued bombing.

Other Obama violations:

  • Syria (2014-2017): Airstrikes against ISIS without new authorization, justified under 2001 AUMF

  • Yemen, Somalia, Pakistan: Ongoing drone strikes under counterterrorism authorities

  • Created the “60-day law”: Anything under the threshold doesn’t need authorization

DONALD TRUMP I (2017-2021)

Syria (2017, 2018): Cruise missile strikes in response to chemical weapons use. No congressional authorization.

Iran (2020): Ordered drone strike killing Iranian General Qasem Soleimani. Claimed imminent threat (never substantiated). No congressional authorization.

The Soleimani precedent: You can assassinate a senior official of a foreign government and claim self-defense.

JOE BIDEN (2021-2025)

Syria (2021, 2023): Airstrikes against Iranian-backed militias. Claimed self-defense in response to attacks on U.S. forces.

Limited violations, but the pattern continued: Every president adds to the precedent. Every president expands the boundaries of what counts as permissible unilateral action.

DONALD TRUMP II (2025-2026)

Systematized evasion into doctrine.

  • 658 strikes across 7 countries

  • 1,093+ deaths

  • Zero authorizations

  • Declared War Powers Act “unconstitutional”

  • When Congress voted to limit operations, ignored the resolution

Trump didn’t invent unauthorized presidential war-making. He perfected it.

IV. WHY EVERY PRESIDENT VIOLATES (THE INCENTIVE STRUCTURE)

Understanding the pattern requires understanding the incentives. Why does every president—Democrat and Republican, hawk and dove—violate the War Powers Resolution?

Political Incentives

Voters reward “strength”: Decisive military action polls well, especially in the short term. Rally-around-the-flag effects boost approval ratings.

Opposition to war = political liability: Politicians who vote against military action get labeled as weak, unpatriotic, or naive about threats.

National security = campaign advantage: Presidents run on being “tough.” Questioning military operations is painted as undermining troops.

Institutional Incentives

Executive power accumulation: Each violation becomes precedent. Each precedent expands what’s permissible. Each president inherits more power than the last.

Office of Legal Counsel: Every administration has lawyers who write secret memos justifying whatever the president wants to do. These memos are not subject to public scrutiny or judicial review. They create a parallel legal universe.

No consequences: Not one president has faced legal accountability for War Powers violations. Not one has been impeached for it. Not one has lost reelection because of it.

If there’s no consequence for violation, there’s no deterrent.

Practical Incentives

Speed: Acting unilaterally is faster than congressional debate. Military operations can begin immediately.

Secrecy: Congressional debate is public. Presidents can act in secret and brief only afterward.

Blame avoidance: If the operation fails, the president can say “I acted alone; Congress wasn’t on board anyway.” If it succeeds, the president takes credit.

Credit claiming: Success belongs to the president. Failure gets distributed across the decision-making apparatus. The president wins either way.

V. WHY CONGRESS WON’T ACT (THE ABDICATION)

So if presidents keep violating the War Powers Resolution, why doesn’t Congress enforce it?

Bipartisan Complicity

Both parties violate when in power. Neither wants to constrain future presidents of their own party.

Democrats under Obama: Defended Libya operation, opposed War Powers resolutions against Obama Republicans under Trump: Defend current operations, opposed War Powers resolutions against Trump I and II Pattern: My party’s president needs flexibility. Your party’s president needs constraints.

Result: Tacit bipartisan agreement to let presidents do whatever they want. Each party enables the other’s violations in exchange for freedom of action when they hold the presidency.

This is why the Venezuela resolution failed 50-50 with VP Vance breaking the tie. The five Republicans who voted for it were the exceptions. The 45 who voted against it represent the norm: protect presidential war-making authority regardless of party.

Political Risk Aversion

“Supporting the troops” politics: Once forces are committed, voting to restrict operations gets framed as abandoning troops in the field.

Electoral vulnerability: “Senator X voted to undermine our military during wartime” is a devastating campaign ad.

Donor interests: Defense contractors want maximum presidential flexibility. They donate to both parties. They don’t want congressional constraints on military operations.

Safer to complain than act: Members can criticize operations, call for briefings, express concern—but stop short of voting to end them. This gives political cover without risking a vote that could be used against them.

Institutional Cowardice

Responsibility avoidance: If the president decides and the operation fails, it’s the president’s fault. If Congress authorizes and it fails, Congress shares blame.

Easier to let the president decide: Wars are hard. Wars are unpopular. Wars cost money and lives. Better to let the president own the decision.

Won’t defend own constitutional authority: Congress has the power to declare war. But it would rather complain about presidents usurping that power than actually exercise it.

The Venezuela vote demonstrates this perfectly:

December 2025: Administration tells Congress repeatedly it will not undertake regime change January 3, 2026: Administration conducts regime change operation January 8: Senate votes 52-47 to advance War Powers resolution January 14: Senate votes 50-50, VP breaks tie against it Result: Congress tried, failed, gave up

VI. THE LEGAL FICTION MACHINE

How do administrations justify these violations? Through creative legal interpretation provided by the Office of Legal Counsel (OLC).

How OLC Works

The Office of Legal Counsel is part of the Justice Department. It provides legal advice to the executive branch. Its opinions are binding within the executive branch—they’re effectively law for the president and federal agencies.

But OLC opinions are often secret. They’re not subject to judicial review. And they’re written by lawyers whose job is to find legal justification for what the president wants to do.

Result: A parallel legal universe where the president’s actions are always legal, regardless of what courts, Congress, or legal scholars might say.

The Creative Definitions

“Hostilities”:

In the Libya operation, the Obama administration argued that seven months of bombing didn’t constitute “hostilities” under the War Powers Resolution because there were no ground troops, no American casualties, and operations were conducted at high altitude.

By this logic, you can bomb a country indefinitely as long as they can’t shoot back effectively.

“Imminent threat”:

Traditionally means an attack is about to occur—hours or days. But administrations have expanded this to mean:

  • Potential future threats

  • Capability to threaten (even if no intent demonstrated)

  • Threats to allies (collective self-defense)

If “imminent” means “could happen someday,” it means nothing.

“Self-defense”:

The Constitution allows self-defense without congressional authorization when the U.S. faces attack. But “self-defense” has been stretched to include:

  • Preemptive strikes against potential threats

  • Defense of allies

  • Protection of protesters in other countries

  • Response to attacks that occurred weeks or months earlier

“Existing authorization”:

The 2001 AUMF authorized force against “those who planned, authorized, committed, or aided” 9/11. It’s been used to justify operations against:

  • Groups that didn’t exist in 2001

  • Countries not involved in 9/11

  • Individuals with no connection to al-Qaeda

  • Operations 25 years later

It’s become authorization for permanent global war.

“Terminated”:

The Trump administration’s May 2026 contribution to the legal fiction canon. On May 1—the exact day the 60-day War Powers deadline expired for the Iran war—Trump sent letters to congressional leaders declaring: “The hostilities that began on February 28, 2026, have terminated.”

The day before, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth told the Senate Armed Services Committee that the ceasefire with Iran, which began April 8, effectively “paused” the 60-day clock. The administration’s position: if nobody is shooting at the moment, the constitutional deadline doesn’t apply.

Senator Tim Kaine called it “a very novel argument that I’ve never heard before” and said it “certainly has no legal support.” The Brennan Center’s Katherine Yon Ebright was more precise: the administration’s interpretation was “a sizeable extension of previous legal gamesmanship.” She added: “To be very, very clear and unambiguous, nothing in the text or design of the War Powers Resolution suggests that the 60-day clock can be paused or terminated.”

Senator Richard Blumenthal captured the absurdity: “There’s no pause button in the Constitution, or the War Powers Act. We’re at war. We’ve been at war for 60 days. The blockade alone is a continuing act of war.”

He was right. While Trump declared hostilities “terminated,” U.S. naval forces were maintaining a full blockade of Iranian ports. Iran continued choking the Strait of Hormuz. Trump himself warned strikes could resume at any time. In his own letter to Congress, he wrote: “The threat posed by Iran to the United States and our Armed Forces remains significant.”

Hostilities terminated—but the threat remains significant. The blockade continues. The strikes could resume. The president won’t seek authorization.

By this logic, you can pause any war long enough to dodge the deadline, then resume it as a “new” conflict. In fact, former Trump NSC official Richard Goldberg publicly recommended exactly that—simply transition to a new operation called “Epic Passage,” a sequel to Operation Epic Fury, to reset the clock entirely.

The creative definitions keep getting more creative. Obama’s “not hostilities” required lawyers to overrule the Pentagon general counsel. Trump’s “terminated” required only a letter and a ceasefire that both sides acknowledged might end at any moment.

Each fiction is thinner than the last. Each is accepted because Congress won’t challenge it.

VII. THE JUDICIAL ABDICATION

What about courts? Can’t judges enforce the War Powers Resolution?

No. They won’t.

The Standing Problem

To sue in federal court, you need “standing”—you have to show you’ve been injured. Who has standing to challenge presidential war-making?

Service members: They could argue orders to fight in unauthorized war are illegal. But courts consistently rule this is a “political question” and refuse to intervene.

Members of Congress: Some have tried suing, arguing the president violated their constitutional war powers. Courts dismiss for lack of standing—the injury isn’t individual enough.

Citizens: No standing—injury too diffuse.

Result: Nobody can sue to enforce the War Powers Resolution.

The Political Question Doctrine

Even when someone has standing, courts invoke the “political question doctrine”—certain issues are reserved to the political branches (Congress and President) and courts won’t intervene.

War powers is the classic political question. Courts say: Congress has remedies (cut funding, pass resolutions, impeach). If Congress won’t use them, courts won’t substitute judicial enforcement.

The War Powers Act Case Record

The Congressional Research Service examined War Powers Resolution litigation from 1973-2012. They found eight judicial decisions involving the resolution.

In every single case, the court declined to rule on the constitutional merits. Every time, the court found a procedural reason to dismiss: lack of standing, mootness, political question, or other justiciability doctrines.

Not one court has ever ruled on whether specific presidential actions violated the War Powers Resolution.

Pattern: Courts will not check presidential war-making.

VIII. THE IRAN MOMENT: ACCOUNTABILITY FAILURE IN REAL TIME

Let’s see how all of this played out with the Iran strikes—not as a hypothetical, but as a 60-day case study in institutional collapse.

The Launch

February 26, 2026: Hillary Clinton testifies for six hours before House Oversight Committee on Epstein connections.

February 27, 2026: Bill Clinton testifies on Epstein connections.

February 28, 2026, early morning: Trump and Netanyahu launch Operation Epic Fury against Iran.

Congressional Notification: Gang of Eight briefed by Secretary of State Marco Rubio “shortly before” strikes (technique from Part 42: notify leadership, not full Congress).

Legal Justification: Collective self-defense with Israel + protecting Iranian protesters.

Senator Andy Kim (D-NJ, former NSC Iraq Director): “Everything I have heard from the administration before and after these strikes on Iran confirms this is a war of choice with no strategic endgame.”

Senators Tim Kaine (D-VA) and Rand Paul (R-KY) introduce bipartisan War Powers Resolution requiring congressional authorization for further hostilities.

The expected outcome: failure.

The Six Votes

What followed was not a single vote but a rolling series of defeats—each one a fresh demonstration that Congress would not defend its own constitutional authority even as the 60-day deadline approached.

Vote 1 (early March): Failed along party lines. Rand Paul the only Republican voting yes. John Fetterman the only Democrat voting no.

Vote 2 (March 24): Failed 47-52. Identical partisan breakdown.

Vote 3 (April 15): Failed 47-52. Identical again. Senator Chris Murphy: “We should not fail to note how extraordinary it is that our Senate Republican leadership has declined to do any oversight of a war that is costing billions of dollars every week.”

Vote 5 (April 30): Failed 47-50. But something shifted: Senator Susan Collins of Maine broke ranks for the first time, joining Paul and the Democrats. Collins, who chairs the powerful Appropriations Committee, issued a statement: “The President’s authority as Commander-in-Chief is not without limits. The Constitution gives Congress an essential role in decisions of war and peace, and the War Powers Act establishes a clear 60-day deadline for Congress to either authorize or end U.S. involvement in foreign hostilities. That deadline is not a suggestion; it is a requirement.”

It still wasn’t enough.

Vote 6 (also around April 30): A sixth attempt also failed.

Six votes. Six failures. One constitutional deadline ignored.

The Deadline: May 1, 2026

On May 1—the day the 60-day clock expired—Trump sent letters to House Speaker Mike Johnson and Senate President Chuck Grassley declaring: “The hostilities that began on February 28, 2026, have terminated.” He added: “There has been no exchange of fire between United States Forces and Iran since April 7, 2026.”

In the same letter, he acknowledged the threat from Iran “remains significant.” He declined to seek authorization. When asked directly whether he would seek congressional approval, he said he would not “because it’s never been sought before” from other presidents.

That’s factually incorrect. Reagan negotiated an 18-month authorization with Congress for Lebanon. George H.W. Bush sought authorization for the Gulf War. George W. Bush obtained AUMFs for Afghanistan and Iraq. But accuracy isn’t the point.

The assertion is the point: I don’t need your permission. I never have. I never will.

Congressional Response

Senate Majority Leader John Thune said he did not plan to hold a vote to authorize force in Iran. “I’m listening carefully to what the members of our conference are saying, and at this point I don’t see that.”

Senator Lisa Murkowski of Alaska began drafting an Authorization for Use of Military Force but had not introduced it. She planned to wait until the Senate returned from recess the week of May 11—ten days after the deadline had already passed.

Representative Adam Smith, ranking Democrat on the House Armed Services Committee, offered the most honest assessment of any lawmaker: “Is the expectation that the Trump administration is going to follow the law? I do not have that expectation.”

What This Reveals

Even when:

  • The president launches what he calls a “massive and ongoing” war

  • The president promises it will last “as long as we want”

  • American service members are placed in harm’s way

  • A Supreme Leader of a nation of 88 million people is killed

  • Hundreds of military and approximately 100 civilian casualties result

  • The statutory 60-day deadline arrives

  • The president declares the war “terminated” while maintaining a naval blockade

Congress still cannot muster the votes to require authorization.

The Senate voted six times. It failed six times. The deadline passed. The president declared the law irrelevant. Congress went on recess.

Adam Smith was right. Nobody expects the administration to follow the law. And nobody is going to make them.

New—and alarming.

The administration’s argument that the ceasefire paused the clock was, she said, “a sizeable extension of previous legal gamesmanship.” She added: “There is a long history of executive branch lawyers willfully misinterpreting the War Powers Resolution to allow presidents to conduct hostilities even past that 60-day clock.”

A long history. And Trump just added the most brazen chapter.

X. TRUMP’S INNOVATION: MAKE THE ABDICATION EXPLICIT

Every president before Trump violated the War Powers Resolution while maintaining the legal fiction that it was constitutional.

They claimed they were complying. They offered legal justifications. They showed some deference to Congress—performative, perhaps, but present.

Trump rejects the fiction entirely.

On January 8, 2026, five days after extracting Maduro from Caracas, Trump posted on Truth Social:

President Donald Trump signs executive actions. Trump did not invent unauthorized war-making — he perfected it and made the abdication explicit by declaring the War Powers Resolution unconstitutional.

This is factually incorrect—courts have never ruled the War Powers Resolution unconstitutional. But accuracy isn’t the point.

The declaration is the point.

It’s not going to change anything about how we conduct foreign policy over the next couple of weeks, the next couple of months. And that will continue to be how we approach things.”

A fake law. Not merely unconstitutional in the traditional legal sense—the Vice President didn’t argue the finer points of Article II or cite competing OLC opinions. He called the law fake. A thing that doesn’t exist. A thing that will change nothing.

The Vice President of the United States, in his constitutional role as President of the Senate—the body that passed the War Powers Resolution over Nixon’s veto by a vote of 75-18—called the law that body enacted a fake.

Trump is asserting:

  • He has unlimited war-making authority under Article II

  • Congress has no power to constrain him

  • The War Powers Resolution is invalid

Previous presidents:

Trump:

The difference:

Obama argued Libya wasn’t “hostilities.” Trump argues he doesn’t need congressional authorization for hostilities.

On May 1, when the deadline arrived, Trump didn’t seek authorization, didn’t invoke the 30-day extension, didn’t even acknowledge the law’s legitimacy. He wrote a letter claiming hostilities had “terminated”—while maintaining a naval blockade and warning strikes could resume—and went to a rally in Florida.

XI. WHAT CONGRESS COULD DO (BUT WON’T)

Congress has tools. It chooses not to use them.

Available Remedies

  1. War Powers Resolutions

Force vote on terminating operations. Fast-track process (no filibuster in Senate). Requires simple majority in both chambers.

Problem: Requires political will. Faces presidential veto. Override requires 2/3 vote—nearly impossible in partisan environment. The Senate voted six times on Iran. Failed six times.

  1. Power of the Purse

Refuse to appropriate funds for operations. Cut defense budget. Defund specific missions.

Problem: “Abandoning troops” attack ads. Defense contractor lobbying. Political suicide. The administration hasn’t even submitted a supplemental appropriations request for the Iran war, and Congress hasn’t demanded one.

  1. Oversight

Demand briefings. Hold hearings. Subpoena documents. Investigate operations.

Problem: No enforcement mechanism. Administration can refuse to comply. What’s Congress going to do—impeach?

  1. Authorization for Use of Military Force

Pass an AUMF with conditions: defined objectives, timeline, geographic scope, reporting requirements.

Problem: Senator Lisa Murkowski began drafting an Iran AUMF but hadn’t introduced it as of the May 1 deadline. She planned to wait until the Senate returned from recess the week of May 11. The deadline had already passed. The constitutional moment came and Congress was on vacation.

  1. Impeachment

“High crimes and misdemeanors” includes waging unauthorized war. The Founders considered this an impeachable offense.

Problem: Requires 67 Senate votes. Political impossibility. Has never been used for war powers violations.

What They Actually Do

Complain. Hold hearings. Introduce resolutions. Watch resolutions fail. Express concern. Go on recess. Do nothing.

The Iran war demonstrates this with a precision the Venezuela operation only hinted at:

February 28: Trump launches Operation Epic Fury without authorization. March 2: Administration notifies Congress, starting 60-day clock. March–April: Democrats force six War Powers votes. All fail. April 30: Susan Collins breaks ranks. Still not enough. May 1: 60-day deadline arrives. Trump declares hostilities “terminated.” May 1: Senate Majority Leader says he sees no need for authorization vote. May 2: Congress begins recess.

The pattern: Try, fail, accept defeat, go home.

XII. THE CONSTITUTIONAL CRISIS

We are living through a constitutional crisis that most people don’t recognize as such because it’s happening in slow motion over 50 years.

The Framers’ Design:

James Madison, Federalist 51: “Ambition must be made to counteract ambition.”

The Constitution splits war power:

  • Congress: Declares war, funds military

  • President: Commands forces once war is declared

  • Courts: Interpret laws

Each branch checks the others.

Modern Reality:

  • President: Decides when to go to war, where, against whom, for how long, with what objectives

  • Congress: Complains, holds hearings, introduces resolutions, votes six times, fails six times, goes on recess

  • Courts: Refuse to intervene, call it “political question”

All checks removed.

The president has accumulated sole authority over war-making. Congress won’t defend its own power. Courts won’t enforce constitutional limits.

The result: One person decides whether America goes to war.

XIII. THE FOUNDERS WARNED US

They knew this would happen. They designed the Constitution specifically to prevent it.

James Madison:
