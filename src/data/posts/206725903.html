Part VI left England in 1225, with a nine-year-old Henry III’s regency council reissuing Magna Carta as desperate political leverage rather than sacred principle. Four centuries then pass before this series returns to English soil, in Part VIII, to watch Charles I die for refusing to accept the very limits his own great-great-grandfather’s family had, by then, already learned to work around rather than defy outright. That four-century gap is not empty. It contains the Plantagenet consolidation of the common law, the Wars of the Roses that nearly extinguished the crown’s own bloodline entirely, and, most consequentially for everything that follows, the Tudor dynasty’s own invention of an entirely new religious legitimating structure, built by a king who wanted something no pope was prepared to give him and who concluded, with a directness this series has not seen since Napoleon crowned himself in Part XIII, that the solution was simply to build a church that would give it to him instead.

The centuries immediately following Magna Carta saw English kingship consolidate its administrative machinery considerably further than John’s own reign, chaotic and financially desperate, had ever allowed. Edward I, reigning from 1272 to 1307, expanded the reach and consistency of English common law and summoned what later historians would call the Model Parliament in 1295, drawing together nobles, clergy, and elected representatives of counties and towns in a combination this series recognizes as an early ancestor of the two-chamber structure this series traced through Britain’s own later constitutional evolution in Part XVII, even though Edward’s own Parliament remained a body he convened chiefly to secure funding for his wars against Scotland and Wales rather than any genuine check on his own authority. The following two centuries brought the Hundred Years’ War against France, a prolonged dynastic and territorial conflict that drained English resources without ever securing the French crown English kings periodically claimed, and then, most destructively of all for the monarchy’s own institutional stability, the Wars of the Roses, a series of civil conflicts between the rival houses of Lancaster and York fought intermittently from 1455 to 1487 over competing claims to the same throne, a dynastic bloodletting this series recognizes as considerably closer in spirit to the succession wars this series traced through the Mughal Empire in Part IX than to any of England’s own earlier crises.

It was the eventual victor of this prolonged civil conflict, Henry Tudor, defeating and killing King Richard III at the Battle of Bosworth Field in 1485 and founding the Tudor dynasty as Henry VII, who brought a genuine and durable end to the Wars of the Roses through the same mechanism this series has traced binding kingdoms together across Part V’s own account of medieval European marriage diplomacy: Henry married Elizabeth of York, uniting the two warring houses’ own competing bloodlines within a single new dynasty, and spent the remainder of his reign rebuilding a royal treasury the preceding decades of civil war had left genuinely depleted, through a combination of careful fiscal administration and a notably tight-fisted personal frugality that later Tudor monarchs, his own son especially, would abandon almost entirely.

Henry VIII, ascending the throne in 1509 at only seventeen years of age, inherited this restored treasury and, for the first two decades of his reign, presented himself as a genuinely orthodox and even zealous defender of the Catholic Church, going so far as to write a theological treatise attacking Martin Luther’s own Protestant reforms in 1521, a work that earned him the title Defender of the Faith from Pope Leo X, a title English monarchs, in one of this series’ more quietly ironic footnotes, continue to hold formally to this day despite everything that followed. What changed this trajectory entirely was a dynastic problem this series recognizes immediately from nearly every monarchy examined across its previous seven parts: the absence of a surviving male heir. Henry’s marriage to Catherine of Aragon, originally his own brother’s widow and inherited as a bride through the same kind of dynastic marriage diplomacy this series traced through the Habsburgs in Part V, produced only a single surviving child, a daughter, Mary, and by the late 1520s Henry had become convinced, whether from genuine theological conviction or convenient self-interest, that his marriage to his brother’s former wife had been cursed by God under a specific biblical prohibition, and that only an annulment, freeing him to marry again and finally secure a male heir, could resolve it.

Pope Clement VII’s refusal to grant this annulment is the hinge on which this entire essay, and much of the rest of English religious and political history, turns, and the refusal owed rather less to theological conviction on the Pope’s own part than to the same kind of practical political leverage this series has traced determining nearly every legitimacy dispute examined across its previous parts: Catherine of Aragon was the aunt of Holy Roman Emperor Charles V, the same Habsburg ruler this series traced assembling an empire on which the sun never set in Part V, and Charles’s own armies had, only shortly before, sacked Rome itself in 1527 and left Clement effectively a prisoner within his own besieged city, making any papal decision humiliating Charles’s own aunt a practical impossibility regardless of the underlying theological merits either way.

Henry’s response to this refusal, worked out over several years with the assistance of reform-minded advisors including Thomas Cromwell, was not to simply defy the Pope on this single question but to dismantle the Pope’s authority over the English church entirely. The Act of Supremacy, passed by Parliament in 1534, declared Henry himself Supreme Head of the Church of England, severing the English church’s institutional obedience to Rome in a single legislative stroke and granting Henry the authority to resolve his own marital question through an entirely new, English ecclesiastical structure answerable to no foreign pope at all. This is, this series would note directly, a considerably more radical solution than anything examined across its previous seven parts: rather than negotiate around an existing religious authority, as Akbar would later attempt through inclusive accommodation in Mughal India, or invoke a rival religious framework, as Wu Zetian reached for Buddhist scripture in Tang China, Henry simply declared himself the new religious authority outright, collapsing the church-state division this series has traced maintaining a genuine separation of legitimating power in nearly every monarchy examined so far into a single office held by one man.

The practical consequences of this declaration extended far beyond Henry’s own marital convenience, and reshaped English society at a scale this series has not documented any single legislative act achieving anywhere else in its survey. The Dissolution of the Monasteries, carried out between 1536 and 1541 under Cromwell’s direct administration, closed every monastery, priory, and convent in England, seized their considerable accumulated land and wealth for the crown, and redistributed much of that property to a new class of gentry and nobility whose own fortunes and, crucially, whose own loyalty became directly bound to the continuation of Henry’s own religious settlement, since any restoration of Catholic authority would have required these new landholders to surrender property many of their own families would hold for centuries afterward. This is, this series would observe, a genuinely brilliant piece of legitimacy engineering considered purely as statecraft: Henry did not merely declare a new religious authority, he manufactured, in a single decade, an entire class of English subjects whose own material self-interest was now permanently tied to defending it.

The human cost of enforcing this new religious settlement fell not only on the dissolved monasteries but on individual men who had served Henry loyally in other capacities and could not, in conscience, follow him this far. Thomas More, Henry’s own former Lord Chancellor and one of the most respected legal and humanist scholars in Europe, refused to sign the Act of Supremacy or acknowledge Henry’s new authority over the church, maintaining a careful public silence rather than open defiance for as long as the law technically permitted, before being tried for treason on the strength of a single disputed private conversation reported by a hostile witness, and executed in 1535, a casualty this series notes as a demonstration that Henry’s new legitimating structure, however cleverly engineered to secure the loyalty of England’s new landed gentry, still required, at its foundation, the same willingness to eliminate principled dissent this series has traced through nearly every monarchy examined across its previous six parts. Thomas Cromwell himself, the chief architect of the entire dissolution and supremacy program, fared little better in the end: having fallen from Henry’s favor following the political embarrassment of arranging the king’s brief and unhappy fourth marriage to Anne of Cleves, Cromwell was arrested on fabricated charges of treason and heresy and executed in 1540, a reminder that even the men who build a king’s new legitimating machinery remain, in the end, as disposable as any other subject the moment their usefulness expires.

Henry’s own marital history following the break with Rome produced, in rapid and often brutal succession, the six wives English schoolchildren still learn by the mnemonic divorced, beheaded, died, divorced, beheaded, survived, a sequence this series notes primarily for what it reveals about the genuinely desperate weight Henry placed on securing a male heir even after having remade an entire national church specifically to solve that same problem. Anne Boleyn, the very woman Henry’s break with Rome had been substantially undertaken to marry, produced only a daughter, the future Elizabeth I, before being executed on charges of adultery and treason in 1536 that most modern historians regard as largely fabricated to clear the way for Henry’s next marriage. It was Henry’s third wife, Jane Seymour, who finally delivered the male heir he had spent over a decade and an entire national religious revolution pursuing, a son, Edward, born in 1537, though Jane herself died shortly after childbirth, leaving Henry a widower once again within the same year his dynastic problem had finally, however briefly, appeared solved.

Edward VI, ascending the throne in 1547 at only nine years of age, reigned for a brief six years under a series of regency councils that pushed England’s religious settlement considerably further toward Protestant doctrine than Henry’s own more theologically conservative break with Rome had ever intended, before dying of what was likely tuberculosis in 1553 at only fifteen, without any direct heir of his own, plunging the Tudor succession into exactly the kind of crisis Henry’s entire marital saga had been designed to prevent. The throne passed briefly and disastrously to Lady Jane Grey, a cousin installed by a faction attempting to preserve Protestant succession against Henry’s Catholic elder daughter Mary’s own legitimate claim, before Mary herself, rallying popular and noble support within days, deposed Jane after a reign of only nine days and had her cousin executed the following year. Mary I’s own five-year reign attempted to reverse Henry’s religious revolution entirely, restoring Catholic authority and earning, through her aggressive persecution of Protestant reformers, including the burning of some three hundred individuals at the stake, the enduring nickname Bloody Mary, a reign this series notes as a genuinely stark demonstration of how quickly a legitimating religious settlement, however thoroughly institutionalized under one monarch, could be reversed by the next.

It was Henry’s second daughter, Elizabeth I, ascending the throne in 1558 following Mary’s own death without an heir, who finally gave the Tudor religious settlement the durability that had eluded every one of her father’s own increasingly desperate marital and dynastic maneuvers. Elizabeth’s own reign, lasting nearly forty-five years until her death in 1603, established a moderate Protestant religious settlement, the Elizabethan Religious Settlement of 1559, that avoided the more extreme doctrinal swings of her brother’s and sister’s respective reigns, presided over the defeat of the Spanish Armada in 1588, a naval victory that secured English Protestant independence against the same Catholic Habsburg power this series traced across Part V, and cultivated, through a deliberate and remarkably sophisticated program of public image-making this series recognizes as directly comparable to Queen Victoria’s own later ceremonial prominence examined in Part XVII, a personal cult of the Virgin Queen that substituted for the dynastic marriage alliance every other monarch examined in this series’ medieval European chapters had relied on, since Elizabeth, deliberately and famously, never married at all, choosing instead to present her own permanent virginity as itself a form of national marriage, wedded symbolically to England rather than to any single foreign prince.

Elizabeth’s own reign carried its own version of the succession-crisis violence this series has now traced through nearly every dynasty examined so far, centered on her cousin Mary, Queen of Scots, a Catholic claimant whose own hereditary proximity to the English throne made her, whether she actively sought the role or not, the natural rallying figure for every Catholic plot against Elizabeth’s Protestant settlement across several decades. Mary, having already been forced to abdicate her own Scottish throne amid scandal and rebellion in 1567, fled to England seeking her cousin’s protection, and spent nineteen years there instead as a closely watched prisoner, until Elizabeth’s own advisors, having intercepted correspondence implicating Mary in a specific assassination plot, finally persuaded a genuinely reluctant Elizabeth to sign her cousin’s death warrant, and Mary was executed in 1587, a decision Elizabeth herself reportedly agonized over and later claimed had been carried out without her full final authorization, a genuinely rare moment of a monarch in this series expressing something resembling regret over an execution her own government had all but forced upon her.

Elizabeth’s own death without an heir in 1603 ended the Tudor line entirely, passing the throne to her Scottish cousin James VI of Scotland, who became James I of England and whose own theological convictions about divine-right monarchy, examined at length in this series’ account of his son Charles I’s reign in Part VIII, drew directly and explicitly on the same absolute authority his Tudor predecessors had assembled through Henry’s own break with Rome, without inheriting the same practical political skill Elizabeth had used to hold that authority’s more dangerous implications in careful check.

What this four-century gap between Magna Carta and the English Civil War adds to this series’ broader argument is a genuinely important refinement of the Rome comparison this series drew in Part IV: where Augustus built an entire vocabulary of borrowed Republican terms specifically to disguise the substance of one-man rule, Henry VIII took the opposite approach entirely, declaring the substance of his own new authority as loudly and as legislatively explicitly as any ruler examined anywhere in this series, while dressing that declaration in the borrowed clothing of religious reform rather than constitutional continuity. Both men were solving the same underlying problem, a gap between the power they wanted and the legitimating structure available to grant it, and both men found their own durable solution not by seizing more power through force alone, but by rewriting the very institution that would certify whether that power was legitimate in the first place.

Part VIII returns to where this series’ original account of England left off, tracing how Charles I inherited this same Tudor-built theology of near-absolute authority, and discovered, at the cost of his own head, exactly how far his own Parliament was prepared to let that inheritance stretch before deciding a scaffold was the only remaining remedy.
