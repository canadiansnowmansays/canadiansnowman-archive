For eighteen years I worked in the Office of the President at Rogers, where the complaints went once every other door had closed. They arrived from customers, regulators, reporters and executives. Every one of them came down to the same three questions: what was promised, what actually happened, and what is owed now.

Those questions never went away when I retired. They simply changed address. These days they are aimed at governments rather than phone companies, and the file folders have become ledgers.

An essay is an argument made on a particular day. It can be right, wrong or overtaken by events within a week. A ledger is a different animal. It sits underneath the essays and keeps counting after the essays have scrolled out of sight. When a court rules, a budget passes or a promise quietly lapses, the ledger changes and the essay that relies on it stays honest.

This page is the front door. It lists every ledger I have made public, grouped by the job each one does, with a short note on what you will find inside. Each was built in open collaboration with Claude. I set the questions, the reporting is checked against more than one source wherever a second exists, and every ledger shows the date of its latest update.

They come in two kinds. The accountability ledgers keep the record of what was done, and in two cases what Congress can do about it. The draft bills go a step further and set out, section by section, what a fix could look like. A last entry sits closer to home: my own filing with Canada’s telecom regulator.

Part One: The accountability ledgers

The Reversal Ledger

Companion to The Pendulum and the Ratchet

This is the master table: every policy, rule, law, institution or commitment that the Trump administrations, in 2017 to 2021 and from 2025, have undone or tried to undo.

Each entry is sorted three ways. The first is who created what was undone. The second is where the idea for undoing it came from: Project 2025, the conservative legal movement, an industry request, congressional allies or the president himself. The third, which I find the most revealing, is how hard it would be to put back. That runs from a pen stroke, which a new president can reverse on the first day, through rulemaking and acts of Congress, to “locked”, where a court ruling, a contract or partners who have moved on make restoration impossible. Click any row for its current status and for what cannot be restored even if the policy returns.

The series title explains the arrangement. A pendulum swings back. A ratchet only turns one way. The ledger shows which is which.

The Seed Corn Ledger

Companion to The Endless Frontier

Eighty years of federally funded American science, laid out in three columns: the dividend, the strain and the gain.

The dividend is what the money bought. ENIAC led to UNIVAC, ARPANET to the commercial internet, the National Bureau of Standards’ atomic clock to GPS, and NIH-funded RNA work to the COVID vaccines. The strain is what is breaking now: frozen grants, money Congress appropriated that never reached a laboratory, and science agencies stripped of staff. The gain is who is harvesting what America planted. That means researchers moving to Canada, Europe and China, often by name.

Inside there is a “Vought Gap” table comparing what the White House asked for, what Congress gave and what is actually happening. A payoff chart shows how long past research took to pay off and when today’s cancelled work would have paid off on the same clock. A promise tracker logs official claims against the record.

The Recovery Ledger

Companion to The RFK Files

This one looks forward. It is a working agenda for the Democratic Congress that takes office in January 2027. It is also written for anyone who came to Make America Healthy Again because they wanted cleaner food and fewer chemicals in their children’s lives.

Each entry names what Robert F. Kennedy Jr. did and what should replace it. It names the lever that can actually move it: appropriations, oversight, a bipartisan bill, the courts or the states. It also says whether MAHA voters are likely to be allies, split or opposed. Some entries reverse Kennedy decisions. Others keep and strengthen the things he got right, such as phasing out synthetic food dyes and closing the loophole that lets companies declare their own additives safe. You can filter by what belongs in the first hundred days.

The Reckoning Ledger

Companion to He Loves War: The Hegseth Files

On 10 November 2026, the House voted 211 to 204 to send Thomas Massie’s articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to committee instead of debating them. The questions behind those articles did not go to committee with them.

The Reckoning Ledger sets those questions out in the same format as the Recovery Ledger, grouped by area. They include war powers and the unauthorised war with Iran, the Minab school strike, the boat strikes, the dismissal of senior commanders and military lawyers, the expulsion of the Pentagon press corps and the record budget request. Each entry gives the lever Congress holds and whether Republicans are likely to help. Where the Pentagon claims a success, such as its recruiting figures, the ledger says so and asks for it to be verified rather than dismissed. A ledger that credits what works is harder to wave away as partisan.

The Overlord Ledger

Companion to An Oligarch Review

This one is a single place to compare the billionaires that An Oligarch Review assesses one by one. Elon Musk and Jeff Bezos are there, but so are the others who now sit close enough to power to bend it.

The scores from each essay sit side by side, so readers can compare across the whole field instead of holding one essay in their heads while reading the next. Each oligarch also carries a “political contributions” line. It is deliberately unscored, because the money speaks for itself. The ledger is updated whenever a score changes.

Part Two: The draft bills

Criticism is cheap. Anyone can point at a broken thing. The harder exercise is to write down, in the dull language of statute, what you would do instead and what it would cost. These two ledgers are my attempt.

Both are a neighbour’s draft. I am a Canadian, a retiree living on the Canada Pension Plan and Old Age Security, and not legislative counsel. The figures are estimates built from official scores of similar provisions, not an official score of either bill. Both are designed for the Washington that actually exists from January 2027: Democratic majorities in Congress and a Republican president holding the veto. Neither side can pass anything alone. The 1983 Social Security rescue was born in exactly that arrangement.

The Code Blue Ledger: The American Health Security Act of 2027

Companion to Code Blue: America’s 80-Year Failure to Solve Healthcare

This is the closing bill from Code Blue, rebuilt after a review of the original. It takes steps that have already won support from both parties and pays for them with savings the Congressional Budget Office has scored. Universal coverage stays as the long-term goal.

The bill restores the enhanced premium subsidies for three years and brings back the incentive for states to expand Medicaid. It writes price transparency into law and pays hospital outpatient departments the same as doctors’ offices for the same care. It ends surprise ambulance bills, bans spread pricing by pharmacy benefit managers in Medicaid, and funds 14,000 new residency positions. Each side’s gains and concessions are set out plainly.

The ledger also does something I would like to see more often in Washington. It sets out, line by line, what was wrong with the December 2025 version and what changed. The bigger reforms, such as a public option, Medicare at 60 and new taxes on high earners, sit in a separate “Going further” section with their price tags attached, so the ambition stays honest.

The Third Rail Ledger: The Social Security Solvency and Fairness Act of 2027

Companion to The Third Rail

The 2026 Trustees Report projects that Social Security’s retirement trust fund will run dry in late 2032. After that, every beneficiary faces an automatic cut of about 22 per cent. Doing nothing is itself a decision, and it is the cruellest one on offer.

This draft protects everyone already receiving benefits, and everyone who turns 57 by the end of 2027. It asks something of everyone else. The taxable earnings ceiling rises until it again covers 90 per cent of earnings, as it did after 1983. The payroll tax rises in small steps over twenty years. Benefits grow more slowly for the top 40 per cent of earners. The minimum benefit is strengthened, with credit for years spent caring for children or ageing parents. The retirement age stays at 67, because raising it falls hardest on the workers who die soonest.

Together those measures close about 70 per cent of the long-range gap. The rest goes to a bipartisan commission whose plan gets a guaranteed up-or-down vote. The full bill text is there, section by section, along with a table of what each party gets and gives up.

Part Three: On the record at home

The ledgers above are aimed at Washington. This last entry is aimed at Ottawa, and it is personal.

My intervention to the CRTC

Companion to Your Phone Bill and Mine

In December 2025 I helped my partner switch carriers four days into a billing cycle. Four days of service came to about $4.10. The old carrier billed $42.38 for the whole month in advance, then added a $1.22 late fee when the overcharge went unpaid. The Commission for Complaints for Telecom-television Services settled that one case, but said the practice itself was a matter for the regulator.

So in July 2026 I filed a formal intervention, under my legal name, Stephen Lawton, in the CRTC’s proceeding to harmonise its consumer protection codes (Notice of Consultation 2026-134). It asks the Commission to end advance billing and require billing in arrears. Failing that, it asks for real-time balances on cancellation and late fees calculated only on what is actually owed. The CRTC has confirmed that advance billing is within the scope of the proceeding, and the public hearing opens in Gatineau on 30 November 2026.

The intervention has drawn coverage from the Toronto Star, CBC Radio’s Cost of Living, MobileSyrup, iPhone in Canada and Peter Nowak’s Do Not Pass Go. The Public Interest Advocacy Centre quoted it in its own filing to the Commission. Both submissions, mine as intervention 14, sit on the proceeding’s public record.

These are the same three questions from my Rogers years, now asked of my own industry. The filing is the record. The series follows what happens to it.

Others keeping count

I am not the only one keeping count. The Center for Progressive Reform, with Governing for Impact, tracks the Project 2025 agenda agency by agency. The community-run Project 2025 Observer logs each objective as it is met. Just Security maps the dismantling of oversight and accountability, and the Immigration Policy Tracking Project, run with Stanford and Yale law students, catalogues every change to immigration policy across both Trump terms. They are worth bookmarking. What these ledgers add is the question I spent a career asking: not only what was done, but what it would take to put it back.

How the ledgers are kept

A ledger is only as good as its upkeep. Every one of these shows the date it was last updated. Several carry an update log so you can see what moved and when. When something turns out to be wrong, it is corrected in the open. The Code Blue Ledger is the proof: it publishes its own corrections.

If you find an error, tell me. I spent a career on the receiving end of complaints, and I would rather hear about a mistake from a reader than from events.

The ledgers are free to read, share and argue with. Governments count on short memories. A promise made in the spring is expected to be forgotten by the autumn, and a programme cut in one budget is expected to be ancient history by the next. A ledger is a small act of refusal. It remembers on the reader’s behalf, and it does not get tired. Accountability rarely arrives by accident. Someone has to keep count.

More ledgers will join this index as other series grow their own. When one does, it will be listed here.
