Parts IX and XI of this ledger traced the data-centre backlash through county tax rolls, recall elections, and the 2026 midterm map. Part XII follows the same backlash to where it is, in some ways, most surprising: high school hallways, public-records requests filed by fourteen-year-olds, and TikToks with ten million views teaching other teenagers how to speak at a city council meeting.

As Callie Holtermann reported for the New York Times on August 20, 2026, Gen Z activists across California, Colorado, Texas, and Virginia are organising against data-centre construction in their own communities — not as an abstraction about AI’s future, but as a fight over noise, water, air pollution, and property taxes happening within walking distance of their schools. What distinguishes this wave from the county-level and electoral fights this ledger has already covered is the activists’ own relationship to the technology they are protesting: most of them use the chatbots these facilities power every single day, and many are wrestling openly with what that contradiction means. The ages given below are as Holtermann reported them in August 2026.

The Speech That Started With Pink Hair

Sadie Cisneros, seventeen, had been using ChatGPT casually as a study aid before she learned a 300,000-square-foot data centre was planned near her neighbourhood in Pittsburg, California, less than half a mile from a middle school. She read a three-minute statement at a Pittsburg City Council meeting, objecting to the project’s water consumption, air pollution risk, and noise. Her own account of walking into that meeting is worth preserving directly: “I came in there with pink hair, I came in there dressed like a teenager. I don’t think they expected me to actually bring up real stuff that’s going on.” One of her TikToks teaching other teenagers how to speak at a council meeting had been viewed more than ten million times by the time Holtermann’s piece ran, and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez commented approvingly on one of her Instagram posts — a small but telling sign that this youth movement is already visible enough to draw attention from sitting members of Congress.

Cisneros’s response to her own dependence on the technology she’s protesting is direct rather than performative: she downloaded a browser that suppresses AI-generated search summaries, stopped feeding offhand questions to ChatGPT, and told Holtermann plainly, “I didn’t even need A.I. for half the stuff that I was using A.I. for.” That is a more concrete personal audit than most adult commentary on AI dependency tends to offer, and it is worth taking at face value rather than reading as activist performance.

The Fourteen-Year-Old Who Filed Records Requests

Kyle McGuffey, fourteen, of Colorado Springs, represents the wonkiest end of this youth movement, and arguably its most consequential individual contribution. Over his summer break between middle and high school, McGuffey filed public records requests under the Colorado Open Records Act related to Project Taurus, a data centre approved roughly three miles from his home. What he found in the resulting planning documents was a discrepancy this ledger’s readers will recognise as a familiar pattern from Part IX’s account of Independence, Missouri: an early report suggesting the site could draw 300 megawatts of power, against a much lower 50-megawatt figure that had been publicly advertised.

McGuffey shared his findings with local opposition groups, at least one of which used his research directly in a formal appeal against the project.

Jason Green, president of Raeden, the company behind Project Taurus, told Holtermann the 300-megawatt figure predated formal due diligence and that the approved development plan caps the site at 50 megawatts — a defence structurally identical to the “the number moved during negotiation” explanations this ledger has heard before, in Part V’s account of Nvidia’s Piketon financing figure dropping from $250 billion to $105 billion without full public explanation. Green’s response to McGuffey personally, though, stands out as notably more gracious than most corporate responses to teenage scrutiny this series has documented: “I applaud the interest, even if it’s critical of what we’re doing.” McGuffey’s own framing of his motivation avoids ideology entirely in favour of a narrower, harder-to-dismiss concern: “A.I. is already a part of our lives, and something we can’t reverse. My concern is that it’s advancing way too quickly and without public oversight.”

The project did not get the smooth passage its approval seemed to promise. As Brennen Kauffman and Alexander Edwards reported for the Colorado Springs Gazette on September 17, 2026, Colorado Springs City Council, hearing the appeals against Project Taurus, voted 7-2 to postpone its decision until April 13, 2027, so that Raeden could negotiate a large-load power contract with Colorado Springs Utilities and the city could write standards for future data centres. Of the speakers that day, 126 opposed the project and 22 supported it. The two dissenting councillors, Kimberly Gold and Nancy Henjum, said they were ready to vote and would have rejected it. Councillor Roland Rainey summed up the room: “They want some type of standards.” The meeting was disrupted enough to end in four arrests, KRDO reported on September 18, 2026, and Raeden said afterwards that it was “carefully evaluating whether or not this is the right partnership for RAEDEN.” A fourteen-year-old’s records request did not decide that outcome, but at least one of the appeals before the council had drawn on his documents.

A Coalition With No Litmus Test

The El Paso story Holtermann’s reporting surfaces is the clearest evidence yet that data-centre opposition functions as the same kind of cross-ideological binder this ledger’s Part XI already identified in Michigan’s Lawrence-Barrett race. Marco Sanchez, fifteen, founded the El Paso Youth Alliance after learning of Meta’s multibillion-dollar data centre in his city and, specifically, the 80 per cent property-tax break Meta received to build it — an incentive structure this ledger’s readers will recognise immediately from Part IX’s account of Independence, Missouri’s own lopsided tax arrangement with Nebius. Close to one hundred high school students have joined Sanchez’s group, explicitly without any political screening. His own description of the coalition’s logic is the sharpest line in the entire piece: “I don’t care if someone disagrees with me on who should be the next president if we both agree that the data center is bad.”

Sanchez’s objection is not diffuse anti-tech sentiment; it is a pointed argument about who captures the benefit of a tax break versus who bears its cost, phrased in exactly the terms this ledger has used since Part IV: “You’re screwing over working families while you give Mark Zuckerberg’s company hundreds of millions of dollars in tax rebates.” Meta’s response to Holtermann — a pledge to offset 100 per cent of its El Paso electricity use with renewable energy and a promise not to pass costs onto Texans — mirrors almost verbatim the Ratepayer Protection Pledge language this ledger’s Part XI already noted the Trump administration proposing nationally, suggesting this specific reassurance has become the industry’s standard defensive script regardless of which company or region is being challenged.

Sanchez had already taken his case to the city before Holtermann found him. Christian Andrade reported for KFOX14 on July 2, 2026 that Sanchez, then a rising sophomore living less than a mile from the Northeast El Paso construction site, had spoken during public comment at El Paso City Council and described his ambition for the Youth Alliance as building a “powerful voter force.” His stated goal was generational rather than tactical: “If I’m able to become that first domino to bleed onto years and years of active youth community involvement, that is my final goal.” The adult version of that fight had already been settled against him, at least for now. KVIA reported on June 9, 2026 that council voted 5-3 against directing city leadership to terminate Meta’s tax-incentive agreement, after a meeting with 183 registered speakers, and that Mayor Renard Johnson had warned that a losing legal dispute with Meta could leave the city paying attorney fees. The tax break Sanchez objects to survived that vote; what remains open is whether the teenagers who watched that vote will still be organising when the next one comes.

The Teenager Who Grew Up Inside Data Center Alley

Mihir Nimkar, seventeen, offers this reporting’s most nuanced voice, because he is not an outsider discovering data centres for the first time — he grew up in Loudoun County, Virginia, the subject of this ledger’s Part IX. Nimkar does not dispute the technology’s value: “Without them, we couldn’t be having this call,” he told Holtermann, referring to data centres’ basic necessity for digital life. His objection is narrower and procedural — a proposed high-voltage power line route that would run directly across his high school’s property to supply a new data centre. He and a friend, Josh Weber, formed a club called Students Against High-Voltage Expansion, organising fliers and packing school board meetings. The line in question is Dominion Energy’s Golden to Mars project, an 8.3-mile route between substations near Dulles Airport and Sterling carried on 40 monopoles 165 feet high.

As Evan Goodenow reported for the Loudoun Times-Mirror on July 20, 2026, the State Corporation Commission approved the route on June 26, 2026, and on July 15 the Loudoun County School Board itself appealed to the Virginia Supreme Court to stop the line crossing school property at Rock Ridge High School and Rosa Lee Carter Elementary School. The court has not yet ruled. Students who started out handing out fliers ended up on the same side as their own school board in a case before the state’s highest court. Nimkar continues using AI for schoolwork feedback and test preparation, and his reflection on what the fight has taught him is the most quietly devastating line in the piece: “I’ve definitely become more cognizant of what it is that happens when I use A.I. There are so many consequences that you don’t see.” That is not a rejection of the technology. It is the collapse of the assumption — one this ledger has implicitly challenged across eleven prior parts — that AI’s convenience arrives without a physical, locatable cost somewhere.

The Numbers Behind the Movement

The polling data Holtermann cites gives this youth movement more structural weight than a handful of compelling individual stories might otherwise suggest. Pew Research Center data shows young adults hold more negative views of data centres than older Americans do, even though a Gallup and Walton Family Foundation survey conducted in spring 2026 found more than half of American Gen Z-ers use generative AI regularly. Less than 20 per cent of Gen Z respondents in that same survey reported feeling hopeful about AI’s trajectory, a figure Holtermann notes has declined sharply from the previous year. Rachel Janfaza, founder of the Gen Z-focused research firm the Up and Up, offers this ledger’s most useful framing device for understanding why: many young people already feel their generation served as the uncompensated test subjects for social media’s harms, and are determined, in her words, not to repeat that history with AI.

The Booing That Wasn’t in Any Poll

One detail in Holtermann’s reporting captures the mood behind the survey numbers better than the survey numbers themselves: at graduation ceremonies across the country in spring 2026, college students booed commencement speakers who offered optimistic framing of AI’s future. A boo is not a data point Gallup can easily quantify, but it is a more honest signal than a survey response, because it costs the booer something — social friction, a moment of visible dissent in a ceremony built around consensus and celebration — in a way that answering a phone survey does not. That these speakers, presumably invited specifically because their professional standing made them credible messengers for AI’s promise, were met with organised disapproval rather than polite silence suggests the industry’s messaging problem runs deeper than unfavourable poll numbers. It suggests the standard case for AI’s benefits — the one venture capitalists and frontier labs have been making publicly for several years now — has stopped landing with the exact demographic cohort AI companies most need as long-term customers, employees, and voters.

A Familiar Corporate Script, Recognised for What It Is

What should trouble the AI industry more than any individual protest is how quickly its youngest critics have learned to recognise the industry’s standard defensive playbook and route around it. Avaio Digital’s response to Pittsburg’s opposition — that objections stemmed from misunderstanding a closed-loop cooling system — is the same genre of technical reassurance this ledger has catalogued from CloudHQ’s Hossein Fateh in Part IX and from Meta’s own spokesperson in this reporting: a company insisting the science is more reassuring than public perception allows, without directly engaging the deeper question of consent over where the facility gets sited in the first place. Teenagers organising against these projects are demonstrably capable of parsing that distinction. McGuffey did not accept Raeden’s account of the megawatt discrepancy at face value; he filed the records request that surfaced the discrepancy in the first place, and let the documents make the argument for him. A generation raised on media literacy curricula built specifically to counter algorithmic misinformation appears to be applying those same instincts to corporate public relations, and finding the same gaps between official framing and underlying documentation that this ledger has spent twelve parts finding as well.

Where This Sits in the Ledger

This series has spent eleven parts tracing AI’s financial architecture from balance sheets to bond markets to electoral politics. Part XII supplies something the earlier parts could only gesture toward: evidence that the opposition to that architecture is not a temporary artefact of the current news cycle but a generational position being formed early enough to outlast any single election, tax fight, or recall vote this ledger has documented so far. Cisneros, McGuffey, Sanchez, and Nimkar are not yet voters in most cases — several are years away from their first ballot — which means the coalition Part XI’s Michigan race described as scrambling adult partisan alignment is, underneath it, being built by people who will be voting on this exact issue for decades after the November 2026 midterms resolve one way or another. An industry that has spent this series’ first eleven parts optimising lease structures, circular financing arrangements, and bond issuance schedules has, on the evidence gathered here, done comparatively little to earn the trust of the generation that will live the longest with the infrastructure it is building today.
