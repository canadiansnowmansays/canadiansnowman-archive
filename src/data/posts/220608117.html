On 16 June 2015, Donald Trump rode an escalator down into the lobby of Trump Tower on Fifth Avenue and announced that he was running for president. The speech that followed, documented in detail on Wikipedia, is remembered for one passage: Mexico, he said, was sending people who brought drugs and crime and who were “rapists,” adding that “some, I assume, are good people.”

The political class reacted the way it had reacted to Pat Buchanan in 1992. It decided this was a stunt. The Huffington Post announced it would cover the campaign in its entertainment section. Party strategists assumed he would fade by Labor Day, or by the first debate, or by Iowa. Seventeen major candidates were in the Republican field that summer, and almost every one of them made the same calculation: let the others deal with Trump, and be standing when he collapses.

He did not collapse. That is the story of Part II, and it is less a story about Trump’s genius than about the habits of the people who were supposed to stop him.

The rules stopped applying

The first sign came on 18 July 2015, at a gathering of social conservatives in Ames, Iowa. Asked about Senator John McCain, who had spent more than five years as a prisoner of war in North Vietnam, Trump said McCain was a war hero only “because he was captured,” and added that he liked people who weren’t captured.

Under every rule of American politics since 1945, that should have ended the campaign. Mocking a POW was the sort of thing that sank candidates in an afternoon. Instead, Trump’s poll numbers rose. The lesson his supporters took was not that he had insulted a veteran. It was that he had refused to apologise to the establishment, and that the establishment’s outrage was itself proof he was on their side.

At the first televised debate, on 6 August 2015, the moderators asked whether any candidate would refuse to pledge support for the eventual nominee. Trump alone raised his hand. He was announcing, on national television, that his loyalty was to himself and not to the party. The party’s response was to keep treating him as a guest who would eventually go home.

On 7 December 2015 he called for “a total and complete shutdown” of Muslims entering the United States. Senator Lindsey Graham called him a “race-baiting, xenophobic, religious bigot.” Within two years Graham would be one of his most reliable allies and golf partners, and the arc of that one senator’s relationship with Trump is a decent short history of the whole party’s.

On 23 January 2016, at a college in Sioux Center, Iowa, Trump said he could stand in the middle of Fifth Avenue and shoot somebody and not lose voters. It was meant as a boast. It turned out to be the most accurate piece of political analysis anyone offered that year.

The free airtime

A candidate who violates every norm gets something money cannot buy: coverage. On 15 March 2016, Nicholas Confessore and Karen Yourish reported in the New York Times, under the headline “$2 Billion Worth of Free Media for Donald Trump,” that the analytics firm mediaQuant estimated he had received close to $2 billion in free media coverage, far more than any rival. Jeb Bush’s allied super PAC, Right to Rise, raised more than $100 million in 2015 and could not buy what Trump got for nothing by being outrageous on Sunday morning.

The networks knew what they were doing. On 29 February 2016, CBS chairman Les Moonves told an investor conference that the Trump campaign “may not be good for America, but it’s damn good for CBS.” That sentence deserves to be engraved somewhere. The media that had spent two decades selling the right’s audience its own siege mentality, and the mainstream media that spent two decades despising that audience, discovered together in 2016 that the same man was very good for ratings.

The collective action problem

Why did the party not unite against him? Political scientists have a dry name for it: a collective action problem. Every serious candidate preferred that Trump lose, but each preferred even more that someone else pay the price of attacking him. Bush’s super PAC spent heavily attacking Marco Rubio. Rubio and Ted Cruz attacked each other. John Kasich stayed in long after he had any path, to win Ohio. Chris Christie destroyed Rubio in a New Hampshire debate on 6 February 2016 by pointing out that Rubio kept repeating the same memorised line, and three days later Trump won New Hampshire with about 35 per cent of the vote while the establishment candidates split the rest.

The party’s primary calendar made things worse. Many later states awarded delegates on a winner-take-all or winner-take-most basis, which turned a plurality of 35 or 40 per cent into a delegate landslide. A candidate opposed by most of the party could win most of the party’s delegates, as long as the opposition stayed divided.

Jeb Bush, the candidate the donors had chosen before the race began, left it after South Carolina on 20 February 2016. His campaign is remembered best for a moment in New Hampshire on 2 February when, after a line about his brother’s presidency met silence, he asked the audience to “please clap.” The donors’ class had spent a fortune on a man who had to ask for applause. Trump had never once had to ask.

The last stand that wasn’t

The establishment did try, eventually. National Review, the magazine William F. Buckley Jr. had founded in 1955 as the house organ of fusionist conservatism, published an issue in January 2016 titled “Against Trump,” with essays by more than twenty conservative writers. On 3 March 2016, Mitt Romney, the party’s previous nominee, gave a speech at the University of Utah calling Trump “a phony, a fraud.”

None of it worked, and the reason matters for everything that follows in this series. These were exactly the voices Trump’s voters had learned to discount. Since Buchanan’s day, the base had been told by its own media that the conservative intellectual class was soft, compromised and more interested in being invited to Georgetown dinner parties than in fighting for them. When National Review and Romney came out against Trump, many of his supporters heard confirmation rather than warning. Stephanie Slade, in her October 2026 exchange with John Guida published by the New York Times and republished by the Salt Lake Tribune, describes voters choosing a fighter over figures like Romney. In 2016 the fighter was chosen partly because the gentlemen objected.

Rubio dropped out after losing his home state of Florida on 15 March. Cruz, who on the morning of the Indiana primary on 3 May 2016 called Trump “a pathological liar” and “utterly amoral,” dropped out that night after losing it. Kasich left the next day. Reince Priebus, chair of the Republican National Committee, declared Trump the presumptive nominee. Lindsey Graham posted that if the party nominated Trump, “we will get destroyed,” and “we will deserve it.”

Trump finished the primaries with roughly 14 million votes, more than any Republican primary candidate before him. Whatever the establishment thought of him, it could no longer claim he was a fluke.

The fold

What happened next is the part of the story that the next decade would repeat on a loop.

House Speaker Paul Ryan said on 5 May 2016 that he was “just not ready” to endorse Trump. On 2 June he endorsed him anyway, in an op-ed in his hometown newspaper in Janesville, Wisconsin, arguing that Trump would sign more of the House Republican agenda than Hillary Clinton would. That same week, Trump said that a federal judge, Gonzalo Curiel, could not fairly hear a fraud case against Trump University because of his Mexican heritage. On 7 June, Ryan called that “the textbook definition of a racist comment.” He did not withdraw his endorsement.

That sequence, condemnation followed by accommodation followed by condemnation that changes nothing, would become the standard Republican response to Trump for the next ten years. Readers who follow this publication’s other work will recognise it as the shape behind the TACO framework, run in reverse: it was the party, not Trump, that threatened, accommodated, capitulated and then obfuscated. Ryan’s calculation was transactional and, on its own terms, rational. He wanted tax cuts and judges. Trump could deliver both. The party leadership decided it could use the man to pass its agenda while holding its nose.

It is the oldest bargain in politics, and it rests on an assumption that the party leadership never examined: that the man was the tool and the party was the hand. In fact the base belonged to Trump, not to the party, and the moment the leaders signed on, they lost the one thing that might have restrained him, the threat of withholding support.

Cleveland

The Republican National Convention opened in Cleveland on 18 July 2016. A group of anti-Trump delegates tried to force a roll-call vote on the rules that might have freed delegates to vote their conscience. The convention chair ruled the motion down by voice vote, and the last organised effort to deny Trump the nomination ended in a few minutes of shouting on the convention floor.

On 20 July, Ted Cruz took the stage and declined to endorse the nominee. He told delegates to “vote your conscience.” He was booed off the stage. Two months later, on 23 September, he endorsed Trump anyway. Of all the capitulations of 2016, Cruz’s was the most instructive. He had been personally insulted, had his wife mocked and his father accused of a connection to the Kennedy assassination, and he still concluded that his future in the party required the man who had done it.

On 21 July 2016, Trump accepted the nomination. The speech painted the United States as a country besieged by crime, terrorism, illegal immigration and corrupt elites, and offered a single remedy: “I alone can fix it.”

That sentence is the hinge between the soil described in Part I and everything that followed. Buchanan had offered a movement. The Tea Party had offered a caucus. Trump offered himself. The grievances were collective, but the cure was personal, and that is the difference between a political movement and whatever Trumpism was becoming.

What the capture left behind

On 8 November 2016 Trump won the presidency with 304 electoral votes, while losing the popular vote by nearly three million. The party leadership that had tried and failed to stop him now had to govern with him. Most of it chose to. Ryan got his tax cut in December 2017. Mitch McConnell got his judges, including three Supreme Court justices over the full term. The donors got lower corporate rates. In exchange, the party gave Trump something it would never be able to take back: the institutional legitimacy of the Republican Party itself.

There is a lesson here that the Canadian reader may recognise from closer to home. In 2003 the Progressive Conservatives merged with the Canadian Alliance on terms that left the older party’s leaders believing they would set the tone. Within a few years the newer party’s instincts governed the merged one. Parties are not captured only by the people who storm the gates. They are captured by the people inside who decide that opening the gates is cheaper than holding them.

By the end of 2016, then, the Republican Party belonged to Trump. But a party is not an ideology, and a man who says “I alone can fix it” has a governing slogan, not a governing philosophy. The movement still lacked a theory, a cadre and a manual.

Within three years the intellectuals arrived to supply all three. That is Part III.
