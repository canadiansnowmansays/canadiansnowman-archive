Sepp Blatter liked to call FIFA a family. It is a revealing choice of word. Families are not audited, their quarrels are settled in private, and the patriarch is not voted out by the cousins. For seventeen years, from June 1998 to the spring of 2015, Blatter presided over the world’s most lucrative sporting organisation in roughly that spirit, and the remarkable thing about his reign is not how many scandals it produced. It is how few of them touched him.

That record is the subject of this essay. I want to be careful about it from the outset, because the most important legal fact about Sepp Blatter came long after the period covered here. In November 2021 Swiss federal prosecutors charged Blatter and the former UEFA president Michel Platini with fraud, disloyal management, misappropriation and forgery over a 2011 payment of two million Swiss francs. In July 2022 the Federal Criminal Court in Bellinzona acquitted both men on every count. Prosecutors appealed, and on March 25, 2025, the court’s appeals chamber acquitted them again. In August 2025 the Office of the Attorney General said it would not take the case to the Federal Supreme Court, and the acquittals are final. Blatter has never been convicted of any crime, and nothing in this essay suggests otherwise. FIFA’s own ethics bodies did ban him from football, first in December 2015 and again in March 2021; those are ethics-code sanctions, not criminal judgments.

What follows is an account of how a man who was never convicted of anything presided over an organisation in which so much went wrong.

Paris, June 1998

Blatter had been FIFA’s general secretary since 1981: Havelange’s administrator, salesman and heir apparent. When Havelange retired, the European federations put up Lennart Johansson, the Swedish president of UEFA, who campaigned on transparency and a dispersal of power away from the presidency. Blatter campaigned as the continuity candidate, with Havelange’s blessing.

At the congress in Paris on June 8, 1998, on the eve of the World Cup in France, Blatter won the first round 111 votes to 80. That was short of the two-thirds required to win outright, but Johansson conceded rather than face a second round. Most observers had expected Johansson to win.

The allegations came later, and they are allegations. In February 2002 Farah Addo, a vice-president of the African confederation and head of the Somali federation, told the London-based Daily Mail that he had been offered $100,000 to vote for Blatter, that he had refused, and that eighteen African voters had accepted money at a Paris hotel on the night before the vote. As CNN reported on February 28, 2002, Addo said he believed people working for Blatter’s campaign were involved but not Blatter himself. Johansson called the claims rumours and invited anyone with proof to produce it. No one was ever charged over the 1998 vote, and Blatter has always denied any wrongdoing in connection with it.

Seoul, May 2002: the general secretary’s dossier

The first real test came four years later, and it came from inside. Michel Zen-Ruffinen, who had succeeded Blatter as general secretary in 1998, presented a confidential report of some twenty-five pages to FIFA’s executive committee in Zurich on May 3, 2002. As Swissinfo reported the next day, the dossier alleged misleading accounting, conflicts of interest and irregularities going back to the 1998 congress. Among the specific claims reported by the Mail & Guardian on May 11, 2002, were a backdated $100,000 payment to an executive committee member, and decisions favouring the CONCACAF president Jack Warner and his family, including a nominal one-dollar fee for Caribbean World Cup television rights.

Eleven of the twenty-four executive committee members, led by Johansson, used the dossier as the basis of a criminal complaint against Blatter in Zurich. Blatter denied the allegations, said he had answered them in detail, and treated the whole business as a campaign against him ahead of the election. On May 29, 2002, in Seoul, he defeated Issa Hayatou of Cameroon by 139 votes to 56. Within days the complainants agreed to withdraw their complaint, and Zen-Ruffinen agreed to leave FIFA on July 4, 2002.

On December 4, 2002, Zurich prosecutors closed their investigation. As a separate Swissinfo report noted that day, prosecutors found it was not possible to attribute any punishable behaviour to him, and that the complaints were either factually incorrect or concerned actions the executive committee had itself approved.

That outcome deserves to be stated plainly, because it is the template for much of what followed. A serious insider made serious allegations. The man accused was re-elected by a landslide. The accuser left. The prosecutors found nothing they could charge. In my view, the lesson the institution drew was not that its president was blameless, but that its electorate would forgive him.

Port of Spain, May 2011

Blatter was re-elected unopposed on May 31, 2007. For 2011 he finally faced a challenger from inside the family: Mohamed bin Hammam of Qatar, president of the Asian Football Confederation since 2002 and a member of the executive committee since 1996.

On May 10, 2011, bin Hammam addressed delegates of the Caribbean Football Union at the Hyatt Regency in Port of Spain, Trinidad, at a meeting arranged by Jack Warner. Afterwards, delegates were invited to collect envelopes. Fred Lunn, vice-president of the Bahamas federation, received one containing $40,000 in cash, photographed it and reported it. The CONCACAF general secretary Chuck Blazer took the matter to FIFA. Bin Hammam said the money was for travel and administrative costs and that the allegations were false.

On May 29, 2011, the day of his ethics hearing, bin Hammam withdrew from the presidential race, and FIFA’s ethics committee provisionally suspended both him and Warner. Blatter, who had also been the subject of a complaint, was cleared to stand. On June 1, 2011, in Zurich, he was re-elected with 186 of 203 votes cast, with no opponent on the ballot.

Warner resigned from all his football posts on June 20, 2011. As Andrew Warshaw reported for Inside World Football that day, FIFA then closed its ethics proceedings against him and stated that the presumption of innocence was maintained. Warner’s later encounter with American prosecutors belongs to Part III.

On July 23, 2011, the ethics committee banned bin Hammam from football for life. He appealed. On July 19, 2012, the Court of Arbitration for Sport annulled the ban by a two-to-one majority. Its panel said FIFA’s investigation had not been complete enough to sustain the case, that its ruling was not an affirmative finding of innocence, and that it considered it more likely than not that bin Hammam had been the source of the money. The reprieve lasted five months. As the Associated Press reported in a story carried by Al Jazeera on December 17, 2012, FIFA announced that bin Hammam had resigned from all his football positions and that its ethics committee, acting on an investigation by its chief ethics investigator Michael Garcia into his conduct as AFC president, had banned him for life for repeated conflicts of interest. He has consistently denied wrongdoing.

Zurich, December 2, 2010

Six months before Port of Spain, FIFA had done something no one had done before: it awarded two World Cups on the same afternoon.

The double vote for 2018 and 2022 was meant to maximise commercial certainty. Instead it maximised opportunity. On November 18, 2010, two weeks before the vote, the ethics committee suspended two executive committee members, Amos Adamu of Nigeria and Reynald Temarii of Tahiti, after an undercover investigation by the Sunday Times, as Al Jazeera reported when the story broke on October 17, 2010. As Graham Dunbar reported for the Associated Press in a story carried by the Christian Science Monitor that day, Adamu was banned for three years and Temarii for one, and four other officials were also sanctioned. Twenty-two men, not twenty-four, would choose the hosts.

On December 2, 2010, they chose Russia for 2018, which won in the second round with 13 votes, and Qatar for 2022, which led every round and beat the United States 14 votes to 8 in the fourth. England, which had spent heavily on its bid, received two votes in the first round and went out. Russia and Qatar have always denied any wrongdoing in their bids.

The Qatar decision gets an essay of its own in Part V. What concerns us here is how FIFA investigated itself.

The Garcia report

In July 2012, under pressure, FIFA split its ethics committee into two chambers: an investigatory chamber under Michael Garcia, a former United States Attorney for the Southern District of New York, and an adjudicatory chamber under Hans-Joachim Eckert, the judge who had closed the ISL affair. In August 2012 Garcia opened an inquiry into the 2018 and 2022 bidding.

He had no subpoena power. He could not interview bin Hammam, who was by then banned. He could not enter Russia; the Russian bid committee told him that the computers it had leased had been destroyed and that several email accounts were inaccessible. Garcia delivered a report of about 430 pages in September 2014. FIFA declined to publish it.

On November 13, 2014, Eckert published a 42-page summary instead. It cleared Russia and Qatar to keep their tournaments, criticised several of the losing bids including England and Australia, and concluded that nothing found was serious enough to reopen the vote. Garcia said within hours that the summary contained numerous materially incomplete and erroneous representations of his findings. He appealed. On December 16, 2014, FIFA’s appeal committee ruled his appeal inadmissible. On December 17, 2014, Garcia resigned. As the Associated Press reported in a story carried by Al Jazeera that day, he cited a lack of leadership at FIFA and wrote, in his public statement, that no independent investigator “can change the culture of an organization.”

The full report did not see daylight until June 27, 2017, when FIFA published it after the German newspaper Bild obtained a copy, as Al Jazeera reported that day. FIFA called the leak illegal. The report described questionable conduct during the bidding but did not, in FIFA’s view, provide grounds to strip either host. By then, Blatter was gone.

The FIFA Files

Between the vote and the summary, journalism did what FIFA’s investigators had not. On June 1, 2014, the Sunday Times, in an investigation led by Heidi Blake and Jonathan Calvert, published the first instalment of what it described as a vast leaked cache of documents, the “FIFA Files”: emails, letters and bank transfers, as Al Jazeera reported in an Associated Press story that day. The paper alleged that bin Hammam had paid some $5 million to football officials to build support for Qatar’s bid, including more than $1.6 million to Jack Warner, some of it before the vote, and €305,000 towards Temarii’s legal costs, which kept Temarii’s suspended seat from passing to a deputy. Qatar’s bid committee strongly denied the allegations and said bin Hammam had no role in its bid and acted independently. Blake and Calvert later expanded the investigation into a book, The Ugly Game, published in 2015.

These remain allegations reported by a newspaper. No court has found that Qatar’s bid was won by bribery.

The kingdom on the eve

Step back and count. In seventeen years Blatter’s FIFA produced a disputed election, an internal dossier that became a criminal complaint, a cash-for-votes affair that ended a challenger, the suspension of two of the twenty-four men who would award two World Cups, an internal investigation whose investigator resigned in protest, and a newspaper archive alleging that a member of his own executive committee had spent millions around a bid. Over the same period Blatter was investigated by Zurich prosecutors in 2002, who found nothing to charge; cleared by FIFA’s ethics bodies in 2011 and 2013; and elected president four times.

It is hard not to conclude that impunity had become the institution’s operating assumption. Not proven wrongdoing by Blatter, which no court has ever found, but a settled confidence, shared by the president and the 209 associations that kept voting for him, that whatever came out of the archives, the family would close ranks and carry on.

On the evening of May 26, 2015, FIFA’s executive committee members began arriving at the Baur au Lac, the discreet lakeside hotel in Zurich where they always stayed before congress. Blatter’s fifth election was three days away, and he would win it. Shortly after dawn the next morning, Swiss police walked through the hotel’s front door with a list of names supplied by the United States Department of Justice. Part III begins there.

A note on the title: “FIFA Fraud” is this series’ characterisation of an institution’s conduct over five decades, offered as commentary and opinion. Where individuals have been charged with, convicted of, or acquitted of specific offences, including fraud, each essay says so plainly. No person named in this series should be taken as having been found guilty of any crime unless the text states that a court has done so.
