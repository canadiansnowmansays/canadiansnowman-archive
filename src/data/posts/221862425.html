Poe’s prisoner never stops the pendulum. He cannot reach it, and he would not have the strength if he could. What saves him is humbler. He smears the last of his food on the bands that hold him, and the rats of the dungeon gnaw through them while the blade keeps swinging. He rolls free with the steel a few inches from his chest. The pendulum is then drawn back up into the ceiling by the people who built the machine, and they move on to something worse.

That is worth keeping in mind in late October 2026, because the Democratic Party has begun to talk openly about what it would do if it held part of the machine.

On Friday, 23 October 2026, Annie Karni reported in The New York Times that Democratic leaders have drawn up a plan for a return to power, beginning with what aides describe as a sprint of bills in the first hundred hours of a new House majority. The same day, the Cook Political Report moved the House to “Lean Democratic” for the first time this cycle, meaning that Republicans could win all seventeen races it rates as toss-ups and still lose control of the chamber.

Part III of this series, posted the day before, argued that most of what the ledger lists as needing Congress depends on the composition of the next Congress, and that even the best result for the restorers would be limited by the presidential veto. The Democrats have now said, in effect, which part of the ledger they intend to start with. So it is fair to hold their plan up against it.

The four bills

According to aides to Hakeem Jeffries, who would become Speaker, the first hundred hours would bring four measures straight to the floor, skipping committees that would not yet exist:

  • reinstating the enhanced Affordable Care Act premium subsidies;

  • reversing the Medicaid cuts in the 2025 budget law, close to a trillion dollars;

  • moving to end the president’s tariffs and return trade policy to Congress;

  • directing the president to end the war with Iran or seek Congress’s authorisation to continue it.

Run against the Reversal Ledger, which now holds 109 items, the list looks rather different than it does on a campaign stage.

The subsidies are a rerun. The ledger records the enhanced premium subsidies as a third-tier item, needing Congress. They were created by the American Rescue Plan in 2021, extended in 2022, and expired on 31 December 2025. The House has already voted to bring them back: a three-year extension passed 230 to 196 in January 2026, with Republican votes, and died in the Senate. Passing it again in January 2027 would be a statement, not a restoration, unless the Senate behaves differently the second time.

There is a lesson buried in this item that the plan does not mention. The subsidies were not repealed. Nobody signed anything. They were written with an end date, and the end date did the work. Of all the methods in the ledger, “allowed to lapse” is the quietest, and it was supplied by the people who built the programme. A restoration that again carries a three-year sunset would be building a pendulum on purpose.

The Medicaid cuts arrive first. The ledger lists Medicaid eligibility as pending: an eighty-hour monthly work requirement for adults aged nineteen to sixty-four, and renewals every six months, both from 1 January 2027. Montana began enforcing early, on 1 October 2026. A new Congress is not sworn in until the first days of January 2027. By the time the first bill reaches the floor, the requirement will already be the law in force in every state. People who lose coverage in the gap will not be restored by a later vote; they will have to reapply, and some will have run up medical bills in the meantime.

The same 2025 law ended Medicaid and CHIP eligibility for refugees and asylees on 1 October 2026, a right they had held since the Refugee Act of 1980 and kept through the 1996 welfare overhaul. The ledger records them as being removed from the rolls through October 2026. Whether “reversing the Medicaid cuts” includes them is a question worth putting to Mr Jeffries’s office.

The tariff bill would assert a power the Court has already confirmed. The ledger does not count the tariffs themselves as a reversal. It counts Congress’s power over them, and it marks that item as stopped: in Learning Resources v. Trump, on 20 February 2026, the Supreme Court ruled six to three that the emergency-powers statute does not authorise tariffs. The administration moved within days to other statutes, Section 122 and then Section 301, and the tariffs went on. Congress did not need the Court’s permission to take trade back. It needed the will, and a president’s signature or two-thirds of both houses.

The war is not on the ledger at all. It is something done rather than something undone. But it rests on something undone. The War Powers Resolution of 1973 required the president to withdraw forces within sixty days unless Congress authorised the hostilities. That clock ran out in August 2026 without a vote. The resolution was passed over Richard Nixon’s veto, by Democrats and Republicans together. It belongs in the ledger, under “Bipartisan”, and I will propose adding it in the next weekly check.

What isn’t on the list

Two of the four bills touch the ledger directly. Three, if the refugees are included. That leaves the rest of the third tier, the seventeen items that need Congress, almost untouched.

The Corporation for Public Broadcasting is not on the list. Nor is a statutory charter for foreign assistance after the dismantling of USAID, nor protection for the career civil service, nor the inspectors general fired in January 2025, nor the independence of the federal statistical agencies. Nor is the Corporate Transparency Act, which Congress passed in 2021 over Mr Trump’s veto and which a Treasury rule of 14 August 2026 emptied for domestic companies.

This is not a criticism of the sequence. A hundred-hour sprint is supposed to contain what the caucus already agrees on and what voters already feel in their pocketbooks. Health premiums and grocery prices win elections; inspectors general do not. But it confirms what the ledger has suggested since the Introduction. The items most easily restored are the ones people notice. The items hardest to restore are the ones nobody campaigns on, and they are where the ratchet sits.

Reverse, stop, slow

The most honest sentence in the Times’s report came from Senator Brian Schatz of Hawaii, who is in line to become the second-ranking Democrat in the Senate. While Mr Trump is in office, he said, nobody should expect visionary legislation. A majority could reverse some decisions, stop some others, and slow the rest.

Those three verbs match the series’ own vocabulary better than most of what has been written about the midterms.

Reverse is the rare one. With Mr Trump in the White House until January 2029, every bill faces his veto, and overriding a veto needs two-thirds of both houses. Budget reconciliation, the route Part III identified as the way around the filibuster, does not help here. It avoids sixty votes in the Senate; it does not avoid the president’s signature. Democrats would need a net gain of thirteen Senate seats to reach sixty, which not even the most optimistic of them expects. In divided government, nothing on the ledger comes back without Mr Trump agreeing to it.

Stop is where a House majority has real force, because the House originates spending bills and nothing can be spent without its vote. That is the meaning of the talk, in the Times’s account, of some Democrats withholding votes to fund the war. But the ledger has an entry that complicates it. On 25 September 2026 the administration sent Congress a “pocket rescission” of $810 million, five days before the funds expired, so that the money lapsed whatever Congress decided. The Government Accountability Office considers the practice illegal, and seven states sued on 1 October. A Congress that intends to use the purse to stop things will first have to defend its claim that appropriated money must be spent.

Slow is oversight. Representative Jamie Raskin of Maryland, in line to chair the Judiciary Committee, named three early areas of interest: the trade in pardons, no-bid contracts, and the cryptocurrency flowing anonymously to the government and to the president’s family. He expects subpoenas to be ignored and plans to rely on whistle-blowers and private parties instead. On impeachment, he told the Times that Democrats are “sober about the constitutional arithmetic.”

Each of Mr Raskin’s targets sits beside a ledger entry. The roughly 1,500 January 6 pardons are a fourth-tier item, locked: a pardon cannot be revoked, and no hearing will change that. The anonymous money he wants to follow travels more easily since the Corporate Transparency Act stopped covering domestic shell companies, and since the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act was paused and then narrowed in 2025. Investigations would also be working without seventeen of the inspectors general who used to do this kind of work from inside the departments. Oversight can document what was done. It cannot, by itself, put back the people and the tools that would have caught it earlier.

The case for treating it as Congress’s own business

Rahm Emanuel, advising House leaders and considering a presidential run of his own, described the strategy in the Times as a dilemma for the president: if he signs a popular bill, Democrats win; if he vetoes it, they still win. That is a sound plan for an election. It is a pendulum plan. It aims at the next swing, in 2028, and treats each veto as a campaign advertisement.

The ratchet asks a different question: what could be made to last even if the swing never comes?

The ledger offers one answer, and it is not partisan. Several of the items on the Democrats’ list, and several that are not, are losses of Congress’s own power rather than of any Democratic policy. The tariffs that the Court said Congress controls. The war that Congress never authorised. The money it appropriated and watched lapse. And a law it passed and the president signed: on 16 October 2026 Mr Trump waived nearly all the sanctions and tariffs in the Graham Act on Russia two days before they took effect, over the objections of Republican Senators Thom Tillis and John Cornyn.

A Republican senator may not vote to restore a Democratic health subsidy. But a Republican senator might vote to restore the Senate’s own authority, especially if he wrote the law being waived. The first hundred hours could be framed as the return of what Congress owns, the purse, the war power and trade, rather than as an affordability package with a war resolution attached. It would draw fewer cheers on election night and more votes in the Senate.

Mr Emanuel’s dilemma would still apply. A president who vetoes a bill restoring Congress’s power over war and trade is not vetoing a Democratic programme. He is telling the legislature it governs at his pleasure, in writing, and the senators who voted for the bill would have to answer for whether they accept that.

The view from the shore

From Toronto, the tariff bill is the one that matters most to my neighbours, and it is the one least likely to become law before 2029. I have watched the Section 122 and Section 301 tariffs replace the emergency tariffs one after another, which is a reminder that a ruling in favour of the Constitution does not lower a single duty on a single shipment. Only a statute, or a president, does that.

Canadians who live under minority parliaments know a version of what the Democrats are proposing. An opposition without the government cannot govern, but it can make governing very expensive, and it can choose which costs to impose. The choice tells voters what the opposition actually values. The Democrats’ first hundred hours, as reported, value the things voters can feel. That is reasonable politics. It is not yet a plan for the ratchet.

Poe’s prisoner was not saved by stopping the blade. He was saved because something small and persistent kept chewing at the bands while the pendulum kept swinging, and because, at the very end, an army came through the gates of Toledo. A House majority in January 2027 would not be the army. At best it would be the rats: slow work, out of sight, on the straps that hold the country down. That is not nothing. In the story it was the difference between the edge of the pit and the bottom of it.
