On December 6, 1992, a mob of approximately 150,000 Hindu militants — organized, transported, and incited by the Vishva Hindu Parishad and the Bajrang Dal, with the tacit support of the BJP — demolished the Babri Masjid, a sixteenth-century mosque in Ayodhya, Uttar Pradesh. They dismantled it with hammers, pickaxes, and their bare hands. They did it in broad daylight, on live television, while the Indian army stood by and the BJP’s senior leadership — including L.K. Advani, who had led a nationwide “rath yatra” (chariot procession) to the site — watched from a nearby platform.

The pretext was historical: the mosque, they claimed, had been built by the Mughal commander Mir Baqi on the ruins of a Hindu temple marking the birthplace of Lord Ram. The claim was archaeologically contested and politically incendiary. But the demolition was not really about a mosque or a temple. It was about power. It was a declaration — televised, unmistakable, irreversible — that the secular compact on which independent India had been built was being challenged by a movement that defined India not as a republic of citizens but as a civilization of Hindus.

The Babri Masjid demolition is the hinge event of modern Indian politics. Everything before it is prelude. Everything after it is consequence.

I. The RSS

You cannot understand Hindu nationalism without understanding the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh — the National Volunteer Organization, known universally as the RSS.

The RSS was founded in 1925 by Keshav Baliram Hedgewar, a doctor from Nagpur, in response to what he perceived as the twin threats of British colonialism and Muslim demographic growth. Its intellectual architecture, however, was built largely by Madhav Sadashiv Golwalkar, who led the organization from 1940 to 1973 and whose 1939 text We, or Our Nationhood Defined remains the foundational document of Hindutva ideology.

Golwalkar’s thesis was straightforward: India is a Hindu nation. Its culture, its civilization, its identity are Hindu. Non-Hindus — particularly Muslims and Christians — are foreign elements who can remain in India only by assimilating into Hindu culture, adopting Hindu traditions, and subordinating their separate religious identities to the Hindu whole. Golwalkar explicitly admired the racial nationalism of 1930s Germany, writing that the German treatment of its Jewish minority offered “a good lesson for us in Hindusthan to learn and profit by.” He later distanced himself from the most extreme implications of this admiration, but the intellectual framework — the definition of national identity in ethno-religious terms, the categorization of minorities as guests in someone else’s home — has never been repudiated by the RSS.

The RSS operates as a cadre organization — think of it as a cross between the Boy Scouts and a political party, with the discipline of the former and the ambitions of the latter. It runs daily shakhas (branches) across India — early morning meetings where volunteers perform physical exercises, sing patriotic songs, and receive ideological instruction. The network is vast: an estimated 50,000 to 60,000 shakhas operating daily, with millions of members across the country. The RSS does not contest elections itself. It breeds the people who do.

The BJP is the RSS’s political party. The VHP (Vishva Hindu Parishad) is its religious arm. The ABVP is its student wing. The Bajrang Dal is its street muscle. Together, they constitute what is called the Sangh Parivar — the RSS family — an ecosystem of organizations that collectively cover every aspect of Indian public life: education, labour, tribal welfare, women’s organizing, media, legal advocacy, and cultural programming. The Sangh Parivar is the most sophisticated ideological infrastructure in Indian politics, and it has been building since 1925. Its patience is its most dangerous quality.

II. The Rath Yatra

The path from the RSS’s shakhas to national power ran through Ayodhya.

In 1990, L.K. Advani — then president of the BJP — launched the Ram Rath Yatra, a motorized procession across northern India in a vehicle styled as a chariot from the Hindu epics. The yatra’s stated purpose was to mobilize support for the construction of a Ram temple at the site of the Babri Masjid. Its actual purpose was to consolidate Hindu identity as a political force and to transform the BJP from a minor party into a mass movement.

The yatra worked. It cut across caste lines — lower-caste Hindus who had no historical connection to temple politics were drawn into the movement by the emotional power of Ram, the most beloved deity in the Hindu pantheon. It reframed Indian politics around a religious axis: you were either for Ram or against Ram, for the temple or against it, for Hindu civilization or for its enemies. The nuances of the secular constitution, the complexities of Hindu-Muslim coexistence, the legal status of the disputed site — all of this was swept aside by the primal simplicity of the claim: this is Ram’s birthplace, and a foreign invader built a mosque on it.

The demolition of the Babri Masjid in 1992 was the yatra’s culmination. The riots that followed — in Bombay, in Surat, in cities across northern India — killed over 2,000 people, the majority of them Muslim. The violence was not spontaneous. It was organized, targeted, and in some cases directed by local BJP and Sangh Parivar leaders. The secular state that Nehru and Ambedkar had built was being tested not by an external enemy but by a movement that emerged from within the democratic system itself and used the tools of democratic mobilization to challenge the democratic compact.

III. Gujarat 2002

The event that defines Narendra Modi’s biography — and that his supporters and opponents interpret in diametrically opposite ways — occurred in the state of Gujarat in February and March 2002.

On February 27, a train carrying Hindu pilgrims returning from Ayodhya was set on fire at the Godhra railway station. Fifty-nine people were killed. The cause of the fire — whether it was set by a Muslim mob or was accidental — remains disputed, though subsequent legal proceedings convicted several Muslim men of criminal conspiracy.

What is not disputed is what happened next. Over the following three days, anti-Muslim pogroms swept across Gujarat. Mobs — organized, armed, and in possession of voter rolls and property records that allowed them to identify Muslim homes and businesses — attacked Muslim neighbourhoods in Ahmedabad and across the state. The violence was systematic. Women were raped and burned alive. Children were killed. Mosques were destroyed. Estimates of the death toll range from the official figure of 1,044 (of whom 790 were Muslim) to independent estimates exceeding 2,000.

Narendra Modi was Chief Minister of Gujarat. He had held the office for less than five months. His role during the violence — whether he directed it, enabled it, failed to prevent it, or merely stood aside while it happened — is the most contested question in Indian politics. A Special Investigation Team appointed by the Supreme Court found insufficient evidence to prosecute him. His critics argue that the SIT’s investigation was constrained and its conclusions politically influenced. His supporters argue that he was exonerated by the highest judicial authority in the land.

What is beyond dispute is the aftermath. Modi was denied a visa to the United States for nearly a decade. He was treated as a pariah by the international community. And within India, he became a hero to millions of Hindus who saw him as a leader who would not apologize for defending Hindu interests.

He won re-election in Gujarat in 2002 with an increased majority.

The TACO framework I have applied throughout my Substack work maps onto Modi with uncomfortable precision. Threaten: the targeting of Muslims in Gujarat, the subsequent passage of the CAA-NRC (Citizenship Amendment Act and National Register of Citizens), the revocation of Kashmir’s special status. Accommodate: the diplomatic charm offensive toward Gulf states, the embrace of Israel, the strategic partnership with the United States. Capitulate: rarely, selectively — the withdrawal of the farm laws after prolonged protest is the most notable instance. Obfuscate: the management of domestic media, the world’s most frequent internet shutdowns, the use of sedition and anti-terror laws against journalists and activists, the cultivation of a personal brand that transcends and obscures the ideology beneath it.

IV. The Modi Era

Modi became Prime Minister in 2014, winning a majority that no single party had achieved since 1984. He won again in 2019 with an even larger majority. His political dominance is the defining fact of twenty-first-century Indian politics.

The economic narrative is real. Under Modi, India has become the world’s fifth-largest economy. Infrastructure investment — highways, airports, digital payment systems, electrification — has been significant. The Goods and Services Tax, implemented in 2017, unified India’s chaotic tax system for the first time. The Digital India initiative has connected hundreds of millions of people to the internet and to government services through biometric identity systems. The aspirational middle class that liberalization created sees in Modi a leader who delivers — who builds, who acts, who projects Indian power on the global stage.

The democratic narrative is also real. Under Modi, India has experienced what international observers describe as democratic backsliding. Press freedom has declined — India ranks 159th out of 180 countries on the Reporters Without Borders Press Freedom Index. The use of sedition laws, the Unlawful Activities Prevention Act, and tax investigations against opposition politicians, NGOs, and media organizations has intensified. The independence of the Election Commission, the judiciary, and the Reserve Bank has been questioned. Muslim citizens face increasing legal and social discrimination through laws like the CAA, which offers fast-tracked citizenship to refugees from neighbouring countries — unless they are Muslim. Mob lynchings of Muslims accused of cattle slaughter or beef consumption have become a recurring horror. Kashmir has been under effective military lockdown since the revocation of Article 370 in 2019.

The two narratives — economic development and democratic erosion — are not contradictory. They coexist, as India has always made its contradictions coexist. The question is whether the coexistence is sustainable, or whether one narrative will eventually consume the other.

V. The Ideology

Hindutva — the ideology that the RSS has cultivated for a century and that the BJP now implements as governing philosophy — is not Hinduism. This distinction matters.

Hinduism is a vast, plural, internally contradictory religious tradition that accommodates atheists, polytheists, monotheists, and philosophical monists within its tent. Hindutva is a political ideology that claims to speak for Hinduism while reducing it to a single identity — a national identity defined by what it excludes (Muslims, Christians, secularists) rather than by the capacious philosophical tradition it claims to represent.

The Hindutva project has several components, all of which are currently being implemented. The rewriting of history — textbooks are being revised to minimize the Mughal period, to emphasize Hindu victimhood under Muslim rule, and to present India’s civilizational identity as fundamentally Hindu. The renaming of cities — Allahabad has become Prayagraj, Mughal Sarai has become Deen Dayal Upadhyaya Nagar. The legal architecture — the Uniform Civil Code, which would replace Muslim personal law with a single civil code, is being advanced. The cultural assertion — the construction of the Ram Temple at Ayodhya, on the site of the demolished Babri Masjid, consecrated by Modi in January 2024 in a ceremony of extraordinary political and religious symbolism.

The question this poses for the series is the question it poses for India: Is the Hindutva project compatible with the constitutional democracy that Ambedkar built? Can a government that defines national identity in explicitly Hindu terms maintain the secular framework that protects 200 million Muslim citizens? Can a movement that venerates the man who assassinated Gandhi — and that has never fully repudiated Godse’s ideology — claim continuity with the democratic traditions of the independence movement?

The answers are not reassuring. But they are not final either. Indian democracy has surprised its critics before. It survived the Emergency. It survived Partition. It may yet survive the saffron turn. The mechanism of correction is the same as it has always been: the vote. Whether the vote is sufficient — whether elections conducted in a media environment dominated by the ruling party, in an atmosphere of communal polarization, with an opposition that is fragmented and an institutional architecture that is weakened — remains the open question.

VI. The Roommate Test

I am careful, in this essay, about what I claim to know. I am a Canadian. I am not Hindu. I am not Muslim. I do not live in India, and the violence and discrimination I describe in these pages has never touched my body or threatened my family.

But I live with Indian men. And I have noticed something over the years that is relevant.

Rikesh is from Gujarat — Modi’s Gujarat. He is Hindu. He is a professional, educated, globally mobile man who represents everything the BJP’s development narrative promises. Gurneet is Sikh, from Punjab, a community with its own complex relationship to both the Indian state and to Hindu nationalism. They live in my house. They share meals. They navigate their differences with the casual fluency of men who have been doing it their whole lives.

This is not a political argument. It is an observation. The India that works — the India that holds together, that negotiates its diversity daily, that produces the roommates in my kitchen — is not the India of ideological purity. It is the India of accommodation, of code-switching, of the pragmatic coexistence that happens when people who are different from each other share a limited space and find a way to make it work.

Whether the political system can protect that everyday India — or whether it is determined to replace it with something narrower, harder, and more exclusive — is the question that hangs over everything that follows.
