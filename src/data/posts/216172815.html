Carmen Carballo García is sixty-six years old. She sat on a bench in Havana recently while her five-year-old granddaughter, Yaquelin, sucked her thumb beside her. “The doctors tell me she is underweight,” Ms. Carballo said. “She needs milk, but she doesn’t have any.” As The New York Times reported on September 17, 2026, that scene is not the result of an empty shelf in a country with no money. It is the result of a shelf that has milk sitting for it in a shipping container somewhere in the Caribbean, going nowhere, because the company that would carry it has decided the paperwork risk isn’t worth the fare.

This series has spent two years documenting a blockade built on financial architecture — SDN designations, the Visa and Mastercard shutdown, the oil sanctions, the hunt for a compliant successor. What the newest reporting surfaces is a different mechanism entirely, one this series hasn’t yet given its proper name: not a blockade of what’s banned, but a blockade of what’s merely adjacent to what’s banned. Call it the overcompliance doctrine, because that is what the United Nations’ own coordinator in Havana is calling it.

The Word Francisco Pichón Used

Washington’s position, stated and restated, is that its Cuba sanctions do not target food or medicine. The Trump administration has said so explicitly. It is, on paper, true — there are carve-outs, humanitarian exemptions, the standard architecture every sanctions regime includes to avoid the accusation of starving civilians as policy.

Francisco Pichón, the UN humanitarian coordinator in Cuba, described what actually happens to that architecture in practice. “We are facing something very real, which is a phenomenon called ‘overcompliance,’” he said, as the Times reported. “Overcompliance is a really excessive precaution from companies to not expose themselves to sanctions.” The distinction matters more than it sounds like it should. Nobody has to ban a colostomy bag for a colostomy bag to stop moving. A shipping line only has to conclude that the legal cost of guessing wrong about which containers count as “compliant cargo” exceeds the commercial value of the Cuba route altogether — and then it stops taking Cuba-bound bookings of any kind, exemption or not, because sorting the exempt cargo from the sanctioned cargo is itself a liability nobody wants to own.

That is precisely what happened. Hapag-Lloyd, the German shipping giant, and CMA CGM, the French one — which together, by expert estimate, carried at least half of everything Cuba imports, including most of its food — both stopped delivering to the island after the Trump administration’s May 2026 order threatened to freeze the assets of foreign companies doing business with certain Cuban state entities, and to lock them out of U.S. banks and markets. The order was aimed at GAESA, the military conglomerate this series has tracked since Part 1. The result, as the Times documented, was that two of the world’s largest container carriers concluded the entire island was too risky to touch, humanitarian cargo included.

The Number Is Seven Thousand

Rodolfo Benítez Verson, Cuba’s ambassador to the UN in Geneva, told the Human Rights Council last week that more than 7,000 containers bound for Cuba were stranded. “The cruelty knows no bounds,” he said. “They will have to answer to the Cuban people, and they will not be forgiven.” Cuban officials say things like this constantly, and this series has generally treated Havana’s own rhetoric with the same scepticism it applies to Washington’s — GAESA’s mismanagement is not a fiction this series has ever excused. But the 7,000-container figure isn’t a Havana talking point. It shows up independently, container by container, in the accounts of the aid organizations trying to move the cargo.

Dr. Valia Rodríguez runs a UK-based charity called Aid for the Caribbean. A Hapag-Lloyd container she filled with more than $500,000 in resuscitation kits, colostomy bags and intravenous tubing left Northern Ireland in April and reached the Port of Mariel on June 4 — one day before the new sanctions took full effect. “They decided to stop unloading the containers,” she said of Hapag-Lloyd. The shipment was diverted to Panama instead, where her charity has now paid more than $6,000 in storage fees for cargo that was never sanctioned, never contraband, and never in dispute — just inconveniently timed. “Right now, people in Cuba are using shopping bags, because there are no colostomy bags,” she said.

Thirty more containers — water treatment kits, antibiotics, contraceptives, pregnancy tests, all purchased by UN agencies — were still sitting in port as of last week. Spare parts donated by Uruguay’s state power company for a decommissioned Cuban plant can’t find a shipping line willing to carry them at all. Cuba has suffered at least five nationwide blackouts this year; the parts that might have helped are stuck in Montevideo, waiting on a company willing to accept a risk the exemption was supposed to have already resolved.

The Sky Version of the Same Story

It isn’t only ships. A lack of jet fuel has gutted air cargo to the island as thoroughly as the sea routes, and the casualties there are just as specific. The World Health Organization had arranged donated shipments of doxorubicin, a cancer drug, after several airlines stopped flying to Cuba; the vials never arrived. Separately, the WHO says tuberculosis medication it purchased from an Indian pharmaceutical manufacturer, meant to travel by DHL, was returned to the manufacturer in May “without explanation.” Nobody banned the TB drugs. A courier simply decided, somewhere in its own internal compliance process, that Cuba wasn’t worth the exposure — and sent the shipment back to India rather than find out.

This is the mechanism working exactly as it will always work once a sanctions regime reaches a certain density: the state doesn’t need to prohibit the humanitarian shipment. It only needs to make the private actors moving that shipment more afraid of getting it wrong than they are motivated to get it right. Once that threshold is crossed, the exemption becomes theoretical. Overcompliance isn’t a bug in the sanctions architecture. It’s the architecture doing exactly what density of threat is designed to do — the chilling effect is the effect, whatever the State Department’s press language says about intent.

Rubio’s Diagnosis, and the Diagnosis’s Convenience

Secretary of State Marco Rubio, asked about the humanitarian toll in September, offered the administration’s now-standard framing: “If Cuba is a humanitarian disaster, it is because their regime is a disaster, because the economic model they follow doesn’t work.” There is a version of that sentence this series has never disputed — GAESA’s decades of misallocated resources, the tourism-over-everything bet chronicled back in Part 20, are real and are Havana’s own doing.

But the sentence performs a sleight of hand that the container numbers make visible. It asks the reader to choose between two explanations — regime mismanagement, or American pressure — as though only one can be operating at a time, and then answers its own question before the shipping data can be introduced into evidence. Cuba’s grid didn’t need American help to be brittle. But Uruguay’s donated spare parts for it are sitting in a port because of a decision made in Washington in May, not a decision made in Havana in 1990. Both things are true. Rubio’s framing only works if you agree not to look at the second one.

Who’s Left to Carry It

This series has tracked, since Part 20, what it called the boomerang effect: every American measure designed to sever Cuba from Russian and Chinese influence has instead deepened Cuba’s dependence on exactly those two countries, because they are the only major powers willing to absorb the risk Washington has made too expensive for everyone else. The container crisis is the same pattern rendered in shipping manifests. Hapag-Lloyd and CMA CGM are gone. CMA CGM says it has resumed a limited service carrying what it calls “compliant cargo” — agricultural products, medicines, medical devices — but limited is doing a great deal of work in that sentence, and the 7,000-container backlog didn’t accumulate because the exempt categories were ambiguous in theory. It accumulated because no carrier wanted to be the one making that judgment call, container by container, with its own U.S. banking access on the line.

Into that vacuum steps whoever is left standing. Cuba’s Visa and Mastercard networks were replaced by China’s UnionPay and Russia’s Mir back in June. The oil comes from Russian tankers now, when it comes at all. The pattern with shipping will very likely rhyme: Chinese state-linked carriers, less exposed to U.S. banking penalties and less answerable to Western shareholders worried about reputational risk, have far less reason to overcomply — which means the humanitarian corridor into Cuba, if one survives at all, will increasingly run through Beijing rather than Rotterdam or Hamburg. Washington’s sanctions architecture keeps producing the exact geopolitical outcome it claims to be trying to prevent, one supply chain at a time.

The Price of a Dozen Eggs

The numbers underneath all of this have moved since Part 20 documented them in June. Food prices rose 60 percent from January to August, according to the Cuban economist Omar Everleny Pérez Villanueva. A carton of thirty eggs now costs $11 in Havana markets — more than the average monthly salary. Frozen chicken has doubled in price. Quisicuaba, a religious nonprofit that runs soup kitchens across the capital, is waiting on five stranded food containers while its kitchen staff describe “working miracles” to stretch dwindling supplies. Brazilian and Mexican exports to Cuba were both down more than 90 percent in July compared with a year earlier — a collapse steep enough that Mexico has resorted to naval deliveries and Brazil to military air transport, simply to get rice, beans and powdered milk onto the island at all.

None of this is new in kind. It is the same blockade this series has tracked since the Introduction. What’s new is the mechanism this reporting isolates: not a policy that starves Cuba by design, but a policy dense enough with threat that it starves Cuba by side effect, while remaining, on paper, entirely deniable. Washington can point to its own exemptions and say, accurately, that food and medicine were never prohibited. It will never have to explain why they stopped arriving anyway. That is not a loophole in the overcompliance doctrine. It is the doctrine’s entire design.

Yaquelin is five years old, underweight, and waiting on milk that exists, has been paid for, and is sitting in a port somewhere in the Caribbean because a shipping company decided her country wasn’t worth the risk of finding out whether the exemption applied to her.
