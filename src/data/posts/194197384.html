Federal Election Commission meeting. Designed with six commissioners (three from each party) and a four-vote requirement for any enforcement action, the FEC has become the textbook example of an agency structurally engineered for deadlock

The Federal Election Commission was created by the Federal Election Campaign Act of 1974 to do one thing: enforce the rules that Congress had just written to clean up the campaign finance system in the aftermath of Watergate. It was given subpoena power. It was given the authority to impose civil penalties. It was given a staff of lawyers and investigators. It was given a budget.

It was also given six commissioners — three Republicans and three Democrats, appointed by the president and confirmed by the Senate, with no more than three from either party at any time — and a rule requiring four votes for any official action.

This was not an oversight. It was a design choice. Congress, in its wisdom, had decided that an agency with unchecked authority to investigate and penalize political spending was more dangerous than an agency with checked authority. The partisan balance requirement ensured that no party could weaponize the FEC against the other. The four-vote threshold ensured that enforcement required bipartisan consensus.

In theory, this was a reasonable precaution against political abuse. In practice, it created an enforcement agency that enforces almost nothing, because the commissioners appointed by Republican presidents have consistently voted against enforcement actions and the three-three deadlock has become the FEC’s defining characteristic.

The FEC has not once penalized a political candidate or organization for unlawful coordination with a super PAC since Citizens United was decided in 2010. Not once. In sixteen years of super PAC activity, in dozens of documented cases where the technical independence requirement appeared to have been violated, the FEC has deadlocked, dismissed, or declined to pursue every significant coordination case.

This is not bad luck. It is the system working as its current occupants intend it to work.

The mechanics of FEC deadlock are worth understanding in some detail, because they illuminate a broader pattern in American regulatory design: the strategic use of structural dysfunction to achieve outcomes that cannot be achieved through straightforward repeal.

When a complaint is filed with the FEC — alleging, for example, that a super PAC coordinated impermissibly with a candidate’s campaign — the commission’s Office of General Counsel reviews it and issues a recommendation. If the OGC finds reason to believe a violation occurred, it recommends that the commission vote to investigate. If four commissioners vote in favor, an investigation begins. If they deadlock at three-three, the complaint is dismissed.

The Republican commissioners, as a consistent pattern, vote against finding reason to believe in coordination cases involving Republican-affiliated super PACs. They do this on the grounds that the FEC’s coordination regulations are vague, that the evidence is insufficient, or that the First Amendment concerns about chilling political speech weigh against aggressive enforcement. The result is that every three-three deadlock effectively constitutes a finding of no violation — not because the commission has determined that no violation occurred, but because the commission cannot agree that one did.

This is not hypothetical. The Brennan Center, Democracy Docket, and various campaign finance watchdog organizations have documented case after case in which the OGC found credible evidence of coordination, the commissioners deadlocked, and the complaint was dismissed. The FEC’s own internal records show the pattern clearly. The Republican commissioners are not embarrassed about it. Some have argued publicly that the FEC is over-aggressive in its interpretation of coordination rules and that the deadlocks represent appropriate restraint rather than dysfunction.

The FEC as a captured watchdog — chained, starved, and structurally unable to enforce campaign finance rules against super PACs and dark money.

There is a name for an enforcement agency whose majority of commissioners believe it should enforce less: an agency captured by the interests it regulates. The FEC is that agency.

The commission’s structural dysfunction was compounded in 2014 by McCutcheon v. Federal Election Commission, the Supreme Court’s second major campaign finance ruling of the Roberts era.

Shaun McCutcheon was an Alabama businessman who wanted to give money to more candidates and party committees than the aggregate contribution limits allowed. Under the law as it existed before his lawsuit, an individual donor could give a maximum of $48,600 to all federal candidates combined, and $74,600 to all party committees combined, in any two-year election cycle. These aggregate limits did not restrict how much any individual contribution could be — the base limits of $2,600 per candidate per election remained — but they limited the total number of candidates and committees a donor could support at maximum levels.

McCutcheon argued, and the Supreme Court agreed by a 5-4 plurality, that the aggregate limits violated the First Amendment. The majority, written by Chief Justice John Roberts, found that the only legitimate government interest in restricting campaign contributions was preventing quid pro quo corruption, and that aggregate limits — which left the base contribution limits intact — did not sufficiently serve that interest to justify their burden on First Amendment rights.

The immediate practical effect was to allow wealthy donors to write checks to as many candidates as they wished at the maximum base level. The broader effect was to reinforce and extend the logic of Citizens United: the government’s anti-corruption interest is narrow, the First Amendment protection of political spending is broad, and restrictions that do not tightly target explicit transactional corruption cannot survive constitutional scrutiny.

McCutcheon also produced a passage from Chief Justice Roberts that campaign finance scholars have found either illuminating or alarming, depending on their perspective. Roberts wrote that ingratiation and access — the general goodwill that flows from large donations, the phone calls returned, the meetings granted, the considerations extended — do not constitute corruption within the meaning of the First Amendment. Only explicit quid pro quo transactions count.

The Supreme Court’s campaign finance decisions — Citizens United (2010) and McCutcheon (2014) — opened the floodgates while narrowing the definition of corruption the FEC is allowed to police.

This was the majority’s explicit acknowledgment that money buys access and influence in American politics, combined with its explicit declaration that this is constitutionally unproblematic. The Court was not unaware of the relationship between large donations and political responsiveness. It had decided that the relationship, being general rather than transactional, was outside the law’s reach.

The FEC’s dysfunction and the Court’s narrow definition of corruption interact in ways that compound each other’s effects.

The Court has said: only explicit transactions can be regulated. The FEC has said: we cannot agree on whether any transaction is explicit enough to pursue. The result is an enforcement environment in which the most aggressive political money operations — the single-candidate super PACs run by former campaign staff, the coordinated spending funneled through technically independent organizations, the strategic alignment of dark money with campaign messaging — operate with near-complete impunity.

Consider what enforcement would require under current conditions. A campaign finance watchdog identifies what appears to be unlawful coordination between a super PAC and a candidate’s campaign. The watchdog files a complaint with the FEC. The OGC reviews it and recommends investigation. The six commissioners vote. Three vote yes, three vote no. The complaint is dismissed. The watchdog can seek judicial review of the dismissal, but judicial review of FEC deadlocks is limited and slow, and the election that the coordination was meant to influence has already happened.

How dark money and super PACs operate with near-impunity. The FEC has not successfully pursued a single major coordination case since Citizens United.

Speed matters in campaign finance enforcement in a way it does not matter in most regulatory contexts. A penalty imposed two years after an election, on conduct that influenced that election, deters future conduct only if the deterrence effect outweighs the benefit of the original violation. Given the stakes involved — a Senate seat, a congressional district, a presidential contest — the math rarely favors restraint.

The FEC knows this. The commissioners who consistently deadlock coordination cases know it. It is not clear they consider it a problem.

There have been periodic efforts to reform the FEC — to change the commission structure, break the deadlock, require it to actually enforce the law it was created to enforce. None has succeeded. Proposals to add a seventh commissioner, to change the voting threshold, to limit commissioners’ terms to prevent entrenchment, to require reasons for deadlock votes — all have been introduced, none have passed.

The reason is straightforward: the party that benefits from FEC dysfunction has enough power to prevent its reform. The Republican Party has, since the Citizens United era, been better positioned to exploit the super PAC and dark money landscape — though Democrats have learned to operate within it — and has had little incentive to strengthen an enforcement agency that might constrain its operations. The result is that the FEC remains structurally broken by design, maintained in its broken state by people who benefit from the breaking.

This is the most honest description of the situation. It is not a comfortable one.
