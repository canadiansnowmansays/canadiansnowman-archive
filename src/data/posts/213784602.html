This series opened in “Wake Up, Dems” with Abdul El-Sayed’s one-point primary win over Haley Stevens and a warning against mistaking primary purity for a governing mandate. It revisited him in Part VI as the exhibit for the stacking problem — a candidate whose past positions draw disproportionate fire regardless of their substance. Part VIII, citing Frank Bruni, called him “the gotcha piñata” of the cycle and noted, almost in passing, that a candidate who wins a primary on a base-pleasing record and has no plan for the general-election reckoning that follows is heading for exactly the kind of trouble El-Sayed found himself in. That trouble arrived on schedule, at the Michigan Democratic Party’s nominating convention in Lansing on 29 August 2026, and El-Sayed’s response to it deserves its own instalment, because it is the first time in this series that a central figure has done, under pressure and in public, precisely what the “loyal opposition” thesis has been arguing for since Part I.

As the Associated Press reported on 29 August 2026, El-Sayed apologised to the Michigan Democratic Jewish Caucus for comments he made in March, after a terrorist attack on Temple Israel, a synagogue in West Bloomfield Township near Detroit. The attacker, Ayman Mohamad Ghazali, drove a vehicle into the building and opened fire on a security guard before dying at the scene; he had lost four relatives in an Israeli airstrike in Lebanon a week earlier. El-Sayed’s initial response at the time condemned the attack unambiguously but added that “violence is a cycle” and that “hurt people hurt people” — language that critics, including Michigan Attorney General Dana Nessel, read as offering a justification for the attacker’s motives rather than simply context for them. At the convention, El-Sayed told reporters plainly: “I made a mistake.” He went further with the Jewish Telegraphic Agency the following day, saying, “If I had the opportunity to take it back, I would take it all the way back,” and adding, “there is never a justification for a terrorist act, period.”

I want to be precise about what makes this notable, because an apology alone is not remarkable in American politics; candidates apologise constantly, usually for the smallest possible offence phrased in the passive voice. What distinguishes this one, and what makes it worth a full instalment of a series that has spent nine parts complaining about Democrats’ failure to do exactly this, is the absence of a “but.” El-Sayed did not say his words were misunderstood by people looking for reasons to be offended. He did not say the criticism was unfair, even though Part VIII of this series argued, following Bruni, that a fair amount of the criticism aimed at El-Sayed specifically has in fact been disproportionate next to what Republicans like Mike Rogers get away with. He said, in the Jewish Telegraphic Agency’s own account of the room, that he wished he could retract the comments entirely, and that “you don’t ever want to trample somebody’s pain.” That is a different register from the reflexive half-apology this series’ companion pieces on the Trump administration catalogue constantly under the TACO framework’s Obfuscate heading — the statement engineered to sound like contrition while preserving every substantive position intact. El-Sayed’s apology preserves nothing. It concedes the specific words were wrong, not merely that people were wrongly hurt by words that were actually fine.

This matters directly for the Part VII thesis, built from Daron Acemoglu’s big-tent populism argument, that the difference between a coalition that expands and one that fractures is whether its politics sorts people into the saved and the damned or invites them into a shared frame. El-Sayed’s original March comments, whatever their intent, did the sorting thing: they implicitly asked Jewish Michiganders grieving an attack on their synagogue to also hold space for the grievance of the attacker’s family, in the same breath, with no acknowledgment that this is an extraordinarily difficult thing to ask of anyone in the raw aftermath of a targeted act of violence against their community. The apology reverses that structure entirely. It does not ask anything of the audience. It simply concedes the original framing was the wrong one to offer people in pain, which is the single sentence Bruni’s cage-fight interview format, discussed in Part VIII, seems designed to make almost impossible for a besieged candidate to say without sounding coerced. El-Sayed said it anyway, and in a room, according to the JTA’s reporting, that included Democratic figures who have been sharply critical of him, honouring Nessel herself at the same event where he was apologising for the very comments she’d criticised most pointedly.

There is a second thread running through this story that connects directly to the coalition-discipline argument this series made as far back as Part I, and it concerns Hasan Piker, the left-wing streamer who campaigned alongside El-Sayed during the primary. Piker had recently told his audience that American Jews who are seen as “singularly invested in Israel” could eventually face violence — a comment House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries called “dangerous and antisemitic.” CNN reported that El-Sayed downplayed his relationship with Piker over the same weekend, telling reporters he has “no plans” to continue campaigning with him and that “nobody speaks for this campaign besides me and my campaign spokespeople.” This is precisely the coalition-math test this series set up in Part I: does a candidate, once the primary is won, actively narrow the circle of people whose comments he’s implicitly vouching for, or does he keep every primary-season ally in the tent regardless of what they say once the cameras are rolling for a general audience? El-Sayed chose the narrowing. Whether that choice was made a few months too late for the Jewish Democrats who needed to hear it during the primary itself is a fair question, and one Nessel and others have clearly been asking. But the direction of the choice — away from an ally whose rhetoric works against big-tent coalition-building, not toward him — is the direction Part VII’s Acemoglu framework calls for.

It is worth pausing on the timing, because the sequencing here inverts the usual criticism this series has levelled at Democratic candidates since Part VI. The stacking problem, as originally diagnosed, was about candidates who never adjusted their positioning after a primary and then paid for it in the general. El-Sayed’s timeline runs differently: the March comments predate the primary itself, meaning Michigan Democratic primary voters chose him with this exposure already on the public record, largely because the primary electorate, as Part I noted, was not weighing the same considerations a general-election coalition requires. The apology, coming five months later and only after the general-election spotlight made the exposure unavoidable, is less a case of a candidate failing to adapt than a case of adaptation arriving exactly as late as the incentive structure allowed. A primary electorate did not punish the original comments; a general electorate, and specifically the Jewish Democratic bloc within it, did. That is not a flattering story about foresight, but it is a coherent one about incentives, and it is worth remembering the next time this series is tempted to treat a late apology as evidence of bad character rather than as evidence of exactly the sequencing problem Part VI already predicted would recur.

I don’t want to overstate what one apology accomplishes, because this series has been careful throughout not to mistake a single gesture for a structural fix. The Washington Post’s reporting notes this was El-Sayed’s second apology to the Jewish community in as many days, which on one reading shows genuine effort and on another suggests the first apology didn’t land cleanly enough to close the story on its own. Rabbi Paul Yedwab of Temple Israel’s clergy declined to comment on the apologies at all, citing the congregation’s position against commenting on electoral politics — a studied neutrality that reads, in context, less like satisfaction than like a community choosing not to relitigate its own trauma for a reporter’s benefit. And the race itself, per the Meet the Press transcript flagged in Part IX, remains one Michigan Democrats consider genuinely uncertain, with the same broadcast noting the party “divided” heading into a must-win Senate contest. An apology in late August does not undo six months of accumulated distrust between now and 3 November, and El-Sayed himself seemed to acknowledge as much, telling reporters he understood the words had “trample[d] somebody’s pain” in a way no amount of subsequent explanation could fully repair.

What the apology does accomplish, and what makes it worth this series’ attention, is a demonstration that the coalition-discipline this series has spent nine parts calling for is not a hypothetical the party’s candidates are incapable of executing. It is a specific, learnable behaviour: concede the substantive error rather than relitigating the audience’s reaction to it, distance yourself from allies whose rhetoric undercuts the coalition you’re now trying to build, and do both of these things in the room with the people you’ve wronged rather than in a press release written to minimise the appearance of having wronged anyone. Part VIII’s complaint about the gotcha-piñata dynamic remains valid — Mike Rogers’s own reversal on January 6th has received nothing like this scrutiny, and that asymmetry is real. But an unfair spotlight is still a spotlight, and El-Sayed’s response to standing in it, whatever its shortcomings and its timing, is closer to the big-tent instinct Acemoglu describes than anything else this series has documented from a candidate actually on a ballot this November. Whether Michigan’s Jewish Democrats, and the broader coalition El-Sayed needs to hold together to beat Rogers in a race that could decide control of the Senate, judge the apology sufficient is a question only the returns in nine weeks can answer. But the shape of the gesture itself is, finally, the shape this series has been asking for since “Wake Up, Dems.”

Postscript, 6 October 2026

This essay was drafted on 1 September 2026, two days after El-Sayed’s apology in Lansing, and it treated that apology as the first live example of a candidate doing the coalition repair this series had been asking for. Five weeks of polling since then allow a cautious check on how the repair is holding, though not on whether it caused anything.

The early read was encouraging. A Susquehanna poll of 800 likely voters taken from 11 to 17 August, which FOX 2 Detroit reported, had El-Sayed ahead of Mike Rogers by seven points, 46 per cent to 39, and leading among independents and unaffiliated voters by 22 points.

The latest read is not. A Fox News poll conducted from 24 to 28 September found El-Sayed ahead by a single point among likely voters, 50 to 49. That is happening in a state where the same survey found voters prefer Democratic control of the Senate by five points, 52 to 47. Daron Shaw, the Republican half of the bipartisan team that runs the Fox poll, put the gap plainly: the short-term forces helping Democrats in Michigan are partly cancelled out by voters’ reservations about the Democratic candidate himself. A national environment five points better than the candidate is the stacking problem from Part VI, expressed as a number.

There is a more hopeful reading in the same data. El-Sayed now takes 93 per cent of Michigan Democrats, against Rogers’s 95 per cent of Republicans, and the small pool of independents leans narrowly his way. Whatever the apology did or did not do, the Democratic coalition that looked fractured after the 4 August primary has largely come home. Voters who rate the cost of living as extremely important back him by 13 points. Voters who call Trump extremely important to their vote back him by 22.

So the repair this essay described seems to have worked on the people it was aimed at. What it has not done is close the distance between the party’s standing and the candidate’s. That gap, rather than any single remark, is now what decides Michigan. This series will return to it once more before 3 November.
