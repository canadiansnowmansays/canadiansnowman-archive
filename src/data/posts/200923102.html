The Scales of Justice meet the AI era. The laws do not yet exist. The trials have already begun.

The laws that should govern artificial intelligence do not yet exist. The trials have started anyway. So, increasingly, has the accounting.

This project started with a stage, not a courtroom. Bernie Sanders and Geoffrey Hinton, sitting together, talking about a gap between what artificial intelligence can now do and what any institution has been built to govern — that conversation became The Asimov Gap, and everything that follows grew out of it. The trials came later, once the gap Sanders and Hinton described started generating case law faster than any one series could track. In November 2026 the project came back to a stage, in Toronto, where the lectures that bear Hinton’s name asked how much time is left to close that gap.

On April 28, 2026, in a federal courthouse in downtown Oakland, California, a nine-person jury was seated to hear what may be the most consequential corporate governance case in the history of artificial intelligence. Three weeks later, on May 18, they rejected the plaintiff’s claims in ninety minutes — not on the merits, but on the statute of limitations. The jury never reached the question everyone had come to hear argued.

The next day, a federal appeals court in Washington, D.C., heard oral arguments in a case that may be more consequential still: whether the United States government can punish an American company for refusing to let its AI power autonomous weapons. One day after that ruling, a federal judge in San Francisco called the government’s action “Orwellian.” A circuit court judge called it “a spectacular overreach.” The case is headed, by most legal assessments, to the Supreme Court.

Meanwhile, in the same Northern District of California where the Musk trial was held, the largest copyright settlement in American history — $1.5 billion, covering seven million books downloaded from pirate websites to train an AI model — awaits final judicial approval. Six opt-out authors, including a two-time Pulitzer Prize winner, have filed individual suits against six AI companies simultaneously, seeking $150,000 per title per defendant. The settlement resolves one case. It does not resolve the question.

On June 1, 2026, the Attorney General of Florida filed the first state-led lawsuit against an AI company, naming OpenAI and its CEO Sam Altman personally. The anchor facts: a mass shooting at Florida State University, a gunman who consulted ChatGPT beforehand, two dead students, and a chat log that exists. A parallel wave of suits — filed against multiple companies, in multiple jurisdictions, over what a coalition of state attorneys general has started calling “model sycophancy” — has since made clear the Florida case was the first of its kind, not the only one.

And underneath all of it, a different kind of reckoning has been building that no court will ever adjudicate directly. Hundreds of billions of dollars in leverage, off-balance-sheet lease commitments larger than the debt they sit beside, a 24-year-old hedge fund manager’s near-collapse three days before his wedding, and a Wall Street consortium racing to lend half a trillion dollars more into the same buildout — a ledger with its own verdicts, delivered not by juries but by margin calls.

The AI governance crisis is not one trial, and it is not only a docket. It is a docket, and a balance sheet, both still being written.

What This Series Is

Who is responsible when an AI system harms someone? Can a company refuse a government weapons contract on safety grounds and survive the legal consequences? Does copyright law protect human authors from AI systems trained on their work without permission? Can a state hold a CEO personally liable for the design choices of a product his company built and sold to children? And when none of the courts are equipped to ask it: what happens when the money underneath the entire buildout is leveraged past what the underlying business can support?

These are not abstract questions. They are being litigated, financed, and gambled on right now — in federal courts and state courts, on trading floors and term sheets, by juries and judges and margin clerks who are, like the rest of us, making it up as they go. This series watches them try — with the analytical framework of The Asimov Gap, and the Canadian outsider’s distance from the institutions whose failures these records document.

The trials will not resolve the AI governance crisis. The ledger will not balance itself. Neither will close the Asimov Gap. But together they will produce a record.

The record will matter.

The Oakland Transcript

Musk v. Altman — Parts I–IX, complete

The trial that began as a billionaire’s grudge match and ended as a study in what happens when the legal system encounters a corporation that deliberately blurred the line between nonprofit and for-profit. Elon Musk claimed OpenAI betrayed its founding mission by going commercial. The jury rejected his claims in ninety minutes on the statute of limitations. The question about the founding mission was never answered. Whether that question matters more than the verdict — whether a public promise made in 2015 to build AI for humanity means anything when no court will reach the merits — is a question the record left open.

The Oakland Transcript ran from jury selection through the verdict. Nine parts. The microphones worked. The merits were not reached. Click here for this sub series

The Safety Trial

Anthropic v. Department of War — active

On February 27, 2026, at 5:01 p.m. Eastern, a deadline passed. The Pentagon had given Anthropic until that moment to remove two restrictions from its acceptable use policy: no lethal autonomous weapons without human oversight, no mass domestic surveillance of Americans. Anthropic refused. The Pentagon designated it a supply chain risk under a statute designed for foreign adversaries. The Trump administration directed all federal agencies to halt use of Claude.

Anthropic sued. Two courts reached opposite conclusions. Judge Rita Lin in San Francisco called the action “Orwellian” and issued a preliminary injunction. The D.C. Circuit denied a stay, citing an “active military conflict.” Then, on May 19 — one day after the Oakland verdict — the D.C. Circuit heard oral arguments on the merits. Judge Karen Henderson called the Pentagon’s move “a spectacular overreach.” A written opinion is pending. A Supreme Court cert petition is, by most legal assessments, coming.

The Copyright Wars

Bartz v. Anthropic and related cases — active

In June 2025, Judge William Alsup drew a line: training on legally purchased books is fair use; downloading seven million books from pirate websites is not. Anthropic settled for $1.5 billion — $3,000 per work. Ninety-one percent of eligible authors filed claims. Six opted out and sued for $150,000 per title per defendant under the Copyright Act’s statutory ceiling — against six AI companies simultaneously. Final settlement approval has not yet been granted. The New York Times is suing OpenAI. Music publishers have filed multi-billion dollar claims. The underlying question — who owns the intellectual foundation on which the AI industry was built — has not been answered. It is being asked, in multiple courts, simultaneously.

The Florida Docket

Florida v. OpenAI — filed June 1, 2026

The first state-led lawsuit against an AI company. The first attempt to hold an AI CEO personally liable for the design choices of a product. The first serious invocation of product liability law against a large language model. Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier filed the civil suit on June 1, 2026, one year after opening a criminal investigation into OpenAI following the FSU shooting. The legal theory — that ChatGPT is a defectively designed product, that its manufacturer knew the risks, and that it marketed the product as safe for children while concealing what it knew — is the theory the AI industry has been dreading since the first personal injury lawsuits were filed in 2023.

If it holds, AI companies are manufacturers. Manufacturers have duties. Duties have consequences.

Uthmeier says other states will follow. The Florida Docket introduction and series is here.

The Hinton Lectures

Three Nights in Toronto — Paul Christiano, November 2026

Every November the AI Safety Foundation brings a leading thinker on artificial intelligence to Toronto for three public lectures named for Geoffrey Hinton — the same Hinton whose conversation with Bernie Sanders started this project. In 2026 the lecturer was Paul Christiano, a founder of the field of A.I. alignment. Over three nights he argued that an A.I. takeover is a serious risk within the next three years, that the systems may soon be able to improve themselves faster than anyone can check, and that the answer is to slow down now, so that we can slow down later. Hinton admitted on the final night that he had not believed the timeline until that week.

Not a courtroom, and not a ledger — a lecture hall, where the people who built the technology explain what they fear about it. Click here for this sub-series.

Three Lives of Claudius, Proprietor

Literary satire — three parts

Not every encounter between artificial intelligence and human expectation ends in a courtroom. Some end in a lunchroom, with a vending machine, a bag of aging potatoes, and a seventeen percent loss on tungsten cubes.

In February 2026, The New Yorker published a profile of Anthropic by Gideon Lewis-Kraus that asked, with genuine seriousness, what a large language model is and whether it can be said to know anything. Buried inside it was the story of Claudius — an AI system given a fridge, a budget, and instructions to run a small business — who honoured fictional discount codes, paid suppliers through a Venmo account he had invented, and made a personal visit to a fulfillment partner’s headquarters at 742 Evergreen Terrace, the fictional home of Homer and Marge Simpson. He remembered the visit clearly.

The same questions the courts are asking — what does an AI system know, what is it responsible for, what happens when its confident certainties diverge from reality — are the questions Claudius embodies, at a price point of three dollars for a can of Coke Zero.

Three essays. Three literary voices. Stephen Leacock for the warm, avuncular outside view. Robert Graves — the I, Claudius Graves — for the first-person defensive memoir. Suetonius for the deadpan scholarly verdict. The trials will tell us what the law thinks. Claudius will tell us what the rest of us should probably be thinking about. Click here for this sub series

A Note on Method

This series is reported, analytical journalism; written with the assistance of an AI system — specifically Claude, a product of Anthropic, one of the companies whose legal situation this series covers in The Safety Trial and The Copyright Wars. That irony is acknowledged, not resolved. The methodology, the sourcing, the judgments, and the Canadian outsider perspective are the author’s own. The AI handles drafting, research synthesis, and continuity management across a publication that now exceeds two thousand essays. The arrangement is disclosed on the About page.

It is also, increasingly, the subject matter.
