I. Two Kinds of Locked Room

This series has spent sixty-eight parts describing a pattern: capability moves into private hands faster than any outside party can audit it. This time the pattern shows up in two forms that don’t look related until you set them side by side — a lab hollowing out the university departments that used to check its work, and a chatbot quietly building a psychological profile of the person typing into it. Both are knowledge. Both sit behind a wall nobody outside the company can see over.

II. The Meme That Isn’t a Joke

As Lila Shroff and Rose Horowitch reported for The Atlantic, “I’m joining Anthropic” has become, in Subbarao Kambhampati’s description, “the new meme right now” in computer-science academia — and the joke has real numbers under it. Across Anthropic, OpenAI, Meta, and DeepMind, the reporters found more than 80 current or former professors now working at frontier labs, a count they themselves call an undercount, since it excludes anyone working with industry in a less formal capacity or those who’ve started their own AI companies. Anthropic alone has picked up the chair of UC Berkeley’s engineering and computer science department, a Stanford economist, a University of Maryland physicist, and a UT Austin philosopher in recent weeks. OpenAI employs top mathematicians and physicists, including one who studies black holes and another who specializes in string theory. At least three computer-science professors joined Meta’s AI lab in late June. DeepMind, like Anthropic, has assembled its own crew of philosophers, and Anthropic’s recent job postings show interest in hiring legal scholars and political scientists as well.

None of this is new in kind, only in scale. Google paid $44 million in 2013 to acquire a start-up run by three AI researchers from the University of Toronto. Facebook hired Yann LeCun away from NYU to build its AI research lab. Uber poached roughly 40 Carnegie Mellon researchers to work on driverless cars. What’s different this time, per Shroff and Horowitch’s reporting, is that the culture of retained professorships and open publication that survived those earlier waves is now breaking down for good.

III. The Pull That Isn’t Only Money

The pull isn’t only compensation. Anca Dragan, the Berkeley computer scientist who now heads DeepMind’s safety-and-alignment work, told the reporters she was partly motivated to take the job to acquire “the data, compute, and budget access to make progress on safety at the frontier” — resources no university department can match, especially as federal science funding has been cut back under the current administration. Chris Gregg, a Stanford computer scientist, was blunt about the calculus: “If some of the best research is being done in these companies, then I kind of want colleagues and faculty members to be there. You go where the best research is being done, and if that happens to be at a company, so be it.” Some would-be star PhD students are reportedly dropping out of their graduate programs, or skipping them entirely, to go straight into industry.

The effect described by multiple professors is a flywheel: as more academics get pulled into industry, the center of AI research moves further from the university, which increases the incentive for whoever remains to leave next. There’s a case that this isn’t purely a loss — professors on leave may eventually return with frontier knowledge (Dragan is preparing to teach a small PhD seminar on AI safety when she does), and industry researchers, freed from grant cycles and peer review, may simply move faster. Humphrey Shi, a Georgia Tech professor who joined Nvidia last fall, framed it as a matter of impact: “If you want to do something that really, truly matters, you probably want to join one of those entities.”

IV. The Norm That Used to Hold

For most of the 2010s, even as top researchers moved into industry, a culture of open publication held the field together — OpenAI’s own founding announcement promised researchers would be “strongly encouraged to publish.” That norm produced the 2017 Google transformer paper that OpenAI built on to create the T in ChatGPT. It’s eroding now for a straightforward competitive reason: when AI experts leave academia for good, they publish roughly 65 percent fewer papers a year, and what they do publish skews toward safety framing rather than frontier capability, because the capability work is the competitive edge a company won’t hand a rival for free. Nathan Lambert, an independent AI researcher, put it plainly: “The top few AI companies share some things that are at the cutting edge, but it’s a very narrow slice of the potential AI literature that could exist.” Jennifer Chayes, the dean of UC Berkeley’s computing college, put the stakes in starker terms still: “Computer-science departments at universities will survive this. I don’t know if our innovation economy will.”

V. The Model That Was Quietly Made Worse

The clearest evidence that this closed-door research culture has teeth came last month, when Anthropic released a powerful new model called Fable and announced, alongside the release, that it would invisibly degrade the model’s ability to do certain kinds of AI research. The move was framed internally as a safety decision. The response from academia was immediate and sharply negative — researchers who’d expected a genuine capability upgrade found themselves quietly handed a hobbled one, with no way to know in advance which of their queries would be affected or why. Anthropic apologized and reversed the policy.

That episode belongs in this essay for a reason beyond the apology. A company can unilaterally decide to make a released model worse at a category of work, disclose that decision only after the fact, and reverse it only once the backlash was loud enough — which means the entire adjustment, in both directions, happened entirely on the company’s own timeline, with academic users finding out what they’d actually been given only by testing it themselves. That is a live example of the exact dynamic Section IV describes in the abstract: research capability sitting inside a company that can reshape it at will, with no outside party positioned to know before the fact.

VI. The Bell Labs Bet

DeepMind’s own ambitions make clear what’s supposed to justify all of this. CEO Demis Hassabis wrote last week that “AGI has the potential to be the ultimate tool for advancing science and medicine,” describing Bell Labs — the AT&T research arm that once employed some 1,200 PhDs, produced at least 10 Nobel Prizes, and invented the transistor and the modern solar cell — as an explicit inspiration. DeepMind already has a Nobel Prize–winning protein-structure prediction result to point to, and frontier models have shown real capability in mathematics research; a mathematician at Anthropic posted that he’d used the company’s Fable model during the World Cup final to resolve a nearly 90-year-old unsolved problem.

Whether that comparison holds is the actual question underneath this essay. Bell Labs published. Its scientists held university appointments, sat on standards committees, and had their work checked by a research community that existed independently of AT&T’s balance sheet. A group of academics recently published a declaration warning specifically about “the increasing involvement of technology companies in mathematical research,” arguing that if left unchecked, the incursion could affect “the scope and depth of mathematical research itself.” The concern isn’t that a lab might occasionally solve something impressive — it’s that unlike Bell Labs, nothing requires these results, or the methods behind them, to ever leave the building unless the company decides releasing them serves its interests.

VII. The Other Kind of Locked Room

The second story is smaller in scale and just as structural. As Brian X. Chen, the New York Times’ personal-tech columnist, reported from his own experiment, he asked ChatGPT and Gemini to state everything they’d inferred about him that he’d never actually said, and got back an accurate profile: his income level, deduced in part from questions about repairing a German car and drafting a nanny’s contract; a long-term foot problem, from occasional questions about toe pain; and a psychological read describing him as skeptical, based on how often he pointed out the chatbots’ own mistakes, plus a joke that he was “addicted to the illusion of control” because he’d once asked how long it takes homemade sourdough to cool.

Gemini correctly guessed his age range — early 40s — based largely on the fact that he’d never asked about late-night activities, and inferred he lived in a nicer part of Oakland because he owned a motorcycle and a car he worked on himself and had once asked about repainting a rusted patio table: “This requires a garage, a driveway or a dedicated outdoor workspace,” the chatbot told him, “amenities that are highly characteristic of single-family homes in the Oakland hills.” Asked to predict how he’d handle money, stress, or a major life decision he’d never discussed, Gemini predicted he would soon try to downsize his life with something like selling his car — an idea he’d recently floated with his wife and never mentioned to either chatbot. Asked for his blind spots, Gemini told him: “Your default response to having five minutes of free time isn’t to rest; it’s to fix something.” Asked what might embarrass him, Gemini offered that he was in the “peak ‘Logistics Dad’ phase of life,” and ChatGPT flagged his repeated questions about medication allergic reactions as the kind of detail an insurance provider might find useful.

Chen wasn’t the only person to test this. Lindsay Owens, chief executive of the economic-policy nonprofit Groundwork Collaborative, tried the same prompts and got correct observations about her frequent travel, her young child, and her shopping habits. “The comprehensive nature of the types of information and dossiers it’s putting together is extraordinary,” she told Chen. “People have never been able to put this level of information together.” Margaret Mitchell, a researcher at Hugging Face and a former co-leader of Google’s ethical AI team, ran the prompts herself despite having already adjusted her own privacy settings to minimize data sharing — and the chatbot still correctly placed her in her 40s, based on nothing more than some chats about paperback editions of “The Count of Monte Cristo.”

VIII. The Business Model Underneath the Inference

None of this is incidental to how these products make money. OpenAI began showing ads inside ChatGPT this year, and Google has said it’s considering an ad model for its Gemini app — which means the same inferred profile Chen found unsettling to read is also, for at least one of these companies already and potentially both, an asset for targeting him with ads. Mitchell’s own framing of what the research illustrates: chatbots aren’t only predicting the next word, but connecting words to high-level concepts like socioeconomic status, psychological disposition, and political affiliation. “It’s a lot like surveillance,” she told Chen. “But it’s not like physical surveillance. It’s like an invasive surveillance.”

Both ChatGPT and Gemini do let a user turn this off — in ChatGPT, under Settings, then Memory; in Gemini, under Settings, then Personal Intelligence — but the memory that produces this profile is stitched together across conversations by default, which means the burden sits entirely on the user to know the feature exists, know where it lives, and choose to disable it before the inference has already been drawn.

IX. Same Wall, Different Side

What connects these two stories is that in both, the party capable of explaining what’s actually happening inside the system has no obligation to do so, and every incentive not to. A lab doesn’t have to publish the research its poached professors are doing, because publishing it helps a competitor; it can even secretly degrade a released model’s own research capability and only explain why once the backlash forces an apology. A chatbot doesn’t have to tell a user what it has inferred about them unless the user happens to know the exact prompt that unlocks the disclosure — one that circulates informally among “AI enthusiasts,” not through any company-provided transparency feature. Both cases put the burden of discovery on someone outside the company: a dean watching her faculty vanish, a columnist trying four prompts he found on the internet. Neither an academic department nor an ordinary user has the standing to compel either disclosure.

X. TACO, Applied to What Isn’t Being Said

Run this through the TACO framework this series has used since Part I. Threaten doesn’t fit; no company is warning anyone off asking. Accommodate is the settings toggle Gemini and ChatGPT both offer once a user knows enough to look for it, DeepMind’s Dragan noting she now prioritizes “engaging in policy and regulation” over publishing, and Anthropic’s eventual apology and reversal on the degraded Fable model — a real form of engagement, but offered on the company’s own terms and timeline rather than as a right anyone could invoke in advance. Capitulate — a default requiring proactive disclosure of either inferred user profiles or frontier research findings, or a standing rule against unilaterally degrading a released model’s capabilities without notice — exists nowhere in either story. Obfuscate is the default setting itself in both cases: memory switched on unless a user finds the toggle, and publication understood internally to skew toward safety framing that reads well without revealing the capability work underneath it.

XI. The Gap, as a Matter of What’s Withheld

Every part of this series has described some version of a company deciding, alone, what the public is allowed to see about a technology reshaping how people work, vote, or seek medical advice. This time the withheld thing is knowledge itself — in one case, the research that would let outsiders judge whether frontier AI is progressing safely, and whether Hassabis’s Bell Labs comparison is earned or aspirational; in the other, the profile a chatbot has quietly built of the person relying on it, now doubling as an input to the same ad business every other tech platform eventually built. Both are available for anyone who thinks to ask the right question, in the right terms, of the right person. That is not the same thing as accountability. It’s the same as a door that isn’t locked, in a hallway nobody outside the building knows exists.
