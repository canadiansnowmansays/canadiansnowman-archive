In San Salvador, in a neighbourhood where everyone knew everyone, a woman named Cecilia ran a pupusa shop out of her home. Pupusas — the fat little tortilla pouches stuffed with cheese and beans and slow-cooked pork — are El Salvador’s signature dish, the kind of food that anchors a family to a place. Every country has one. In Canada, it’s the butter tart or the poutine stand on the corner. In Bhutan, it’s the family making ema datshi in a kitchen where the recipe has never been written down because it doesn’t need to be. The pupusa shop is not just a business. It is a declaration that a family is rooted, that it belongs somewhere, that it intends to stay.

Cecilia’s whole family worked the business. Her husband was a former police officer. Her sons bought ingredients from the grocery store and made deliveries. As CNN reported in a detailed profile of the family’s background, “Everyone in the town knew to get their pupusas from ‘Pupuseria Cecilia,’” his son’s lawyers would later write, years afterward, in an American federal court filing that no one in San Salvador could have imagined. It is worth pausing here, before the story darkens, to understand what El Salvador was in those years. The country had been torn apart by civil war from 1979 to 1992 — a conflict that killed 75,000 people in a nation smaller than New Jersey. The peace accords brought an end to the fighting but not to the violence. Into the vacuum left by war came the gangs — MS-13 and Barrio 18 chief among them — many of them seeded, ironically, by deportees returned from the United States, where Central American refugees had been absorbed into the gang culture of Los Angeles. The gangs metastasised across El Salvador, Guatemala, and Honduras, extorting families, recruiting children, and turning entire neighbourhoods into territories where the law of the state was a polite fiction and the law of the clique was the only one that mattered.

A drone view of Los Nogales neighborhood, where Kilmar Abrego Garcia used to live, in San Marcos, El Salvador, on April 21. Jose Cabezas/Reuters

This was the El Salvador that Kilmar Abrego Garcia was born into. His father was a former police officer — a detail that, in a country where police corruption was endemic and gang retaliation against officers’ families was routine, carried its own particular danger. His mother Cecilia sold pupusas. The family was not wealthy. They were not connected. They were, by every measure, the kind of people that history is supposed to leave alone.

History did not leave them alone.

The gang came for the family the way gangs always come — first for money, then for bodies. As the Nashville Banner’s comprehensive timeline documented, Barrio 18 began extorting the family for “rent money” — the mordida, the bite, the weekly tax that gang-controlled neighbourhoods impose on anyone trying to make a living. They threatened to kill Cecilia’s older son César, or force him into their ranks, if the family didn’t pay. The family complied. They always do, at first. The arithmetic of survival in gang territory is simple: you pay, or you die, or you run. There is no fourth option.

They paid. And then they sent César to the United States. He would eventually become a U.S. citizen — a fact that matters later in this story, because it means that when the American government came for his younger brother, it was reaching into a family that had already woven itself into the American fabric.

But Barrio 18 wasn’t finished with the family. With César gone, they turned to the younger son. As CNN’s detailed account of his childhood described, gang members found Abrego Garcia’s family and began demanding money again, this time trying to recruit the teenage boy directly. They visited his home twice, threatened to rape and kill his two sisters, and threatened Kilmar himself. The family was so afraid for his safety that they kept him inside as much as they could. A boy who should have been walking to school, playing football in the street, buying ingredients for his mother’s pupusa shop — confined to the house because the gang wanted him, and the family had no one to call. The police were not coming. In the El Salvador of those years, the police were as likely to be part of the problem as the solution. The family closed the pupusa shop. They moved to another neighbourhood — a place called 10 de Octubre. The threats followed them. They fled to Guatemala. The threats did not stop. After about four months of running, the family made the decision that families across Central America have made by the millions: they sent the boy north.

In 2011, at sixteen years old, Kilmar Abrego Garcia crossed the border near McAllen, Texas, and entered the United States without authorization. He made his way to Maryland, where César was already living. He found work in construction. As the Britannica entry on his case notes, he “settled in Prince George’s County, Maryland, near Washington, D.C., where he would begin his new life.”

He was, by every available account, an ordinary young man doing ordinary things: working with his hands, learning a trade, sending money home when he could, building what Americans like to call a life. He was not political. He was not prominent. He was not, by any definition that would matter in a courtroom, dangerous. He was a teenager from a pupusa family who had been chased out of his country by a gang and had landed in the suburban sprawl of greater Washington, where he did what immigrants have always done — he worked. The years between 2011 and 2016 are less well documented. He worked construction. He stayed out of trouble — no criminal record, no arrests, no charges of any kind. He was, in the language of immigration law, present without authorization but living within the rules of the life he’d built. He checked in with immigration authorities when required. He was invisible in the way that millions of undocumented workers in the United States are invisible — essential to the economy, present in the community, absent from the political conversation until someone decides to make them visible.

In 2016, he met Jennifer Stefania Vasquez Sura. As NBC Philadelphia reported in its profile of his case, Jennifer was a U.S. citizen, born in Fairfax, Virginia. She had two children from a prior relationship — one with epilepsy, the other with autism. She was, by her own account, just getting out of an abusive relationship when she met Kilmar. He moved in with her and the children in 2018, after she learned she was pregnant. They lived in Prince George’s County, just outside Washington. They were, by any measure, a family — complicated in the way that families are, held together by love and necessity and the daily labour of raising children with special needs in a country where the safety net has holes you can fall through.

Their son was born while Kilmar was in immigration detention — a detail we will come to in Part II. He missed the birth. He would later marry Jennifer through the glass barrier of a visitation room. These are the kinds of details that news coverage tends to mention in passing, a single sentence in a timeline, but they are the details that tell you who a person is. A man who marries a woman through glass, who takes on two stepchildren with disabilities, who works construction six days a week to support a family that the United States government will later try to scatter across three continents — that man is telling you something about what he values. He is telling you that he has planted his feet. This is where the story should end — or rather, where it should continue in the unremarkable way that most immigrant stories continue, with work and children and the slow accumulation of an American life. A union apprenticeship in sheet metal. School meetings for the kids. Grocery runs and rent payments and the annual check-in with immigration authorities that confirmed, each year, that the government knew where he was and had decided, for another year, to let him stay.

But this particular American life was about to collide with the machinery of the state, and the collision would begin, absurdly, in a Home Depot parking lot. In March 2019, Kilmar Abrego Garcia went looking for day labour at a Home Depot in Hyattsville, Maryland. He was wearing a Chicago Bulls hat. That hat — a piece of sportswear that you can buy at any mall in North America — would change his life, and eventually, test the Constitution of the United States.
