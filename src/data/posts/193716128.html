By Stephen Gordon | CanadianSnowmanSays

Late Ottoman-era Jerusalem photograph, circa 1890s

There is a four-hundred-year chapter in the history of Jerusalem and the surrounding lands that Western coverage of the Middle East has almost entirely deleted from its account of how the current catastrophe began.

It runs from 1517, when the Ottoman Sultan Selim I defeated the Mamluk Sultanate and absorbed the Levant into the Ottoman Empire, to 1917, when a British general walked through the Jaffa Gate and announced that the city had been liberated. Four centuries. Sixteen generations. A period longer than the entire history of European settlement in North America, longer than the United States has existed, longer than the gap between Shakespeare and the present day.

During those four centuries, the Ottoman Empire governed Palestine, Jerusalem, Syria, Iraq, Egypt, and most of the Arab world within a single administrative system. Jews, Muslims, and Christians lived in the same cities, traded in the same markets, and prayed at their respective holy sites under a framework that was unequal, frequently unjust, occasionally brutal, and — compared to what came before and after — remarkably stable.

This chapter is absent from most Western discussion of the conflict for a simple reason: it disrupts the narrative. The story that Israel’s supporters most commonly tell — and that Western media most commonly repeats — locates the conflict’s origins in the encounter between the returning Jewish people and the Arab world’s refusal to accept their presence. The story that Palestinian advocates most commonly tell locates the conflict in the 1948 dispossession, sometimes in the 1917 Balfour Declaration.

Both stories skip the Ottoman period almost entirely.

This is not a minor omission. The Ottoman period tells us something neither side particularly wants to hear: that Jews and Arabs lived in the same territory for four hundred years without permanent large-scale conflict, under a framework that was built on legal distinctions between communities rather than on the elimination of any of them. That framework was imperfect. It was not apartheid. The distinction matters and the deletion of it serves no one’s honest understanding.

The Ottoman System: How It Actually Worked

The Ottoman Empire at its greatest extent, in the late 17th century, stretched from the gates of Vienna in the northwest to the Persian Gulf in the east, from the Crimea in the north to Yemen and Morocco in the south. It encompassed within its borders more religious diversity than any other political entity on earth — Sunni and Shia Muslims, Greek and Armenian and Syriac Orthodox Christians, Roman Catholics, Jews of Sephardic and Ashkenazi and Mizrahi tradition, Druze, Alawites, and dozens of smaller communities. Governing that diversity across a territory that vast, without modern communications or transportation, required a system that could accommodate difference rather than eliminate it.

The Ottomans developed the millet system — from the Arabic/Turkish word for nation or community — which granted each recognized religious community the right to govern its own internal affairs according to its own law. The Jewish community had its own courts for personal status matters — marriage, divorce, inheritance. The Greek Orthodox community had its own hierarchy. The Armenian Apostolic community had its own Patriarch. Each millet paid taxes to the Ottoman state, acknowledged Ottoman sovereignty, and in exchange was left largely to manage its own communal life.

The system was explicitly unequal. Muslims were the dominant community — the Ottomans were a Muslim dynasty and Islamic law was the law of the empire’s public sphere. Non-Muslims paid the jizya, a poll tax that marked their subordinate status and was periodically resented and occasionally a source of genuine hardship. Non-Muslims faced restrictions on building new houses of worship, on the height of those houses of worship, on riding horses, on certain public displays of their faith. These were real disabilities and should not be romanticized.

But they were not extermination. They were not expulsion. They were not the systematic destruction of a population’s ability to exist on the land.

Jews had lived in Palestine continuously under Ottoman rule. Jerusalem had a Jewish community. Safed, in the Galilee, was a major centre of Jewish religious scholarship — the 16th century Kabbalistic school there produced the Shulchan Aruch, the foundational code of Jewish law that remains authoritative today. Hebron had a Jewish community. These were not prosperous communities by the standards of European Jewish life. They were, for the most part, small, poor, and dependent on charitable support from Jewish communities abroad. But they were there. The idea that no Jews lived in Palestine before the Zionist immigration waves of the late 19th century is a myth, and it is a myth that serves no one well.

What the Ottoman system produced, across four centuries, was not harmony. It produced management — the management of irreducible difference within a framework that gave each community enough stake in the system’s stability to make outright rebellion more costly than accommodation. That is a lower bar than justice. It is a considerably higher bar than what replaced it.

The Photographs That Should Be Required Viewing

In the 1890s and 1900s, a group of American Christian missionaries established a photographic studio in Jerusalem that became known as the American Colony. They photographed everything — the Old City’s streets, its markets, its residents, its holy sites, its daily commerce. Their archive, now held at the Library of Congress, contains thousands of images of a city that the subsequent century has done its best to make unimaginable.

Fascinating photos from the 1890s restored to full color reveal daily life in Jerusalem

You can see in those photographs Arab merchants and Jewish traders conducting business in adjacent stalls. You can see the crowds outside the Church of the Holy Sepulchre during Easter — Christian pilgrims from a dozen countries, Arab Christian locals, curious Muslim neighbours. You can see the Jewish Quarter’s narrow streets and the Muslim Quarter’s caravanserais and the Armenian Quarter’s quiet courtyards, all within the same city walls, all functioning simultaneously.

You can see what the conflict has told us was impossible: people of different faiths living on the same land, in the same city, in conditions of imperfect but functional coexistence.

I am not romanticizing those photographs. Ottoman Jerusalem was not a paradise of religious brotherhood. There were tensions, discriminations, periodic violence, deep social hierarchies that mapped onto religious community in ways that disadvantaged Jews and Christians. There were years of drought, epidemic, economic hardship. There were political crises within the Ottoman system that had local reverberations.

But those photographs exist. They are not fabrications. And they are evidence that the premise underlying much of the current conflict’s worst rhetoric — that Jews and Arabs are inherently, permanently, cosmically incapable of coexisting in the same territory — is not a historical truth. It is a political choice, made and remade by specific actors for specific reasons, that has been allowed to harden into the appearance of inevitability.

The Cracks Begin: Nationalism as the Solvent of Empire

The Ottoman system had maintained its imperfect stability for four centuries. What dissolved it was not ancient hatreds suddenly released from constraint. It was something new, something European, something that the Ottoman world encountered as an import and could not ultimately survive: nationalism.

The 19th century was the age of European nationalism. The idea that the world’s populations were naturally organized into nations — defined by shared language, ethnicity, history, and culture — and that each nation had the right and the duty to govern itself as a sovereign state, was a European intellectual invention that remade the map of Europe through a series of revolutions and wars. It was an idea of enormous mobilizing power and enormous destructive potential, because almost no territory on earth contained a single homogeneous population that mapped cleanly onto a national boundary.

The Ottoman Empire was the most extreme example of this problem. It contained dozens of peoples, languages, religions, and cultures that the nationalist logic insisted were separate nations — Greek, Bulgarian, Serbian, Romanian, Armenian, Arab, Kurdish, Turkish, Jewish — each of which could now claim, on the nationalist model, the right to its own state. The empire spent the 19th century in managed collapse, losing territory in Europe as one subject people after another broke free with the assistance of European powers who found the empire’s weakness convenient.

Two nationalisms are directly relevant to this story.

The first is Arab nationalism — the idea, developing in the late 19th century among Arab intellectuals, many of them Christian, that the Arabic-speaking peoples of the Ottoman Empire constituted a nation that deserved self-governance. Arab nationalism was not primarily religious — its early intellectual leaders included many Arab Christians who were skeptical of Islamic political authority — but it was shaped by the Ottoman context and by the grievances of Arab populations who felt culturally and politically subordinated to Turkish imperial authority. By the early 20th century, Arab nationalist sentiment had developed a significant political movement, particularly in Syria and among the Hashemite clan of the Hejaz, that the British would exploit with considerable cynicism during the First World War.

The second is Zionism — Jewish nationalism. This is where the modern conflict truly begins.

The Invention of Zionism

Theodor Herzl before 1900

In August 1897, in Basel, Switzerland, a 37-year-old Austro-Hungarian journalist named Theodor Herzl convened the First Zionist Congress. Two hundred delegates from seventeen countries attended. They debated, argued, and ultimately agreed on the program of the World Zionist Organization: the establishment of a home for the Jewish people in Palestine, secured by public law.

That night, Herzl wrote in his diary: “At Basel I founded the Jewish state. If I said this out loud today I would be greeted by universal laughter. In five years perhaps, and certainly in fifty years, everyone will perceive it.”

He was off by one year. Fifty-one years after the Basel Congress, the State of Israel declared independence.

The First Zionist Congress 1897

Zionism was a response to a genuine and urgent problem. European Jews in the late 19th century faced systematic persecution of increasing severity — pogroms in Russia, structural antisemitism in France (the Dreyfus Affair occurred in 1894, directly radicalizing Herzl), legal discrimination across central and eastern Europe, the ever-present threat of violence that had characterized Jewish life in the Diaspora for centuries. The question Zionism was answering was a real one: how do a stateless people protect themselves when the nations that contain them are demonstrably willing to murder them?

Herzl’s answer was: become a nation. Get a territory. Build a state. The logic was compelling and the urgency was genuine. The problem was the territory.

Palestine was Herzl’s preference — its historical and biblical connection to the Jewish people made it the obvious choice for Zionist purposes, the only location that could mobilize the mass of Jewish sentiment necessary to build a movement. But Palestine was not empty. It was home to approximately 500,000 people, the vast majority of them Arab Muslims and Arab Christians, living under Ottoman administration, with their own communities, their own social structures, their own sense of belonging to the land.

This was a problem that early Zionist thought handled in various ways, ranging from denial to rationalization to genuine discomfort. The phrase “a land without a people for a people without a land” — often attributed to early Zionist propaganda, though its precise origins are disputed — captures the denial strategy: if you simply do not count the existing population as a people in the relevant sense, the problem disappears. Israel Zangwill, an early Zionist, said it more directly: Palestine had to be “gently persuaded” to “trek,” a word that in the South African context he was drawing on meant something considerably less gentle than persuasion.

Herzl himself was more ambiguous. His 1902 utopian novel Altneuland — Old New Land — depicts a Palestine transformed by Jewish settlement into a prosperous modern state in which an Arab character named Reschid Bey is enthusiastic about the Jewish enterprise and grateful for the prosperity it has brought. It is a fantasy of frictionless colonialism — the newcomers arrive, everyone benefits, no one is displaced, the indigenous population cheers. Herzl genuinely may have believed some version of this. He also may have understood that the mass of European Jews needed the fantasy to mobilize.

Ahad Ha’am — the pen name of Asher Ginsberg, one of the most clear-eyed of early Zionist thinkers — did not believe the fantasy. After visiting Palestine in 1891, he wrote with remarkable prescience: “We abroad are used to believe that Eretz Yisrael is now almost totally desolate... But this is not true. It is hard to find fields that are not sowed... Arab workers are very experienced in the work of the land... We abroad are used to believe that the Arabs are all wild desert people who do not see or understand what is happening around them. But this is a grave mistake.” He warned that any attempt to dispossess the Arab population would produce permanent and violent resistance.

He was right. No one in the movement’s mainstream listened.

The British Enter, Bearing Promises

By the time the First World War began in 1914, the Ottoman Empire had been losing territory and stability for a century. Its entry into the war on the German side made it a target for British strategic dismemberment. Britain needed allies in the Arab world to destabilize the Ottomans from within. It also needed — or believed it needed — Jewish financial and political support in the United States and Russia. Two sets of promises were made to two groups of people about the same territory.

King Hussein in 1916

To Sharif Hussein of Mecca — the Hashemite guardian of the holy cities of Mecca and Medina, whose descendants still rule Jordan today — the British promised, through the Hussein-McMahon correspondence of 1915-1916, that Britain would support Arab independence in the former Ottoman Arab territories if the Arabs revolted against the Ottomans. The correspondence was artfully vague about which territories precisely would be included in Arab independence. The British intended that vagueness. Palestine was a territory they wanted to keep their options open on.

To the Zionist movement — or more precisely, to Lord Walter Rothschild as representative of the British Jewish community — the British promised, through the Balfour Declaration of November 1917, that His Majesty’s Government would “view with favour the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people.” The Declaration, which we will examine in forensic detail in Part IV, was issued while Britain was in the process of conquering Palestine from the Ottomans — promising a territory it did not yet fully control to a people who did not live there, in a letter that contained, like a ticking bomb, a single qualifying clause about not prejudicing the civil and religious rights of the existing non-Jewish communities.

Those communities were not told about the letter. They were not consulted. Their preferences were not solicited. They were described, in the letter that would determine the fate of their land, not by name, not as Arabs, not as Palestinians, but as “the existing non-Jewish communities” — defined entirely by what they were not.

This is where the compound interest on the original sin begins accruing at a rate that the 20th century could not contain. In Part IV, we will read that letter, word by word, and trace exactly what it set in motion.

What the Ottoman Collapse Destroyed

The Ottoman Empire formally ended in 1922 when Mustafa Kemal — Atatürk — abolished the Sultanate and established the Turkish Republic. But it had functionally collapsed years earlier, and what its collapse destroyed was something that has never been adequately reconstructed.

The last Sultan of the Ottoman empire, Mehmed VI, departs from the back doorof the Dolmabahce Palace November 1, 1922

The Ottoman system, for all its inequalities, provided a framework within which multiple communities could claim legitimate presence in the same territory simultaneously. Jews in Palestine had a place in that framework. Arabs in Palestine had a place in that framework. Christians in Palestine had a place in that framework. No community’s presence required the elimination of any other community’s presence.

The British Mandate that replaced Ottoman rule did not provide this framework. The Balfour Declaration’s promise of a Jewish national home was, by definition, in tension with the Arab majority population’s aspiration to self-governance. The British spent the Mandate years — which we will examine in Part V — attempting to balance these incompatible commitments, satisfying neither side, inflaming both, and ultimately handing the catastrophe to the United Nations and departing with what they imagined were clean hands.

They were not clean. They are not clean today. The decisions made in London between 1915 and 1920 — the promises made, the maps drawn, the populations described or not described, the interests weighed or not weighed — are the direct ancestors of every photograph of destruction we see today from Gaza, from Lebanon, from the rubble of what was once a functioning human civilization.

The Ottoman peace was imperfect. What replaced it was catastrophic. And the people who replaced it never bore the consequences.

They never do.
