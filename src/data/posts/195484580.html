The United States possesses the largest economy in human history — $28 trillion GDP, roughly 26% of global output from just 4% of the world’s population. No nation has ever had greater raw capacity to improve the human condition. On the Contribution Index, it scores 77 out of 200. The gap is the widest in the series.

Underperformer #1

We have arrived.

Over the past nineteen essays and one special edition, we have counted down through twenty-one nations — ten that are genuinely contributing to humanity and the planet, ten that possess the resources and capacity to lead but are choosing not to, and one — Bhutan — that defied categorisation entirely. We began at home, with Canada — the well-meaning hypocrite that barely scraped into the top ten — and climbed through the countdown to Norway, a nation that scores highest on this index despite the fundamental contradiction of funding its virtue with petroleum revenue.

On the underperformer side, we descended from Turkey through Brazil, Israel, the UAE, Australia, India, Saudi Arabia, China, and Russia — each essay documenting a wider gap between what a nation has and what it gives back. Russia, at 53 out of 200, holds the lowest total score in the series — the wasted inheritance of a nation that chose kleptocracy and war over everything its talent and resources could have built.

Now we arrive at the nation that ranks above Russia on the underperformer list. Not because its score is lower — it is not. But because its capacity is so vast, so unprecedented, so without parallel in human history, that the gap between what it could contribute and what it actually contributes is the widest canyon in the series.

And we arrive in the middle of a war that it chose to start.

The Scorecard

Note on trajectory: The democratic governance score of 6 reflects current conditions under the second Trump administration and represents a significant decline from where the United States would have scored as recently as 2022. The systemic behaviour and Neighbour Test scores reflect Operation Epic Fury and its cascading consequences. If the trajectory continues, several scores on this card will be lower within the year.

The Analysis

I want to begin with a number: $28 trillion.

That is the approximate GDP of the United States of America. It is, by a significant margin, the largest economy in human history. It represents roughly 26 percent of global economic output, produced by approximately 4 percent of the global population.

The United States possesses the most advanced university system on Earth. The most sophisticated technology sector. The deepest capital markets. The most powerful military. The most extensive diplomatic network. The most influential cultural exports. On the composite capacity measure I defined in the Introduction — GDP per capita, total GDP, tertiary education rates, and institutional quality — the United States ranks first in the world. No nation has more raw ability to improve the human condition. No nation has ever had more.

And on the Contribution Index, it scores 77 out of 200.

To feel the weight of that number, hold it against what we learned in Part VII. Costa Rica — a nation with a GDP one four-hundredth the size of America’s, a nation with no army, a nation whose entire economy is smaller than what the US spends on military catering — scores 153. The nation with the most to give is giving the least relative to its capacity. The nation with the least to give is giving the most.

The Capacity-to-Contribution Gap for the United States is not merely large. It is the largest of any nation assessed in this series. It is, I believe, the largest such gap in human history. And it is growing wider.

Human Development: 30 out of 50

The United States scores an 8 on educational attainment — its universities remain the finest in the world, its research institutions are unmatched, and its capacity to attract global talent is extraordinary. Harvard, MIT, Stanford, Caltech — these are not merely American institutions. They are global infrastructure, producing research and graduates that benefit every nation on Earth. The 8 is earned.

But even here, the score conceals a troubling reality: American primary and secondary education is radically unequal, with outcomes tied directly to zip code and family income. A child in Palo Alto receives an education comparable to Finland’s. A child in rural Mississippi receives an education comparable to a developing nation’s. The nation that produces more Nobel laureates than any other also produces millions of functionally illiterate adults. The 8 reflects the peaks. The valleys would score a 4. The average is not the experience. The average is a fiction that obscures two separate education systems separated by class and race.

It is on healthcare that the American story becomes genuinely incomprehensible from the outside — and I say this as a Canadian who has watched the American healthcare debate for decades with a mixture of bewilderment and horror.

The United States spends more per capita on healthcare than any nation on Earth — roughly $13,000 per person annually, nearly double the average of other wealthy democracies. This is not a marginal difference. America outspends the next highest nation by roughly 40 percent. And for this extraordinary expenditure — for these trillions of dollars poured into the most expensive healthcare system ever constructed — it achieves outcomes that would embarrass a middle-income country.

Life expectancy in the United States has been declining — a phenomenon virtually unique among developed nations. It now stands at approximately 77 years — below Costa Rica, below Cuba, below every Western European nation, below Canada, below Japan, below Australia, below every nation on the top performer list. The gap between American healthcare spending and American healthcare outcomes is the most damning single statistic in this series. It is proof, expressed in years of human life, that spending is not the same as caring.

The reason is straightforward and has been documented so exhaustively that restating it feels almost redundant: the United States is the only wealthy democracy on Earth without universal healthcare coverage. Approximately 27 million Americans have no health insurance at all. Millions more are functionally underinsured — carrying plans with deductibles so high that they cannot afford to use them, which means they have insurance cards in their wallets and no actual access to care. Medical debt is the leading cause of personal bankruptcy in the United States — a sentence that, to a Canadian or a European or a Costa Rican, reads like a dispatches from a dystopian novel. Insulin rationing — the deliberate under-dosing of a medication discovered over a century ago because patients cannot afford the price that pharmaceutical companies charge — kills Americans every year.

This is not a failure of capacity. The United States can afford universal healthcare. It already spends more than enough — more than any nation that actually provides it. This is a failure of political will so profound that the Contribution Index struggles to score it. The United States chooses not to provide universal healthcare. That choice costs American lives every single day.

And here is what makes the American healthcare failure not merely a domestic tragedy but a moral obscenity visible from space: while 27 million Americans go uninsured, the nation spends $886 billion annually on its military — and has just launched a new war against Iran. The cost of Operation Epic Fury’s first week of Tomahawk cruise missiles and F-35 sorties would fund insulin access for every diabetic American for a year. The nation that cannot provide healthcare to its own citizens has unlimited resources for destroying the healthcare infrastructure of others.

Democratic governance scores a 6 — down from the 7.85 that the Economist Intelligence Unit awarded the United States in its 2024 Democracy Index, which already classified it as a “flawed democracy.” I have adjusted downward to reflect the trajectory under the current administration, and I want to be transparent about why.

The EIU’s score captures institutional structure — courts, legislatures, constitutional frameworks. On those measures, the United States still has significant democratic architecture. But the Contribution Index weights trajectory heavily, and the trajectory is alarming. The weaponization of the Department of Justice — using prosecutorial power to punish political opponents and protect political allies. The systematic attacks on press freedom — designating critical journalists as enemies, revoking press credentials, threatening media organisations with regulatory retaliation. The open defiance of judicial rulings — ignoring court orders when they produce inconvenient outcomes. The purging of independent inspectors general — eliminating the internal watchdogs whose job is to hold the executive accountable. The use of executive power to reward loyalty and punish dissent in ways that would be recognised immediately as authoritarian if they occurred in any nation that Americans were not conditioned to think of as exceptional.

The United States maintains an $886 billion annual military budget — larger than the next ten nations combined — and has launched Operation Epic Fury against Iran. The same nation that cannot guarantee healthcare to its own citizens has unlimited resources for discretionary war.

And a nation that launches a military campaign against a sovereign country of 88 million people without meaningful congressional debate — relying on two-decade-old authorisations for the use of military force that were written for Al-Qaeda and Saddam Hussein, not the Islamic Republic of Iran in 2026 — is not practising democratic governance of its most consequential decisions. It is practising executive unilateralism dressed in constitutional clothing. The constitutional architecture of American war-making has been reduced to a legal fiction: a president can bomb any nation on Earth, at any time, by citing authorizations that Congress has been too cowardly to revoke and too complicit to challenge.

Poverty reduction scores a 5. In the wealthiest nation on Earth, roughly 37 million people live below the poverty line — a number that, if it were a country, would be the thirty-sixth most populous nation in the world. Child poverty, which briefly plummeted during pandemic-era expanded tax credits, surged again when those credits expired — a political choice that demonstrated with brutal clarity that the United States knows exactly how to reduce child poverty and simply chooses not to sustain the effort. The knowledge is there. The mechanism is proven. The political will to maintain it beyond a single budget cycle is absent.

Gender equality and minority rights score a 6, reflecting a complex and actively deteriorating reality. Legal protections for women, racial minorities, and LGBTQ+ communities expanded significantly over several decades — the arc that Americans were taught to see as bending toward justice. That arc is now bending backward. The reversal of Roe v. Wade stripped reproductive rights from millions of women. The escalating attacks on transgender rights — banning healthcare for minors, restricting participation in public life, vilifying a vulnerable population for political advantage — represent a deliberate targeting of the most marginalized. The resurgence of racially targeted voter suppression, the mainstreaming of white nationalist rhetoric in political discourse, and the systematic rollback of diversity and inclusion programmes all pull this score downward from where it stood even five years ago.

Environmental Stewardship: 28 out of 50

The United States is the largest cumulative emitter of greenhouse gases in human history. No nation has contributed more to the atmospheric carbon burden that is driving global climate change. This is not a political position. It is a physical fact — measured in gigatons, documented in ice cores, and visible in the rising seas that threaten the Pacific Island nations that appear nowhere on any American news broadcast. This historical responsibility frames everything that follows.

Per capita emissions earn a 3. The average American produces roughly 14 metric tons of CO2 equivalent per year — more than double the global average, more than triple the average European, roughly seven times the average Chinese citizen, and approximately 28 times the average citizen of sub-Saharan Africa. The American lifestyle — built around suburban sprawl, automobile dependency, single-family homes, air conditioning, and energy-intensive consumption at every level — is the most carbon-intensive mode of living ever devised by a large population. It is a lifestyle that the planet cannot afford for 330 million people and that would be catastrophic if the other 7.7 billion attempted to replicate it.

Renewable energy share scores a 5, reflecting both genuine progress and inadequate ambition. The United States expanded its renewable energy capacity significantly in recent years, particularly in wind and solar, and the Inflation Reduction Act — signed in 2022 — represented the largest climate investment in American history. Solar installation costs have fallen dramatically. Wind energy is cost-competitive with fossil fuels in most markets. The infrastructure for a clean energy transition exists.

And the current administration is dismantling it. Expanding fossil fuel production on federal lands. Withdrawing from international climate commitments. Rolling back vehicle emissions standards. Attacking the IRA’s incentive structure. Appointing fossil fuel industry executives to regulatory positions. Signalling, in every way available, that the clean energy transition is not merely a lower priority but an active target for reversal.

America’s National Parks — one of its strongest environmental achievements — are increasingly threatened by climate change, drought, wildfires, and development pressure. Biodiversity protection scores a 6, but the overall stewardship score of 28 out of 50 reflects the gap between capacity and action.

The trajectory component of this score is what pulls it below where it might otherwise land. A nation moving from 22 percent renewable energy toward 50 percent scores higher than one moving from 22 percent back toward fossil fuels. Under current policy direction, the United States risks becoming the only major economy to deliberately reverse its clean energy transition. The level is a 6. The trajectory makes it a 5.

And now add the environmental dimension of Operation Epic Fury. The Persian Gulf, through which roughly 20 percent of the world’s oil supply transits daily, is now a conflict zone. The threat to shipping lanes has already caused oil price spikes that will, inevitably, slow the global energy transition — because when oil prices surge, the economic calculus for renewable investment shifts, capital flows toward fossil fuel production, and the urgency of transition fades behind the immediate pain of energy costs. The nation that launched the strikes is the same nation that claims to support clean energy. You cannot bomb the Gulf and champion the energy transition simultaneously. The contradiction is not subtle. It is measured in barrels and degrees.

Biodiversity protection scores a 6, reflecting the genuine strength of the National Park system — one of America’s most admirable institutional achievements, now threatened by development pressure, budget cuts, and the rollback of Endangered Species Act protections.

Systemic Behaviour: 11 out of 50

This is where the United States Contribution Index score collapses — and where Operation Epic Fury transforms an already dismal picture into something historically damning.

Arms exports: 1 out of 10.

The United States is the world’s largest arms exporter by an enormous margin — so large that the word “margin” understates the reality. According to SIPRI, the US accounted for 43 percent of all international arms transfers in the 2020-2024 period — more than the next eight largest exporters combined. American weapons flow to 107 countries. They fuel conflicts on every continent. They arm regimes that the State Department’s own annual human rights reports document as abusers — creating the peculiar spectacle of one branch of the US government cataloguing the human rights violations that another branch’s weapons sales make possible.

The United States is by far the world’s largest arms exporter, accounting for 43% of all global arms transfers — more than the next eight exporters combined. In 2026 it is not only selling the weapons but actively using them in a new discretionary war. This earns a 1 out of 10.

Global arms company revenues hit a record $679 billion in 2024, with American companies — Lockheed Martin, Raytheon, Boeing, Northrop Grumman, General Dynamics — leading the surge. These companies are among the most profitable in the American economy. Their products are designed to kill human beings. Their markets are the world’s conflicts. And in 2026, the United States is no longer merely selling the weapons. It is using them — in a new theatre, against a new target, in a conflict it initiated.

The strikes against Iran employed the full spectrum of American military technology: Tomahawk cruise missiles launched from destroyers in the Gulf, F-35 stealth fighters from carriers and regional bases, B-2 bombers flying from Missouri, cyber weapons that disabled Iranian air defence networks, and intelligence capabilities that located and eliminated an entire national leadership in a matter of hours. The message to the world was unmistakable: the United States possesses the capacity to destroy any government on Earth from the air, without warning, without a declaration of war, and without meaningful accountability to anyone — its own citizens, its allies, or the international community.

This is not arms export. This is arms deployment. And a nation that both dominates the global weapons trade and uses those weapons to wage discretionary war cannot score above the floor.

Tax haven and corruption facilitation: 3 out of 10.

Delaware — America’s corporate secrecy capital. It is now easier to create anonymous shell companies here than in many traditional offshore tax havens. U.S. states have become premier destinations for hidden wealth from kleptocrats, oligarchs, and tax evaders

The United States has emerged as one of the world’s most significant enablers of financial opacity — a development that would have seemed absurd a generation ago but that has been documented exhaustively in recent years. States like Delaware, Nevada, South Dakota, and Wyoming offer corporate secrecy that rivals or exceeds traditional offshore tax havens. It is easier to create an anonymous shell company in Delaware than in the Cayman Islands. South Dakota trusts now shelter hundreds of billions of dollars in assets behind legal structures designed to be impenetrable.

The Pandora Papers revealed that American trusts and shell companies are increasingly the vehicle of choice for global elites seeking to hide wealth from tax authorities and accountability mechanisms — from African kleptocrats to Russian oligarchs to domestic tax evaders. The nation that lectures the world about corruption and imposes anti-money-laundering requirements on every other country’s banks has become one of the world’s premier destinations for money that prefers not to be seen.

Refugee and humanitarian contribution: 2 out of 10.

The United States was once the world’s leader in refugee resettlement — a legacy built over decades, anchored by the post-World War II commitment to the displaced, the Vietnamese boat people, the Soviet émigrés, the Balkan refugees, and the post-9/11 Afghan and Iraqi interpreters who served alongside American forces. That legacy is being systematically dismantled.

Under the current administration, refugee admissions have been slashed to levels that would have been unrecognisable a decade ago. Asylum seekers face militarized borders, family separation policies that were condemned as cruel by the administration’s own officials, and detention conditions that international observers have compared to internment. The nation built by immigrants — a nation whose founding mythology centres on the welcome it extends to the world’s huddled masses — is closing its doors with increasing hostility and decreasing shame.

And Operation Epic Fury will create refugees. It is already creating them. When you decapitate the leadership of a nation of 88 million people, the resulting instability does not stay within borders. Iranian refugees. Iraqi displacement from regional destabilisation. Lebanese civilians fleeing renewed Hezbollah-Israeli conflict. The humanitarian consequences of the strikes are still unfolding, and the nation that launched them has simultaneously dismantled its own capacity to accept the people it is displacing.

A nation that creates refugees while refusing to accept them earns a 2. The score would be lower if the floor permitted it.

A nation built by immigrants that once led the world in refugee resettlement is now militarizing its borders, slashing admissions, and creating new refugee crises through its wars — while refusing to accept the people it displaces.

Adherence to international law: 1 out of 10.

The United States has withdrawn from the Paris Climate Agreement — twice now, under two different administrations of the same party. It does not recognize the jurisdiction of the International Criminal Court — and has, in previous administrations, threatened to sanction ICC officials who investigate American conduct. It has invaded sovereign nations without Security Council authorization — Iraq in 2003, and now, effectively, Iran in 2026. It maintains a network of military bases in over 80 countries — a global military footprint without historical precedent. It has used its Security Council veto more frequently than any other permanent member to shield allies from accountability — a power exercised most visibly and most consequentially on behalf of Israel.

And now it has launched a military campaign against Iran without a declaration of war, without UN authorization, without NATO invocation, and on the basis of legal authorities — the 2001 and 2002 Authorizations for Use of Military Force — that were written to address Al-Qaeda and Saddam Hussein’s Iraq, not the Islamic Republic of Iran in 2026. The constitutional architecture of American war-making has been stretched beyond recognition — a president can, as a matter of established practice, bomb any nation on Earth by citing authorizations that Congress passed for entirely different conflicts two decades ago and has been too politically craven to update or revoke.

The international order that the United States built after 1945 — the United Nations, the Geneva Conventions, the Bretton Woods institutions, the principle that sovereign nations do not attack one another without collective authorization — was imperfect but real. It was humanity’s best attempt, however flawed, to constrain the use of force through law rather than might. The United States designed that system. It articulated the principles. It built the institutions. It insisted, for decades, that other nations follow rules that it wrote.

The United States is not merely failing to uphold that order in 2026. It is actively, visibly, violently demolishing it. The house it built, it is now burning down. And it is burning it down in the same region where it burned the last one — Iraq, 2003, on the basis of intelligence and legal reasoning that proved catastrophically, lethally wrong. Twenty years. Hundreds of thousands of dead. Trillions of dollars spent. A region destabilised for a generation. And the lesson learned was: nothing. Do it again.

Exploitation versus investment in developing nations: 4 out of 10.

The relatively higher score here reflects genuine American development investment — USAID, PEPFAR (the President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief, which has saved millions of lives in sub-Saharan Africa), and bilateral aid programmes that, while politically motivated, have delivered real benefits. PEPFAR alone represents one of the most significant humanitarian programmes any nation has ever undertaken. The 4 rather than a lower score acknowledges this — even as the current administration cuts development funding, politicises aid distribution, and treats international development as a line item to be sacrificed rather than an investment to be protected.

The Neighbour Test: 8 out of 50

The second-lowest Neighbour Test score in the series. Above only Russia’s 6.

Let the five sub-parameters speak for themselves, because they tell the story of a nation that has systematically abandoned every principle it once claimed to represent.

Multilateral orientation: 2. The United States has withdrawn from the Paris Climate Agreement twice. It has rejected the ICC. It has defunded international organizations — including, at various points, the WHO, UNRWA, and the UN Population Fund. It has imposed tariffs that violate WTO frameworks. It has launched a military campaign against Iran without UN authorization, using legal authorities written for entirely different conflicts in an entirely different era. The post-war multilateral architecture that America designed and built and insisted the rest of the world respect is being dismantled by its architect. The 2 reflects the institutional legacy that still technically exists — the buildings in New York and Geneva, the treaties not yet formally abrogated, the alliances not yet officially dissolved. The trajectory is toward 1.

Response to global crises: 2. Operation Epic Fury is not a response to a global crisis. It is the creation of one. When you decapitate the leadership of a nation of 88 million people — when you kill the Supreme Leader, the president, the foreign minister, and over forty senior officials in a coordinated strike — the resulting instability does not stay within borders. It cascades. Iranian refugees fleeing uncertainty. Iraqi displacement from renewed sectarian tension. Lebanese civilians caught between Hezbollah and Israel. Global oil markets convulsing. Energy prices spiking. The clean energy transition slowing. Allied nations scrambling to distance themselves from an escalation they were not consulted about and do not support.

The nation that launched the strikes has simultaneously dismantled its own capacity to absorb the humanitarian consequences. Refugee admissions slashed. Borders militarized. Asylum policies gutted. A nation that creates crises while closing its doors earns a 2.

Treatment of allies: 1. The lowest possible score. And it is earned.

The United States has threatened its closest ally — Canada, my country — with annexation. Not as a joke. Not as a negotiating tactic that anyone found amusing. As a stated policy objective from the president of the United States, repeated publicly, defended by administration officials, and left uncorrected by a political establishment that has normalised the abnormal to a degree that future historians will find difficult to explain.

It has imposed punitive tariffs on partners who have been reliable allies for generations — tariffs designed not to correct trade imbalances but to inflict economic pain as political leverage. It has launched a military campaign against Iran without consulting the allies whose troops serve alongside American forces in the region — troops who are now at risk from the retaliation that the strikes provoked. It has used economic coercion against Europe, dismissed NATO obligations as transactional, embraced autocrats while alienating democracies, and treated the alliance structure that kept the peace for seventy years as a protection racket rather than a partnership.

A nation that treats Canada as a target, Europe as a customer, and the Middle East as a shooting range does not have allies. It has audiences.

Consistency of values versus interests: 1. The gap between what the United States says and what the United States does is the defining feature of its international presence in 2026.

It proclaims democracy while its own Democracy Index score falls year after year. It champions human rights while selling weapons to regimes that violate them — and using those same weapons to violate them directly. It promotes free trade while imposing tariffs that its own economists describe as destructive. It advocates for the rules-based international order while bombing nations without the authorisation of any international body. It celebrates innovation while restricting the immigration that fuels it. It preaches freedom while its government weaponizes the Department of Justice against political opponents, purges inspectors general, and defies court orders.

The gap between American rhetoric and American behaviour is not a gap. It is a canyon — the same Grand Canyon of underperformance that defines this entire essay and this entire series.

Long-term stewardship: 2. The United States is the largest cumulative emitter of greenhouse gases in human history. It has launched a war that threatens global energy markets and will slow the clean energy transition. It maintains $35 trillion in national debt — the largest in human history, accumulated by a nation that has chosen to fund tax cuts and military spending rather than invest in the infrastructure, education, and social systems that represent genuine long-term stewardship. It is cutting scientific research funding, international development aid, and environmental protection — consuming its own future, and the world’s, at an accelerating rate.

A nation that borrows from future generations to fund wars in the present is not practising stewardship. It is practising intergenerational theft.

The Innovation Question

American innovation — mRNA vaccines, Silicon Valley breakthroughs, GPS, the internet — has genuinely benefited humanity. These spillovers are real. They do not, however, cancel out arms exports that fuel global conflicts, the dismantling of the rules-based order, or the political choice to leave 37 million Americans in poverty.

Before reaching the editorial conclusion, I owe the reader a direct engagement with the strongest counterargument to this essay’s thesis — because it is a serious argument, and dismissing it would be intellectually dishonest.

The United States accounts for roughly 25 to 30 percent of global research and development spending. American institutions and companies invented or distributed GPS, internet protocols, semiconductor technology, mRNA vaccines, and countless innovations that have transformed human life on every continent. Silicon Valley alone has produced more technological advancement in a single generation than most nations produce in a century. The research output of American universities is unmatched. The scientific talent pipeline — fuelled by immigration that the current administration is now restricting — has generated discoveries that benefit every human being on Earth.

This is real. It matters. And the Contribution Index must not pretend otherwise.

But consider what this argument actually says. It says that the United States deserves credit for the byproducts of its own self-interested investment — that GPS, developed for military navigation, should offset the arms exports that fuel global conflicts; that mRNA vaccines, developed by companies seeking profit in a healthcare system that bankrupts the patients who need them, should offset a system that leaves 27 million uninsured; that internet protocols, created with Department of Defence funding, should offset the surveillance infrastructure and misinformation ecosystem they also enabled.

Innovation spillovers are real contributions. They are not intentional contributions. A nation that invents GPS while exporting $45 billion in weapons annually and launching discretionary wars against sovereign nations is making a different moral choice from one that invests in international development directly. The spillover benefits humanity. The arms exports destroy it. Both are happening simultaneously, and one does not cancel the other.

And American mega-philanthropy — the strongest case for intentional American contribution — underscores the paradox rather than resolving it.

The Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation has donated over $60 billion to global health, disease eradication, and education — more than the total foreign aid budgets of most nations on Earth. PEPFAR, the US government programme that Gates helped advocate for, has saved an estimated 25 million lives from HIV/AIDS in Sub-Saharan Africa. MacKenzie Scott has given away over $19 billion since 2019, with a speed, breadth, and trust-based methodology that has transformed American philanthropy. Warren Buffett’s Giving Pledge has catalysed tens of billions more in commitments from the ultra-wealthy. These are genuine, extraordinary acts of generosity that have saved millions of lives and improved millions more.

But here is the uncomfortable truth that the Contribution Index must name: American mega-philanthropy exists in the precise shape of American policy failure. The Gates Foundation fights malaria because the US government will not fund the fight at scale. MacKenzie Scott funds food banks because the wealthiest nation on Earth has 37 million people living in poverty. American billionaires donate to global health because the nation that spends $886 billion on its military and has just launched a new war cannot be bothered to spend a fraction of that amount on the developing world through its own aid budget.

Philanthropy as a substitute for policy is not contribution. It is an indictment dressed in generosity. When individuals must give away tens of billions of dollars to fill gaps that their government has chosen to leave open, the giving — however admirable, however life-saving — is evidence of the failure, not a rebuttal of it.

Costa Rica did not need a billionaire to fund its universal healthcare. It chose to fund it publicly, through the collective decision of its citizens, with a GDP one four-hundredth the size of America’s. The United States has more billionaires than any nation in history, and they are working overtime to compensate for a government that has abdicated its responsibility to its own people and to the world.

The philanthropists are doing extraordinary work. The nation they live in is not.

The Editorial Conclusion

The United States had every structural advantage required to lead the world in contribution. Instead, it has become the greatest underperformer relative to capacity in the series. Score: 77 out of 200. The gap is not inevitable. It is a choice.

The United States scores 77 out of 200 on the Contribution Index. It is the number one underperformer in this series — and, I would argue, the greatest underperformer in human history.

That claim requires defence, and after twenty essays of building toward this moment, let me offer it as directly as I can.

Over the course of this series, we have assessed twenty-one nations across four continents and across the full spectrum of wealth, governance, and development. We have seen small nations outperform superpowers — Costa Rica’s 153 against America’s 77. We have seen Nordic countries demonstrate that wealth, properly directed, can produce extraordinary contribution — Norway’s 168 built on choices, not accidents. We have seen underperformers squander their capacity through authoritarianism, environmental negligence, military aggression, and the elevation of self-interest above solidarity.

But none of them had what the United States has. None of them possessed $28 trillion in GDP, 4,000 universities, the most advanced technology sector in human history, the most productive research institutions, the deepest capital markets, the most influential cultural exports, and seven decades of institutional infrastructure specifically designed for global leadership. Russia’s underperformance is tragic but unsurprising given its authoritarian trajectory. China’s is a consequence of a political system that prioritizes control over contribution. Saudi Arabia’s is the predictable outcome of theocratic petrostate governance.

The American case is different. The United States built the international order. It designed the institutions. It articulated the values. It wrote the rules. It insisted that other nations follow them. It had every advantage — economic, institutional, intellectual, cultural, geographical — that a nation could possibly need to be the highest-scoring contributor on this or any index.

And it chose not to. Repeatedly. Across administrations. Accelerating dramatically under the current one.

And now it has chosen war. Again. In the same region where its last war of choice produced catastrophe. On the same legal foundations. With the same hubris. With the same certainty that American military power can reshape the Middle East — a certainty that twenty years of evidence has thoroughly, bloodily, irrefutably disproven.

The consequences are still multiplying as I write this. First American casualties reported. Hezbollah re-entering the conflict from Lebanon. Diplomatic back-channels collapsing as fast as they open. Global energy markets destabilised. Oil prices spiking. Allied nations scrambling to distance themselves from an escalation they were not consulted about. The Persian Gulf — through which one-fifth of the world’s oil flows — transformed into a conflict zone whose instability will slow the clean energy transition that the planet’s survival depends on.

The United States possesses everything it would need to be the highest-scoring nation on this index. It has the wealth to provide universal healthcare, eliminate child poverty, and fund a full renewable energy transition — all simultaneously, with money left over. It has the institutional capacity to lead international cooperation rather than undermine it. It has the scientific talent to pioneer environmental solutions for the world. It has the cultural influence to model democratic governance for every nation watching. It has philanthropists — Gates, Scott, Buffett — whose individual generosity exceeds the total foreign aid budgets of most nations on Earth.

It has all of this. And it chooses — actively, deliberately, and with increasing hostility toward anyone who suggests otherwise — to spend its resources on military dominance, to wage discretionary wars, to arm the world’s conflicts, to close its borders, to gut its own democratic institutions, and to treat the rest of humanity as either a market to exploit or a threat to neutralize.

José Figueres Ferrer abolished Costa Rica’s army with a sledgehammer in 1948 and built a society dedicated to human wellbeing. In 2026, the United States maintains an $886 billion military budget — larger than the next ten nations combined — has launched a new war in the Middle East, and 27 million of its citizens lack health insurance.

One of these nations has a GDP of $65 billion. The other has a GDP of $28 trillion.

The Contribution Index does not ask which nation is more powerful. That question answers itself. It asks which nation is making humanity better.

The answer, measured by evidence rather than ideology, by scorecards rather than slogans, by what nations do rather than what they say, is not even close.
