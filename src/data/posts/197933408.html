Charlie Warzel speaks with Chris Hayes about how to emotionally calibrate our response to this dizzying AI moment

Chris Hayes has a phrase for what artificial intelligence does to him. He calls it The Bad Feeling — capital T, capital B, capital F. He describes it as “a feeling of kind of like anxiety, doom, shutdown” that arrives when he encounters the AI discourse. It feels, he says, like the end of something. “It feels like it’s going to destroy things I love, or maybe lead to the end of human civilization.”

Hayes is the host of All In on MSNBC and a writer whose book on the attention economy, The Siren’s Call, established him as one of the more careful thinkers about what technology does to people. He is not a technophobe. He is not a Luddite. He is a person who has spent months trying to understand what is happening in artificial intelligence and has arrived, after extensive reporting, at a somatic response. The technology makes his body feel bad.

He is not alone. The polling on American attitudes toward AI is, by the spring of 2026, the most uniformly negative technology sentiment recorded since the early internet surveys of the 1990s — and it is moving in the wrong direction.

An NBC News survey found that 26 percent of American voters view AI positively. Forty-six percent view it negatively. Artificial intelligence, as Fortune noted, ranks less favourably than U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, Donald Trump, Kamala Harris, and the Republican Party. Only the Democratic Party and Iran poll worse. A separate NBC News poll found that 57 percent of registered voters said AI’s risks outweigh its benefits. A Quinnipiac University poll found that 55 percent expect AI would do more harm than good in their daily lives. A Fox News poll found that voter concern about AI has risen to 66 percent, with nearly two-thirds uncomfortable with military use of autonomous weapons. A Pew Research Center survey found that a majority of Americans are more concerned than excited about increased use of AI. The Stanford HAI 2026 AI Index found that even globally — in countries far more enthusiastic about the technology than the United States — 52 percent of respondents say AI products make them nervous.

The demographic breakdown is the most revealing number in the data. The NBC News poll found that voters aged 18 to 34 have a net AI favourability rating of minus 44. Women aged 18 to 49 are at minus 41. The only demographic groups with even marginally positive views are men over 50 and upper-income voters — both at plus 2. The people building AI are overwhelmingly young men in the upper income bracket. The people with The Bad Feeling are everyone else.

This essay is about the distance between those two groups and what that distance means for governance.

The Hothouse and the Outside

Hayes described the AI discourse to Charlie Warzel on the Atlantic podcast Galaxy Brain in terms that are worth sitting with. He called it “cultish, hothouse, true-believer.” He said most people find it “scary and alienating.” He said the insiders — the builders, the investors, the model-makers — are “all doing each other’s podcasts constantly,” generating “this entire discursive engine that’s just churning out code and content and models.” He said he was trying to penetrate it from the outside, as a guide for the people who are not inside it. “Because it is alienating. I got to say — it really is.”

The observation is not new. This series has been making a version of it since Part 1. But Hayes states it with a specificity that earns the record: the AI discourse is a closed system. It is produced by people who build AI, consumed by people who build AI, and conducted on platforms — primarily X — that are owned by people who build AI. The discourse is not a public conversation about a public technology. It is an internal conversation within an industry that happens to be audible to the public.

The public hears it. The public does not understand it. The public does not feel included in it. The public feels The Bad Feeling.

Warzel extended the point with a detail that captures the speed problem. He noted that someone on X had said, “We don’t talk about Claude Code anymore, because we talk about Codex. Claude Code was last week’s thing.” If the price of participating in the AI conversation is daily, sometimes hourly attention to product releases and model updates, then the conversation has been structured — whether deliberately or not — to exclude anyone who has a job, a family, or a life that does not revolve around artificial intelligence. The discourse is not merely alienating. It is exclusionary by velocity.

This matters for governance because democratic governance requires public understanding. You cannot govern what you cannot discuss. You cannot discuss what you cannot follow. You cannot follow a conversation that moves faster than your capacity to attend to it and that is conducted in a language you have not been taught. The AI discourse has created a comprehension gap between the people who are building the technology and the people who will live with its consequences, and that gap is widening with every product cycle.

The Bad Feeling is the public’s recognition that the gap exists. It is not irrational. It is not Luddism. It is the correct emotional response to being excluded from a conversation about your own future.

What the Feeling Is About

Part of The Bad Feeling is abstract — a generalised anxiety about a future that feels out of control. But part of it is concrete, and the concrete part is now empirically documented.

A study published in May 2026 by researchers at Carnegie Mellon, MIT, Oxford, and UCLA tested what happens to people’s cognitive abilities after even brief AI use. The study involved 1,222 participants across three randomised experiments. Some participants were given access to a chatbot powered by GPT-5 to solve math and reading comprehension problems. After 10 to 15 minutes, the chatbot was removed without warning.

The results were stark. Once the AI was taken away, the assisted group solved only 57 percent of math problems, compared with 73 percent for participants who had never used the AI. Their reading comprehension scores dropped from 89 percent to 76 percent. They skipped questions at twice the rate of the control group. Ten minutes of assistance produced a measurable decline in the ability to think independently.

The lead researcher, Michiel Bakker of MIT, described the finding carefully: “The takeaway is not that we should ban AI in education or workplaces. It is fundamentally a cognitive question — about persistence, learning, and how people respond to difficulty.”

Hayes had discussed this study on the podcast. His response was blunt: “It’s obviously going to make people dumber.” He drew a distinction that matters for the governance argument: between using AI for generative thinking — brainstorming, creating, problem-solving — and using it for what he called “grunt work.” He uses AI to sort his email. He does not use it to come up with ideas. “If you’re having it do your brainstorming, your brainstorming muscles are going to get weaker. And my livelihood, my career is coming up with stuff. I gotta keep that sharp.”

The cognitive atrophy finding is The Bad Feeling stated as a research result. People intuit that relying on AI will make them worse at the things they need to be good at. The study confirms the intuition. The technology that promises to make everyone more productive may be, at the same time, making everyone less capable of functioning without it. That is not a conspiracy theory. It is a peer-reviewed finding from four of the most respected research universities in the world.

AI was the leading reason for layoffs in March and April 2026, according to Challenger, Gray & Christmas. The Working Families Party has released a federal policy platform that includes a data centre moratorium and a jobs guarantee for workers displaced by AI. CNBC reported that Sam Altman’s home was targeted in an attack. The Bad Feeling is no longer ambient. It is becoming political.

The Structural Reason

The concentrated power of the AI industry. As Hayes noted, AI’s architecture is built for “as much power in as concentrated hands as possible.

Hayes identified the structural cause of The Bad Feeling with a clarity that this series has been approaching from a different angle.

He asked his listeners to compare AI with the internet. The internet, he noted, “was created in a noncommercial environment in which there was zero profit motive. In which it was created purely for continuity of government purposes, at first, and then communication between research agencies, and whose entire guiding, structural-engineering philosophy was distribution and nonconcentration.”

Then he described AI: “A technology whose entire inherent philosophy engineering is ‘as much power in as concentrated hands as possible.’”

The comparison is the Asimov Gap stated as an engineering observation. The internet was built to distribute. AI is built to concentrate. The internet’s architecture made decentralisation its default. AI’s architecture makes concentration its default. The internet required enormous effort to monopolise — and it was monopolised, eventually, by the platforms. AI requires enormous effort to distribute — and it has not been distributed, and there is no structural incentive to distribute it.

Dario Amodei — the CEO of the company this series examined in Part 34, the safety-focused alternative to OpenAI — confirmed the structural point on a podcast Hayes cited. Amodei said AI is “naturally a concentrated industry.” The CEO of the company that left OpenAI because it was concerned about concentration described his own industry as naturally concentrated.

The word “naturally” is doing significant work in that sentence. It implies that the concentration is inherent — that it follows from the physics of compute, the economics of scale, the cost of training frontier models. It implies that the concentration is not a policy choice but a structural inevitability. It implies that the question is not whether AI will be concentrated but what you do about a technology that is concentrated by nature.

The physical infrastructure tells the story. The AI industry is building data centres at a pace that has produced its own backlash — and the backlash is now the most significant grassroots technology opposition movement in the United States since the anti-nuclear campaigns of the 1970s. According to Heatmap Pro, in the first quarter of 2026 alone, at least 20 proposed data centre projects were cancelled after local opposition, doubling the previous quarterly record. These cancellations represent more than $41.7 billion in planned investment and 3.5 gigawatts of electricity demand. Over the past three years, at least $85 billion in data centre projects have been cancelled. Data Center Watch has identified 142 activist groups across 24 states working to block construction. Roughly 100 new local fights were logged in Q1 2026 alone.

The opposition is bipartisan. An analysis of public statements by elected officials in districts with large data centre proposals found that 55 percent of the officials opposing projects are Republicans and 45 percent are Democrats. Republicans raise concerns about tax incentives and energy grid strain. Democrats focus on environmental impact and resource consumption. Both raise concerns about electricity costs, water use, noise, and the conversion of farmland. Communities that have never agreed on anything are agreeing on this: they do not want the physical infrastructure of AI concentration in their backyards.

This is The Bad Feeling expressed as civic action. The abstract anxiety about concentrated AI power has a concrete manifestation — a 3.5-gigawatt, $41.7 billion concrete manifestation — and ordinary people are fighting it with the tools available to them: zoning meetings, town councils, petitions, lawsuits, and the recall of local officials who approved projects without adequate public consultation.

Generational divide on AI. Younger Americans show sharply declining support, creating cross-pressure for politicians between donors and constituents.

The Cross-Pressure

Hayes described the political dynamics of The Bad Feeling with the precision of someone who has spent decades watching politicians navigate impossible pressures.

The politicians, he said, are “in rooms with their donors and at fancy conferences with all the people making the models.” They hear the pitch — transformative technology, American competitiveness, the race with China. Then “they’re going to go back home and get yelled at about electricity prices and data centres. And they’re going to be totally pulled in two different directions.”

This is the lobbying problem from Part 38 stated as a human experience. A quarter of all federal lobbyists in Washington now work AI issues. OpenAI has a showroom blocks from the White House. Anthropic has hired six lobbying firms. The AI industry’s super PACs have deployed $125 million for the 2026 midterms. The politicians are being pulled toward the industry by money and toward the public by votes, and the midterms are in November.

Hayes called it a “jump ball” — neither party has claimed the issue. Ron DeSantis has offered populist rhetoric. Bernie Sanders has called for a national data-centre moratorium. The Working Families Party has a platform. Progressives are, as NOTUS reported this week, betting that AI anxiety will win them elections. But no party has yet offered a comprehensive position. The political system has not yet decided what it thinks about AI, because the political system is still being lobbied by both sides and has not yet heard from voters in a way that forces a decision.

The voters will be heard in November. The 46 percent who view AI negatively will be heard. The 142 activist groups in 24 states will be heard. The communities fighting data centres will be heard. The parents worried about chatbots and children will be heard. The workers anxious about automation will be heard. What they will not have, when they are heard, is a legislative framework to channel their concerns into policy. They will have their vote. They will not have the laws.

Hayes reached for two analogies: the Federal Reserve and the Food and Drug Administration. Both are institutions created to provide democratic control over technically complex, high-risk domains. “We don’t want Congress voting on every drug to come to market, and we don’t want Congress and the president deciding interest rates,” he said. But we want democratic accountability over both. The solution, in both cases, was a mediating institution — technically sophisticated, democratically accountable, structurally independent.

Then he landed the punch: “Which, by the way, are under attack from both this conservative Supreme Court and the Trump administration.”

The institutions AI would need are the same category of institution the current government is dismantling. The regulatory model that democracies developed over a century to govern complex, powerful, high-risk industries — the FTC, the FCC, the SEC, the FDA, the Fed — is being weakened at the precise moment a new industry arrives that would require the most sophisticated version of that model ever built.

That is the gap. Not a theoretical gap. A structural one. The technology that most urgently needs democratic governance is arriving at the moment when democratic governance is least capable of providing it.

The Asimov Frame

Asimov did not write about feelings. He wrote about laws. His Three Laws of Robotics were not designed to address the emotional experience of living alongside powerful machines. They were designed to make that emotional experience unnecessary.

The First Law — a robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm — was not a suggestion. It was not a corporate value statement. It was not a line in a terms-of-service agreement. It was a structural constraint, built into the architecture, enforced before the machine was activated, not after the consequences arrived.

The Three Laws existed so that the public did not need to feel The Bad Feeling. The governance came first. The technology came second. The public did not need to lie awake wondering whether the robots would serve human interests, because the laws that guaranteed it were already in place.

The AI industry has inverted this. The technology came first. The governance has not come second. It has not come at all — not in comprehensive form, not at the federal level, not in any of the major AI-producing nations. What has come instead is lobbying, and super PACs, and showrooms in former Masonic temples, and a discourse conducted at a velocity designed to exclude the public from the conversation about the public’s own future.

Isaac Asimov’s Three Laws of Robotics. He placed governance first so the public would never need to live with “The Bad Feeling.” Today’s AI industry has inverted that order.

Hayes said something near the end of his conversation with Warzel that deserves the last word. He was talking about what AI had done to his thinking — not his productivity, not his workflow, but his thinking about what it means to be human.

“It’s scary to think about a challenge from machines to my own personhood,” he said. “But it also is doing something useful and important, which is for all of us to think about: What do we have in common? What does make us human? What do we care about? Where do our values come from? And why should you treat people with kindness?”

The Bad Feeling may be, in the end, the most democratic response to AI available. It is the response of people who have not been consulted, who have not been included, who have not been governed on behalf of — and who know it. It is the response of a public that understands, at the level of the body, that something consequential is happening without their input.

The question is whether the political system will hear The Bad Feeling before November, or after. The question is whether the institutions that could translate The Bad Feeling into governance will exist when they are needed. The question is whether the laws will come before the consequences, or after.

Asimov knew the answer. He wrote it into the premise.

The laws come first. Or the feeling never goes away.
