Part F: Appendix — Ask the Office of Etiquette

The Office’s etiquette desk receives a great many letters in the week of an election. This one arrived with the filing.

Dear Office,

I lost a close contest last summer. I promised to support the winner, and I did. Then, with early voting already more than a week old, I was asked not to come to his party. At ten o’clock that night I told a colleague I was still with the winner. The next morning I woke up and decided to go to church, a different one, with the winner’s opponent, and said so on video from the pew. My fiancé came too. Was this rude?

— Uninvited in Oakland County

Dear Uninvited,

The rules for losing are older than the Senate and a good deal simpler. The guest asked to stay home stays home. She may sulk. She may write a strongly worded letter. In extreme cases she may decline to send the casserole. What she may not do is turn up at the house across the street, wave from its front window, and tell the neighbours she was driven to it.

You will know that in Canada we have a name for walking over to the other side. We call it crossing the floor, and it is held in low enough regard. Still, the members who do it have at least been elected first. You have found a way to cross the floor of a chamber you were never voted into.

You raise a real complaint about an advertisement, and the Office does not dismiss it. But a complaint about an advertisement is answered with a statement, or a phone call, or a long walk. It is not answered by handing a Senate seat to the party you spent the spring and summer telling Michigan to vote against, while predicting in the same breath that your own party will win the Senate anyway.

You say you were never invited to a campaign event, and also that you were booed off the stage. The Office does not know which stage, and has decided not to ask.

A loser is only someone who came second, which is no shame at all; most of us do it most of the time. A sore loser is someone who, having come second, works hard to make sure her own side comes second as well.

The Office suggests three thank-you notes: one to Mrs Dingell, who gave up her weekend; one to Mr Raskin, who asked a clear question at ten o’clock at night and got a clear answer; and one to your former staff, who did the decent thing on your behalf.

Yours, with regrets,

The Office of Etiquette
