Before the joke, the ledger.

This series has spent seven essays on a Secretary of Defense who performs strength in place of doing the job. The performance has a cost that no memo, real or invented, can make funny. As of early October 2026, at least 18 American service members have been killed in the war with Iran, and hundreds more wounded. Thousands of Iranians have died. Among them were more than 160 civilians, most of them children, killed when an American strike hit a school in southern Iran on February 28, 2026, the war’s first day. They died in a war waged in the name of American superiority, and declared won within weeks of its start.

This interlude is dedicated to all of them, on both sides.

What follows is satire. It is aimed at the men who confuse a hymn with a battle plan, never at those who have paid for the confusion. The memorandum is invented, and its author and office are fictional. The Secretary’s quotations, the hymn, the history and the dates are real.

DEPARTMENT OF WAR OFFICE OF RELIGIOUS AFFAIRS Directorate of Hymnody, Lethality and Morale

MEMORANDUM ORA-001

FROM: Acting Deputy Assistant Director for Hymnody (Interim) TO: The Secretary of War SUBJECT: Adoption of an Official Departmental Hymn; Related Historical Findings CLASSIFICATION: Unclassified (we checked; nobody had asked before)

1. Purpose

On September 30, 2026, at Marine Corps Base Quantico, the Secretary established this Office, reporting directly to him, and informed the troops that “the real battle is spiritual.” The Office has been operating for one week. It has a desk, a chair and an acting deputy assistant director, who is also the director, the staff and the person who refills the printer.

The Office’s first task, assigned verbally and with some enthusiasm, is to select a hymn the whole Department can sing. The Secretary has made clear that the Department is no longer the “woke department” or the “weak department.” It has not yet been told what it is instead. A hymn seemed a good place to start.

2. Selection

After a review of the available hymnals, the Office recommends Onward, Christian Soldiers, words by the Reverend Sabine Baring-Gould, 1865, music by Sir Arthur Sullivan, 1871.

The case for it is strong. It has soldiers in the title. It has marching. It has banners. It was sung by Winston Churchill and Franklin Roosevelt on the deck of HMS Prince of Wales in August 1941, which the Office regards as the last time anyone sang it at a military event without a follow-up memo.

The Office must, however, report four difficulties discovered during due diligence.

Difficulty one: the children. Baring-Gould did not write the hymn for an army. He wrote it in 1865 for the schoolchildren of his Yorkshire parish, so they would have something to sing while walking to the next village for a Whitsun treat. The original Christian soldiers were therefore approximately eight years old, carrying a cross and some banners, and marching towards tea. The Office notes that this was, by some distance, the most successful Christian military operation of the nineteenth century: every objective was met, there were no casualties, and there were buns.

Difficulty two: the word “as.” The first line reads “Onward, Christian soldiers, marching as to war.” The Office’s legal adviser — the Office does not have one, the Department having removed three of its senior judge advocates general in February 2025, so the Office consulted a dictionary instead — confirms that “as” introduces a simile. The hymn does not say Christians are soldiers marching to war. It says they are like soldiers, and the war is a metaphor. The vicar was talking about the Church, not the Department. A Christian marching as to war is a churchgoer. A Christian marching to war is a crusader. The whole difference between the two fits into a two-letter word, which the Office suspects is why nobody at Quantico noticed it.

Difficulty three: the second verse. It reads, in part:

We are not divided, all one body we, One in hope and doctrine, one in charity.

The Office has reviewed the Department’s personnel actions since January 2025 and cannot, in good conscience, recommend singing this verse in front of Congress.

Difficulty four: the third verse. It reads:

Crowns and thrones may perish, kingdoms rise and wane, But the Church of Jesus constant will remain.

Again, the Church. Not the Department. The vicar was quite specific, and the Office is beginning to suspect he did this on purpose.

3. Proposed Revisions

To bring the hymn into line with Department policy, the Office submits the following revised verses for the Secretary’s approval. They have been checked for metre by the acting deputy assistant director, who sang them in the car.

Onward, Christian soldiers, marching straight to war — “As to” was a simile; we don’t do those any more. Turn the other cheek, the Master told us, mild and meek; “Overwhelming violence” is the sermon for this week.

(The phrase is the Secretary’s own, from the prayer he offered at a Pentagon Christian service, reported on March 26, 2026. The Office has merely set it to music.)

Like a mighty army moves the War Department now, Beardless, fat-free, tested, and unsure exactly how. We are not divided, all one body we — Ten percent of generals are no longer part of “we.”

Crowns and thrones may perish, kingdoms rise and wane; Interceptors perish, and are hard to buy again. Seven pages ask the Congress for the Lord’s supply; Congress, being heathen, wants to know exactly why.

The chorus is unchanged, except for the “as,” which has been sent for review.

4. Historical Findings

In the course of selecting a hymn, the Office was referred by the Department’s Records Branch to a matter it believes the Secretary will wish to know. It is reproduced here in full.

RECORDS BRANCH — NOTICE TO THE SECRETARY

The Records Branch regrets to inform the Secretary that the Crusades are over.

They began at the Council of Clermont in November 1095, when Pope Urban II called for an armed expedition to the East and the crowd is said to have answered Deus vult, “God wills it.” The Branch understands the phrase is still in circulation in some quarters, including, according to widely published photographs, on the Secretary’s own arm.

They ended, for practical purposes, in 1291, when the city of Acre, the last major Crusader stronghold in the Holy Land, fell. The Branch has searched its files for any subsequent order reopening them and has found none.

The Branch also draws the Secretary’s attention to the Fourth Crusade, which set out in 1202 to recover Jerusalem and instead, in 1204, sacked Constantinople, the greatest Christian city in the world. The Branch flags this as an instructive example of a campaign whose objectives drifted after the first few weeks, and respectfully suggests it be added to the reading list at the war colleges, if any remain.

Finally, the Branch notes the so-called Children’s Crusade of 1212, which historians now think may have been neither a crusade nor composed entirely of children. It is mentioned here only because it is the closest historical parallel to the original 1865 performance of the Department’s proposed hymn, and because both ended with the children going home.

5. A Note on Terminology

During the review, a junior member of the Records Branch asked whether the phrase “Christian soldier” is an oxymoron. The Office looked it up.

Oxymoron: from the Greek oxys, sharp, and mōros, dull or foolish. Literally, “pointedly foolish.” A figure of speech in which two contradictory terms are joined, for effect.

The hymn, the Office notes, avoided the problem with its “as.” The Department has removed the “as.” The Office therefore compiled a short list of terms now in official use, for reference:

  • Department of War Office of Religious Affairs

  • A prayer, in the name of the Prince of Peace, for overwhelming violence

  • A colourblind, gender-neutral purge

  • A seven-page war budget

  • Victory, declared March 31, 2026

  • Military intelligence (included out of tradition only; the Office means no disrespect to the intelligence professionals, several of whom are still employed)

The junior staff member then asked whether the Secretary and the President, considered as a pair, might themselves qualify. The Office replied that an oxymoron requires two contradictory parts, and that it was not in a position to confirm that both parts were present. The staff member has since been reassigned to the new base. Its location is still being decided.

6. Recommendation Regarding the Chapel

The Office visited the Cadet Chapel at the Air Force Academy, where the Secretary has lately been reminding cadets that they are “the real one percent.” The building has seventeen aluminium spires and was opened in the early 1960s. The Office was surprised to find that its architects gave it separate chapels for Protestants, Catholics and Jews under one roof, with space since added for Buddhist cadets and others. It has, in other words, several doors.

The Office understands this to have been deliberate. The Academy was built on the assumption that the young people who would one day fly the country’s aircraft would arrive with different faiths, or none, and that a chapel’s job was to let each of them in without asking the others to leave.

The Office therefore makes the following recommendation, which it expects to be its last:

That the Department keep all the doors.

Respectfully submitted,

Acting Deputy Assistant Director for Hymnody (Interim) Office of Religious Affairs (desk, chair, printer)

Afterword

The memo is invented. Very little else in it is.

The Secretary did establish an Office of Religious Affairs at Quantico on September 30, 2026, reporting to himself, and did tell the troops the real battle is spiritual. He did pray, at a Pentagon Christian service reported on March 26, 2026, during a war with a Muslim country, for “overwhelming violence of action against those who deserve no mercy.” He does bear, as Eliot Cohen pointed out in The Atlantic on October 5, 2026, in “Hegseth’s One Percent,” a Crusader tattoo. His department did remove senior military lawyers in February 2025. And Baring-Gould really did write the hymn for children walking to a village tea, with the word “as” in its first line.

Part VII of this series argued that the American state holds the door open for every faith and walks through none of them. The chapel in Colorado Springs was built on exactly that idea, in aluminium and stained glass. It is worth noticing which part of the hymn the Secretary has kept, and which two letters he left out.
