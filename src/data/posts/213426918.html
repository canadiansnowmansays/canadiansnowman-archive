There is a particular kind of financial analysis that flourishes precisely because a company has declined to provide the underlying data, and Tesla’s robotaxi business has become one of the finest specimens of the genre currently in circulation. The company does not publish an official fleet count. It does not publish daily miles per vehicle. It does not, with one important exception, publish growth rates. Into that vacuum has rushed an entire cottage industry of trackers, chartered financial analysts, and amateur statisticians, all trying to reverse-engineer a business from crumbs — and the crumbs, read carefully, are more interesting than the headline numbers most coverage settled on.

The one crumb that matters

On Tesla’s second-quarter 2026 earnings call, it was not Elon Musk who gave the load-bearing guidance on robotaxi growth. It was Ashok Elluswamy, the head of Tesla’s AI software effort, and the distinction matters more than it sounds like it should. Musk, in the same period, had made looser comments about roughly 10 percent week-over-week growth, and it is Musk’s number that most of the subsequent coverage seized on — because it is a round number, and because Musk’s public statements travel faster than the transcript of an earnings call. But Elluswamy’s actual language was more specific and, properly read, considerably more aggressive: “double-digit growth rates” in the number of unsupervised miles the fleet drives weekly, a “high compounding rate,” and an explicit statement that this rate was expected to continue “through the rest of this year.” He repeated the emphasis more than once, which in earnings-call language is usually a company’s way of underlining a sentence it wants analysts to actually use in their models rather than skim past.

An independent analysis circulating under the byline “Jenma JP” made the useful point that “double-digit” is doing real interpretive work here: at a bare minimum it means 11 percent, a realistic central reading sits around 14 to 15 percent, and anything meaningfully above 17 percent would likely have been described in stronger language than “double-digit.” That is a reasonable piece of close reading, and it is the basis for the three-scenario model — 11 percent, 14.5 percent, and 17 percent weekly growth, each tapering down toward 11 percent over roughly two years — that has become the closest thing to a consensus framework for modelling this business absent actual company disclosure.

Three scenarios, one exponential curve

Run the maths, and the divergence between scenarios is startling given how close the starting assumptions are. At 11 percent weekly growth, the fleet reaches somewhere in the range of 500 vehicles by the end of 2026, generating an annualised revenue run rate of roughly $60 to 65 million — not a large number, but a run rate, not a year’s actual earnings, and one climbing quickly. Extend the same 11 percent scenario out to the end of 2027 and the fleet reaches roughly 80,000 vehicles, cumulative paid autonomous miles cross a billion, and the annualised revenue run rate reaches something in the neighbourhood of $14 to 15 billion.

Nudge the growth rate to 14.5 percent and the 2026 fleet roughly doubles to somewhere between 900 and 1,000 vehicles, with a run rate around $120 million; by the end of 2027, the fleet reaches roughly 200,000 to 220,000 vehicles and an annualised run rate near $40 billion. Push to 17 percent — the aggressive end of the “double-digit” reading — and the 2026 fleet reaches something like 1,200 to 2,000 vehicles with a run rate around $180 million; by 2027, the fleet approaches 450,000 to half a million vehicles, cumulative miles exceed six billion, and the annualised revenue run rate reaches roughly $80 billion, comparable to the company’s entire current revenue.

A three-and-a-half-percentage-point difference in weekly growth rate — the gap between the low and middle scenarios — is enough to roughly double every downstream number. That sensitivity is the actual lesson here, more so than any individual figure: this is not a business where a modest miss on growth produces a modest miss on outcome. It is a business where being wrong about the growth rate by an amount smaller than most analysts’ margin of error produces an outcome that is off by a factor of two.

The cross-check that actually held up

The reason to take any of this seriously rather than filing it under speculative Tesla-bull arithmetic is that the baseline assumption — roughly 64 to 70 vehicles currently operating — was checked, independently, against a community-run Austin robotaxi tracker that reported 69 unsupervised vehicles at the same moment. That is a genuinely useful piece of triangulation, for a business this opaque. It is also, worth noting immediately, an Austin-only figure; it excludes the Bay Area, Dallas, Houston, Miami, Orlando, and Tampa, all of which Tesla has also expanded into, meaning the true current fleet size across all markets is almost certainly higher than any single-city tracker can show. The caution runs in the other direction too: Tesla does not publish an official count, different trackers use different counting methodologies, and a number that happens to align with a model’s assumption on one particular day is a reassuring coincidence, not a validated data source.

The Cybercab-specific launch yesterday on September 3, supplies the first real subset of that baseline to check against. Texas state records showed 45 registered Cybercabs at the moment of the event — a number that sits inside, not alongside, the roughly 64-to-70 total already cited, meaning the overwhelming majority of Tesla’s current Austin fleet is still Model Y, with the purpose-built vehicle only just beginning to enter service.

That is consistent with the early, unresolved stage of all three growth scenarios rather than confirmation of any one of them: 45 dedicated units, geofenced to a limited area of the city, with zero registrations yet in any of the other five metros Tesla operates in, is exactly what the low end of an exponential curve looks like in its first weeks — and exactly what a stalled rollout dressed up as a launch would also look like. The event itself did little to resolve which of those it is: invite-only, no public livestream despite considerable demand for one on X, and a genuine milestone buried inside the theater — paying customers, not just employees or invited testers, rode in a Cybercab for the first time. Whether that milestone represents the start of the 11-, 14.5-, or 17-percent curve, or a hand-picked best case ahead of a slower public reality, is not answerable from a single evening’s reporting.

Why this business resists being valued the normal way

The more interesting argument buried in this modelling exercise is not about the robotaxi fleet at all — it is about how you are supposed to value a business that is growing exponentially rather than linearly. The conventional instinct is to look at projected 2027 earnings and compute a price-to-earnings ratio, and by that measure Tesla’s robotaxi segment looks absurd on almost any scenario, because near-term earnings are small relative to the market capitalisation being attributed to the business. But a price-to-earnings ratio is a snapshot tool, built for businesses growing at a roughly constant linear rate. It handles a business whose revenue in 2029 and 2030 could be many multiples of its 2027 revenue rather badly, because it captures none of that future acceleration. The more honest comparison, on this reading, is closer to how a venture investor prices an unapproved pharmaceutical: does the drug work or does it not, and if it works, how large is the addressable market once it does. Applied here — does the unsupervised-driving technology generalise safely at scale, or does it not — and the valuation question becomes a probability-weighted discounted cash flow across a decade rather than a ratio computed against next year’s earnings. Whether Tesla’s stock is currently priced correctly under that framework is a separate and much harder question than whether the framework itself is the right one to be using; I think it plainly is the right one, and I think almost none of the retail commentary arguing Tesla’s valuation is disconnected from its earnings has bothered to apply it.

The bottleneck that might not be demand at all

The modelling exercise also asks a question most Tesla coverage skips entirely: even granting the growth rate, can the company actually build enough vehicles to fill it? Elluswamy’s own earnings-call language addressed this directly — Tesla, he said, has aligned its manufacturing targets to roughly match the projected growth rate of unsupervised miles, while acknowledging that in the ride-share market as it exists today, some 85 percent of trips carry two passengers or fewer, leaving a real minority of demand that needs a vehicle larger than the two-seat Cybercab. That is Tesla’s own admission that the robotaxi fleet will remain a mixed one — Cybercabs and Model Ys together — rather than a pure Cybercab fleet, at least for the foreseeable future, which matters because it changes the manufacturing-capacity question from “can Tesla build enough Cybercabs” to “can Tesla build enough vehicles of either kind.”

Run that capacity question against the three growth scenarios and the numbers get large quickly. Under the 11 percent scenario, meeting 2027 demand requires Cybercab-equivalent production capacity in the neighbourhood of 400,000 to 500,000 units annually — a stretch from today’s stated capacity, but one Tesla could plausibly reach given the vehicle’s design intent as a stripped-down, easier-to-manufacture platform, built with roughly half the parts of a Model 3. Under the 14.5 percent scenario the requirement climbs toward half a million to a million. Under the 17 percent scenario it reaches a genuinely startling two million units of annual capacity, a level that would require not just Giga Texas but very likely additional lines — perhaps, as some of this commentary has speculated, a Cybercab line eventually sited in Berlin to serve a growing European market, though nothing resembling a confirmed timeline exists for that.

The reassuring detail, if you are inclined to be reassured, is that production capacity does not need to arrive all at once; a manufacturer can build a surplus ahead of demand and let deployment catch up over a period of months, the same way Tesla historically pre-built inventory ahead of demonstrated demand for the Supercharger network and battery production during the original electric vehicle ramp. The less reassuring detail is that pre-building a surplus of unused, capital-intensive hardware is itself a bet — an expensive one, if the growth rate underperforms even the conservative scenario.

There is a second bottleneck sitting alongside the manufacturing-capacity question, and it surfaced explicitly around the September 3 launch: federal rules cap how many vehicles a manufacturer can sell to the public without conventional controls like a steering wheel, even though companies face no such limit on units deployed purely for testing. That distinction matters more than it sounds like it should, because it means the gap between “pilot” and “network” isn’t only a question of how many Cybercabs Giga Texas can physically produce — it’s also a question of how many Tesla is legally permitted to put into paying customers’ hands at all, versus how many it can quietly keep on the road under a testing designation while the regulatory ceiling catches up. Nothing in the current reporting suggests Tesla is anywhere near that cap with 45 vehicles. But it’s a constraint the three growth scenarios don’t currently model, and one that could bind well before manufacturing capacity does if the looser “testing fleet” numbers and the stricter “vehicles sold to the public” numbers start to diverge.

The uncomfortable arithmetic on safety

None of this is worth writing without the caveat that made even the most bullish participants in this modelling exercise visibly uneasy: scale multiplies edge cases, and edge cases are where autonomous vehicle programmes actually die. Waymo, with a fleet in the low thousands of vehicles across roughly ten metro areas, has still generated documented incidents of multiple vehicles becoming simultaneously confused by the same unusual road condition — flooded streets, in one widely circulated example. If Tesla’s fleet reaches even the conservative 2027 scenario of 80,000 vehicles, an intervention rate that looks perfectly manageable at a few dozen cars becomes, at that scale, tens of thousands of individual incidents a year. The honest version of the bull case is not that Tesla has solved this problem; it is that Tesla’s underlying safety-and-intervention rate needs to improve by roughly two orders of magnitude relative to where Waymo currently sits, not merely by half, for a fleet that size to operate without becoming a recurring feature of the evening news.

The September 3 launch did not settle this question, and it’s worth being precise about why. Reporting from the event could not confirm whether the paying-customer rides were genuinely unsupervised or whether safety monitors or chase vehicles remained present off-camera — the single most important technical fact about the milestone went unreported, whether by design or by the constraints of an invite-only event with no independent press access. One attendee’s account, describing the ride as smooth, is worth noting as color and nothing more; it is not a substitute for the disclosure that would actually matter. Whether the forthcoming version of Tesla’s driving software delivers the two-orders-of-magnitude improvement this fleet size requires is, by the modellers’ own admission, something nobody outside the company actually knows yet — and September 3, whatever else it accomplished, did not change that. It added one real number (45) to a model built almost entirely out of inference, and left the question that actually determines the outcome exactly where it was the day before.

The market’s own reaction traced almost too neat a picture of this gap between hype and data. Tesla shares rallied hard into the event, climbing as much as 7 percent intraday and closing Thursday up 5.42 percent at $376.37 on anticipation alone. By Friday, with the details of a closed-door, no-livestream, 45-vehicle launch now digested, shares had given back a meaningful share of that gain, trading around $365 — a pullback of roughly 3 percent from the close, though still comfortably above where the stock stood before the rally began. Neither move should be read as evidence about the underlying capability question this essay has spent several thousand words trying to model properly; a single session’s price action, in either direction, has historically been a poor guide to whether Tesla’s actual autonomous-driving capability advanced. But the shape of it — anticipation up, thin details down — is a fairly honest mirror of everything this essay has been arguing about the gap between the story Tesla tells and the numbers Tesla declines to publish.
