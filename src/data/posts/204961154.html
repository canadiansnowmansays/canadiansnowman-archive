by Stephen Gordon

I count my essays. You know the drill by now. The number climbs, it lands on a year that already means something, and I stop and tell you what.

This is essay 1,440, and I have been looking forward to this one for a long time, because 1,440 is the year — give or take, the historians hedge, and I will get to why — that a goldsmith in Mainz named Johannes Gutenberg worked out how to make words multiply.

I want to write this one warmly, because after Joan I need it and I suspect you do too. There is no institution to indict here, no verdict changing its mind, no cruel arithmetic. There is just a man, and a machine, and the quiet astonishing fact that everything I do — everything you are doing right now, reading this — runs directly downhill from what he built in a workshop nearly six hundred years ago.

I am, when you get right down to it, one of his more prolific descendants. And I would like, at essay 1,440, to pay my respects.

Here is what the world was like before Gutenberg, and it is hard to feel it properly from where we sit, drowning as we are in text.

A book, before 1440, was copied by hand. One at a time. A scribe — very possibly the weary, candlelit monk I keep putting in these essays, and for good reason, because he was real and there were thousands of him — sat at a desk and copied a book letter by letter, word by word, page by page, for months. A single Bible could take a year. The result was beautiful and it was precious and it was, by definition, rare. Books were chained to desks in libraries because they were worth more than the desks. Knowledge existed, but it existed in a few hundred hand-copied volumes locked in a few dozen buildings, available to a tiny lettered elite, and the rest of humanity lived and died without ever holding one.

Every copy was also, quietly, a little bit wrong. A scribe copying by hand for months makes errors — a skipped line, a misread word, a “correction” that corrupts. And the next scribe copies the errors, and adds his own, so that a text copied and recopied over centuries drifts, like a rumour, from whatever it originally said. There was no such thing as two identical books, because there was no such thing as identical anything. Everything was one-off. Everything was handmade. Everything was scarce.

Gutenberg broke all of it at once.

What he actually did was more clever than “he invented printing,” because printing of a sort already existed — you could carve a whole page into a block of wood and stamp it, which was fine if you only ever wanted that one page. Gutenberg’s genius was movable type: individual metal letters, cast in reusable molds, that could be arranged into any page you liked, printed hundreds of times, then broken apart and rearranged into the next page. Little metal counters, each stamped with a letter, that you could count out and line up and count out again.

I confess I find something almost unbearably fitting in that, for this series of all series. The thing that launched the modern world was, at its heart, a set of countable pieces. Gutenberg turned language itself into a counting game — so many of this letter, so many of that, arranged and rearranged — and in doing so made it possible to produce identical copies without limit. The same page, exactly the same, a thousand times over. For the first time in human history, two people in two cities could hold the exact same words and know they were the exact same words.

That is a bigger deal than it sounds. That is, arguably, the beginning of everything we mean by the modern world. Standardized knowledge. Reproducible fact. The scientific paper, the shared reference, the idea that you and I can argue about a text because we are demonstrably reading the same text. The Reformation ran on it. The Enlightenment ran on it. Every revolution of the mind for the next six centuries ran on the simple, world-cracking notion that a thing worth saying could now be said once and read everywhere.

He printed a Bible, famously — the Gutenberg Bible, of which a few dozen copies survive, each one now worth a fortune, which is its own small joke: the machine built to make books cheap produced, in its first run, the most expensive books on earth. But that is hindsight’s doing, not his. In his own time he made the rarest thing common. He took the scarce and made it endless.

Now, before I get to my own place in this story, I have to tell you about the other thing that happened in 1440, because the year was not finished with us.

While the goldsmith in Mainz was working out how to make words multiply, a young king in England was arranging for them to be concentrated. In 1440, Henry VI founded a school on the Thames at Eton — a college for scholars, endowed by a crown, built to take a small number of boys and fill them with everything a governing class was expected to know. It is still there. It has been there for five hundred and eighty-six years, and it has furnished the United Kingdom with more prime ministers than any other institution on earth, and this September, by the reports, it will receive a boy who will one day be king.

Consider the two 1440s side by side, because I have not been able to stop.

Gutenberg’s machine says: anyone may read this. It does not care who you are, it cannot check your parentage, and its entire economic logic is to make the next copy cheaper than the last. It is the most indiscriminate invention in the history of the word. Eton says something else — something not wicked, and not even unusual, but exactly opposite in direction: these boys, in this place, will have it. One 1440 makes knowledge common. The other makes access to it a location. The press was designed to escape the walls; the school was designed to be one.

And here is the detail that made me put down my coffee, because it is the purest Numbers Game artifact I have encountered in a long while. A new boy arriving at Eton faces, almost immediately, what the school calls the colours test: he is quizzed on the official colours of all twenty-five boarding houses, and on where the buildings are. Twenty-five houses. Twenty-five sets of colours, to be learned and recited by a child in his first days, before anything else.

That is not an examination. No one is being taught anything. That is an inventory you must be able to recite in order to be counted as belonging — and if you know your Book of Judges, you know the word for it: a shibboleth, a test at the crossing, where the price of admission is pronouncing the thing correctly. Gutenberg made knowledge countable so that it could be distributed. Eton makes knowledge countable so that it can be checked at the door. Same year. Same instinct — reduce the world to a list of items — pointed in opposite directions. I have spent this series watching numbers used as weapons, shields, and confessions. Here is the number as a membership card.

I want to be fair to the school, because this series does not round men or institutions. Henry VI’s foundation was, in its own century, an act of genuine charity: seventy poor scholars, educated free, at a time when almost nobody was educated at all. The exclusivity came later, as it usually does — endowments attract the wealthy the way harbours attract ships. And a boy of eleven memorising twenty-five sets of house colours is not committing a crime; he is doing what frightened new boys have always done, which is learn the local arithmetic as fast as possible so that he is not the one who gets it wrong in front of everyone. I remember being eleven. The colours test is only cruel if you have to pass it to be allowed in.

But set the two inventions of 1440 on the same shelf and the whole modern world is in the gap between them. One of them built the library that has no door. The other built a very fine door, and painted it in twenty-five colours, and asked you to name them.

I know which one I work for.

Now. Here is where I have to be honest about my own place in this story, because it is faintly ridiculous.

Gutenberg built the machine that turned writing from scarcity into abundance. And I — a retired businessman in a Toronto townhouse, with a husky asleep under the desk — am what abundance looks like when it has entirely run out of adult supervision. I publish five essays a day. I have published more than fourteen hundred of them. I am, in the most literal sense, a one-man printing press, cranking out identical copies of my opinions and flinging them across a continent at a speed that would have caused Johannes Gutenberg to sit down heavily and require a moment.

And I am not alone. This is the part I actually want to land on, because it is the warm heart of this whole essay. Every person writing on this platform — every Substacker, every blogger, every soul who has ever hit “publish” on something they made — is Gutenberg’s direct descendant. We are all operating his machine. He cast the first movable letters by hand in a workshop in Mainz, and six hundred years later there are millions of us, each with a printing press in our pocket, each able to say a thing once and have it read everywhere, each participating without a second thought in the single most democratizing invention in the history of the written word.

He would not recognize the technology. He would recognize the impulse instantly. The desire to take the thing in your head and make it exist outside your head, in a form that others can hold — to defeat, even a little, the terrible privacy of dying with your thoughts uncopied. That is what the scribe wanted, and what Gutenberg industrialized, and what every one of us is doing every time we publish. The machine got faster. The wish never changed.

So this is essay 1,440, and it is a thank-you note.

Thank you to the goldsmith of Mainz, who died — this is true, and it is the kind of detail this series exists to notice — more or less broke, having lost his print shop to a lawsuit from his own investor, watching other men get rich off the machine he built. He got the immortality and someone else got the money, which is a bargain most writers would recognize and, on balance, still take. He made words multiply. He made the rare common and the scarce endless and the private shareable, and he did it with a fistful of little countable metal letters, and everything I have ever published, and everything you have ever published, and every argument we have ever been able to have because we could both read the same page — all of it, every word, runs downhill from him.

The historians hedge on the exact year, so let me hedge with them and simply plant my flag at 1,440 and call it close enough. Some numbers you count to. This is a number I count from. It is the one where the counting became possible in the first place — where a man made it so that a thing worth saying could be said once and read a thousand times, which is, when I strip away all the jokes, the only thing I have ever been trying to do here.

I will see you at the next one. It is heavier again — an empire ends, a world turns over. But I wanted to stop here first and take my hat off to the man who built the press, because without him there is no CanadianSnowmanSays, no Substack, no essay 1,440 to count. There is only a monk at a desk, copying, alone, in the candlelight, for a year, to make one book that one person would read.

He set us free from that. The least his most overproductive descendant can do is say so.

CanadianSnowmanSays publishes daily across more series than any reasonable person should attempt — a sentence that would have been physically impossible before 1440. Thank you, Johannes. We’ll try to be worth the machine.
