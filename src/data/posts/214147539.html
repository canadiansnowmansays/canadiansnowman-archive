The first two parts of this series covered robotaxis as an industry story — fleets, regulators, national standards, corporate rivalries spanning continents. This one is about a single person, because the industry story eventually lands on somebody, and in London it has landed, for more than a century and a half, on the people who pass the Knowledge.

Besart Bilalli is 39. A decade into driving for Uber, he grew frustrated with what he saw as the platform’s excessive commissions and his own dependency on an app he didn’t control, as Isabella Kwai profiled for The New York Times. He has three children and a wife, Dafina, who works as a teacher’s assistant, and he’d come to believe he could do better as an independent black-cab driver — if he could pass the single hardest professional licensing exam most people have ever heard of.

What the Knowledge actually demands

More than 150 years after it was created to train cabmen driving horse-drawn carriages, the Knowledge still covers roughly the same ground it always has: a six-mile radius around Charing Cross, encompassing some 25,000 streets and 6,000 points of interest, all of it committed to memory rather than looked up. Aspiring drivers first face a multiple-choice exam covering those landmarks and 320 fixed routes — a stage that alone can take two years to prepare for. Only then do candidates reach what’s widely considered the hardest part: a series of oral examinations at London’s transport authority, where an examiner names two points anywhere in that six-mile radius and the candidate must recite the shortest legal route between them, aloud, from memory, with no room to think out loud or hedge. Fail too many of these “appearances” and a candidate is “redlined” — sent back to repeat an entire stage of preparation, sometimes losing the better part of a year. The full process routinely takes three years. Some candidates take longer. Many don’t finish at all.

Bilalli had tried once before, years earlier, and quit — his children were young, the family’s finances were tight, and he went back to driving full-time for Uber. It was his wife who encouraged him to try again once the children were old enough to need less of his time. He signed up for classes at the Knowledge Point School in West London, mounted a spaghetti-tangle map of the city on his living room wall, and began spending up to eight hours a day drilling practice questions between school drop-offs and Uber shifts. “It’s like a puzzle,” he said. “We have to get as straight a line as possible.”

A decade of driving without ever needing the map

The detail that makes Bilalli’s story more than a story about difficulty is a small, almost throwaway moment he mentioned in passing: even while still working Uber shifts, he stopped using the GPS. “Even when I get riders on Uber, I do not use the map,” he said. “That’s when I realized that, oh, yeah, I could be doing this job.” A decade spent driving London’s streets for a platform that assumes its drivers need a screen to find their way had, without his fully registering it, already taught him the city by heart. The Knowledge, in his case, wasn’t teaching him something new so much as formalizing something he already possessed and Uber’s own technology had never asked him to prove.

The toll of formalizing it was still real. Sleepless nights before oral appearances. His wife waking him because he was reciting turn-by-turn directions in his sleep. Sticky notes with route fragments scattered around the bedroom, sometimes slipping behind the bed and staying lost for weeks. A painful appearance where he knew a route correctly but named the wrong side of the street for a hotel entrance — the kind of error that, compounded with others, can cost a candidate an entire year. And there was the cost measured differently: missing another summer trip to see relatives in his native Kosovo, and trying, not always successfully, to explain to his youngest daughter, seven years old, why he kept choosing the map over the plane ticket. He tells his older son, Gerti, a version of the same story he tells himself — that he wasn’t able to pursue higher education because he had to move to a new country and learn a new language as a young man, and that this, now, is his equivalent of that degree.

What’s waiting on the other side

Lukasz Labuda offers the version of this story with an ending. A former construction worker, he spent five years preparing for the Knowledge while working full shifts, drawn by what he described simply as the freedom of being his own boss — working to live, he said, instead of living to work. This spring, after seventeen prior appearances at London’s transport authority, he arrived for what he expected to be another oral test and instead received his badge: his official license as a London cabbie. “Unbelievable,” he said afterward. “Now I’m one of the best in London.” He rented his first black cab that same day, at roughly £350 a week, and by evening was still testing the taxi light switch, turning it on and off, savoring a small mechanical action that had just become his livelihood. “I really kiss this badge at the end of every shift,” he said. “I’m really grateful that I am one of the special forces.”

The economics beneath both men’s ambitions are genuinely uncertain. Some licensed black-cab drivers report earning around £50,000 a year; others describe six-figure incomes as achievable. The number of black-cab drivers in London has fallen from roughly 21,000 a decade ago to about 15,000 today, even as the ranks of app-based drivers — tethered permanently to GPS, answerable to an algorithm rather than an examiner — grew from about 78,500 to roughly 106,000 over the same period. And yet applications to attempt the Knowledge are climbing again after cratering during the pandemic, when new registrations fell to fewer than 200 a year: more than 600 people applied this year, with roughly twice that number somewhere in the multi-year process as of last count.

Why the exam still matters to the people who run it

Katie Chennells, who oversees the Knowledge examination process for London’s transport authority, frames its value in terms that go beyond nostalgia. It gives drivers the ability to actively recommend a better route around a closure or an accident rather than waiting for an app to recalculate, she argues, and black cabs remain unusually well-equipped to serve passengers with accessibility needs in ways many private-hire vehicles aren’t built for. Her framing of the comparison is memorable enough to repeat carefully: ride-hailing services are budget lodging, she suggested, and black cabs are a five-star hotel — different products serving different expectations, not simply an old technology losing to a new one. “People, they just trust us on a different level to which they trust a lot of other drivers,” she said.

The collision this series exists to track

Read against Parts I and II of this series, Bilalli’s story is what the industry-level material actually costs somebody, measured in years rather than dollars. Steven McNamara, the Licensed Taxi Drivers’ Association general secretary who dismissed robotaxis as a solution looking for a problem in Part II, is defending exactly the credential Bilalli is still in the process of earning — and London’s own hardest driving test is, not coincidentally, also the best publicly available explanation for why Wayve’s Alex Kendall considers the city the most legitimate proving ground in the world for an autonomous system. A 60 Minutes segment revisiting London’s cabbies as the robotaxi rollout accelerated this year, produced by CBS News, captured the stakes in a single juxtaposition: companies promising vehicles that are safer and smarter than any human driver, arriving in a city where the human standard for driving competence is deliberately, almost punishingly, high. Whether that standard turns out to be an asset the city’s cab trade can hold onto, or simply the longest goodbye in the history of urban transport, is not a question this series can answer from the outside. It’s Bilalli’s question to answer from the inside, one appearance at a time, and — as of his last update — he was still in the exam room, still reciting streets, still choosing the map over almost everything else his time could otherwise go toward.
