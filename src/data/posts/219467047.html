🖋 Wry, with a serious current under it — because this is the year energy itself was freed from geography, and because the whole titanic fight over how to do it came down, in the end, to an argument about which number to count in. Also there is a waterfall in it that belongs, in part, to us.

I count my essays, and every so often one year rhymes so cleanly with an earlier one that I can hear the series clicking into place like a well-cut gear. Essay 1844 was the year distance stopped counting for news — the telegraph abolished the speed limit on information. Essay 1895 is the year distance stopped counting for power — the year energy learned to leave the place it was made and arrive, intact, somewhere else entirely. They are the same revolution applied to two different quantities, fifty-one essays apart, and the second one was proved at a waterfall that thunders half in my country.

The tyranny of the waterfall

For the entire history of the species up to 1895, power was local in the most absolute sense. If you wanted to run a mill, you built it on the river, because the only way to use the river’s force was to sit directly on top of it. Waterwheel, millrace, the factory bolted to the exact spot where the water fell. Coal loosened this a little — you could carry coal to where you wanted the heat — but even steam power was a thing you generated on-site, in the building, next to the work. Energy had an address. You went to it; it did not come to you. The geography of industry was the geography of falling water and burnable rock, and it had always been so.

Niagara ended it. On August 26, 1895, the first great generator at the Adams Power Station on the Niagara River began turning, driven by the falls, and the following year its current reached Buffalo, some twenty-six miles away, and lit it. Twenty-six miles does not sound like much. It was everything. It was the proof — commercial, public, undeniable — that you could take the force of a river, convert it to electricity, send it across a county, and have it arrive still usable at the far end. After Niagara, you no longer had to build your city at the waterfall. You could build the city wherever you liked and bring the waterfall to it, on wires, at the speed of light. Power had been given, for the first time, an outbox.

That is the number-idea, and it is the twin of 1,844’s. The telegraph made the distance between two people stop mattering for the purpose of speaking. Niagara made the distance between a river and a factory stop mattering for the purpose of working. In both cases a quantity that had governed all of human life — how far away a thing is — was quietly set to zero for one specific use. Half the modern world is just the list of things we have, one by one, stopped having to be near.

The war was about a number

Now the part this series was built to love.

You will have heard of the War of the Currents — the brawl, foreshadowed at 1,882, between Thomas Edison’s direct current and the alternating current championed by George Westinghouse. Edison’s DC could not travel; it faded within a mile of the station, which is why Pearl Street lit only its own neighbourhood. Alternating current could be stepped up to high voltage, sent far, and stepped back down — which is precisely why it, and not Edison’s system, won the Niagara contract and lit Buffalo. When the commissioners chose AC for the greatest power project on earth, the war was effectively over, and Edison — the man who taught the world to trust the meter — had backed the losing number.

But here is what the popular telling leaves out, and what makes 1895 a Numbers Game year rather than merely an engineering one. Alternating current alternates — the flow reverses, back and forth, many times a second. And that immediately raises a question that direct current never had to answer: how many times a second? The current’s reversal rate — its frequency, measured in cycles per second, what we now call hertz — is a number somebody has to choose, and then everyone attached to the grid must agree to it forever, because a motor built for one frequency will not run properly on another. The deepest fight of the electrical age was not only AC versus DC. It was, underneath, an argument about which integer the entire civilisation would count its electricity in.

And the civilisation could not agree. North America eventually settled on sixty cycles. Europe settled on fifty. And Niagara, the pioneer, the proof of the whole concept — Niagara was built to run at twenty-five. The reasons were sound in 1895 and obsolete within twenty years, and so southern Ontario, drawing its power from that same river, spent the first half of the twentieth century running on twenty-five cycles while the continent around it standardised on sixty.

Ontario recounts everything

This is my favourite consequence in the entire electrical century, because it is this series’ thesis rendered in copper and clocks.

By the 1940s, Ontario was stranded on the wrong number. Its grid hummed at twenty-five cycles; the continent hummed at sixty; and a twenty-five-cycle motor, clock, or appliance simply will not keep proper time or speed on a sixty-cycle supply. So Ontario Hydro undertook one of the largest and least-celebrated engineering projects in Canadian history: the standardisation, over roughly a decade, of the entire province’s electricity from twenty-five cycles to sixty. Crews went building by building, town by town, replacing or re-fitting every motor, every clock, every piece of equipment that had been built to the old count. An entire province had its electricity recounted, one number swapped for another, at colossal expense, because a choice made at a waterfall in 1895 had been correct for its moment and wrong for the century.

Think of what that means. A whole society’s wall clocks had been keeping time against the wrong cycle for fifty years, and the only remedy was to go into every room and change the number the power itself was counted in. This series has watched people argue about which year it is, which day the century turns, how many ships make an Armada. Ontario is the one place that had to send a man to your house to correct the frequency of your electricity. The count is never merely the count. Sometimes the count is the alternating heartbeat of the current in your walls, and sometimes a province picks the wrong one and spends a generation setting it right.

The man whose numbers won

The system that triumphed at Niagara — polyphase alternating current, the rotating magnetic field, the induction motor that could take that far-travelled current and turn it back into the honest work of a spinning shaft — was, at its mathematical heart, the invention of Nikola Tesla, whose patents Westinghouse had licensed. I will give Tesla his exact due and not a cycle more, because he has in recent years been buried under a cult that does him no favours. He was a genuine original who saw, before almost anyone, how to make alternating current do useful work, and the lights of Buffalo were in a real sense the proof of his mathematics. There is a statue of him at Niagara — there are two, in fact, one on each side of the border, which is the most appropriate possible fate for a man whose work made sides matter less. He also died with far less money than the men who commercialised his ideas, which is the ordinary rather than the tragic fate of inventors, and this series has seen enough of them — at 1844, at 1876, at 1882 — to know that the person who controls the patent and the person who controls the payout are rarely the same person, and it is almost never the one who did the counting.

The waterfall that is partly ours

Let me plant my small Canadian flag, because I am entitled to it. The Niagara River is the border; the great Horseshoe Fall is, by the accident of the line, mostly on the Canadian side; and if the American Adams Station proved the concept in 1895, the Ontario side was harnessing the same river within the decade, and the enormous generating stations that still run there made cheap public power one of the quiet foundations of twentieth-century Ontario. The river does its work in two countries at once and asks neither for a passport. There is something fitting in the fact that the place where distance stopped counting for power is a place where the border itself is just a line drawn across falling water, meaningless to the water and increasingly meaningless, after 1895, to the power the water made.

Dior and the hum

The dog, I should report, is the household’s most sensitive instrument for the thing this essay is about, though she would never call it frequency. She knows the exact pitch of the refrigerator’s hum and sleeps through it; she knows the different hum of the furnace and ignores it; and when the power failed last winter and every hum in the house stopped at once, she lifted her head before I did, because the silence of a de-energised house is, to a dog, a loud event. We live inside a sixty-cycle hum we have entirely stopped hearing — the steady alternating count that Edison lost and Tesla won and Ontario had to correct — and it took a waterfall in 1895 to put it in the walls, and it takes a husky to notice when it stops. The whole modern world sings one note. We are just too used to it to hear the number.

The closer

So the wires carried news at 1844, voices at 1876, the unseeable unit at 1882, and now, at 1895, the raw force of a river across a county. Energy has an outbox; power has stopped being local; and a province will shortly have to recount every clock it owns to prove that even the right revolution can pick the wrong number. Distance, which this series has watched govern human life since the first courier, is losing one more of its old jobs.

The count moves on, and it moves from the harnessing of a river to the counting of a reign. The next landmark is essay 1897, and it is the purest counting ritual the series has yet reached — a Diamond Jubilee, sixty years on a throne, a number held up to a watching empire as proof of legitimacy itself. We have spent this run of essays watching new powers measured: the current, the railway, the river. At 1897 we watch an old power measure itself, at the very summit of its reach, by the one quantity it could still be certain of — the years. And we will ask, as this series always must, what the celebrated number left uncounted.

See you at 1897, where an empire counts its own years because the years are the one thing it cannot lose.
