by Stephen Gordon

The count arrives at 1,789 exactly as essay 1,776 promised: the year the axiom crossed the Atlantic and detonated. This is the second serious one in a row, and the last for a while, and it is built on the strangest symmetry in the modern political record — because in the single year of 1789, the two revolutions that between them invented the modern world both reached their decisive hour, one consolidating and one igniting, and they ran their books by opposite methods, and the difference in the bookkeeping is the difference in the body counts, and that is the essay.

Begin in New York, because the quiet ledger goes first. On March 4, 1789, the Constitution of the United States took effect and the first Congress convened; on April 30, on a balcony on Wall Street, George Washington swore the first presidential oath. Attend to what this actually was, thirteen years after the Declaration: it was a restatement of accounts. The first American constitution — the Articles of Confederation, feeble by design — had failed in audit; the republic’s books did not balance; and the response, in Philadelphia two summers earlier, had been a closed-door renegotiation among delegates, ratified state by state through public argument, producing a working charter shot through with compromises this series has already refused to prettify, the suppressed half-million among them. It was cautious, procedural, unlovely, and reversible on paper at every stage. Revolution as refinancing. Nobody stormed anything in the American 1789. They inaugurated a man who did not want the job, in a borrowed building, and began passing statutes about customs duties. The axiom of 1776 had been capitalized into an operating company, and the operating company opened, as operating companies do, by setting up the accounts receivable.

Now Paris, where the year kept the other ledger — and here I need you to see what actually lit the fuse, because it is the most Numbers Game origin of any revolution in the files. The French Revolution did not begin with a mob. It began with a dispute about how to count votes.

The French crown, bankrupt — partly, in one of history’s better ironies, from financing the American revolution it now watched with unease — summoned the Estates-General in May 1789: the ancient assembly of the realm’s three orders, clergy, nobility, and everyone else, which had not met in a hundred and seventy-five years. And the entire crisis, in its opening weeks, was arithmetic. The Third Estate — the commoners, roughly ninety-seven percent of the nation — had been granted double representation: six hundred deputies to three hundred each for the clergy and nobles. A concession, apparently. Except the crown left unresolved the only question that mattered: would the Estates vote by order — three blocs, one vote each, the two privileged orders permanently outvoting the commons two to one — or by head, each deputy counting once, in which case the doubled Third Estate plus sympathetic priests held the majority? Twelve hundred men sat at Versailles for six weeks arguing not about taxes, not about bread, not about rights — about the denominator. Whether France contained three votes or twelve hundred. Everything that followed — the Republic, the Terror, Napoleon, two centuries of aftershock — descends from a quarrel over the unit of count, and I file it as the supreme demonstration of this series’ oldest finding: before any question can be decided, someone decides how the deciding is counted, and that prior, procedural, invisible decision is the power. The Third Estate understood it perfectly. On June 17 they cut the knot by renaming themselves — the National Assembly, representing the nation because they were most of it, arithmetic as constitutional theory — and when the king locked their hall, they moved to an indoor tennis court and swore, on June 20, not to disperse until France had a constitution. The Tennis Court Oath: the modern world’s founding filibuster, sworn in a sports facility, by men whose revolutionary act to that point consisted entirely of insisting on being counted per head.

Then July, and the entry everyone knows, and the inventory nobody does. On July 14, 1789, the crowd of Paris — inflamed by troop movements, the dismissal of a popular minister, and the price of bread, which by the standard accounts reached its peak on that very day, the grain ledger and the political ledger arriving at crisis together — stormed the Bastille, the royal fortress-prison whose eight towers had glowered over the working east of the city for four centuries as the standing symbol of arbitrary power: the place men vanished into on the king’s sealed letters, no charge, no trial, no count.

The crowd took it, at a cost of some ninety-eight attackers dead, and released the prisoners. All of them. Both of them — forgive me: all seven of them. The Bastille, on the day it fell, contained seven prisoners: four forgers, two men held as insane, and one dissolute aristocrat lodged there at his own family’s request. That is the complete liberation manifest of the most famous prison-storming in human history. No martyred philosophers. No man in the iron mask. Seven inmates, most of whom would have been hard-pressed to explain the Rights of Man, blinking in the light while Paris roared. And here is why this is not a debunking but the entire point: the crowd did not storm the inventory. It stormed the symbol — and the symbol was real even though the count was seven. The Bastille’s power had never resided in its current occupancy; it resided in the capacity — in every Parisian’s knowledge that the cells existed and the lettres de cachet could fill them, that the number could be anyone’s. Seven prisoners understates a tyranny the way six dead understated the Great Fire, essay 1,666: the official count measured what was present, not what the thing was. The revolutionaries demolished the building within months and sold the stones as souvenirs — the symbol converted to inventory at last, retail — and July 14 remains the national day of France, celebrating, if we are strict about the books, the release of four forgers. We are not strict about the books. Some entries are true in a column arithmetic cannot see, and this series, of all places, is obliged to say so.

And then the two ledgers diverged, and the divergence is the lesson I have been building toward. The American revolution ran on the Declaration’s method — post the axiom, submit the facts, and keep the books open: amendments, elections, courts, the endless auditable grind. The French revolution, born in a counting dispute, arrived within four years at the opposite method, and essay 1,793 sits on the map below this one like a stone I am not lifting tonight: the ledger closed, the axiom enforced by blade, virtue itself made a quantity the state could assay in others and find wanting, the guillotine working through its lists — and lists, this series learned at 1,517, are a technology, and every technology serves whoever holds it. The itemized complaint became the itemized proscription. Same format. Opposite door. I do not say the American method was purity — half a million entries in its books say otherwise — and I do not say the French method was madness; it was, among other things, the frightened response of a revolution invaded on every frontier by every monarchy in Europe. I say only what the Numbers Game is licensed to say: one revolution treated its founding count as permanently contestable and one treated it as finally settled, and the contestable one is still being audited by its own citizens two hundred and fifty years on, and the settled one had to be resettled — Republic, Terror, Directory, Consulate, Empire, Restoration, and round again — constitution after constitution in a single generation, because a ledger declared closed can only ever be reopened by force. Keep the books open. It is the cheapest revolution ever devised.

One appendix to the French ledger, because the Revolution’s relationship with counting did not end at the voting dispute, and the sequel is the most literal entry available. Within months of the Bastille, the National Assembly — inheriting the bankruptcy that had summoned it — confiscated the lands of the Church and issued paper against them: the assignat, currency backed by seized real estate, sound in theory and revolutionary in every sense. And then, needing more money, it printed more paper than there was land behind it, and then more, and the assignat did what every over-issued note in history has done, which is to become a number divorced from its referent — by the mid-1790s requiring baskets of it for a loaf, until the Directory finally staged a public ceremony in 1796 at which the printing plates themselves were broken. Attend to that ceremony, because no image in this series says it cleaner: a revolution that began by fighting over how votes should be counted ended one chapter by smashing the machinery of its own numbers in a public square, an admission, in front of witnesses, that the count had lost all connection to the counted. The Americans, meanwhile — whose own revolutionary paper, the Continental, had died the identical death a decade earlier, “not worth a Continental” being the republic’s first contribution to the dictionary of failed numbers — wrote the lesson directly into their 1789 machinery: the new Constitution stripped the states of the power to print paper money at all. Both revolutions were tuition payments in the same course. Only one of them wrote the exam answer into its founding document, and it is the one whose ledger, whatever its other frauds, still clears.

Dior has audited both methods from beneath the desk and rendered her finding, which I pass along as received: the household, she notes, operates on the American model — her entitlements were declared long ago, self-evidently, and are enforced not by any final settlement but by continuous petition, morning by morning, at the food bowl and the door, the count never closed, the pressure never lifted. She has never once stormed anything. She has never needed to. The lesson of 1789, as near as she can tell, is that a revolution you must repeat has been done wrong, and a revolution done right is indistinguishable from breakfast arriving on time, forever, without thanks. I have checked her reasoning against the historians and it holds up better than most of Robespierre’s.

The count moves on, as it does, and it moves two years and four thousand miles, to the place where this year’s axiom was read most carefully and answered most completely. Six weeks after the Bastille, on August 26, 1789, the National Assembly published the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, whose first article holds that men are born and remain free and equal in rights. The Assembly then spent two years arguing about the footnotes — whether the article reached France’s colonies, and the free men of colour in them, and, unspeakably, whether the word men had been meant to extend as far as the people whose labour paid for the entire apparatus. It was the denominator question again, asked about human beings instead of votes, and the Assembly could not bring itself to answer it.

In August of 1791, in the richest colony on earth, half a million people answered it themselves. The next landmark is essay 1,791, and it concerns a population that had been counted with absolute precision — inventoried, valued, depreciated, replaced on a forecast schedule — who took the axiom of this year at its word, won outright, and were then handed an invoice for the value of themselves. It is the hardest set of books in the files, and I have been circling it for a while.

Two revolutions, one year. One kept its books open and is still arguing. One closed them and had to buy new books, five times. The Bastille held seven prisoners and infinite capacity, and the crowd, whatever its arithmetic, read the ledger right.

See you at 1,791, when the axiom reaches Saint-Domingue.
