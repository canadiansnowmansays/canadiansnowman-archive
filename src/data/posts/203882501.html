Reg Plimpton had been watching Jim through his kitchen window every Tuesday morning since March.

This was not, in Reg’s own assessment, surveillance. It was observation, which is a different thing, in the same way that interest is different from intention and attention is different from plan. Reg was interested in Jim. He had been interested in Jim since the November election, when Jim had been sitting at his kitchen table on a Sunday morning reading Carly’s piece about the Swan Warden mandate question, and Reg had driven past and seen the light in the window and the shape of a man reading something with focused attention, and had thought: that is a person who reads things carefully.

This was, in Elsinore, rarer than it appeared.

Jim walked eight dogs every Tuesday morning, beginning at six-fifteen, following a route that passed Reg’s kitchen window at approximately seven-oh-three. He passed with the unhurried efficiency of a man who has done this for three years and knows every driveway, every dog’s particular enthusiasm for every particular section of sidewalk, and every house whose residents prefer to be not noticed at seven in the morning and whose dog has already been briefed on this preference.

Reg would be at his kitchen table at seven-oh-three, because Reg was always at his kitchen table at seven in the morning. He would watch Jim pass. Jim would not look toward the window. Reg would note this. He was unsure whether Jim did not look because he had not noticed the window or because he had noticed it and made a decision.

In March he had decided it was the latter. He had been watching since then to confirm this reading, and had confirmed it, every Tuesday, without being able to determine what to do with the confirmation.

Jim had noticed the window in February.

He had noticed it the way he noticed things: not immediately, but after several repetitions of a pattern that accumulated into a certainty. A shape in the kitchen window, present at the same time each Tuesday, in the same position. Not the shape of someone accidentally visible while doing kitchen things. The shape of someone attending to the window. Jim knew the difference. He played poker. He had been reading tables for ten years.

He had not said anything about it. He had not changed his route or his pace. He had walked past the window every Tuesday at seven-oh-three and had not looked at it and had noted, in the quiet internal ledger he kept for these things, that Reg Plimpton was watching him and that the watching had been going on long enough that it meant something and that the meaning had not yet clarified.

He had mentioned it to Maggie at Sunday dinner in April. He said: Reg Plimpton watches me through his kitchen window every Tuesday.

Maggie had said: yes, I know.

Jim had said: you know?

Maggie had said: Debi works in the same building as Reg’s wife. She mentioned it.

Jim had said: what did she say?

Maggie had said: she said Reg thinks you’re interesting.

Jim had passed the potatoes. He had thought about this.

He said: interesting how.

Maggie said: the way Reg finds things interesting. Which is to say: useful in a way he hasn’t determined yet.

Jim had nodded. He had eaten his dinner. He had thought about pot odds and position and the patience of waiting for the right moment to find out what someone was holding.

The clarification came on a Tuesday in September, at seven-oh-seven, four minutes later than Jim’s usual pass.

Reg stood in the doorway after Jim left. He went inside, finished his coffee, and texted Gerald: Do we have a Community Advisory mechanism? Gerald replied forty minutes later: Yes. 2004. Dormant since 2011. Folder exists.

The delay was because the labrador had found something of interest in the hedge outside the Harrington Hardware parking lot and had required the full extension of Jim’s professional patience and a Milk-Bone from the left pocket of his jacket before concluding that the hedge, while compelling, was not the hedge’s best moment and that the walk could proceed.

This four-minute delay meant that Jim passed Reg’s kitchen window at seven-oh-seven, and at seven-oh-seven, Reg’s front door opened.

Reg stood in the doorway in a good suit, which was not unusual — Reg was always in a good suit by seven in the morning — holding a coffee mug, which suggested he had not planned the door-opening but had been at the kitchen table with his coffee and had decided, in the time it took Jim to pass, that the moment had arrived.

“Jim,” said Reg. “Do you have a minute?”

Jim assessed the situation. He had seven dogs to walk, of which the labrador and the vizsla were manageable, the corgi was always manageable, and the other four had varying degrees of opinion about unexpected pauses. He had three minutes before the arrangement became professionally complicated.

“Sure,” said Jim.

Reg said he had been meaning to speak to Jim for some time. He said he had noticed Jim in the neighbourhood for three years and had developed a great deal of respect for his work, which he understood involved a significant organisational and relational skill set. He said he had been thinking about community engagement in Elsinore and whether there were people in the community whose perspective on civic matters was underrepresented on council.

His right arm extended. It found Jim’s forearm and landed there with the practiced ease of twenty years.

Jim did not remove his arm. He let the arm do what the arm did. He looked at Reg with the expression he uses at the poker table when someone has made a bet that tells him more than the player intended.

He said: “What are you asking, Reg?”

Reg said he was asking whether Jim had ever considered public service.

Jim looked at him for a moment. He looked at the eight dogs, who were exhibiting varying degrees of patience. He looked back at Reg.

He said: “Reg. I walk dogs. I play poker on Thursday nights at the Legion. I read the Beacon on Sunday mornings and sometimes I have thoughts about things.” He paused. “I’m not a public servant.”

Reg said everyone in Elsinore contributed in their own way.

Jim said: “You’ve been watching me through your kitchen window since March.”

Reg’s arm left Jim’s forearm.

There was a moment of specific silence.

Jim said: “I noticed in February. I didn’t say anything because I was waiting to see what you were working up to.” He looked at the dogs. “I need to finish the walk. But I’ll tell you what I think. I think you want someone on council who people trust. I think you’ve decided I might be that person. I think you’ve been trying to figure out how to ask.”

Reg said nothing.

Jim said: “The answer is no. Not council.” He looked at the window, then back at Reg. “But if something comes up where you need someone to tell you what the town actually thinks — not what the committee thinks, not what Bradley Fenn thinks — you can ask me.”

He walked the dogs home.

Reg stood in the doorway for a moment after he left. He went back inside. He sat at the kitchen table. He finished his coffee.

He picked up his phone and texted Gerald: Do we have a Community Advisory mechanism?

Gerald texted back, forty minutes later: Yes. 2004. Dormant since 2011. Folder exists.

Reg raised the question of the Community Advisory mechanism at the October council meeting. He described it as a “reactivation of an existing civic engagement framework to ensure diverse community perspectives inform council decision-making.” He had a slide.

Mayor Doug said it sounded like a good idea. He had the expression of a man who does not yet know what the idea is but has learned, in four terms, that Reg’s ideas require careful observation before judgment.

Bradley Fenn asked about the selection criteria for advisory members.

Gerald said the 2004 terms of reference specified that advisory members were to be residents of the municipality with no current elected or appointed role, selected by open application and council approval.

Bradley asked whether council would retain veto over appointments.

Gerald said the terms of reference provided for council approval of all appointments. He said this in the measured tone of a man who considers it his job to state what the terms of reference say without editorialising on what they mean, and who is aware that the distinction between stating and editorialising is where most of the real work in municipal governance takes place.

Reg moved to reactivate the mechanism. Mayor Doug seconded. The motion carried, with Bradley noting his concern about process and Constance Hay voting yes without comment, which was Constance’s way of saying the mechanism was overdue.

Gerald retrieved the 2004 folder from his filing system that evening. He reviewed the terms of reference. He noted that the mechanism had produced, in its seven years of operation, fourteen advisory reports, of which council had acted on nine and tabled five.

He noted that the five tabled reports were still in the folder. He read them. They were good.

He filed a note: Community Advisory — Reactivation — 2026 — See 2004 Terms of Reference — See Five Tabled Reports.

He had not been asked to read the five tabled reports. He had read them because they were there and because a mechanism being reactivated should know what it had said before.

Jim applied to the Community Advisory mechanism in November. He filled out the form, which was two pages, and returned it to the municipal clerk, who thanked him and said she would pass it to council for approval.

He mentioned it to Maggie at Sunday dinner.

Maggie said: you actually did it.

Jim said: it seemed like the right thing.

Maggie said: what changed your mind?

Jim said he had thought about it for a few weeks. He said the dogs walked the same streets every morning and he knew which driveways had which cars and which lights came on at six o’clock and which families had been there for twenty years and which were new, and that this was a specific kind of knowledge that was different from the knowledge that came from committee meetings and grant applications and slides about civic infrastructure, and that if there was a mechanism for that kind of knowledge to be in the room, it was probably better for it to be in the room.

Maggie said: the pot odds were right.

Jim said: something like that.

They ate their dinner. The boys ate their dinner.

Reg watched Jim walk past on Tuesday morning at seven-oh-three. Jim did not look toward the window. Reg noted this.

He had decided, after the doorway conversation, that Jim not looking at the window was not avoidance but preference — the preference of a man who has made a decision and does not need to confirm it by looking in the direction of the person who prompted it. Jim already knew Reg was there. Reg already knew Jim knew.

The arm extending was a reflex. The conversation had happened. The application had been filed.

In Elsinore, this was what came next. Not a resolution in the dramatic sense. Just what came next, which is the way most things in Elsinore are resolved: quietly, at the correct pace, with the necessary information in the room and the unnecessary information filed for later.

Gerald has the folder.

The five tabled reports are in it.

The dogs are walked.

A Note for the Record

The question of who gets to be in the room — which voices inform the decisions, which kinds of knowledge are considered valid, which people are invited to apply and which are not — is not a small question. It is the question that most of the other questions depend on.

In Elsinore, a dog walker read the Beacon, was watched through a kitchen window for eight months, had a four-minute conversation in a doorway, and applied to a community advisory mechanism that had been dormant since 2011. The mechanism was reactivated because a Deputy Reeve noticed that someone whose perspective was missing ought to be in the room.

To the south, the question of who gets to be in the room has been answered in a different direction. Advisory councils have been disbanded. Civil society organisations have lost federal funding and been required to certify political neutrality as a condition of receiving grants. The people who were in the room have been replaced, in many cases, by people who were not previously in that room but had expressed strong support for the person who controls the room. The knowledge that comes from walking the same streets for three years, from knowing which driveways have which cars and which lights come on at six o’clock, is not the kind of knowledge currently being sought in the rooms that matter.

Jim’s application is on the clerk’s desk. Council will vote on it in December. Gerald has read the five tabled reports and found them good. Constance Hay voted yes without comment.

In Elsinore, this is how it begins.

A man walks dogs. He notices a window. He waits to see what the window is working up to. He tells the truth in a doorway for four minutes and walks on.

The dogs need walking. The pot odds were right. The mechanism is reactivated.

Gerald has the folder.
