Every previous instalment of this ledger has tracked money flowing toward AI — capex, leases, circular financing, tokenomics, the sheer scale of capital being committed to a technology whose returns remain unproven. This part flips the ledger around and asks what flows back, specifically into the one account this series has otherwise left untouched: the United States Treasury.

As Andrew Duehren reported for the New York Times, a growing number of economists have begun to treat widespread AI adoption as a potential structural improvement to the federal government’s fiscal trajectory — not a fix, but a genuine tailwind, on the theory that more productive workers generate faster economic growth, and faster growth generates more tax revenue without anyone having to raise a rate. Gross federal debt sits at roughly $40 trillion. Nobody serious is arguing AI erases that number. The live debate is narrower and more interesting: does AI bend the curve down a little, bend it down a lot, or bend it in the wrong direction entirely, depending on who ends up capturing the gains.

Two Paths, One Word Doing All the Work

The mechanism Duehren’s sources describe hinges on a single distinction this series has returned to since Part IV’s account of the wealth-sharing backlash: labour income versus capital income. Most federal revenue comes from taxing what people earn working, and it is taxed at a materially higher rate than capital gains or business profits, a design choice reflecting Washington’s long-standing bet that lighter taxation of capital encourages investment. If AI makes workers more productive without displacing them, wages rise, tax receipts on those wages rise, and companies still generate more profit and pay more tax on it too, just at the lower capital rate. Everybody’s slice grows. Douglas Elmendorf, formerly the nonpartisan Congressional Budget Office’s director, offered the cleanest summary available: faster AI-driven productivity growth will help the budget imbalance, but it will not solve it.

The less comfortable path is the one where AI boosts productivity precisely by replacing labour rather than augmenting it. In that scenario, a larger share of the nation’s total income shifts from the higher-taxed labour column into the lower-taxed capital column. Total revenue can still rise in absolute terms — the economy is bigger, after all — but it rises by meaningfully less than in the augmentation scenario, because the growth is landing in the pocket the tax code was built to treat gently. Researchers at the Yale Budget Lab modelled this directly and found the 2030 revenue bump from AI-driven growth could be roughly halved if a greater share of income accrues to capital rather than labour. That single modelling result is the fulcrum this entire fiscal debate balances on, and it is worth noticing how much of the current AI investment thesis — cutting headcount, automating tasks, extracting margin — points toward the capital-heavy outcome rather than the labour-friendly one.

The Amodei Line This Series Keeps Returning To

Duehren’s reporting includes a line from Anthropic’s Dario Amodei that belongs in this ledger permanently, because it is one of the only instances of a frontier AI lab leader arguing publicly against his own industry’s narrow self-interest on tax policy. Writing in January 2026, Amodei argued that the world’s billionaires should get ahead of the distributional problem AI is creating by supporting a more robust tax policy themselves, warning that if they do not help design a good version, they will eventually get a bad version imposed by what he called a mob. It is a strikingly candid acknowledgment, from inside the industry generating the capital concentration in question, that the current tax architecture was not built for a world where productivity gains flow overwhelmingly to owners of capital rather than to the people doing the work — and an implicit admission that the industry’s own wealth accumulation is politically unsustainable without some pre-emptive redistribution.

This is not a new thread for the Capex Ledger. Part IV traced the same fault line through Bernie Sanders’ proposal for a sovereign wealth fund funded by a mandatory equity stake in AI companies, and through the state-level backlash — Virginia’s data centre tax fight, New York’s Hochul moratorium — that Ben Harris at Brookings has been tracking as a bipartisan phenomenon rather than a purely progressive one. Amodei’s essay sits at an odd angle to that backlash: it is a call for redistribution issued from inside the industry being asked to redistribute, which makes it either genuinely prescient or a hedge against a worse outcome, and probably both at once.

Two Tools on the Table, and Why One Keeps Losing

Duehren’s reporting surfaces a live policy argument about which lever to pull if labour’s share of income does shrink. One camp wants to raise taxes on capital directly — higher capital gains rates, new levies on partnership income, tighter enforcement against international corporate tax manoeuvring. A second camp treats the tax preference for capital as close to untouchable and would rather backfill lost revenue with a value-added tax, a consumption levy standard across most advanced economies but historically resisted in Washington. Lee Lockwood, an economist at the University of Virginia, called the VAT option a fairly robust recommendation precisely because economists across the ideological spectrum already favour consumption taxation on its own merits, AI or no AI.

There is also a narrower, more politically charged option Duehren notes almost in passing: taxing AI companies or AI-specific activity directly, an idea with obvious populist appeal to some Democrats but one that most tax experts he cites prefer to fold into the broader capital-taxation debate rather than carve out as its own category. The reasoning tracks with something this series flagged as early as Part I — AI capital expenditure is already so entangled with existing corporate structures, cross-holdings, and circular financing arrangements between chipmakers and their own customers that isolating “AI activity” as a distinct taxable category is closer to an accounting fiction than a workable policy lever. You cannot easily tax the thing when the thing is inseparable from the balance sheet it is sitting inside.

The Spending Side Nobody Wants to Talk About

Every fiscal conversation about AI defaults to the revenue side, because revenue is the flattering half of the ledger. Duehren’s reporting is useful precisely because it refuses to stop there. If AI genuinely accelerates medical research and extends average life expectancy — a plausible outcome, and one this series has treated elsewhere as a real upside case for the technology — that longevity gain arrives with a bill attached, in the form of more years of Social Security and Medicare obligations per retiree. A longer life is an unambiguous human good and an unambiguous new fiscal liability at the same time, and the fiscal optimists rarely sit with both halves of that sentence together.

The interest-rate channel is the other spending-side risk, and arguably the more immediate one. A capital expenditure boom on the scale this series has documented — the hyperscaler leases, the $500 billion financing consortiums, Oracle’s leveraged pivot — competes for financing with the rest of the economy, and that competition can push interest rates up more broadly. Rates matter enormously to a government already paying $1.2 trillion in interest on its debt this fiscal year alone, more than it spends on the military or nearly any other single programme. If AI-driven borrowing pushes financing costs higher even as it (maybe) grows the tax base, those two effects are working against each other inside the same ledger, and nobody currently modelling the “AI shrinks the deficit” scenario has fully priced in AI’s own debt-financed buildout as a headwind against itself. The Capex Ledger’s earlier parts on Oracle’s debt-to-equity ratio and Nvidia’s circular lease guarantees are not a separate story from this fiscal one. They are the mechanism by which AI’s investment boom could raise the very borrowing costs that determine whether any productivity dividend shows up as deficit reduction or gets consumed by debt service before it arrives.

The Dystopian Case Nobody Is Pricing In Yet

Duehren’s reporting also acknowledges, almost as an aside, the scenario sitting beyond the augmentation-versus-displacement spectrum entirely: that AI eventually automates enough of the labour market to upend the taxation system as currently constructed, producing a far more stratified economy in the process. He notes this is not the base case for most economists modelling near-term fiscal outcomes, and this series has no basis for asserting it is more likely than the mainstream forecasts suggest. But it is worth registering as the tail risk shaping policy conversation even now, because Amodei’s tax-policy essay only makes sense as a response to that tail risk rather than to the mild-augmentation scenario Elmendorf describes. Nobody writes an essay warning billionaires to design their own tax regime before “a mob” designs a worse one in response to a world where AI merely makes existing workers seventeen percent more productive. That essay gets written in anticipation of the harder scenario, which tells you something about how the people closest to the technology are actually weighting the odds, whatever the consensus economic models currently say in public.

There is a stranger wrinkle buried even further into the dystopian case: Duehren’s reporting notes economists are beginning to worry not just about AI displacing workers, but about AI becoming genuinely skilled at inventing novel tax-avoidance strategies faster than tax authorities can close them. This is a distinct problem from the labour-versus-capital question, and this series has not yet given it dedicated treatment. An AI system optimised to minimise a company’s tax liability is not doing anything qualitatively different from what tax attorneys and accountants already do — but if it does it faster, more creatively, and at a scale no human advisory practice can match, the entire premise of a stable tax base predicated on enforceable rules starts to erode from a direction none of the revenue-side optimism accounts for. A more productive economy generating more taxable income is only a fiscal win if the income in question actually gets taxed at the rate the law intends, and an AI arms race between avoidance and enforcement is a live variable this series should keep watching alongside the labour-share question.

A Conference From 2001, and the Discipline of Not Believing Rosy Numbers

Ben Harris’s recollection, buried near the end of Duehren’s piece, is the essay’s most useful corrective, and this series should hold onto it as a standing rule rather than a passing anecdote. Around 2001, in the afterglow of the 1990s productivity boom, Harris attended a conference dedicated in part to the question of what America would do once it retired its entire national debt — a genuine policy conversation at the time, given the fiscal surpluses on the books. None of it happened. Congress cut taxes repeatedly instead, fought two wars, and the debt resumed its climb as if the surplus had never existed. Harris’s conclusion, translated for this series’ purposes: he will always distrust forward-looking growth projections after watching that particular rosy scenario evaporate, and the AI-fuelled optimism circulating now reminds him specifically of that earlier moment of unearned confidence.

This is the discipline the Capex Ledger has tried to apply from Part I onward, and it applies with equal force here. AI may well be a genuine productivity accelerant, and a faster-growing economy genuinely does generate more revenue without anyone raising a rate — that much is not in serious dispute. What is very much in dispute, and what history counsels real scepticism about, is whether the political system does anything disciplined with a fiscal tailwind once it materialises, as opposed to simply spending or cutting it away, the way it did the last time a transformative technology handed Washington a surplus and Washington handed it right back.
