Every entry in this series so far has looked at AI spending from the supply side — the hyperscalers, the chipmakers, the financing structures holding up half-trillion-dollar data centers. This one looks at the demand side, at the ordinary companies actually buying the compute those data centers produce, and it turns out they are running an experiment nearly as reckless as anything Larry Ellison signed up for, just at a scale small enough that nobody’s written a Wall Street research note about it yet.

As Lydia DePillis reported for The New York Times on August 3, 2026, corporate enthusiasm for AI has curdled, in engineering teams and boardrooms alike, into something closer to sticker shock. The piece traces a two-phase whiplash with names only Silicon Valley could produce: first “tokenmaxxing,” as executives pushed employees to use AI tools as much as possible, then “tokenmourning,” once finance departments noticed how fast that usage was burning through company budgets. The underlying problem is not that AI doesn’t work. It’s that almost nobody buying it has any reliable way to measure whether it’s working relative to what it costs — a genuinely new category of corporate spending, arriving faster than the accounting tools built to make sense of it.

A Commodity With No Exchange

Howard Rubin, an economist who advises companies on technology spending, offered the Times the cleanest description of the actual problem: it’s a currency companies have no instinct for using, priced by a market with no accounting practices built for it yet. Tokens — the units in which AI usage gets sold, each representing roughly a few characters of text — are, on the surface, a commodity not unlike crude oil: a raw input, refined into different products, theoretically reducible to a standard unit of measurement. But the analogy collapses almost immediately. Oil trades on public exchanges with transparent, continuously updated prices. Tokens are priced differently by every provider, with no consensus on how much energy a given token actually requires to produce, no agreement on which model is the right tool for a given task, and — this is the detail that should worry anyone building a corporate budget around this technology — no reliable way to know in advance how many tokens a given task will even consume, because some AI agents will keep grinding at an unsolvable problem in an expensive loop rather than recognizing failure and stopping.

The response has been the emergence of an entire minor industry devoted to helping companies figure out what they’re actually buying. The Linux Foundation — the nonprofit that once standardized cloud computing pricing comparisons — launched a Tokenomics Foundation this June specifically to build common disclosure standards providers would agree to follow, on the theory that a market this opaque cannot function as a market at all without some baseline everyone measures against. Vendors like Revenium have built businesses around the specific problem of mapping a company’s token spending to concrete outcomes — shipped features, resolved tickets — so that a finance department can answer a question that currently has no reliable answer: is this actually worth what we’re paying for it?

The Metric That Says the Quiet Part Out Loud

The single most revealing detail in the piece is a metric invented by a cybersecurity company called Elisity: “bionic head count.” The calculation is exactly as blunt as it sounds — total a company’s AI spending, divide by the average cost of a human employee’s salary and benefits, and the result is treated as a count of virtual “employees” added to the workforce. Human staff at Elisity reportedly write formal job descriptions for individual AI agents to justify “hiring” them, and each time an engineer assigns a new task to a model, that use gets evaluated the way a manager might evaluate a new hire’s output. Charlie Treadwell, the company’s chief marketing officer, framed the whole exercise as a capital-allocation question dressed in HR language: is this virtual head count producing incremental revenue, or is it just eating into margins the company would rather protect. That the language of hiring and firing has migrated so completely onto a line item in a cloud computing bill says something neither flattering nor especially subtle about where corporate America expects this to end up, regardless of how carefully any individual company insists — as Elisity does — that it has no current plans to reduce human staff.

The fragility of even this measurement is worth dwelling on, because Treadwell’s own numbers show how quickly the whole framework could become obsolete. Elisity currently runs on a flat-rate subscription for 150 users; if the company were switched to per-token pricing, the norm once an organization crosses a few hundred users, its AI spending would reportedly quadruple overnight. A “bionic head count” metric built on today’s flat-rate economics is a metric built on sand, liable to swing wildly the moment a vendor changes its pricing model — which is precisely the kind of instability a company would never tolerate in its actual, human payroll.

The Constraint Nobody’s Talking About

Jue Wang, a partner at the consultancy Bain, offered the Times an observation that cuts against the entire premise of this AI spending wave, and I think it deserves more attention than the pricing mechanics do: the binding constraint on realizing value from AI isn’t computing capacity at all. It’s the shortage of people who actually know how to deploy the technology productively inside a specific organization. Bain’s own modeling suggests tokens could eventually make up roughly a quarter of corporate operating expenses in a medium-term scenario — a genuinely enormous reallocation of spending, assuming companies can find the expertise to convert that spending into anything worth having. That is the same structural problem this series has traced at the level of entire economies — capital outrunning the human and institutional capacity to actually use it well — showing up now inside individual firms’ own budgets, at a scale small enough that it will resolve itself company by company, quietly, long before anyone convenes a Senate hearing about it.

A Small Data Set for an Enormous Question

The honest caveat sitting underneath this entire piece, and one DePillis is careful to include, is how little anyone studying this actually knows. Aleh Tsyvinski, a Yale economist who co-authored a recent working paper on token economics, built his analysis on a dataset from the platform OpenRouter representing roughly 2 percent of total AI spending — a genuinely small window onto a genuinely enormous phenomenon, in a field with no centralized exchange, no futures market, and no government survey tracking prices or volumes the way agencies routinely track them for oil, labor, or nearly every other major input to the American economy. Tsyvinski’s paper did find one specific silver lining: the growing use of cheap “cache tokens” — reused, already-processed computations that cost a fraction of a fresh request — has been holding spending down relative to where it would otherwise land, even as posted prices for frontier models and overall token consumption both continue rising. That is a genuine efficiency gain, and it complicates the simple story of runaway corporate AI spending. But it is also, per Tsyvinski’s own description, a finding built on a sliver of the total picture, in a market whose most basic facts — what a token actually costs to produce, what a fair price for one should be, how much energy the whole system consumes in aggregate — remain, as of this writing, essentially unknown even to the economists trying hardest to measure them.

The Petroleum Analogy’s Actual Value

It’s worth pushing the oil comparison a bit further than the Times piece does, because where the analogy breaks down is as instructive as where it holds. Crude oil earned its exchange-traded status only after decades of standardization — API gravity grades, benchmark crudes like Brent and WTI, regulatory bodies tracking flows in and out of every major port. That infrastructure took most of the twentieth century to build, under far less time pressure than AI token markets are currently operating within. The Linux Foundation’s new Tokenomics Foundation is, in effect, trying to compress that entire standardization process into a matter of quarters rather than decades, for a commodity whose underlying cost structure changes every time a lab ships a new model. Ram Bala, the Santa Clara University professor cited in the piece, put his finger on why this is harder than it looks: intelligence, unlike a barrel of oil, doesn’t have one fixed use. The same token can go toward a customer service reply or an open-ended research loop that never terminates, and no barrel of crude has ever behaved that unpredictably once refined. A commodity whose value depends entirely on what question you ask it resists the kind of clean, provider-agnostic pricing that made oil, and later cloud computing, into markets companies could budget against with any confidence.

Where the Money Actually Comes From

One detail buried in the middle of DePillis’s reporting deserves to be pulled out and looked at directly, because it answers a question this series hasn’t asked yet: when a company’s AI bill balloons, what actually gets cut to pay for it? Jason Cumberland, the Revenium executive who works with clients experiencing what he calls spending explosions, described a fairly consistent progression. Companies start by covering AI subscriptions out of discretionary budget — scrounging under the proverbial couch cushions, in his phrase — treating the cost as a rounding error worth absorbing to see what the tools could do. As reliance deepens, the couch cushions stop covering it, and the cuts move to adjacent line items: software licenses that overlap with what the AI tool now handles, and outside services — contract web development, freelance copywriting — that used to get farmed out to humans. That is a far more concrete and far more immediate displacement mechanism than the abstract “AI might cost jobs eventually” framing this series has mostly encountered in its Asimov Gap coverage of labor anxiety. It isn’t a mass layoff. It’s a freelance invoice that quietly stops getting sent, an outside vendor relationship that lapses at renewal, absorbed so incrementally into ordinary budget management that it may never show up in any employment statistic at all — which may be exactly why the political backlash this series covered in Part IV has struggled to find its footing: the harm this piece describes is diffuse, cumulative, and largely invisible until someone adds it up.

Where This Fits the Rest of the Ledger

Every other entry in this series has asked whether the money financing the AI buildout is sustainable at the level of Wall Street — debt structures, lease obligations, circular financing between chipmakers and their own customers. This piece is the same question asked one rung down, inside the ordinary companies whose subscriptions and API bills are supposed to eventually justify all that infrastructure spending in the first place. If Oracle’s bet depends on OpenAI’s customers proving willing and able to pay enough, over enough years, to make the underlying capital expenditure worthwhile, then “bionic head count” spreadsheets at cybersecurity companies with 150 users are not a footnote to that story. They are the actual mechanism by which it either succeeds or doesn’t — multiplied across every mid-sized company in America currently trying to figure out, with tools that barely exist yet, whether what they’re buying is worth what they’re being charged.
