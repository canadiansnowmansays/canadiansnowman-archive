Postmark: Haneda Airport, Tokyo. A humanoid slides a cargo container along a conveyor, waves at the crew, shakes a coworker’s hand. Around it, a workforce that shrinks a little more each year.

In April, Japan Airlines and a Tokyo technology firm called GMO announced something that will sound, to a North American ear, like a novelty item and is in fact a documentary about the future: beginning in May, humanoid robots would go to work on the tarmac at Haneda Airport — Tokyo’s second airport, one of the busiest on Earth, moving somewhere north of sixty million passengers a year — hauling cargo containers, loading baggage, eventually cleaning aircraft cabins, in a phased trial running to 2028 with the explicit goal of commercialization within three years.

The machines are Unitree G1s, the same 132-centimetre, 35-kilogram platform this series has met before — the one that dances at the Spring Festival Gala and sells to university labs. As CNBC reported, the video demonstration showed one of them sliding a payload along a conveyor belt, waving to onlookers, and shaking a coworker’s hand. It is easy to watch that clip and see a gimmick: a toy robot doing a party trick on an airport apron. That reading is a mistake, and correcting it is the whole purpose of this postcard. Because the reason those robots are at Haneda has nothing to do with novelty and everything to do with a number that ought to concentrate the mind of every advanced economy on Earth.

The number that leaves no door

Here is the number. As CNBC reported, citing the OECD, Japan’s working-age population is projected to decline by thirty-one per cent between 2023 and 2060. Not decline in growth rate. Decline — shrink, absolutely, by nearly a third — over a single working lifetime.

Sit with what that means for a place like Haneda. The airport handles record and rising numbers of international tourists — Japan drew more than forty million visitors in a recent year, and arrivals keep climbing — while the pool of people available to do the physically punishing, unglamorous work of ground handling drains away year over year. Japan Airlines employs roughly four thousand ground-handling staff, and it cannot conjure more; the young people who once filled those roles are simply not being born in the numbers the work requires. This is the vise: demand for the service rising, supply of the humans to provide it falling, and no immigration policy on the table large enough to close the gap, because the current Japanese government’s support rests in part on keeping immigration tightly restricted.

That last point is the one North American readers tend to miss, and it is where a Canadian’s vantage becomes useful. Canada has answered its own demographic slowdown primarily through immigration — controversially, incompletely, but as a matter of settled national strategy, we have chosen to grow the workforce by welcoming people into it. Japan has made, over decades, the opposite choice. And a society that will not import workers and cannot birth them fast enough is left with exactly one remaining lever to pull if it wants to keep the airport running and the population cared for: it must manufacture its workers. Not metaphorically. Literally, on an assembly line, in Hangzhou, and ship them to the tarmac. The humanoid at Haneda is not a technology looking for a problem. It is a demographic emergency looking for a body.

Designing machines for the existing world

There is a phrase in the reporting that unlocks the deeper significance of the Haneda trial, and it deserves to be lifted out and examined, because it explains why the robots are shaped like us.

Why humanoids at all? A cargo-hauling task could, in principle, be done by a purpose-built machine — a smarter forklift, a conveyor, a wheeled cart. The airport industry has automated for decades without a single android. The answer, as one analysis put it, is that Japan is not redesigning the world for machines; it is designing machines for the existing world. An airport is built, down to the last doorway and handrail and cargo-hold hatch, around the dimensions and reach and dexterity of the human body. To automate it with conventional robots, you would have to rebuild the airport — a capital expense so vast it would swallow any labour savings. But a machine shaped like a person can be inserted into the human-shaped world exactly as it is, no renovation required. It picks up what a person picks up, fits where a person fits, opens what a person opens.

Japan Airlines said this itself, and plainly: the human shape allows the robots to be introduced without significant modification to existing airport facilities or aircraft. That single sentence is the entire economic case for the humanoid form factor, and it generalizes far beyond aviation. Every hospital, warehouse, hotel, kitchen, and elder-care facility on Earth is already built to human dimensions. The humanoid robot is the skeleton key cut to fit the lock we have already installed everywhere — which is precisely why, of all the shapes a robot could take, the industry keeps returning to the one in the mirror. The uncanny resemblance that Part VIII worried over is not vanity. It is the cheapest possible way to automate a world built by and for humans without demolishing it first.

The honest limits

Now the part where this series keeps its promise to lead with wonder but never to sell you a fantasy.

The Haneda robots are not, at present, very good at their jobs — and the people running the trial say so with a candour the marketing departments would prefer to mute. As CNBC reported, one research director assessing the deployment put it bluntly: these robots are just not very smart yet, the reasoning and programming behind them remain underdeveloped, and any real deployment will still require substantial human involvement. The trial reflects this in its very structure. It does not begin with robots doing the work. It begins with observation — engineers mapping every movement on the tarmac, every handoff between workers, every moment where timing and spatial awareness matter, then carving out narrow “safe zones” where a machine can operate without unacceptable risk, then running simulations, and only then, cautiously, task by task, letting the robots touch real cargo. Safety-critical decisions remain, throughout, under human control. The battery lasts two to three hours before it must be swapped. This is not the robot takeover. This is a three-year, human-supervised, incremental audition, and the auditioning machine needs a nap every afternoon.

But — and this is the pivot the skeptics miss — the modesty of the current capability is not evidence against the trend. It is evidence of where we are on the trend. The same research director who called the robots not-very-smart also estimated that larger-scale deployment is likely no more than five years away. The trial is structured as a slow build precisely because everyone involved expects the capability to arrive during the build. Japan is not betting that today’s robot can run the airport. It is betting that by patiently mapping the work now, it will be ready the moment the robot can — and that the demographic vise will have tightened enough by then that “ready” is not optional.

The graveyard of earlier promises

It would be easy, watching the Haneda robots wave, to assume this is Japan’s first attempt to automate its way out of the demographic vise. It is not — and the graveyard of its earlier attempts is exactly why this trial is structured so cautiously, and why it deserves to be taken more seriously than the ones that failed.

Japan has been promising itself a robot rescue for a generation. There was the famous hotel staffed by robots that had to quietly re-hire human beings after the machines proved more trouble than help — velociraptor receptionists that could not answer questions, in-room assistants that woke guests by mistaking snoring for speech. There were the care-home robots, heavily subsidized and heavily photographed, that ended up gathering dust in closets because the overworked staff found tending to the robot took more time than the task the robot was meant to spare them. A country that wanted, more desperately than any other, for the robots to work, kept discovering that wanting was not enough. The machines of the 2010s were not ready, and Japan’s demographic panic could not make them so by force of need.

So the reasonable question is: why is this different? Why should the Haneda trial end anywhere but the same closet? The answer is the convergence this series has been documenting from the start. The earlier failures came before three things arrived at once — the humanoid form factor cheap enough to deploy at scale, courtesy of the Chinese EV supply chain of Part VIII; the AI good enough to let a machine perceive and adapt rather than merely repeat, however imperfectly; and the demographic pressure crossing from “concerning” to “unignorable.” None of those three alone was sufficient; the robot hotel had novelty but neither cheap hardware nor real intelligence. What is different in 2026 is not that Japan wants the robots more. It is that the robots, for the first time, can at least plausibly attempt the work — and the country has learned, from its own graveyard, to audition them slowly rather than believe the demo.

The government’s thumb on the scale

There is a policy dimension here that a Canadian, attuned to how governments shape markets, should not miss, because it turns a corporate trial into a national strategy.

The Haneda deployment is not happening in a vacuum of pure market forces. As CNBC reported, in March Japan’s Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry — the storied METI, architect of the country’s postwar industrial miracles — published guidelines specifically on using robotics and AI to address workforce challenges caused by the declining birthrate and aging population. And analysts expect the current government, whose political base rests on restricting immigration, to actively encourage humanoid deployment as the politically palatable alternative to the immigration it will not permit. Read those two facts together and the shape of the strategy emerges: a government that has closed one door (immigration) is deliberately propping open another (automation), with the full weight of its industrial-policy apparatus behind it.

This is the same pattern Part XI documented in China — the state placing its thumb firmly on the robotics scale — arrived at from the opposite direction. China is building the robots as an export industry and a bid for standard-setting dominance. Japan is buying them (largely from China, note the irony) as a demographic life-support system. The world’s two aging East Asian giants have become the twin engines of the humanoid century: one manufacturing the machines because it wants to lead the future, the other deploying them because it cannot otherwise staff the present. Between them they are dragging the entire technology forward faster than any market would have on its own — and the rest of us, Canada included, will inherit the machines and the norms they establish, whether or not we participated in the choices that shaped them.

The rich world’s rehearsal

From the balcony, here is why Haneda matters more than any dancing-robot video, and why a Canadian in particular should watch it closely.

Japan is the canary. It is the oldest society on Earth by median age, the one that hit the demographic wall first, and for that reason it has become the world’s involuntary laboratory for the question every wealthy nation is about to face: what do you do when the pyramid inverts — when the old outnumber the young, when the work that keeps a society physically functioning outstrips the bodies available to do it? Japan tried, for years, to answer with robotics of the conventional kind, and the results were mostly disappointing — the famous robot hotels that had to re-hire humans, the care-robots that gathered dust. What is different now, and what makes the Haneda trial worth marking, is that the humanoid form plus cheap Chinese manufacturing plus improving AI has finally produced a machine that can at least attempt the human-shaped work at a price that makes attempting it rational.

Every advanced economy is on the same curve Japan is on, merely further back. South Korea and Italy are close behind; China itself — the very country manufacturing these robots — is aging faster than almost anywhere, which is one reason Beijing’s deployment order in Part XI reads as urgently as it does. And Canada, for all that our immigration strategy has bought us time, is not exempt from the underlying arithmetic; we have merely chosen a different first answer. The Haneda tarmac is a preview of a negotiation that every rich society will conduct in the coming decades, between three options: import the workers, birth the workers, or build the workers. Most will end up doing some of all three, in proportions set by politics as much as economics. Japan, having largely foreclosed the first two, is showing the rest of us what the third looks like in practice — cautious, supervised, battery-limited, and utterly determined, because the alternative is an airport that cannot staff itself and a nation that cannot care for its old.

The robot at Haneda waves at the ground crew. It is a friendly gesture, programmed in, a bit of public-relations warmth. But watch it long enough and the wave takes on a different meaning — less a greeting than a changing of the guard, conducted in slow motion, over three patient years, on the busiest tarmac in the world, because the arithmetic of the aging nation left no other door to walk through.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one was mailed from the far end of a curve we are all already on.
