Part of the series: Israel/Palestine: The Original Sin and Its Compound Interest

By Stephen Gordon | CanadianSnowmanSays

Iran’s ‘Axis of Resistance’ — the proxy network linking Tehran, Hezbollah in Lebanon, Hamas in Gaza, and others. Perpetual conflict serves strategic interests on multiple sides.

There is a question that Western discourse on this conflict almost never asks honestly, because the honest answer is uncomfortable for the narrative of external evil that both Israel and its Western allies find politically convenient.

The question is: who created Hamas?

The answer is not a conspiracy theory. It is documented history, available in Israeli and American archives, reported by the Wall Street Journal in 2009, and acknowledged by figures including former Israeli military and intelligence officials. Hamas — the Islamic Resistance Movement — grew in the late 1980s from the Palestinian branch of the Muslim Brotherhood, which Israel had deliberately cultivated in Gaza during the 1970s and 1980s as a counterweight to the PLO.

Brigadier General Yitzhak Segev, the Israeli military governor of Gaza in the early 1980s, has stated in interviews that he helped finance the Muslim Brotherhood in Gaza specifically to undermine Arafat and the PLO. “The Israeli government gave me a budget,” he told a journalist, “and the military government gives to the mosques.” The logic was straightforward: religious organizations focused on social services and Islamic observance were seen as less politically threatening than the secular nationalist PLO. Dividing the Palestinian political movement served Israeli interests.

It was one of the most consequential miscalculations in the history of the occupation. The organization Israel helped create and fund eventually became the organization that conducted the worst attack on Jewish civilians since the Holocaust.

This is mentioned not to absolve Hamas of responsibility for its own choices — responsibility is not a zero-sum commodity, and the fact that Israel made a strategic error does not diminish Hamas’s moral culpability for the atrocities it has committed. It is mentioned because the story of Hamas is the story of the entire conflict in miniature: powerful actors making strategic calculations about weaker actors, without adequate understanding of or concern for the consequences, and the consequences being paid by ordinary people who had no part in the calculation.

Hamas: Origins, Ideology, and Political Survival

Sheikh Ahmed Yassin, founder of Hamas, in the late 1980s. A quadriplegic cleric who built a Muslim Brotherhood social services network in Gaza before formalizing Hamas during the First Intifada

Hamas was formally founded in 1987, at the beginning of the First Intifada, by Sheikh Ahmed Yassin, a quadriplegic Gaza cleric who had been running a Muslim Brotherhood social services network in Gaza for years. Its founding charter, issued in 1988, was a document of explicit antisemitism — invoking the Protocols of the Elders of Zion, calling for the destruction of Israel, framing the conflict in explicitly religious rather than national terms. It was, by any honest reading, a document that placed Hamas outside the bounds of any legitimate political process.

Hamas revised its founding document in 2017, removing the most explicit antisemitic references and reframing the conflict as one against Zionism rather than against Jews. The revision was welcomed by some analysts as evidence of political maturation and dismissed by others as cosmetic. The operational ideology of Hamas — armed resistance to Israeli occupation, refusal to recognize Israel’s right to exist, commitment to the liberation of all of historical Palestine — was unchanged by the 2017 document.

Hamas’s political survival depends on several factors that are worth examining clearly.

The first is the provision of social services. Hamas built and maintains a network of hospitals, schools, charitable organizations, and social welfare institutions in Gaza that has filled the gap left by the Palestinian Authority’s corruption and inadequacy and by the humanitarian consequences of the Israeli blockade. For ordinary Gazans, Hamas is not only or even primarily a military organization. It is the provider of the services that keep their families alive. This is the foundation of its popular support and the reason that eliminating Hamas militarily — as Israel has stated as its objective in the current war — cannot be accomplished by bombing buildings. You cannot bomb an idea that is also a hospital.

Gazans receiving aid amid the blockade. Since 2007, restrictions on goods, movement, and materials have made the population dependent on humanitarian assistance and local providers like Hamas

The second factor is the blockade. Israel imposed a comprehensive blockade on Gaza in 2007, after Hamas won the Palestinian legislative elections in 2006 and then seized control of Gaza in 2007, removing Fatah by force. The blockade has restricted the movement of goods, people, building materials, and economic activity for seventeen years. The UN has repeatedly warned that the blockade has made Gaza’s economy unviable and its population dependent on humanitarian aid for survival. A population that has been blockaded, impoverished, and periodically bombed for seventeen years is a population that is susceptible to the political message of an organization that tells them the violence is the occupier’s fault and the only response is armed resistance. This is not a justification of Hamas’s ideology. It is an explanation of its persistence that Israel’s military operations have consistently failed to account for.

The third factor is Iranian support. Iran provides Hamas with funding, weapons, and training as part of its broader “Axis of Resistance” strategy — the use of proxy forces across the region to project Iranian power without direct Iranian military engagement. Iran’s support for Hamas is not primarily motivated by concern for Palestinian welfare. It is motivated by Iran’s strategic interest in maintaining pressure on Israel, by the domestic political utility of the Palestinian cause for the Islamic Republic’s revolutionary identity, and by the desire to prevent any normalization of relations between Israel and Arab states that would isolate Iran regionally. Hamas’s relationship with Iran is transactional and has not always been smooth — Hamas’s Sunni ideology and Iran’s Shia theocracy create genuine tensions — but it has been sustained because both parties find it useful.

Hezbollah: The Longer War

Hezbollah — the Party of God — is a Lebanese Shia political and military organization founded in 1982 in direct response to Israel’s invasion of Lebanon that year. Understanding Hezbollah requires understanding the 1982 invasion, because the organization is literally the creation of that invasion in a way that has no parallel in Hamas’s history.

Palestinian civilians after the 1982 Sabra and Shatila massacre. The events, during Israel’s invasion of Lebanon, helped galvanize the formation of Hezbollah as a resistance force

Israel invaded Lebanon in June 1982 with the stated objective of destroying the PLO’s infrastructure in southern Lebanon, from which Palestinian guerrillas had been launching attacks on northern Israel. The invasion was planned and executed by Defence Minister Ariel Sharon, who had considerably more expansive objectives than the cabinet had been told about. Israeli forces advanced not just to the Litani River — the stated boundary of the operation — but all the way to Beirut, which they besieged for months.

The siege of Beirut, the bombardment of civilian areas, and the subsequent massacre of Palestinian civilians at the Sabra and Shatila refugee camps — carried out by Lebanese Christian Phalangist militias while Israeli forces controlled the surrounding area and lit flares to illuminate the camps at night — produced an international outcry that even Israel’s most committed supporters found difficult to defend. The Israeli Kahan Commission subsequently found that Sharon bore “personal responsibility” for the massacre and he was removed as Defence Minister.

Into the vacuum of southern Lebanon, devastated by the Israeli occupation that lasted until 2000, Iran and Syria inserted the nucleus of what became Hezbollah — initially a collection of Shia clerics and militias that coalesced into an organized force with Iranian Revolutionary Guard training, Iranian funding, and a political theology derived from Ayatollah Khomeini’s concept of Islamic governance.

Hezbollah grew from a resistance movement into a state within a state. It provides social services — hospitals, schools, infrastructure — to the Shia community of southern Lebanon in the same way that Hamas provides them in Gaza. It has a political wing that participates in Lebanese elections and has held cabinet positions in Lebanese governments. It has a military wing that is, by most assessments, better equipped and better trained than the Lebanese national army — possessing an estimated 150,000 rockets and missiles, tunnel networks, and battlefield experience from its involvement in the Syrian civil war on Assad’s behalf.

The 2006 war between Israel and Hezbollah — triggered by Hezbollah’s cross-border raid that killed eight Israeli soldiers and captured two — lasted 34 days and ended in a military outcome that neither side could claim as victory. Israel failed to destroy Hezbollah’s military capacity. Hezbollah demonstrated an ability to sustain rocket fire into northern Israel throughout the war and to inflict significant casualties on Israeli ground forces. UN Security Council Resolution 1701 established a ceasefire and an expanded UNIFIL peacekeeping force in southern Lebanon. Hezbollah subsequently rebuilt its weapons stockpiles to levels far exceeding those it held before the war.

The 2024 campaign against Hezbollah — which followed October 7th and the opening of a “support front” by Hezbollah in solidarity with Gaza — has been more militarily successful for Israel than 2006. The assassination of Hezbollah Secretary-General Hassan Nasrallah in September 2024, along with much of the organization’s senior military leadership, degraded Hezbollah’s command structure significantly. The pager and walkie-talkie attacks that preceded the ground campaign were among the most audacious intelligence operations in the history of the conflict. Israel inflicted serious damage on Hezbollah’s infrastructure in southern Lebanon.

Hassan Nasrallah, longtime Hezbollah Secretary-General (killed in 2024). Hezbollah evolved from a militia into a political and military power with Iranian backing.

Whether this damage is permanent or temporary depends entirely on whether the political conditions that created Hezbollah change — whether Iran’s support is severed, whether Lebanon develops a functional state capable of governing its own territory, whether the underlying grievances of the Shia south are addressed by something other than an armed organization offering resistance as its primary political product. None of those conditions is close to being met. The history of both Hamas and Hezbollah is a history of organizations that have survived Israeli military campaigns by being rooted in communities that continue to exist after the bombing stops.

The Resistance Economy: Who Benefits from Permanent Conflict

The most uncomfortable question in this entire series is one that this instalment must ask directly: who benefits from the perpetuation of the conflict?

The answer, on the Israeli side, is examined in Part XII — the political economy of the occupation, the defence industry, the American weapons contracts, the Netanyahu political coalition whose survival depends on the absence of a peace process.

On the Palestinian and regional side, the answer is equally uncomfortable.

Hamas’s political legitimacy depends entirely on armed resistance. A negotiated two-state solution that delivered a Palestinian state would render Hamas ideologically obsolete — its raison d’être is the liberation of all of historical Palestine through armed struggle, not the administration of a West Bank canton. Peace would destroy Hamas as a political force. This is not a secondary consideration. It is the central fact of Hamas’s political existence, and it explains why Hamas has historically acted — through suicide bombing campaigns at Oslo’s critical moments, through the October 7th attack — in ways that predictably derail peace processes and provoke Israeli military responses that radicalize the Palestinian population further.

Hamas is not suicidal. It is strategic. It calculates, correctly, that Israeli military responses to its provocations will produce Palestinian civilian casualties that generate international sympathy, radicalize the next generation of fighters, and prevent the kind of normalization between Israel and Arab states that would marginalize the Palestinian cause regionally. The October 7th attack was planned with full knowledge of the Israeli response it would trigger. The leaders who planned it are not in Gaza absorbing that response. They are in Qatar.

Iran’s interest in perpetual conflict is structural. The Islamic Republic’s revolutionary identity — its claim to lead a resistance against American imperialism and Zionist oppression — depends on the conflict continuing. A resolved Israel-Palestine conflict would remove the most powerful mobilizing tool in Iranian regional politics. Iran does not want a Palestinian state. It wants a Palestinian struggle, because the struggle serves Iranian interests in a way that a state would not.

Hezbollah’s interest is similarly structural. Its weapons, its Iranian funding, its political dominance in southern Lebanon — all depend on the continuing justification of armed resistance to Israeli occupation. A genuine peace would require Hezbollah to disarm, integrate into the Lebanese state, and compete in normal politics with its weapons surrendered. This is not an outcome Hezbollah’s leadership is prepared to accept.

The Arab states that have historically used the Palestinian cause as a domestic political mobilizing tool — deflecting their own populations’ grievances toward an external enemy — have their own interest in perpetual unresolved conflict. This interest has declined as Arab governments have concluded that their own survival depends more on economic modernization and American security guarantees than on Arab nationalist solidarity. The Abraham Accords — the normalization agreements between Israel and the UAE, Bahrain, Sudan, and Morocco — represent the Arab state withdrawal from this political position. But the populations of those states still feel the Palestinian cause viscerally, and the governments that normalized with Israel have paid a domestic political price for doing so.

The resistance economy is real. It sustains organizations, careers, funding streams, and political identities. On both sides of the conflict there are actors whose material and political interests are served by its continuation. This does not mean the conflict is manufactured or that the suffering it produces is not genuine. It means that among the many obstacles to a political solution are actors who are, in the most precise sense, invested in the problem rather than the solution.

Naming that honestly is not cynicism. It is the prerequisite for understanding why every attempted solution has failed.

What Destroying Hamas Actually Means

Children in Gaza amid ongoing conflict. Military operations can degrade armed groups but cannot eliminate the social and political conditions — occupation, blockade, dispossession — that sustain the ‘resistance economy.

Israel has stated repeatedly since October 7th that its military objective is the destruction of Hamas. The statement is politically necessary — no Israeli government could publicly accept a lesser objective after the scale of October 7th — and militarily meaningless.

Hamas is not a building. It is not a tunnel. It is not a weapons stockpile, though it has all of these. Hamas is an idea embedded in a community — the idea that armed resistance to occupation is not only legitimate but obligatory, that negotiation with Israel has been tried and failed, and that the only language the occupier understands is force. That idea was present in Gaza before Hamas was founded. It will be present in Gaza after whatever military operation Israel conducts is completed. It will be more present, held by more people with more intensity, than it was before the operation began, because the operation will have killed tens of thousands of people and destroyed the homes, hospitals, schools, and infrastructure of the survivors.

Military operations can kill Hamas commanders. They can destroy Hamas tunnels. They can degrade Hamas’s weapons stockpiles. They cannot destroy the political and social conditions that produce organizations like Hamas, because those conditions — occupation, blockade, impoverishment, dispossession — are not military targets. They are political ones. And no military operation has a political solution attached to it.

This is the lesson that every serious military analyst, including many Israeli ones, has been articulating since October 7th. It is the lesson of every Israeli military campaign against Gaza since 2008. It is the lesson of the American wars in Afghanistan and Iraq. Military force can suppress. It cannot resolve. Resolution requires political change. And political change requires the will to change the conditions that produce the violence — the occupation, the blockade, the settlements, the dispossession — that no Israeli government in the current political environment is prepared to address.

The resistance economy will continue as long as the conditions that sustain it continue. Those conditions will continue as long as the settlement enterprise continues, the occupation continues, the blockade continues, and the international community continues to condemn rather than act.

Which brings us to the specific catastrophe that began on October 7, 2023.
