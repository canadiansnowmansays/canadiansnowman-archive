Four minutes. $6–13 a month. This is the vote you cast every month — for or against what they’ve done to CBS News.

I have spent four essays describing what has happened to CBS News with the measured language of a journalist trying to be fair to the complexity of the situation. I have explained the mechanisms, traced the timeline, named the actors, and acknowledged the structural forces that make resistance difficult.

I am done being measured.

What has happened to CBS News is not complex. It is straightforward. A billionaire bought a news institution, paid off the sitting President to get his acquisition approved, installed a politically aligned editor to purge the people who objected, and is now running what one veteran television news executive has called “state-run media for Trump” from the building where Edward R. Murrow once stood up to Joseph McCarthy.

And most of us are doing nothing about it.

This essay is a direct request that you do something. Something specific, something immediate, and something that demonstrably works — because we have proof it works.

We Know Exactly What to Do

September 2025. Disney suspended Jimmy Kimmel. Within days, 1.7 million subscriptions were cancelled. Disney folded in five days. The playbook works.

In September 2025, Disney suspended Jimmy Kimmel. The public responded within forty-eight hours: hundreds of protesters outside Disney’s studios, a celebrity boycott, and — most consequentially — 1.7 million streaming subscription cancellations in seven days across Disney+, Hulu, and ESPN. Cancellation rates on Disney+ doubled. On Hulu, they doubled. The financial pain was immediate, measurable, and acute. Disney reinstated Kimmel in five days.

The playbook is not mysterious. It requires only that enough people decide that their monthly subscription fee is not going to a company that is dismantling press freedom on behalf of a friendly administration.

Paramount+ costs between six and thirteen dollars a month, depending on your plan. The Paramount family of services — Paramount+, Showtime, CBS streaming — represents the direct consumer revenue stream of the same company that:

Paid Donald Trump $16 million to make a lawsuit go away, with the money going to his presidential library, in order to secure FCC approval of an $8 billion merger.

Hired an opinion journalist with no broadcast experience to run CBS News and report directly to the owner, bypassing the editorial chain of command entirely.

Cancelled its highest-rated late-night show within days of its host calling that payment what it was.

Fired the executive producer of 60 Minutes when he refused to bend to political pressure.

Fired the president of CBS News when she backed him.

Fired five senior 60 Minutes journalists and producers in a single week.

Shut down a ninety-nine-year-old radio news service.

Fired Scott Pelley — thirty-seven years at CBS, decorated beyond any comparable figure in American broadcast journalism — for standing up in a room and saying what every honest observer already knew.

This is what your subscription is funding. Not Tulsa King. Not Yellowstone reruns. The infrastructure of a news operation that has been systematically converted from an instrument of public accountability into an instrument of political accommodation.

$8 billion merger. $16 million settlement to the President. A politically aligned editor installed. This is what your Paramount+ subscription now funds.

The Objection You Are Already Forming

You are thinking: but I watch CBS shows. I like Survivor. The news division’s problems shouldn’t cost me my entertainment.

I hear it. It is a reasonable instinct. And it is precisely the instinct that corporate media consolidators are counting on.

The Disney precedent is instructive here too. Millions of Disney subscribers who cancelled in September 2025 loved Disney content. They were not indifferent to losing Bluey or Marvel or ESPN. They cancelled anyway, because they understood that a streaming subscription is not a passive act — it is a vote cast with a credit card, once a month, for the company that holds it.

When you subscribe to Paramount+, you are voting — monthly, automatically, often without thinking about it — for the proposition that what Paramount has done to CBS News is acceptable. That the settlement was reasonable. That the Weiss appointment was sound. That Pelley deserved to be fired. That Murrow’s institution deserved to become what it has become.

You may not intend that vote. But you are casting it.

What Cancellation Actually Does

Streaming cancellation is not a gesture. It is an economic signal with a clear transmission mechanism.

Paramount Global is a publicly traded company. Its streaming subscriber numbers are reported quarterly and scrutinised by analysts, investors, and the board of directors. A significant cancellation event — the kind that registers in the data — creates financial pressure that corporate leadership cannot ignore and cannot explain away as “challenging media economics.” It forces a conversation in boardrooms and shareholder calls that no op-ed, no protest outside a building, no social media campaign can force.

Disney felt 1.7 million cancellations in a week and reversed course in five days. Paramount’s streaming base is smaller than Disney’s, which means a proportionally equivalent response requires fewer people. The mathematics of this are actually encouraging.

Cancel Paramount+. Cancel Showtime if you subscribe to it separately. If you have a bundle that includes CBS content, cancel the bundle. When the service asks why — and it will ask — tell it. The cancellation surveys matter. “Political concerns” or “company values” as a stated reason registers differently in the data than passive churn.

Then, if you are so inclined, tell someone. Post it. The Kimmel cancellation wave spread partly because people were posting screenshots of their cancellation confirmations. Visibility amplifies the signal.

Beyond the Subscription

Cancellation is the most direct action, but it is not the only one.

CBS News’s revenue depends on advertising. Advertisers respond to audience pressure. If you are moved to write to the companies that advertise on CBS Evening News — now anchored by Tony Dokoupil, whose ratings have hit historic lows precisely because the audience the Weiss era was supposed to attract has instead departed — and explain why you are not watching, that is a meaningful act. Advertisers left Tucker Carlson’s Fox News programme not because a regulator demanded it but because enough viewers made clear that their association with the programme was costing them something.

CBS affiliates — local television stations that carry CBS News programming — are themselves subject to FCC licence renewal. Several are owned by companies with their own regulatory dependencies and their own sensitivity to public pressure. They are not passive recipients of whatever CBS News sends them. They have relationships with their local communities that national ownership does not.

And there is the most traditional form of accountability: contact your elected representatives. The FCC’s approval of the Paramount-Skydance merger, conditioned on an “unprecedented promise” to “root out bias” — which in context meant bias against the government — was flagged by a Democratic FCC commissioner as “a dangerous precedent for the First Amendment.” That commissioner was right, and she called for a full commission vote rather than a staff-level approval. Democratic senators have raised the same concern. If you believe that a federal regulator conditioning a merger approval on editorial changes at a news division is an assault on press freedom, your senator’s office is a relevant destination for that view.

The Murrow Standard, One More Time

Edward R. Murrow at CBS, 1954 — the standard this building once set. Today that same building broadcasts something very different.

Edward R. Murrow was not a comfortable figure. He was not universally beloved in his time. William Paley, the founder of CBS, ultimately sided with the entertainment executives who wanted Murrow’s confrontational journalism moved to less prominent timeslots — and won. Murrow left CBS in 1961 to become director of the United States Information Agency under President Kennedy, a position he described as a form of honourable exile.

But before he left, in 1958, he gave a speech to the Radio Television News Directors Association that has been quoted ever since because it describes, with uncomfortable precision, the choice that every generation of broadcasters faces.

“This instrument can teach, it can illuminate; yes, and it can even inspire,” he said of television. “But it can do so only to the extent that humans are determined to use it to those ends. Otherwise it is merely wires and lights in a box.”

The instrument is now being used for other ends. The humans who are determined to use it for what it was built for — Owens, McMahon, Pelley, Alfonsi, Vega, the twenty-six union members who lost their jobs when CBS News Radio went dark — have been removed or silenced.

What remains is a box.

The question is whether those of us on the receiving end of that box decide to keep paying for it.

A Direct Ask

If you have read this far, you already know what the right answer is.

Cancel your Paramount+ subscription today. Not next month. Not after the current season of whatever you are watching ends. Today. It takes four minutes. The instructions are straightforward. The financial signal is immediate.

If you do not subscribe to Paramount+ yourself, ask someone who does. Share this. Explain what has happened at CBS. Most people — genuinely — do not know. The story has been covered in the places where people who follow media closely tend to read. It has not yet broken through to the broader public that watches the network casually and has not been tracking the sequence of events across thirteen months.

The audience that Walter Cronkite built was the most trusted in American television because it was composed of people who took seriously their responsibility, as citizens of a democracy, to demand serious journalism. That audience is still out there. It has not gone anywhere. It has simply not yet been asked, clearly and directly, to do what it did when Murrow faced down McCarthy in 1954 — to make its values legible to the institution that claims to represent them.

Make them legible.

Cancel.
