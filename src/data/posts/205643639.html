On the morning of June 5, 1989, a man carrying shopping bags walked out into Chang’an Avenue in Beijing and stood in front of a column of Type 59 tanks moving away from Tiananmen Square.

He stood there for thirty-seven seconds. The lead tank tried to go around him. He moved to block it. The tank tried the other side. He moved again. The tank stopped. The man climbed onto the tank, appeared to speak to someone inside, climbed down, and was pulled away by people in the crowd. He has never been identified. The Chinese government, when asked, has said variously that he was never found, that he was a student who was never arrested, and that he was a counterrevolutionary who was brought to justice. None of these accounts is verifiable. The photograph, taken by four Western photographers from the Beijing Hotel, is one of the most reproduced images in the history of photography.

The Chinese government has never officially acknowledged the image exists.

This is the essential fact about Tiananmen: not the tanks, not the death toll, not the political significance — all of which are contested and enormous — but the image itself and the government’s relationship to it. Every Chinese citizen under forty has grown up in a country where searching for “Tank Man” returns nothing, where the events of June 3rd to 4th are not in the school curriculum, where raising them in certain contexts carries genuine risk. The image is globally iconic and domestically invisible. That gap — between what the world knows and what Chinese citizens are permitted to know — is the Cultural Revolution’s most durable institutional inheritance, applied to a single event and maintained for thirty-five years.

The protests that led to the tanks began not as a democracy movement but as a mourning gathering.

Hu Yaobang, the General Secretary who had been forced to resign in January 1987 for being too sympathetic to student demonstrators and too enthusiastic about political reform, died of a heart attack on April 15, 1989. Students at Beijing universities began gathering in Tiananmen Square to pay tribute. The gatherings grew. Students from other universities joined. Workers began to appear alongside them. Journalists, initially restricted by the government, found ways to cover it. By late April, the square held hundreds of thousands of people on its largest days.

The demands were not, initially, revolutionary. Students called for dialogue with the government, for a reassessment of Hu Yaobang’s legacy, for press freedom, for transparency about official corruption, and for greater accountability in the reform process. These were demands that a different government might have accommodated — that Zhao Ziyang, the reform-minded General Secretary who favoured negotiation, was actively trying to accommodate. They were not demands for the end of Communist Party rule. They were demands for a different kind of Communist Party rule: more responsive, less corrupt, more willing to account for itself to the people it governed.

The economic context in which the protests occurred matters more than Western accounts typically acknowledge. The decade of reform since 1978 had produced extraordinary growth — but it had also produced inflation that was running at roughly 28 percent annually by 1988, eroding the real wages of urban workers who had not shared in the prosperity as visibly as the new class of entrepreneurs and officials who had used their access to allocate resources in a partially marketised system. The word most commonly used in Chinese for the corruption of this period — guandao, official speculation — captured a specific phenomenon: party and government officials using their control over the allocation of licences, contracts, and scarce goods to extract personal benefit from the gap between planned and market prices. The students were protesting political repression. The workers who joined them were also protesting an economy in which reform had benefited the politically connected at the expense of the ordinary. The combination made the movement broader and more threatening than either a pure democracy movement or a pure labour movement would have been alone.

The composition of the movement complicated the government’s ability to dismiss it as youthful naivety. By mid-May, the protests had drawn not just students but workers, factory employees, and ordinary Beijing residents who brought food and water to the hunger strikers in the square and formed human chains to prevent police from moving against them. A poll conducted by a Beijing university sociologist in mid-May found that more than seventy percent of Beijing residents supported the students’ goals. The movement had ceased to be a campus protest and had become something closer to a broad urban uprising — which is precisely what made it existentially threatening to Deng in a way that student protests alone might not have been.

The hunger strike that began on May 13th — two days before Gorbachev’s arrival — brought the crisis to a new intensity. Hundreds of students refused food to dramatise their commitment to the movement’s demands, requiring medical attention and generating images of young people on stretchers that circulated worldwide and aroused public sympathy across China’s cities. The party faced the extraordinary optics of having to deploy soldiers against a population that was visibly trying to save the hunger strikers’ lives. The emotional weight of the images — young people who had given themselves into physical danger in service of an idea — gave the movement a moral authority that political argument alone could not have produced.

The timing added pressure the leadership found intolerable. Mikhail Gorbachev arrived in Beijing on May 15 for the first Sino-Soviet summit in thirty years, and the square was so full of protesters that the planned welcoming ceremony at the Gate of Heavenly Peace had to be cancelled. Gorbachev’s own association with glasnost and political openness — the policies that Deng had concluded were destroying the Soviet Union — made his presence alongside a pro-democracy movement an image that Beijing desperately did not want circulating internationally. Deng was watching the Soviet bloc fracture in real time, Poland’s partially free elections already completed, Hungary’s border with Austria already open. He had decided that the fatal error was political liberalisation ahead of economic consolidation. He was not going to make it.

An April 26 editorial in People’s Daily, written under Deng’s direction, characterised the protests as “turmoil” and accused the students of attempting to “negate the leadership of the Communist Party.” The editorial was a red line. By labelling the protests counterrevolutionary before any negotiation had been seriously attempted, Deng made retreat from confrontation politically impossible without an admission of error that the system could not produce. The students, officially declared enemies of the state, had no option but to escalate or disperse. Many escalated.

The Goddess of Democracy appeared in the square on May 30th, four days before the crackdown.

Art students from the Central Academy of Fine Arts had constructed a ten-metre styrofoam and papier-mâché statue and carried it into the square in sections, assembling it overnight. The finished figure faced north — facing Mao’s portrait on the Gate of Heavenly Peace across the full width of the square. It did not look like the Statue of Liberty, as some Western accounts suggested. It looked like a Chinese figure, straining forward, arms raised, holding a torch with both hands in a posture the students described as representing the Chinese people’s aspiration toward democracy. The government described it as a provocation. The statue stood for five days before armoured vehicles destroyed it during the clearing of the square.

Zhao Ziyang made one last visit to the square on May 19th, walking among the hunger strikers, visibly weeping, telling them: “We have come too late.” He was removed from all his positions that day. He spent the remaining fifteen years of his life under house arrest in Beijing, never formally charged, never tried, forbidden from speaking publicly. His memoirs, recorded secretly on audio cassettes and smuggled out of China after his death, were published in 2009 under the title Prisoner of the State. They describe a man who understood precisely what was happening and was powerless to prevent it. The book has never been published in mainland China.

Martial law was declared on May 20. Armoured columns moved into Beijing from multiple directions beginning around 10pm on June 3rd. They encountered resistance throughout the city — citizens who built barricades, overturned vehicles, and physically blocked troops from advancing. The violence in the streets surrounding the square preceded the clearing of the square itself and may have been more lethal than what happened within it. The students in the square were given a choice when the armoured vehicles finally arrived: leave through a southern corridor or face the consequences of remaining. Most left. Some refused and were beaten or arrested. The Goddess of Democracy was knocked down by armoured vehicles. The square was cleared before dawn.

The death toll has never been officially disclosed. The Chinese Red Cross initially reported 2,600 deaths before retracting the figure under government pressure. Official Chinese statements have put the toll at 200 to 300, including soldiers. A 2017 document from the British Embassy in Beijing, declassified and published, estimated at least 10,000 deaths — a figure most historians regard as too high. The most widely cited scholarly estimates fall between several hundred and approximately 1,000 deaths, with significant uncertainty at both ends. The range is that wide not because the evidence is ambiguous but because the government controls access to it and has blocked every independent attempt to establish the truth.

What is not in dispute: unarmed civilians were killed by soldiers of the People’s Liberation Army acting on orders from the civilian leadership of the Communist Party, in the capital city, to suppress a movement that had not taken up arms and was not, at the moment of the clearing, posing any physical threat to public order. That sentence is the full summary of June 4th. Everything else is detail, contested at the margins by a government that has decided the detail should not exist.

The aftermath sorted itself into two tracks running simultaneously.

Internationally: sanctions, suspended high-level diplomatic contacts, World Bank loans frozen, arms sales cancelled. The isolation was real and damaging, coming precisely when China needed foreign investment and technology to sustain the economic reform programme that Deng had staked his legacy on.

Domestically: Zhao Ziyang was replaced by Jiang Zemin, the Shanghai party chief who had suppressed local protests without violence and was judged reliably conservative on political questions while acceptable on economic reform. Senior officials who had supported the protests or opposed martial law were removed from their positions. Student leaders were arrested — some sentenced to years in prison, others fleeing through Hong Kong via the “Yellow Bird” network organised by Hong Kong democracy activists who understood, correctly, that the “one country, two systems” framework gave them room to act that did not exist on the mainland. Ideological controls were tightened in universities. Surveillance of dissidents was intensified and systematised.

And then Deng made his southern tour in January 1992, and the economic opening resumed, and the world returned for the business opportunity, and the bargain was sealed in the most consequential way possible — not with a formal agreement but with a demonstrated willingness to proceed as if June 4th had not established a principle. It had established one. Deng had shown that the party would use lethal force to protect its monopoly on power. The international community had shown that it would absorb this information and continue trading. Both sides had learned something durable.

There is a coda to Tiananmen that is itself now history.

For thirty years after June 4th, Hong Kong held an annual candlelight vigil in Victoria Park — the largest annual commemoration of the massacre anywhere in the world, attended by tens of thousands of people each year. The vigil was possible because Hong Kong, under the “one country, two systems” framework Deng had negotiated with Britain in 1984, retained freedoms of assembly and expression that did not exist on the mainland. Every June 4th, the candles burned in Victoria Park while across the border the date passed in enforced silence and enforced forgetting.

In 2020, citing COVID restrictions, the Hong Kong government banned the vigil for the first time in thirty years. In 2021, the organising body — the Hong Kong Alliance in Support of Patriotic Democratic Movements of China — was forced to disband under the National Security Law imposed by Beijing in June 2020, and its leaders were arrested. Victoria Park’s June 4th candles went out. The last independent institution in Chinese territory that kept the memory publicly alive was extinguished, legally, using tools that Deng’s framework had not anticipated needing.

The suppression of the vigil was not primarily about COVID or even the National Security Law. It was about memory. A government that cannot tolerate annual candlelight vigils held quietly in a park understands, with precise clarity, the political weight that memory carries. The candles were not a threat. The act of remembering was. The distance between 1989 and 2021 is not the distance between two events. It is the distance between a government that had not yet decided what to do about memory and one that had decided to end it.

The Tank Man stood in the road for thirty-seven seconds. The question he was asking — about the relationship between citizens and the state that governs them — has not been answered. It has been suppressed, which is a different thing entirely, and which the history of this series suggests has a limited shelf life.
