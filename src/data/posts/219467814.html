⚖️ Serious, with a grave turn and a long cold coda — because this is the one year in the whole series that was celebrated for no reason except that a number had been reached, and because the empire that reached it counted its own years to the day while leaving its dead as a range. No wink. The dog sits this one out.

I count my essays, and most of the years I land on earned their fame by something happening — a conquest, a charter, a departure, a last spike. Essay 1897 is different, and its difference is exactly why it belongs in this series above almost any other year I could have chosen. Because in 1897, as a matter of plain record, nothing in particular happened. There was no battle, no invention, no treaty. There was only a number — the number sixty — reached by a single human being, and an entire empire stopping the world to stare at it.

1897 was Queen Victoria’s Diamond Jubilee: sixty years on the throne. And a Diamond Jubilee is the purest specimen this series will ever find of a thing I have been circling for eighteen hundred essays — the moment when the count stops being a measure of the event and becomes the event. Nothing was commemorated in June of 1897 except duration itself. The achievement being celebrated was not having stopped.

The invention of a round number

The very word was minted for her. There was no established “Diamond Jubilee” before Victoria; sixty years was so rarely reached by a monarch that no ritual existed to mark it, and one had to be essentially invented — the “diamond” assigned to sixty the way silver is assigned to twenty-five and gold to fifty, a whole new tier of the counting ceremony created because the old ceremonies had run out of metals. This is the Numbers Game at its most naked. A society reached a figure it had no name for, and rather than let the figure pass unmarked, it manufactured a category so the number would have somewhere to live. The count did not fit the existing ledger, so they ruled a new line.

And what a pageant they staged in the new line. On June 22, 1897, troops from every corner of the empire — Canadian and Australian and Indian and African regiments, the largest military procession London had ever assembled — marched through the capital in honour of a frail woman of seventy-eight who had outlived her husband by thirty-six years and her own relevance to the governing of Britain by rather longer. Colonial premiers were summoned. The whole apparatus of the largest empire in the history of the world — roughly a quarter of the planet’s land and a quarter of its people, the empire on which, in the phrase of the age, the sun never set — arranged itself into a single enormous arrow pointing at one number.

Duration as legitimacy

Here is why they did it, and it is the oldest move in the book of power.

Legitimacy — the right to rule, the answer to the question why you and not someone else — is the hardest quantity any sovereign ever has to produce. You can claim it by conquest, but conquest invites reconquest. You can claim it by consent, but consent can be withdrawn. You can claim it by God, but God has grown quiet. And so the oldest and safest claim, the one available to any ruler who simply manages not to die, is duration. I have been here a very long time; therefore I belong here. The years themselves become the argument. Sixty of them, counted precisely, held up to a watching world, say: whatever else you might question, you cannot question that I have lasted, and lasting, in the end, is a kind of proof.

It is a brilliant trick, because it is the one claim that cannot be rebutted by anyone except time, and time was, for once, on the monarch’s side. Victoria did not need to have done anything in 1897. The number had done it for her. The Diamond Jubilee is the moment the British Empire, at the absolute zenith of its reach, chose to justify itself not by what it was doing — which was, as we are about to see, a great deal that would not survive honest accounting — but by how long it had been doing it. Duration as legitimacy. Count the years, and let the years stand in for the reckoning.

The grave turn: what the number left out

Because the thesis of this series is that the celebrated figure always has an uncelebrated one standing behind it, and in 1897 the uncelebrated figure is as dark as any the series has reached.

At the very moment the imperial regiments marched through London counting Victoria’s sixty years to the day, her Indian subjects were dying of famine in numbers no one troubled to count to the day, or to the hundred thousand, or at all. The famine of 1896–97 was killing people across British India through the Jubilee summer, and a worse one was coming at the century’s turn, and the colonial administration — committed to free-market doctrine and the continued export of grain even from starving provinces — presided over mortality that runs, by the estimates that exist, into the millions. Into the millions, during and around the very years the empire was staging the most precise counting ritual in its history.

Set the two counts beside each other, because this series exists to set them beside each other. London counted sixty years, exactly, with a new word coined to hold the number. India counted its famine dead the way 1,857 taught us the empire always counted its victims — as a range, a shrug, an estimate, a figure no monument would carry home. The empire could number the Queen’s years to the hour and could not be bothered to number the dead its own policy helped make. That is not a coincidence of record-keeping. It is the whole moral architecture of empire in a single year: you count precisely the things that flatter you and you estimate the things that would convict you. The Jubilee counted the glory. It left the cost as a footnote in a province no one in the procession had seen.

I do not raise the famine to spoil the parade. I raise it because the parade raised itself on a claim — we have lasted, therefore we are good — and duration is not goodness, and sixty years of rule is sixty years of a ledger, and the ledger of 1897 has famine in it in letters the Jubilee was specifically designed not to read. The count was real. The number was true. And the number was a way of not looking at the other numbers. That is the most sophisticated thing a count can do, and empires have always been its most sophisticated practitioners.

The coda: the second time, and the only other time

Now the turn that makes this essay, and it took me a long time to see it, and I owe the seeing to a simple, staggering fact: in the entire thousand-year history of the English and British monarchy, only two sovereigns have ever reached sixty years on the throne. Victoria, whose Jubilee was 1897. And Elizabeth II, whose Diamond Jubilee was 2012 — one hundred and fifteen years later.

Two monarchs. The same number. The same invented ritual, dusted off and run a second time across more than a century. And here is the thing the two Jubilees say when you lay them on the same page, which no one in 1897 and few in 2012 quite stopped to do: they stand at opposite ends of the same story. Victoria counted her sixty years at the absolute summit of empire, with a quarter of the earth marching past her. Elizabeth counted her sixty years in 2012 with the empire entirely gone — dissolved into a Commonwealth of equals, the colonies independent, the map redrawn, the power those years had once measured drained almost completely away. The ritual was identical. Everything it was invented to celebrate had vanished.

And that is the deepest entry in this whole essay. The monarchy counted the years more carefully in 2012 than in 1897 precisely because the years were the only thing left to count. When you can no longer measure your power — because you no longer have any — you measure your duration instead, and you measure it with enormous care, because it is all that remains. Victoria’s number was a boast. Elizabeth’s identical number, a century and change later, was something closer to an epitaph with excellent manners: the form of legitimacy outliving the substance it was built to express, the count standing constant while everything it once counted was lost. The ritual outlived the empire it was invented to celebrate. The number stayed sixty. The world it measured turned completely over.

The Canadian spine

Let me tell you why this is my essay and not merely a British one, because it runs straight through my own life.

My country was created under Victoria — Confederation, essay 1867, happened thirty years into her reign; she signed my nation into being, chose, by some accounts, which town would be its capital. She was on the throne for nearly this entire recent run of the series: Confederation, the last spike at 1885, the Niagara current at 1895, and now her own sixty years at 1897. And Elizabeth II — the only other sovereign ever to reach that number — was my queen, Canada’s head of state, for the whole of my life until she died in 2022, her face on the coins I have carried since childhood, the second of only two sixty-year monarchs and the one under whom I actually lived.

So consider the arithmetic of a single Canadian life, mine. My country was born under one sixty-year queen and I came of age and grew old under the only other one history has ever produced. The two Diamond Jubilees, a hundred and fifteen years apart, are the two posts of the Crown’s presence in my nation’s whole existence — one at the zenith that made us, one at the sunset we grew up inside. I have lived my entire life, without ever quite noticing, between the only two sixty-year reigns there have ever been. The count was running the whole time. It was running over my own head, and over my country’s, and I am only now, at essay 1897, stopping to read the number.

The closer

So 1897 is the year nothing happened and everything was counted — the purest proof this series owns that a number alone, with no event beneath it, can stop an empire in its tracks, and that the empire will always count the flattering figure to the day and leave the damning one as a range. Duration as legitimacy; the ritual outliving its reason; the famine in the footnote under the parade. It is the most Numbers Game year of the entire century, because in 1897 the number was not the measure of the story. The number was the whole story, and the story was a way of not telling another one.

And now the count comes, at last, to the third of my three protected numbers — my country, my voice, and now my family. The next landmark is essay 1898, and it is the year Hugh Llewellyn Keenleyside was born: diplomat, public servant, a thread through half the serious essays on this masthead, and my own blood, whatever fraction of it the family rounded up into a word a child could hold. I have been carrying him toward this slot since before the series had a name. He wrote his own life in two volumes, as I have told you before, and I mean to read every page of them before I write his, and so I will take this one slowly, by hand, at my own desk, with the family’s books open — or, if time presses harder than I would like, I may build it from every record the world has kept of him and revisit it later, because his spot in the count comes only once and the count does not wait, even for family.

Victoria counted sixty years to prove she had lasted. At 1898 I count one life to prove a family did.

See you at 1898. Bring the ledger with the names in it. I am bringing ours.
