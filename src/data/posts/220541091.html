An argument worth making is worth testing against its strongest opposition. In the first two parts of this series I counted the women who left Donald Trump’s second administration and the men who, facing comparable or worse, did not. This final part gives the floor to the other side. Then it weighs what the other side has to say.

The other side has a case. It deserves better than a straw man.

The defence

When Erica L. Green of the New York Times asked the White House about the pattern for her October 14, 2026 report, spokeswoman Taylor Rogers replied with two points. First, the president picks people on a single criterion, their ability to deliver. Second, unlike Democrats, he judges by merit rather than what she called immutable characteristics.

A more substantive defence came from May Mailman, a former senior White House official in both Trump terms who now runs the Independent Women’s Law Center. Mailman pointed to the women who remain in powerful posts and argued that female conservatives in the administration are not isolated but empowered. In her account, they are valued as members of the team, and their sex has nothing to do with it.

A month later, with Wiles gone, the White House gave the same answer. Asked about the pattern by Ashley Parker of The Atlantic, the press office sent back a list of nine women the president had elevated. At the top were Wiles, the first woman to serve as chief of staff, and Leavitt, the youngest press secretary in history. Rogers again said the president judges people on merit and qualifications, not immutable characteristics. The administration’s lead evidence that it values women was a woman it had just removed.

Take the argument seriously and it has three load-bearing parts. There are still powerful women in the administration. The women who left had real problems. And a president who wanted women gone would not keep replacing some of them with other women. Each claim needs examining in turn.

Claim one: the women who remain

Mailman’s list is real, and it is not trivial.

Until November 6, 2026, Susie Wiles was the White House chief of staff, the first woman ever to hold the post, and by most accounts the most powerful person in the West Wing after the president. Linda McMahon runs Education, which the administration is trying to dismantle, so in a sense she holds the most ideologically important demolition job in government. Brooke Rollins runs Agriculture. Kelly Loeffler runs the Small Business Administration. Until November 5, Harmeet Dhillon headed the Justice Department’s civil rights division. She had been campaigning for a promotion that never came, and CBS News reported that Trump wanted her fired for sounding insufficiently bellicose about noncitizen voting in a 60 Minutes interview, as David A. Graham noted in The Atlantic. She resigned instead. Erica Schwartz was recently confirmed to lead the Centers for Disease Control.

These are not decorative appointments, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. For nearly two years a woman ran the president’s staff. That mattered. What happened to her, as we will see, matters more.

By one measure, the women who remain are also the hardest workers in the building. Over the summer of 2026, the White House ran an unofficial competition to see which cabinet members made the most trips to competitive districts and states before the midterms. Ashley Parker and Michael Scherer of The Atlantic reported on September 13, 2026 that, by their own count, Kelly Loeffler finished first, with at least 18 trips, and Brooke Rollins second, with at least 17. The women at the top of that table are still in their chairs. I am not sure that is reassuring. It may simply show what staying costs a woman in this administration: outworking the men around her, every week, with no margin for error.

Parker put the question to the women themselves. For her November 10, 2026 piece in The Atlantic, she asked McMahon, Rollins and Loeffler what they made of the departures, and why they had lasted. All three press offices answered promptly, which is not this administration’s habit. Rollins complained that every personnel change gets turned into a story about gender. Loeffler’s spokesman said the president hires on talent and merit. McMahon’s department replied that The Atlantic “cannot even define what a woman is.” Nobody answered the question. It is hard to read the replies as anything other than a demonstration of what staying requires.

But look at what the list cannot tell us. It cannot tell us what would happen to any of these women on their worst day. McMahon, Rollins and Loeffler have not, so far, faced a Signal chat, an inspector general’s report or a page of the Epstein files with their names on it. Neither had Noem, Bondi or Chavez-DeRemer until they did. A list of survivors is not evidence of equal treatment. It is evidence that these particular women have not yet been tested.

Even Wiles, it turned out, was being tested. In an October 16, 2026 piece for the Atlantic, Jonathan Chait pointed to a Politico report that Trump had been asking friends what they thought of his chief of staff. With this president, Chait noted, that question has historically been the prelude to turning on an underling. Three weeks later he turned on her.

There is also the matter of the broader numbers, which Mailman’s list does not address. A Washington Post analysis in the spring of 2026 found that only about fifteen per cent of Trump’s nominees to Senate-confirmed posts were women. That compares with just over half under Biden, and is the lowest share since George W. Bush. Half of the cabinet-level women Trump started with are now gone. The ones who remain can be named in a single breath, and that is part of the problem.

Claim two: the women who left had real problems

They did. I said so in Part I and will say it again, because a series like this loses its value the moment it starts defending the indefensible.

Kristi Noem ran the department whose agents killed two American citizens in Minneapolis. She then spent a reported $220 million of public money on advertisements starring herself. Lori Chavez-DeRemer faced an inspector general over messages to young staffers. Kimberly Guilfoyle, if the Wall Street Journal’s reporting is accurate, asked a donor to quietly pay her credit-card debt days before swearing an oath to the Senate. Bondi turned the Justice Department into an instrument of the president’s grudges, which is something I have documented across many essays.

So the defenders are right that these women gave cause. Where the defence breaks down is the next step. It treats “she had problems” as if it settled the matter, when the matter is comparative. Part II set out the men: Hegseth and the war plans, Patel and the alleged drinking and the jet, Lutnick and the island. The defenders’ argument requires those cases to be lesser. They are not lesser. Several are more serious, and some are identical in kind. Patel stands accused of the same two failings that helped finish Chavez-DeRemer: drinking on duty and personal travel at public expense.

The administration’s best defence, in other words, proves the wrong thing. It proves the women were removable. The question was always why the men were not.

Claim three: Leavitt was replaced by a woman

This is the most interesting point in the defence, and it deserves a closer look than it usually gets.

Karoline Leavitt left as press secretary at the end of August 2026. She was not pushed. As the AP reported via the Jamaica Gleaner on August 13, she wrote that she felt she could not “be the best mom my two young children deserve” while doing the job. Her daughter had been born in May. Trump announced she would become one of his top outside advisers. Her replacement was another woman, Katie Zacharia.

On its face, that is a clean counterexample to any crude theory that Trump wants women out. He kept a woman at the podium, which is the most visible job in the building after his own. As we will see, the counterexample held for exactly one week.

But consider what the podium job has meant under Trump. On August 1, 2025, in an interview on Newsmax, the president was asked about his press secretary. His answer, as Mediaite transcribed it, began: “It’s that face. It’s that brain. It’s those lips, the way they move.” He went on to compare her lips to a machine gun, and asked whether she was in the room. She was. He returned to the lips more than once in later months, including aboard Air Force One.

I raise this not to embarrass Leavitt, who did a hard job with ferocious competence and owed none of us an explanation for wanting to see her children. I raise it because it bears directly on my opening hunch, that Trump likes attractive women around him until they become a liability. The press secretary is the one senior post where the president openly treats appearance as a qualification. It was also, briefly, the one post where he replaced a woman with a woman. Those two facts may not be unrelated.

There is a small, sad coda. At Zacharia’s first briefing on October 13, 2026, the last question came from a reporter asking how she would balance her family and her work. As Hawaii News Now reported, she thanked the questioner and ended the briefing. No one, so far as I am aware, has asked Hegseth, Patel or Lutnick that question at a podium. It is not the sort of question the men are asked.

Three days later, on October 16, 2026, Zacharia was gone. As People reported, citing the New York Times and Fox News, she was out after seven days in the job. When Trump named her on October 9, he praised her “unblemished record of support.” The blemishes, as it turned out, were two. At her first briefing she confused her husband with the president, and she had described herself as a lawyer despite apparently not being licensed to practise, though she does hold a law degree. The woman who announced Guilfoyle’s exit on Tuesday had made her own by Friday.

In fairness, a press secretary who cannot get through a briefing is not doing the job, and the White House was entitled to replace her. But consider the conditions. Asked by Sean Hannity, of all people, to account for the debut, she said she had been on the job “less than maybe eight hours” with a briefing book handed to her that morning, as Jonathan Chait recounted in the Atlantic on October 16, 2026. Asked which state’s bar she had passed, she answered, “When I moved to China.” She was sent out unready, and then blamed for being unready. Now set her offences against the ledger in Part II. A shaky debut and an inflated résumé line cost Zacharia her post in a week. A war plan shared in a group chat has not cost Hegseth his in more than a year and a half.

The first conditional woman

There is one more woman in this story, and in a sense she came before all of the others: the president’s daughter.

Ivanka Trump was a senior adviser throughout her father’s first term, with an office in the West Wing and a seat at summits. Her father’s public remarks about her had long been strange in exactly the way this series has been describing. In 2006, on ABC’s The View, he said that if she were not his daughter he might be dating her. As Snopes confirmed, the line was real: “If Ivanka weren’t my daughter, perhaps I’d be dating her.” He went on to comment on her appearance for years afterward.

Then, in June 2022, she became a liability. The House committee investigating January 6, 2021 played her recorded deposition. In it she said she had accepted Attorney General Bill Barr’s finding that there was no election fraud large enough to change the result. That contradicted the story her father was still telling. The next day, on June 10, 2022, Trump posted that his daughter had not been involved in studying the results and had long since “checked out,” as WISH-TV reported, carrying CNN’s account.

Hold that next to the president’s remarks on October 14, 2026, that Kimberly Guilfoyle was not family and had merely dated his son a long time ago. The method is identical. When a woman close to him says or does something that costs him, she is moved, in a single sentence, to the edge of his life. If his own daughter got the treatment, no woman in his administration should have expected anything else.

Ivanka has stayed out of the second administration, saying publicly that she preferred family to politics. It is hard to blame her. She learned the terms before any of the others did.

The chief of staff

The test this essay said the remaining women had not yet faced came for Susie Wiles on November 6, 2026.

Three days after Republicans lost the House and the Senate, Trump announced on Truth Social that Wiles would move to his political operation and that Stephen Miller would become chief of staff on November 9. “I wanted to make a change,” he told reporters as he left for Florida. “Sometimes in life you have to make moves.” Asked what he wanted Miller to do differently, he said, “Just run things well.”

The reporting behind the announcement, by Maggie Haberman, Jonathan Swan and Tyler Pager for The New York Times, describes a familiar sequence. On election night Trump blamed Wiles for the defeat in front of others at the East Room watch party. For months before that he had berated her in front of staff over leaks and the stalled prosecutions of his political enemies, and in front of several advisers he accused her of being one of the leakers. He had begun cutting her out of hiring decisions, including the choice of Karoline Leavitt’s replacement. Her detractors inside and outside the administration had spread stories about her daughter’s lobbying work. On the morning of her removal, Calley Means, a former Trump health official, went on Joe Rogan’s podcast to accuse her of putting donors ahead of public health.

Her allies’ complaint was simple. The political strategy she was blamed for, they said, had been driven largely by Trump himself. The $400 million his super PAC spent on the midterms was spent with his sign-off, given reluctantly in September. The issues voters punished him for were not hers. The war in Iran was run from the Pentagon. The tariffs came from the president and his Commerce Secretary. The deportation campaign that cost Republicans the Hispanic vote, and every congressional district along the Texas border, was Stephen Miller’s.

Fairness requires the other side of this. Losing both houses of Congress is a real failure, and chiefs of staff have paid for their presidents’ bad years before; Ronald Reagan replaced Donald Regan in early 1987, after his own midterm losses. Wiles also landed softly, with a post in the political operation and public praise for “an outstanding job.” None of that is unusual.

What is unusual is the comparison. Of the people whose work voters actually rejected on November 3, one was promoted into Wiles’s office. Another, Pete Hegseth, celebrated the news online: “Patriots in control.” The Times reported that Miller’s arrival may even help Hegseth, about whom Trump has heard “myriad complaints” from Republicans in Congress. The woman who ran the staff was the one who left.

The Times also counted the year’s turnover at the top, in a graphic that needs no commentary: Wiles, Gabbard, Chavez-DeRemer, Noem and Bondi out; Miller, Clayton, Sonderling, Mullin and Blanche in. Five women out, five men in. A day earlier, Dhillon had resigned from the Justice Department. Chris Cameron of the Times called Wiles the latest in a wave of powerful women to leave.

What the evidence supports, and what it does not

Let me be precise about the verdict, because precision is the only thing that makes it hold.

The evidence does not show that Trump wants women out of his government. He appointed more women to cabinet-level posts at the start of this term than any Republican before him. For nearly two years a woman ran his staff, and when Leavitt left he gave the podium to another woman, if only for a week.

The evidence does not show that every woman who left was treated unjustly. Several gave ample cause. The White House also insists that Gabbard was neither fired nor pushed: she left, it says, after her husband was diagnosed with a rare cancer. That deserves respect rather than suspicion.

The evidence does not prove what is in the president’s head. No honest writer can prove that.

What the evidence does show is this. In Trump’s second term, the women who reached high office held it on a shorter tether. When they embarrassed the president, they were removed within days. Noem went in two, Guilfoyle in six. Zacharia lasted seven days in all. Wiles survived the election by three. When men embarrassed him, the same or worse, they were defended, reassigned or photographed in the Oval Office. The first cabinet departures of this administration were all women. The man whose blunder lost Stefanik her promised post was given that post. Patel kept his job on allegations that helped end Chavez-DeRemer’s. Lutnick kept his while the attorney general who mishandled the Epstein files lost hers. And when the voters rejected the war, the tariffs and the deportations, the woman who ran the staff was removed, and the man who ran the deportations was given her office. The most consistent student of presidential appointments in the country says she has never seen anything like it.

I was not alone in my opening hunch. In the spring, as Noem and Bondi left within weeks of each other, a Trump official told Parker of The Atlantic that the administration risked looking as if it fired only “attractive” women while the president’s top men stayed. Sarah Matthews, a deputy press secretary in Trump’s first term who resigned after January 6, told her the president has shown a far shorter fuse with women in power this time. In her view, the gap between whom he will fire and whom he will not is glaring.

Still, that hunch needs a small but important correction. It was that Trump keeps women around until they become a liability. The evidence suggests something more corrosive. Women in his administration become liabilities sooner, and for less, because the threshold is set differently for them. A man’s scandal is treated as an attack on the president, to be fought. A woman’s scandal is treated as her own failing, to be shed.

That is what I mean by the conditional women. Their tenure is real, and so is their power. Neither comes with the presumption of defence that the men around them simply assume.

A note from outside

From my side of the border, it is tempting to treat all of this as one more American peculiarity, a sideshow to the tariffs and the wars. I do not think it is a sideshow. A government in which one class of official is held to account and another is not is not running an accountability system at all. It is running a loyalty system with selective enforcement. Women in Trump’s Washington are not being judged more harshly because they are worse at their jobs. They are being judged at all, which is the difference.

The midterms have now come and gone. On November 3, 2026, Democrats won both houses of Congress, and with them the committees that can make officials in that building feel chagrin again. Whatever they do with that power, the record of 2026 is already written. Four cabinet women were gone by the end of May. An ambassador followed in October, and a press secretary after seven days. Three days after the election, the chief of staff followed them. Each of the five cabinet-level women was replaced by a man, and the men whose scandals filled Part II are still in their chairs. One of them has been promoted into hers.
