Stephen Gordon September 2026

A short that’s been making the rounds puts the question crudely: “What in hell were all you people thinking.” That’s the question. It isn’t the answer.

Trump posted an AI-generated image of his own face carved into Mount Rushmore next to Lincoln’s this past May, with no caption, no explanation needed. It wasn’t the first time — he told Kristi Noem back in 2018 it was his “dream,” floated it again in a 2020 speech, and Representative Anna Paulina Luna has since introduced actual legislation instructing the Interior Department to make it happen. The National Park Service’s own engineers say the geology won’t allow it: no rock left to carve, the surrounding granite unsuited to bear new weight. The Lakota Sioux have opposed the monument itself as carved into the sacred Black Hills without their consent since it was built — a land claim that predates and outlasts any argument about who else might get added to it.

Washington, Jefferson, Roosevelt, and Lincoln were chosen for that mountain to represent something bigger than any one of them — a nation’s birth, growth, development, and survival, in that order, none of it about the men individually. Trump’s request lands almost a year after his own approval numbers had already fallen well below the level that elected him — asking for a face that lasts millennia, on the strength of numbers that had already stopped lasting the term. That’s the disagreement in one image — and it’s also, as it turns out, the same disagreement running underneath everything else in this piece.

Here is the paradox, stated plainly: Donald Trump’s overall approval rating has been sitting in the mid-to-high 30s for months — historically weak for this point in a term, in the neighborhood of Biden’s 2022 numbers or Nixon’s final year, depending on which cut of the data you use. And yet turn a camera on the crowd behind him at any rally or convention, and you get a wall of faces radiating something that looks, from a distance, like certainty. Not just support. Devotion.

I don’t think those people are stupid. I don’t think they’re brainwashed, not in any literal sense. But I’ve stared at that footage enough times now to want an actual answer, not just a shrug. So here’s my honest attempt at one — five overlapping explanations, none of which require believing the people in that crowd are fools, and one piece of data that complicates the question more than the footage lets on.

The premise is half wrong already

Start with the thing the wide shot hides: the base isn’t holding as steady as it looks, even if it isn’t collapsing either. NBC’s polling found the share of Republicans who identify with the MAGA movement and “strongly approve” of Trump slipped from 78 percent in April to 70 percent by late summer — a real, measurable softening, even if it hasn’t shown up on the jumbotron yet, and even if the drop has likely continued since that reading. Among white voters without a college degree, one of the coalition’s foundational blocs, net approval fell from +36 in early 2025 to underwater by this spring, according to YouGov/CBS tracking. And a Washington Post/Ipsos poll from July put the share of all American adults who strongly approve of Trump at just 15 percent — a national intensity collapse, not specifically a collapse within his own coalition, where strong approval has stayed considerably higher.

That distinction matters, because it cuts against overstating the other direction too: overall Republican approval of Trump has held in the 70s-to-low-80s range through most of this stretch. A party still giving its president that kind of approval isn’t a party in the process of leaving him. What’s actually happening is narrower and, in its way, more interesting — the hard core hasn’t abandoned him, but it’s noticeably less ecstatic than it was a year ago. Those are two different stories, and conflating them is exactly the kind of imprecision this piece is trying to avoid.

So the honest first answer to “why are they still with him” is: fewer of them are quite as sure than the wide shot suggests, even though most of them are, in fact, still with him. The people who show up to stand behind a podium in Dallas in 102-degree heat are a self-selected sample, filtered hard for intensity. They’re not a representative cross-section of anybody. The quieter softening — the people who used to strongly approve and now merely tolerate — doesn’t get a camera angle. That should make us suspicious of the question’s premise without pretending the remaining core is small. It isn’t. It’s just less certain than it looks, and still large enough to matter.

Before getting to the psychology, it’s worth being concrete about what the loyalty is actually surviving. The affordability crisis Trump was elected to fix has instead defined his term — “affordability” is the word his own party’s strategists now say they’re most anxious about. The war with Iran, which he suggested would be resolved quickly, is still running, with gas prices tied to its outcome rather than freed by it. The $2,000 tariff rebate and the DOGE dividend, both promised in his first year back, never materialized. None of that is spin. It’s the record the loyalty has to metabolize, and it’s worth naming plainly before asking why anyone would stay loyal through it.

For the ones who remain, five overlapping things are probably doing the work

These aren’t five separate engines so much as one loop with five entry points — the leader gets fused to a person’s sense of identity, the opposing party gets fused to a sense of threat, and the information environment each of them consumes exists partly to protect both fusions at once. Breaking it into five is useful for seeing where a crack might actually open, even though in practice they reinforce each other constantly.

Negative partisanship. Political scientists have spent a decade documenting something uncomfortable: for a large share of both parties’ bases, the strongest driver of loyalty isn’t enthusiasm for your side, it’s fear and contempt for the other one. If the last two years of convention rhetoric have told you, repeatedly, that the opposing party is a “Bolshevik Revolution” that “hates what it means to be American,” then walking away from Trump doesn’t feel like changing your mind about a president. It feels like defecting to people you’ve been told for a decade want to destroy your country. That’s a much higher bar to clear than “I’m disappointed in the economy.” It’s also worth saying plainly: this isn’t a one-party technique. Democrats have run their own version of existential contrast rhetoric — Trump as a threat to democracy itself — on roughly the same schedule, for roughly the same reason. Contrast rhetoric intensifies whenever a party’s own record stops selling itself. That’s an ordinary feature of opposition politics, not a MAGA-specific invention.

Sunk cost, but for identity rather than money. Nobody wants to have been wrong about something they’ve defended at Thanksgiving, on Facebook, to their kids, for the better part of a decade. Admitting Trump has failed on his own terms — the affordability crisis he was elected to fix, the wars he promised to end — isn’t just a policy update. For a lot of people it would mean admitting they misjudged the person and misjudged themselves. Most humans, across every political persuasion, will hold a losing position rather than pay that particular bill. This isn’t a Trump-specific weakness. It’s a species-wide one.

A closed information loop. This is less a claim about intelligence and more a claim about plumbing. If the media ecosystem you actually consume treats every unfavorable poll as rigged, every critical story as fake, and every defector as a sellout, then the information that would normally erode support simply never arrives intact. It arrives pre-discredited. You can’t update on evidence you’ve been inoculated against before you ever see it. That’s not unique to the right, either — every information silo, left or right, does some version of this. Trump’s has just been unusually effective at it.

A grievance that predates him and will outlast him. For a meaningful slice of that crowd, Trump was never really the point. He’s the vessel for a real and long-standing resentment — of coastal elites, of a globalized economy that hollowed out a lot of towns, of a culture that made a lot of people feel spoken about rather than spoken to. Ipsos’s tracking of his coalition found supporters rating him well on immigration while a smaller, though still majority, share approve of his handling of the cost of living. That’s not a puzzle if immigration is simply the issue a lot of these voters ranked first to begin with — coastal analysts tend to underweight issue hierarchy because they don’t personally rank border policy above their own grocery bill. Some of this is the cost-benefit trade I mentioned above. Some of it is just a different scoring system than the one doing the judging from outside it.

Personal attachment to the leader specifically — the one mechanism on this list that isn’t quite ordinary. The first four explanations are things every political tribe does to some degree; this one is closer to what actually distinguishes this particular coalition. A charismatic bond fused to a person rather than to a platform isn’t built to survive scrutiny of that person’s record — it’s built to survive it. The bond is to the man, not the metrics, which is precisely why bad polling doesn’t dissolve it the way it would dissolve support for a policy position. Calling this a “cult” risks doing more moral work than the word earns; the more precise claim is narrower — that Trump’s personal brand, more than his party’s platform, is still the organizing force holding a chunk of this coalition together after the metrics have turned against him. That’s unusual. It isn’t universal. And it’s the one part of this list I’d genuinely distinguish from ordinary tribal loyalty rather than fold into it.

The fear isn’t incidental — it’s the product

Here’s the part I’d be doing the piece a disservice to leave out: negative partisanship doesn’t sustain itself. Someone has to keep the fear fresh, and this week gave a clean demonstration of exactly how that work gets done.

At the first-ever GOP midterm convention in Dallas, with Trump’s own record too weak to run on, the rhetoric from the stage wasn’t really aimed at persuading anyone undecided. It was aimed at recharging the fear that makes leaving unthinkable for the people already inside the tent. House Majority Leader Steve Scalise told the crowd he calls it “the Bolshevik Revolution in the Democratic Party.” Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent warned of a “socialist hellscape” modeled on “Soviet bloc, Caracas and Havana.” Trump himself said flatly, “we do not negotiate with communists, with socialists, or with radical left lunatics.” Senator Tim Scott told the room the opposition “hate what it means to be American.”

None of that is an accident of a long speech running past its prepared remarks. It’s the maintenance work negative partisanship requires to keep functioning as a loyalty mechanism once the leader’s own performance stops doing the job. If the case for staying can no longer be “look how well things are going,” the case becomes “look how much worse the alternative would be” — and that requires the alternative to be made to look monstrous, regularly, on a stage, whether or not the description is accurate. It’s worth noting plainly that it usually isn’t: there’s a real, narrow leftward shift in some Democratic primaries this cycle, but stretching that into “Bolshevik Revolution” covering every Democrat on every ballot is rhetorical inflation, not reporting.

That’s the piece I’d add to the mechanisms above, not a replacement for the grievance one: the underlying anxieties are real and predate any single convention. But the specific intensity of the fear — the sense that the other side isn’t just wrong but existential — doesn’t stay at that pitch on its own. Dallas didn’t invent the resentment. It concentrated and refreshed it, on schedule, at the exact moment the record could no longer do the persuading on its own.

What I’d actually say to the person asking

If you’re standing in front of your television wondering what’s wrong with those people, here’s the honest three-part answer, in order of how uncomfortable each part should be.

First: the wide shot exaggerates cohesion. The crowd behind the podium is a filtered sample, not a cross-section, and the coalition really has softened at the edges more than it looks on camera.

Second: the core that remains is still large — most Republicans still approve of this president by wide margins — and most of what’s holding it there is ordinary. Negative partisanship, identity sunk costs, information silos, and issue-weighting that outsiders don’t share: none of that is a special MAGA pathology. It’s how tribal loyalty works generally, including in whichever tribe you and I happen to belong to.

Third, and this is the part I wouldn’t want to soften just to be even-handed: what’s genuinely unusual isn’t that people stay loyal to a party. It’s that so much of this particular coalition is still organized around one man’s personal brand rather than around a platform, well after that brand’s own numbers turned against him. Whether that’s charisma, marketing, manufactured threat, or all three at once, “cult” is the least precise word for it, and I’d rather retire the word than keep reaching for it. The more useful question isn’t what in hell you people were thinking. It’s what happens to a party once its performance and its personal brand have come apart, and the personal brand is still winning.
