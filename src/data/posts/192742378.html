Times Square, New York, March 2020. The pandemic emptied the world’s great cities and exposed the fragility of the post-war order. The United States — architect of global health institutions — suffered one of the worst per-capita death tolls among wealthy nations as basic public health measures became politicized.

On March 11, 2020, the World Health Organization declared COVID-19 a global pandemic. By the end of that year, roughly 1.8 million people had died. By the time the acute phase of the pandemic had passed, the global death toll would exceed 6 million — with many epidemiologists arguing that the true excess mortality was two to three times higher.

The pandemic was the most significant test of the post-war international order since the Cold War’s end — and the order failed it.

Not entirely. The scientific achievement of developing effective vaccines within a year of the pathogen’s identification was extraordinary — a product of the international research collaboration, the shared data, and the institutional frameworks that the post-war period had built. The emergency funding mechanisms of the IMF and World Bank provided crucial support to developing economies in the pandemic’s early months. The WHO, however imperfect, provided a coordinating framework that allowed information sharing on a scale that no previous pandemic response had achieved.

But the failures were more visible and more consequential than the achievements. The United States — the country that had built the post-war order, that had designed the global health architecture that should have responded to a pandemic, that in January 2020 had the most sophisticated public health infrastructure in the world — produced one of the worst per-capita death tolls of any wealthy country. The Trump administration’s response — the denial, the misinformation, the politicization of basic public health measures, the withdrawal from WHO coordination — demonstrated the consequences of treating international institutions as liabilities rather than assets.

The vaccine nationalism that followed — wealthy countries hoarding vaccines while low-income countries waited, the refusal to waive intellectual property protections that would have allowed broader production, the failure to coordinate distribution through the COVAX framework that had been designed for exactly this purpose — demonstrated the limits of the order’s solidarity in a more fundamental way. When genuine sacrifice was required — when protecting the global population from a pandemic meant wealthy countries accepting constraints on their vaccine supplies — the solidarity evaporated.

China and Russia exploited the gap. Chinese vaccine diplomacy — the distribution of Sinovac and Sinopharm vaccines to developing countries that were being left behind by the Western vaccine allocation — was the most effective Chinese soft power operation since the Belt and Road Initiative. Russian Sputnik V vaccine distribution served a similar function. The pandemic’s management became a geopolitical competition, with China and Russia offering alternatives to the American-led order’s inadequate response.

COVID-19 vaccine rollout, 2021. A scientific triumph undermined by vaccine nationalism. Wealthy nations hoarded doses while poorer countries waited, COVAX faltered, and China and Russia filled the gap with effective vaccine diplomacy.

The Visibility of American Dysfunction

The pandemic did something that no previous crisis had done as vividly: it made American domestic dysfunction globally visible in real time.

Previous American crises — Vietnam, Watergate, the financial crisis, the Iraq War — had produced periods of damaged credibility. But they had been manageable, in part, because information traveled more slowly and the comparative performance of different countries was less immediately obvious.

COVID changed this. The daily death counts were published. The mask debates were broadcast globally. The politicization of vaccines — the transformation of a basic public health intervention into a tribal identity marker — was watched with disbelief by governments and populations that had assumed American institutional capacity was broadly functional.

The specific numbers were damning. The United States, with approximately 4 percent of the world’s population, accounted for approximately 15 percent of global COVID deaths in the pandemic’s first year. South Korea, Taiwan, New Zealand, and other countries that had invested in public health infrastructure and maintained functional institutional responses performed dramatically better. The comparison was unavoidable, and it was made constantly by governments trying to explain to their own populations why they were following different policies.

The broader lesson — drawn by governments, policy analysts, and ordinary citizens around the world — was not merely that the United States had handled the pandemic badly. It was that the United States was no longer reliably capable of the institutional coordination that effective governance requires. The political polarization that Trump had both exploited and intensified had produced a system in which basic facts were contested on partisan grounds, in which public health agencies were politicized, and in which the country’s response to a shared threat was impeded by the same tribal divisions that were making every other collective challenge more difficult.

This was a different kind of credibility damage than Iraq had produced. Iraq damaged the credibility of American power — the reliability of American commitments and the competence of American strategy. COVID damaged the credibility of American governance — the capacity of American institutions to function effectively in a crisis.

A country that cannot manage a pandemic is a country whose claim to lead a global order requires reexamination.

The Economic Disruption and Its Political Consequences

The pandemic’s economic disruption — the supply chain shocks, the inflation that followed the massive fiscal stimulus deployed to prevent depression, the labor market disruptions of the “great resignation” — had political consequences that shaped the conditions in which Trump’s second election and the Iran war became possible.

The inflation of 2021-2023 was, in its origins, a product of the interaction between pandemic-driven supply disruptions and the emergency fiscal stimulus that the Biden administration deployed. It was not primarily a policy failure — the stimulus was justified by the scale of the economic emergency, and the alternative was a depression. But it produced real pain for real people, concentrated disproportionately in the working-class communities that were already the most economically vulnerable and the most politically alienated.

Gas prices — the most viscerally political of all consumer price indicators — were a particular flashpoint. When gas crossed $4 a gallon in the summer of 2022, following Russia’s invasion of Ukraine and the sanctions-driven energy price spike, the Biden administration’s approval ratings fell sharply. The political mechanism was the same one that this series has documented repeatedly: rising gas prices produce declining presidential approval, and declining presidential approval produces pressure for whatever policy change the opposition offers.

Port of Los Angeles, 2021. Mountains of backed-up shipping containers became the defining image of pandemic supply chain chaos. Disruptions and stimulus-driven demand triggered inflation that hit working families hardest and helped fuel Trump’s 2024 comeback.

The combination of pandemic disruption, inflation, and the perception — encouraged by Republican messaging — that the Biden administration’s policies had caused the economic pain, contributed to the conditions that returned Trump to office in November 2024. The economic grievances were real. The policy attribution was largely false. But false attribution has never been an obstacle to political effect.

China’s Pandemic Pivot

The pandemic also marked the moment when China’s strategic trajectory shifted from the patient “hide your strength, bide your time” approach to something more explicitly assertive.

Xi Jinping’s domestic narrative about the pandemic — China’s authoritarian system had controlled the virus efficiently while Western liberal democracies had floundered — was largely propaganda. China’s actual COVID management was deeply flawed, including the initial suppression of information that delayed global awareness of the outbreak. But the narrative had sufficient basis in the early months of 2020 to be effective, and it fed a growing confidence in Beijing about the relative trajectory of Chinese and Western systems.

Xi Jinping in Wuhan, March 2020. China’s early narrative of authoritarian competence versus Western chaos boosted Beijing’s confidence. The pandemic accelerated technological decoupling and reinforced China’s belief in the superiority of its system.

The pandemic also accelerated China’s decoupling from Western supply chains. The experience of being dependent on Chinese manufacturing for critical medical supplies — personal protective equipment, pharmaceutical ingredients, medical devices — was a wake-up call for Western governments about the vulnerabilities that post-Cold War globalization had created. Their response — investment in domestic manufacturing capacity, export controls on advanced semiconductors, efforts to “friend-shore” supply chains — was a direct challenge to the economic integration on which China’s growth model depended.

China’s response was to accelerate the development of domestic alternatives — domestic chip production, domestic software ecosystems, domestic financial infrastructure that reduced dependence on dollar-denominated systems. The technological decoupling that began as a pandemic response has become a permanent feature of great-power competition.

Ukraine 2022: The Order’s Last Stand

The Russian invasion of Ukraine on February 24, 2022, was the most significant test of the post-war order since Korea — and, unlike Iraq, it was a test that the order broadly passed.

The Western response to the invasion — the sanctions package that isolated Russia from the global financial system, the military assistance that gave Ukraine the capacity to defend itself, the NATO solidarity that held despite Russian pressure and despite Trump-era damage to allied confidence — demonstrated that the order’s institutions retained real capacity when genuinely threatened.

The speed and scale of the sanctions response was remarkable. Within days of the invasion, Russia was cut off from SWIFT, the global interbank messaging system. Russian central bank assets held in Western countries were frozen. The ruble collapsed. The sanctions package was coordinated across the United States, the European Union, Britain, Japan, South Korea, and Australia — a coalition of forty-plus countries representing more than half the global economy.

Ukrainian forces fire a HIMARS system, 2022. The post-war order at its strongest: coordinated sanctions, massive military aid, and NATO solidarity across 40+ nations showed what collective Western action could achieve — before the second Trump administration stepped back.

The military assistance was equally significant. The transfer of Javelin anti-tank missiles, HIMARS multiple rocket launchers, Patriot air defense systems, and eventually — after lengthy hesitation — Western tanks and fighter jets gave Ukraine the capacity to resist a Russian military that had been expected to overrun the country in days.

The order worked. Not perfectly — the hesitations, the delays, the political battles over each escalation ladder rung, the failure to prevent the invasion in the first place — all of these were genuine failures. But the basic architecture of collective action that the post-war generation had built was demonstrated to be functional in the most demanding test it had faced in decades.

The question that the Ukraine experience raised — and that the Iran war of 2026 is now answering, in the negative — was whether this was a genuine restoration of the order’s functionality or a last display of capacity before its terminal decline.

The Answer

The answer, in retrospect, is that Ukraine was the order’s last stand rather than its restoration.

The second Trump administration, entering office in January 2025, immediately moved to reduce American military assistance to Ukraine, pressure Ukraine into negotiations that would cede territory to Russia, and lift sanctions on Russian oil — a gift to Putin that Robert Kagan, writing in The Atlantic, estimated at $38 billion even if the war ended in April 2026.

The institutions that had coordinated the Western response to Russia’s invasion — the Ramstein Air Base contact group, the G7 sanctions coordination mechanism, the NATO solidarity framework — were not dismantled by the second Trump administration. They were simply bypassed. The United States stopped attending some meetings. It reduced its contributions. It pursued bilateral arrangements with Russia that ignored the multilateral frameworks.

And then, in February 2026, it launched a war with Iran without consulting any of its allies, without UN authorization, and without the multilateral framework that had given the Ukraine response its legitimacy and effectiveness.

The contrast between the two responses is the clearest possible illustration of what the post-war order’s presence and absence look like in practice. Ukraine, with the order: collective action, shared burden, sustained commitment, genuine effectiveness. Iran, without the order: unilateral action, alienated allies, no burden-sharing, contested legitimacy, and an outcome whose shape is still being determined as this series goes to press.

The pandemic was the dress rehearsal for disorder. Ukraine was the finale of order. Iran is the opening act of what comes after.
