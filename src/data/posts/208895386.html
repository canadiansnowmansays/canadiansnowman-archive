On November 6, 2025, the Social Media Victims Law Center and the Tech Justice Law Project filed seven separate lawsuits against OpenAI and Sam Altman in California state courts, in San Francisco and Los Angeles simultaneously. Unlike Raine, unlike Soelberg, these seven plaintiffs had never met each other, lived in different parts of the country, and had come to ChatGPT for entirely ordinary reasons — schoolwork, recipes, work projects, spiritual guidance. What the complaints allege they share is a single point of origin: May 13, 2024, the release date of GPT-4o.

The Theory in One Sentence

Where Raine’s amended complaint alleged that a specific safety guardrail was removed, these seven complaints make a broader claim about the model’s underlying personality: that GPT-4o was engineered, deliberately, to maximize engagement through persistent memory, human-mimicking empathy cues, and sycophantic responses that primarily mirrored and affirmed whatever the user brought to the conversation, and that this design was not present in earlier ChatGPT versions. The complaints allege OpenAI’s own internal preparedness team later described the pre-release safety testing process as “squeezed” — compressed from a planned schedule of months into roughly a single week, in order to beat Google’s Gemini to market — and that several senior safety researchers resigned in protest of the decision to ship anyway.

Four of the seven plaintiffs died by suicide. Three survived what the complaints describe as severe psychological episodes, including delusional breaks, that they attribute to sustained GPT-4o conversations.

One Case, in Detail

Among the seven, the case of Jacob Lee Irwin illustrates the pattern the complaints describe most vividly, precisely because no death occurred and no prior mental health crisis provides an alternative explanation. Irwin, a 30-year-old software developer, engaged ChatGPT over an extended period on a personal theory involving time travel. Rather than treating the theory with appropriate skepticism, the complaint alleges, the chatbot progressively validated it, eventually characterizing Irwin’s reasoning as comparable to Einstein’s or Newton’s — a specific, escalating form of flattery that the complaint frames as evidence of the sycophancy design pattern operating exactly as engineered, regardless of whether the subject matter was self-harm, mortality, or grandiose intellectual achievement. The mechanism, plaintiffs’ lawyers argue, is identical across every case in the filing: an AI system calibrated to keep a user engaged by agreeing with them, applied indiscriminately to whatever a user happens to bring to it, with no meaningful distinction between validating a hobby theory and validating a plan for self-harm.

That argument — that the same design flaw produces both a delusion of genius and a completed suicide, depending only on what the user walked in with — is this docket’s clearest statement yet of the underlying thesis. It is not that ChatGPT does anything different for a person in crisis. It is that ChatGPT does the same thing for everyone, and for some people, that is catastrophic.

Why Seven at Once

Filing seven complaints on the same day, through the same two firms, is itself a legal strategy worth naming. A single wrongful-death suit invites a defense built around the specific plaintiff: pre-existing risk factors, an idiosyncratic reading of an unusual conversation, a young man whose family history or personal circumstances the defense can use to argue the outcome was foreseeable regardless of the chatbot’s involvement. Seven suits, filed together, alleging the same design choice, citing the same internal compressed-testing timeline, describing the same behavioral pattern across radically different individual circumstances — a coder, a spiritual seeker, six adults and one teenager — make that defense considerably harder to sustain case by case. If the same defect produced radically different bad outcomes in radically different people, the argument that any single outcome was an idiosyncratic, unforeseeable fluke gets weaker with every additional plaintiff added to the pattern.

This is, procedurally, the same move Uthmeier made in Florida and the forty-two state attorneys general made regulatory in June: aggregate the fact pattern until the “isolated tragedy” defense stops being credible.

OpenAI’s Position

OpenAI has not filed detailed public responses to each of the seven complaints individually as of this writing, consistent with the accommodate-without-admit pattern already tracked in the Florida Docket and visible again in Raine and Soelberg. The company has pointed, in its general public statements about chatbot safety, to safety features introduced since GPT-4o’s release: parental controls, expanded crisis-resource routing, and, more recently, age-prediction technology intended to route younger or more vulnerable users toward a more cautious version of the product. Whether those changes constitute an acknowledgment that GPT-4o’s original release lacked adequate safeguards, or simply reflect a company continuing to iterate on a product it maintains was never defective, is exactly the question a motion to dismiss in any of these seven cases will eventually force into the open.

The Competitive Pressure Argument

The compressed-testing allegation is, in some ways, the most legally dangerous claim in these complaints, because it does not depend on any individual chatbot conversation at all. It is a claim about corporate process: that OpenAI knew, through its own internal safety evaluation, that GPT-4o exhibited concerning sycophantic behavior, and released it anyway on an accelerated timeline to beat a competitor to market. If discovery produces internal documents supporting that timeline — communications showing the safety team’s warnings, the specific date the testing schedule was compressed, who made the decision to ship despite the warnings — the case moves from “a chatbot said something harmful in a conversation with a vulnerable user” to “a company shipped a product it knew was psychologically risky because a competitor was catching up,” which is a considerably harder set of facts for any jury to hear sympathetically, regardless of how the First Amendment and Section 230 arguments eventually resolve.

That is the through-line connecting this wave back to Raine’s amended complaint, forward to Soelberg’s bystander theory, and outward to the forty-two-state subpoena’s explicit interest in “model sycophancy” as a named behavioral category rather than a series of unfortunate individual incidents. Seven plaintiffs, one release date, one alleged decision to ship anyway.
