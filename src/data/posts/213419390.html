Don't worry, Joni Mitchell is alive and well. What died was a joke — a six-word dismissal from the White House press office, delivered on the theory that nobody would check. On 29 March 2026, at the Juno Awards in Ontario, an 82-year-old Canadian woman in a gold lamé beret accepted a lifetime achievement award, thanked her home country for having a government she considered decent, and made a gentle, glancing remark about the state of the one she currently lives in. Asked for comment, White House spokesperson Davis Ingle offered six words: "Who the hell is Joni Mitchell?"

I would like to answer that question properly, since the White House press office apparently could not be bothered.

Who She Is, Briefly, for the Benefit of the Record

Joni Mitchell was born Roberta Joan Anderson in Fort Macleod, Alberta, in November 1943, and raised largely in Saskatoon, Saskatchewan — the kind of prairie town that produces either wheat or poets, and occasionally both. She contracted polio at nine, was sent to a special ward, and taught herself to sing while she was there, striking a bargain with herself that if she survived she would make some kind of noise about it. She kept that bargain for the next seven decades.

By the late 1960s she had become one of the defining songwriters of her generation, and by any honest accounting one of the two or three most important American — pardon me, Canadian — musical minds of the twentieth century. “Both Sides, Now,” “A Case of You,” “River,” “Woodstock,” “Big Yellow Taxi”: these are not merely popular songs, they are load-bearing walls in the architecture of postwar English-language songwriting. She has won ten competitive Grammy Awards and a Recording Academy Lifetime Achievement Award, been inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame, and been awarded the Kennedy Center Honors. In 2015 she suffered a brain aneurysm that left her unable to walk, talk, sing, or play guitar, and relearned all four skills through sheer, cussed persistence, eventually returning to public performance at the Newport Folk Festival in July 2022 in a set that reduced professional music journalists to unprofessional tears.

That is who she is. The White House’s press shop may not have known this. I would gently suggest that not knowing it is not a point in their favour.

Watching the footage, what strikes you is not nostalgia but sheer improbability — a woman told seven years earlier she might never speak again, working her way back through “Both Sides, Now” a cappella-rough at first, the crowd essentially willing her through it. It is the necessary preface to everything that follows: nobody who watches this clip mistakes her for a woman who has run out of things to say.

The Pattern of Her Politics

What seems to have irritated the current administration is not that Mitchell is famous — plenty of famous people say nothing at all, which is precisely how they stay employable in Washington’s good graces — but that she keeps saying things, in public, with her name attached, and has done so for longer than most of the people currently working in that press office have been alive.

On 19 October 2024, at her “Joni Jam” concert at the Hollywood Bowl, Mitchell performed her 1985 song “Dog Eat Dog” for the first time in nearly forty years — a song about, among other things, “snakebite evangelists and racketeers and bigwig financiers” — and ad-libbed a name into the lyric: Donald Trump. When a fan later shouted an insult in Trump’s direction, Mitchell did not demur. “Fuck Donald Trump,” she said, to a crowd of seventeen thousand people, then added, with the deadpan timing of a woman who has been doing this since Nixon, “I wish I could vote. I’m a Canadian. I’m one of those lousy immigrants.” The crowd, evidently unbothered by the presence of a lousy immigrant in their midst, gave her a standing ovation.

The song itself dates to 1985, a commercial flop at the time — critics found it too angry, too synthesizer-heavy, too openly disgusted with Reagan-era televangelism and financial grift to sit comfortably next to her earlier work. Forty years on, it plays less like a period piece than a diagnosis that arrived early. Watching her revive it, note how little updating the lyric actually required before she dropped Trump’s name into it.

On 1 February 2026, at the 68th Grammy Awards, Mitchell accepted the award for Best Historical Album for Joni Mitchell Archives, Volume 4: The Asylum Years, wearing a full sequined pantsuit, a gold beret, and an “ICE OUT” pin — the evening’s quiet uniform of protest against U.S. immigration enforcement, worn without a word of political commentary in her actual speech, because with Joni Mitchell the wardrobe has always been perfectly capable of doing the talking.

The clip is worth watching precisely because it is not political — she rambles happily about David Geffen refusing to let her put a donkey’s backside on an album cover, gets visibly confused about whether she’s presenting or accepting, and never once mentions Trump or ICE by name. The pin does the entire job. It is a masterclass in saying something without saying it, from a woman who has had sixty years of practice choosing which battles get words and which just get worn.

And then on 29 March, in Ontario — yes, the same Ontario Trump would later try to rename "Lake America" from the middle of a lake that predates both countries —at an event ostensibly about Canadian music, she told the room she was relieved to be home, noted pointedly that she still lived in a country where “you know what’s happening,” and called Prime Minister Mark Carney “a blessing,” adding, “You guys are so fortunate.” No profanity this time. No need. A Canadian woman who has spent sixty years choosing exactly the right word for exactly the right moment does not require an expletive to make her meaning land in the back row.

What “Who the Hell Is Joni Mitchell” Actually Reveals

I want to be fair to Mr. Ingle, insofar as fairness is owed to a rhetorical shrug. It is possible he genuinely did not know who she was, in which case the honest answer would have been “let me find out and get back to you,” a sentence available to every press secretary since the invention of the telephone. It is also possible he knew perfectly well and chose contempt as a strategy, on the theory that dismissing a critic as irrelevant is cheaper than engaging with what she said. Both possibilities are unflattering to an administration that spends a great deal of energy insisting it is the only entity in Washington that understands what real Americans think and feel.

Here is the trouble with the strategy, whichever version of it was in play. Joni Mitchell did not become one of the most decorated musicians in American recording history by accident, and the Recording Academy — an American institution, staffed by American professionals, voted on by American members — did not hand her a Grammy two months before someone in the White House press shop claimed never to have heard of her. The dismissal does not diminish her; it simply demonstrates, in six words, precisely how much cultural memory this White House is prepared to discard in order to avoid answering a mild rebuke from an octogenarian in a beret. That is not strength. That is a communications strategy built entirely out of not knowing things, deployed in the hope that the audience won’t know them either.

It is worth noting, too, what she did not do. She did not organize a boycott. She did not call for legislation. She did not even, in the Juno Awards remarks, mention Trump by name — the insult was constructed entirely out of praise for someone else, the oldest and most Canadian form of criticism there is, in which you compliment your neighbour’s fence in a way that makes it very clear whose fence you think needs fixing. This is not the behaviour of an activist seeking relevance. It is the behaviour of an artist who has said what she thinks, on her own terms, at her own pace, since approximately 1966, and sees no reason to stop merely because someone in a briefing room finds it inconvenient.

A Note on Being Told You Don’t Exist

There is a long and undistinguished American tradition of treating Canadian achievement as a rounding error — of discovering, apparently freshly, every few years, that some towering figure in music, science, or public life turns out to have been quietly Canadian the entire time, and reacting with the mild surprise of a man who has just learned his furnace was installed by someone else’s contractor. Joni Mitchell has had this experience for sixty years, being alternately claimed as an American treasure when convenient and dismissed as a foreign nuisance when she declines to stay quiet. Being told, at 82, with ten Grammys and a survived aneurysm and a catalogue that shaped how an entire art form thinks about melody, that the White House does not know who she is, is not really an insult to her. It is a fairly precise self-portrait of the institution making the remark.

A Small Irony, for Those Keeping Score

There is something almost too neat in all this for a woman best known for a song called “Both Sides, Now.” It is the most quoted, most covered, most wept-over piece in her catalogue — a meditation on looking at clouds, love, and life from more than one angle and ending up unsure of anything at all. It has become shorthand, in the culture at large, for a kind of hard-won even-handedness: the wisdom of the person who has seen enough of both sides to stop being certain which one is right.

The re-recorded version is worth setting beside the 1969 original: the same lyric, sung forty years later in a voice worn down by decades of cigarettes and living, somehow more devastating for having lost its youthful certainty about uncertainty itself. It is the song’s reputation for gentle ambivalence that makes its title such an easy, lazy shorthand — and such a poor description of the woman who wrote it.

None of that ambivalence survives contact with Donald Trump. When Joni Mitchell talks about him, there is exactly one side, and she has never pretended otherwise. “Fuck Donald Trump” is not a balanced accounting of competing perspectives; it is a verdict, delivered by someone who has spent sixty years being extremely precise about when to reach for nuance and when to skip it entirely. She has been picking sides in public since the Vietnam War — against the war, against strip mining paradise for a parking lot, against the men in “Dog Eat Dog” who “lie, cheat, skim, scam” — and Trump simply joined a list that was already long before he arrived on it. The song title survives as a beautiful piece of music. It has never once been her actual method for dealing with him.

If the White House press office wants to look for irony in Joni Mitchell, that is where it lives: not in the gap between what she said and what she believes, but in the gap between the gentle, uncertain artist of popular memory and the entirely certain woman standing at the podium. Six words of dismissal cannot bridge that gap. They can only confirm that whoever wrote them was listening to the title of the song, and not to a single thing she actually said.

She will be fine. She generally is. The songs were filed a long time ago, under her own name, in a place nobody in that press office can reach.
