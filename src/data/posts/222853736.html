On April 22, 2025, a small delegation from the Alberta Prosperity Project walked into the Harry S Truman Building in Washington, the headquarters of the U.S. State Department, to talk about leaving Canada. They came back on September 29 and again on December 16. According to the group’s co-founder, Dennis Modry, the conversations ranged over border security, the Canada Pension Plan, taxes, the national debt and switching to the American dollar, and the group asked whether Washington might extend a $500 billion line of credit to a newly independent Alberta, as NBC News reported early in 2026. A senior State Department official described the meetings as staff-level, with no principals in the room and no commitments made.

That is the whole of the official American record, and it is thin. It was also enough. Somewhere in a Tim Hortons in Red Deer, a man who had shrugged at every previous episode of the Neverendum read that story, put down his double-double and said something rather stronger than “Again? Must be Tuesday.”

This essay is about what changes when a foreign government starts taking meetings with one side of a family argument. The short answer, on the evidence of October 19, 2026, is that the family stops arguing with itself and starts asking who let the stranger in.

A Neighbour With Opinions

Americans have taken an interest in Canada’s unity for as long as there has been a Canada, and for most of that time the interest ran in one direction. They wanted the whole thing.

The early record is not subtle. The Americans invaded in 1775 and again in 1812. Irish-American Fenians crossed the border in the 1860s, hoping to seize territory and ransom it to Britain for Irish independence. On July 2, 1866, Congressman Nathaniel Banks introduced a bill in the House of Representatives that would have authorized the president to admit New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island, Newfoundland, the Canadas, British Columbia and Vancouver Island as states and territories, as the cultural historian Ainsley Hawthorn recounts for CBC News. It went nowhere, but the following year the British North American colonies confederated, in part because a large, battle-hardened army lived next door and its politicians kept saying things like that out loud.

Some of the interest was home-grown. In 1849, a group of Montreal merchants and French-Canadian radicals published an Annexation Manifesto calling for union with the United States. The merchants were furious that Britain had dropped the preferential duties on Canadian wheat, flour and lumber; the radicals simply preferred American republican institutions, as Jacques Monet explains in The Canadian Encyclopedia. The movement collapsed once the Reciprocity Treaty of 1854 restored the trade. The pattern is worth noting. The grievance was economic, the threat was to leave, and the threat evaporated the moment the money came back. Alberta separatism did not invent the bargaining position. It inherited it.

What Americans did not do, for most of two centuries, was encourage a piece of Canada to break off on its own. Annexation was a straightforward ambition: one country absorbing another. A fractured Canada on the northern border, with an unstable successor state or two, was nobody’s idea of an improvement in Washington.

The Foreigner Who Did Say It

The first head of government to openly cheer for Canada’s breakup was not American. He was French.

On July 24, 1967, during a state visit tied to Expo 67, Charles de Gaulle stepped onto the balcony of Montreal’s City Hall and ended his speech with “Vive le Québec libre,” pausing for effect before the last word, as Thomas S. Axworthy recounts in The Canadian Encyclopedia. Lester Pearson went on television to call the remarks unacceptable to the Canadian people. De Gaulle cut his visit short and went home.

The episode is usually told as the moment Quebec nationalism found an international voice, and it was. A year later René Lévesque founded the Parti Québécois. But the other half of the story matters as much. De Gaulle’s intervention embarrassed the federalists, energised the nationalists and strained relations between Ottawa and Paris for years. Neither referendum that followed produced a Yes. Foreign enthusiasm is a wonderful thing to have at a rally. At a ballot box, it tends to remind the undecided who is actually being asked to pay.

Washington’s Long Preference

When the question finally came to a vote, the Americans chose a side, carefully.

On October 25, 1995, five days before Quebec’s second referendum, Bill Clinton told reporters in Washington that the vote was an internal Canadian matter, and then added that a strong and united Canada had been a wonderful partner for the United States, as UPI reported that day. Diplomatic talking points released years later showed that the State Department had prepared to say, in the event of a Yes, that it was premature to consider recognising Quebec and that an independent Quebec’s place in NAFTA was not automatic, as CTV News reported when the documents came out. Translated from diplomatese: you are welcome to try, and you will be on your own.

Four years later, on October 8, 1999, Clinton went further, at Jean Chrétien’s request. Speaking at the Forum of Federations conference in Mont-Tremblant, with Premier Lucien Bouchard in the front row, he asked whether minority rights as well as majority rights were respected, and offered the verdict that federalism was not such a bad idea, as Arthur Milnes recalls for National Newswatch. Bouchard was not amused. Ottawa was delighted.

That was the settled American position for the next quarter century: neutral in form, federalist in substance, and quietly useful to the side that wanted the country kept together.

Foggy Bottom, 2025

It is against that history that the meetings of 2025 should be read.

The Financial Times broke the story in January 2026, and the details have accumulated since. In October 2026, a Globe and Mail investigation by Carrie Tait, Grant Robertson and Nathan VanderKlippe filled in the agenda. According to Modry, the separatists delivered a document to U.S. officials setting out what a sovereign Alberta could offer in return for help financing its transition: more water, oil royalties, new pipelines through the United States, and land for a rail link to Alaska, as Adam Harrison summarised the Globe’s findings for Culture Alberta on October 4, 2026. Jeffrey Rath, the lawyer who arranged the meetings, called the document stupid and said the resource proposals were never discussed. A White House official said no support or commitments were conveyed.

So the separatists’ own leaders cannot agree on what was offered to a foreign power in exchange for help dismantling their country. That is not, on reflection, a reassuring disagreement.

The Canadian reaction was immediate and not confined to one party. British Columbia’s premier, David Eby, said there was “an old-fashioned word” for it, and that word was treason, as Elizabeth Melimopoulos reported for Al Jazeera on January 30, 2026. Mark Carney said only that he expected the American administration to respect Canadian sovereignty. In Alberta, Public Interest Alberta called for an inquiry into foreign interference, and the NDP MP Heather McPherson asked for an emergency debate in the House of Commons, which the Speaker refused, as Jordan King reported for Newsweek on October 6, 2026.

Natural Partner

Staff-level meetings can be disowned. A cabinet secretary on television is harder.

In January 2026, the Treasury Secretary, Scott Bessent, was asked about Alberta on Real America’s Voice, a right-wing American television channel. “I think we should let them come down into the U.S. and Alberta’s a natural partner for the U.S.,” he said. “They have great resources. The Albertans are very independent people.” He mentioned a “rumour” of a referendum. Asked whether he knew something, he replied: “People are talking. People want sovereignty. They want what the U.S. has got,” as Kelly Geraldine Malone reported for The Canadian Press on January 23, 2026. Stephen Marche, writing in The New York Times on October 20, 2026, adds that Bessent has also called Canada a “little yipping dog.”

Note the slide in that answer. “Let them come down into the U.S.” is not support for an independent Alberta. It is the Banks bill of 1866 with better lighting. Most Alberta separatists insist they want a country, not a 51st state, and Modry was at pains to tell NBC that the group had made that clear in Washington. Bessent did not seem to have been briefed.

Then there is the premier. Danielle Smith says she told Trump administration officials to “pause” tariffs “so we can get through an election,” meaning the federal election of April 2025, as Curtis Gillespie writes in The New York Times on September 3, 2026. She visited Mar-a-Lago. She told an interviewer that Pierre Poilievre would be “very much in sync” with the new American direction, Marche notes. Smith campaigned against separation in October, and nothing here makes her a separatist. It does make her a sitting Canadian premier who asked a foreign government to time its trade policy around a Canadian federal election.

The App From Michigan

The quietest strand of the American interest turned out to be the most concrete.

10X Votes is a Michigan political app, offered free to Republican campaigns, that asks supporters to identify about ten like-minded contacts who might be nudged to the polls. Its backers credit it with helping carry Michigan for Trump in 2024. Pete Hoekstra, then chair of the Michigan Republican Party and now the U.S. ambassador to Canada, promoted it at rallies that year, including one in Warren on November 2, 2024, where he urged the crowd to download it, as Luke LeBrun reported for PressProgress on May 18, 2026.

In late January 2026, the Globe reports, a delegation tied to 10X Votes met Mitch Sylvestre of the Alberta Prosperity Project in a meeting arranged by David Parker, the founder of Take Back Alberta. No deal was reached. Parker then launched the Centurion Project, an app he says was inspired by 10X Votes, and Elections Alberta alleges it made unauthorised use of a copy of the provincial list of electors, covering the names and home addresses of nearly three million Albertans. Elections Alberta, the provincial privacy commissioner and the RCMP are investigating. Elections Alberta says Parker has refused to co-operate. There is no indication that 10X Votes itself supplied the data, and Hoekstra says he has had “zero involvement” with 10X Votes in Canada.

None of that amounts to an American plot. It does amount to an American toolkit, in the hands of people trying to break up an American ally, built by the party of the ambassador to that ally. Even read as generously as the facts allow, it is the kind of coincidence a Canadian voter notices.

Quebec Kept Its Distance

The sovereigntists in Quebec noticed too, and drew the opposite lesson.

Paul St-Pierre Plamondon, whose Parti Québécois now governs Quebec as a minority, has pledged that there will be no referendum before January 20, 2029, the day Donald Trump’s second term ends. He has said the debate must not be “hijacked” by the upheavals of American politics or by “the outbursts of an unpredictable president.” When reports surfaced that the PQ had sought a meeting with the administration, he denied it outright and called the story an attempt to tie him to Trump, as Canadaland reported on October 8, 2026.

That is the lesson of 1967, learned properly. The PQ has spent fifty-eight years living with de Gaulle’s balcony, and it knows exactly what a foreign leader’s embrace costs at home. Its leader would rather wait out an American president than be photographed anywhere near him. Alberta’s separatists went to Washington three times in eight months.

What Foreign Interest Changes

Daniel Drezner of Tufts University told Gillespie that this administration, which has meddled from Argentina to Germany, has had “zero compunction” about interfering in the domestic politics of American allies, and that its hostility to Carney could embolden divisive forces inside Canada, Alberta’s separatists among them. Gillespie’s own conclusion is sharper: the greatest favour Trump has done Alberta’s separatists was not to promote an independent Alberta but to undermine a sovereign Canada, casting the country as weak, broken and beholden.

Both observations are right, and both describe what American interference was meant to do. Neither describes what it actually did.

Throughout this series, the working thesis has been that Canadian separatism functions mostly as leverage. A province threatens to leave in order to get a better deal from Ottawa, and the threat works precisely because everyone understands it is a family quarrel. Nova Scotia’s repealers in 1868, the Montreal annexationists of 1849, Quebec in 1980 and 1995, Alberta in every decade since the National Energy Program: the threat was always aimed at Ottawa, and Ottawa was always the one expected to pay.

A foreign government at the table changes the question. It is no longer “Will Ottawa give us a better deal?” It becomes “Whose side are you on?” And that question has a much more decisive answer. On October 19, 2026, about three Albertans in four voted to remain, on a ballot that did not even ask them to leave, only whether to begin the process. One estimate, cited by Marche, suggests only a third of those who voted for the process actually wanted independence. The rest wanted to send Ottawa a message. Seen that way, the separatist hard core came to roughly one voter in twelve.

Thomas Lukaszuk, a former Progressive Conservative deputy premier who now leads the pro-unity Forever Canadian campaign, told Gillespie before the vote that he feared a 75 to 25 result would not end the American interest, and that Washington might decide the trapped quarter needed liberating. “I don’t want it to sound far-fetched,” he said, “but that is exactly the argument that Putin used.” That worry has not gone away with the count. As far as I can find, the White House has said nothing about the result.

But the vote itself is the clearest answer the country has given in a generation. The separatists brought Washington into the room, and Albertans, who have complained about Ottawa since 1905 and will cheerfully keep doing so, looked at the guest and decided they would rather keep the family quarrel in the family.

The man in the Red Deer Tim Hortons will go back to shrugging. He has earned it. But this time the shrug came with a ballot attached, and the ballot was addressed to Washington.
