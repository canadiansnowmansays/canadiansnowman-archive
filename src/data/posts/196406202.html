Day 65. Monday morning, Middle East time. Project Freedom has launched. Fifteen thousand American service members, more than a hundred aircraft, guided-missile destroyers — coordinating the passage of stranded ships through a waterway that Iran has effectively owned since February 28. The American blockade of Iranian ports remains in place. Trump has not lifted it. He has simply declared that neutral ships will now pass, and dared Iran to stop them. Gas is $4.46 a gallon nationally — up nearly 50% since the war began. The Touska crew is being handed to Iran in Islamabad as a confidence-building measure. In a glass factory 21 miles from the Taj Mahal, the furnaces are going cold.

I. The Strait of Iran

On April 17, President Trump posted on Truth Social: “IRAN HAS JUST ANNOUNCED THAT THE STRAIT OF IRAN IS FULLY OPEN AND READY FOR FULL PASSAGE.” He meant to write Strait of Hormuz. He accidentally wrote the truth.

The Youssef/Lemire Atlantic analysis — “The Iran War’s Ramifications Have Only Just Begun” — is the strategic accounting that the two months of war now require. None of Trump’s stated war goals have been met: no end to Iran’s nuclear ambitions, no destruction of Iran’s missile capability, no neutralization of proxy forces, no regime change. What the war has produced instead is something Trump did not intend and cannot easily undo. Richard Haass, former State Department official: “Even if Iran does not have explicit control, there is now always an implicit measure of control. Because they have shut it once, now they know they can do it again.”

Iran has absorbed roughly $144 billion in economic damage — approximately 40% of its prewar GDP, per the Foundation for Defense of Democracies. It is still not capitulating. Traffic through Hormuz has been reduced by 90% — from 120-150 daily transits to a handful. Brent crude has touched $126 a barrel. Gas is $4.46 nationally. And in the analysis of a Vanderbilt University panel of a retired general, a retired CIA analyst, and an energy industry executive, the effects of the war will last two to nine months at minimum — “prompting a collective intake of breath from the audience.”

The longer-term consequence: Iran has discovered it possesses a weapon more flexible and more powerful than nuclear material. Haass: “One of the ironies of this war is that Iran discovered that it had this weapon. There was so much talk about nuclear ability, but they have the strait.”

II. Project Freedom

On Sunday evening, Trump announced it. The United States would use “best efforts” to help guide stranded ships out of the Strait of Hormuz beginning Monday morning Middle East time. He called it Project Freedom. Any interference would be dealt with “forcefully.” He said nothing about lifting the American blockade of Iranian ports.

CENTCOM Sunday: guided-missile destroyers, 100+ land and sea-based aircraft, multi-domain unmanned platforms, 15,000 service members. The role: coordinating safe traffic among stranded ships, not escorting them — an important distinction. Trump’s gamble, per the Kanno-Youngs/Sanger/MacFarquhar analysis — “Trump Says U.S. Will Help Stranded Ships Leave Strait of Hormuz” — is that Iran will not want to fire the first shots in a direct challenge to the United States. It is a bet on Iranian restraint from a president who has been telling reporters a resumption of military strikes is a possibility.

Iran’s initial response was muted. IRGC spokesman Mohibi: armed forces have “operational capacity and military equipment” needed to prevail. IRIB called it “Trump’s delirium.” Iranian parliament’s national security commission head Azizi: “Any American interference in the new maritime regime of the Strait of Hormuz will be considered a violation of the ceasefire.”

The risks are significant. Not all mines have been removed from the strait — CENTCOM has said so publicly. Even if Tehran decides not to challenge the operation, an individual IRGC unit or speedboat crew may not receive the message in time. Two ships were attacked Sunday night before Project Freedom was even announced — a tanker struck by unknown projectiles off Fujairah, UAE, and a cargo ship attacked by multiple small craft near Sirik, Iran. Both escaped without casualties.

A photo released by the U.S. Central Command showing the Iranian-flagged MV Touska container ship.Credit...U.S. Central Command, via Reuters

The Peltier/Watson piece — “Pakistan Says It Helped Transfer Iranian Crew From Ship That U.S. Seized” — documents the simultaneous confidence-building gesture running alongside the military announcement. The 22 crew members of the Touska — seized by the US Navy on April 19, held aboard while Iran called the seizure “armed piracy” — were flown to Pakistan Sunday and handed to Iranian authorities Monday. The Touska itself to be returned to Pakistani territorial waters for repairs and handover to original owners. Pakistan described the transfer as a “confidence-building measure.” The timing is not coincidental. Project Freedom needed a human signal to accompany it. Pakistan provided one.

III. Both Sides Believe They Won

The Wright analysis — “The Real Reason Iran Hasn’t Struck a Deal” — is the essential analytical frame for everything happening this week. The Trump administration’s explanation for the diplomatic stalemate is that hardliners with “an apocalyptic vision” are blocking pragmatist negotiators inside Iran. Rubio on Fox News: Mojtaba Khamenei is “untested” and “has not been seen,” leaving hardliners in effective control. The administration is now gaming out strikes targeting not Iran’s military capacity but specifically the faction blocking a deal. Per Axios, General Caine has briefed Trump on military options for a “short and powerful” wave of strikes. Timing is complicated by the Xi summit scheduled for May 14 in Beijing — strikes could come in the next few days to precede it, or immediately after.

Wright’s verdict: the hardliners-blocking-pragmatists explanation is almost certainly wrong. “Iran and the United States have failed to come to an agreement not because hard-liners are blocking pragmatists inside Iran, but because both sides seem to sincerely believe that they have won the war.” The US sees destroyed Iranian military assets. Iran sees that it has withstood a war aimed to topple it, maintained control of the world’s most important energy chokepoint, and demonstrated its ability to attack the Gulf and Israel. It is not likely to give away at the negotiating table what America was unable to gain through war.

Vance reportedly believed the blockade would cause Iran to give in after a few days. Treasury Secretary Bessent on April 27: “While the surviving IRGC Leaders are trapped like drowning rats in a sewage pipe, Iran’s creaking oil industry is starting to shut in production thanks to the U.S. BLOCKADE.” Iran has endured decades of sanctions. It has experience adjusting its oil industry to reduced demand. It benefited from a financial windfall at the start of the conflict when the US lifted sanctions on Iranian oil exports.

The second chokepoint threat: if the US appears hunkered down for the long haul, Iran may activate the Houthis to close the Bab el-Mandeb Strait — connecting the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden, essential to Suez Canal traffic. A second chokepoint added to the first. Wright: “The United States went to war to deal the Islamic Republic a devastating blow from which it would never recover. The war has damaged Iran’s military capacity, but it also has handed Tehran more leverage over global energy markets and the Gulf States than it has ever possessed.”

IV. The Alexander Problem

The Parker/Scherer reporting frames the commander-in-chief making all of this possible. Trump has been privately comparing himself to Alexander the Great, Napoleon, and Julius Caesar. Someone close to him told the reporters: “He is talking about himself as potentially one of the most powerful men who has ever lived and will ever live.” The guardrails are gone. He does not particularly care if Republicans hold the House — “he cares a little more if they hold the Senate because that may make a difference in an impeachment trial.”

The Kanno-Youngs Sunday piece — “Trump Faces the Complicated Reality of a Costly, Unpopular War in Iran” — documents the gap between the self-image and the facts on the ground. Trump at The Villages Friday: “I did something that was, I don’t know, foolish, brave, but it was smart. I would do it again.” Three weeks ago he told Americans a breakthrough was near — Iran would hand over its enriched uranium, energy prices would drop, the global crisis would subside. None of that happened. Trump has cited Vietnam and Iraq to suggest the Iran war is “not very long at all” in historical perspective. The Venezuela model he invoked — where Maduro was ousted and the rest of the government stayed in place and cooperated — does not transfer to a country with extensive military capabilities and 47 years of experience resisting American pressure.

President Trump, on Saturday in Florida, is facing the reality that many Americans in a midterm election year disapprove of the war with Iran.Credit...Tom Brenner NYT

Reuters/Ipsos poll: Trump approval at 34%. Republican strategist Matthew Bartlett: “The messaging has been more than a mess. It’s worth noting this week the political, economic, and even diplomatic aspects continue to get worse. The trajectory was down across the board.”

The War Powers letter sent Friday states the war “hostilities have terminated” because of the ceasefire. Friday evening in Florida, Trump told supporters: “You know we’re in a war. Because I think you would agree we cannot let lunatics have a nuclear weapon.” The Atlantic’s David Graham — “Congress Can’t Meet Its Own Iran-War Deadline” — captures the institutional failure surrounding this contradiction. Congress has blocked six Democratic war powers resolutions. Speaker Johnson: “We are not at war.” Gallup: congressional approval matching its all-time worst — 86% disapproval. Senior House Republican to NOTUS: “This is what happens when you have leadership who can’t organize a one-car parade.” Democrats exploring a lawsuit — legal scholar Chemerinsky notes courts have deemed such suits political questions outside their scope in recent decades. Graham: “Fixing American politics and turning back the tide of authoritarianism will require an empowered and effective legislative branch. Right now, Congress doesn’t seem up to the job.”

V. China

The Wong analysis — “China Seeks an Advantage With Both Trump and Iran as War Evolves” — is the variable that runs beneath everything else. China is prodding Iran to negotiate while quietly allowing dual-use commercial shipments that could help Iran’s military. American intelligence has information that a Chinese company may have tried to send shoulder-fired missiles to Iran. Marines found material on a seized Iranian ship that Trump called “not very nice” and “perhaps a gift from China.” Trump wrote Xi a letter asking him not to send weapons. Xi wrote back saying “essentially he’s not doing that.” Trump: “That’s the way war goes, right?”

China played a crucial behind-the-scenes role in the April 7 ceasefire — Wang Yi made 26 calls to officials across the region beforehand. Pakistan thanked China first in its ceasefire social media post. China and Russia vetoed the UN resolution that would have allowed multinational action to force Hormuz open. Behind the scenes, China told Iran to show flexibility and agree to the ceasefire. Both China and Russia have since told Iran to continue negotiating.

President Trump and President Xi Jinping of China met in South Korea last year and are scheduled to meet later this month.Credit...Haiyun Jiang/The New York Times

The Xi summit is May 14 in Beijing — two weeks away, postponed once already because of the war. Hormuz will overshadow the tariff agenda. Georgetown’s Rush Doshi: “For China, there’s the issue of our blockade. For us, there’s the issue of China’s support of Iran.” International Crisis Group’s Vaez on IRGC thinking: “I see more and more voices affiliated with the IRGC openly saying the failure of Iran was that it was too shy about aligning itself with China and Russia. They say they need to mortgage out part of the country to China to end up where Pakistan is.”

Stimson Center’s Yun Sun: officials in Beijing are “holding off serious pressure on Iran until there is a direct ask from President Trump.” Any such ask could be the most consequential outcome of the Beijing summit.

VI. The $8.6 Billion and the German Lesson

The Boxerman/Wong piece — “U.S. Fast-Tracks Arms Deals Valued at $8.6 Billion to Mideast Partners” — filed Friday night, is the production paradox made visible. Rubio invoked emergency provision — third time the second Trump administration has bypassed congressional review on arms sales during the war. Qatar: $4+ billion for Patriot missile interceptors, global stockpiles of which are already critically depleted. Israel, UAE, Qatar: Advanced Precision Kill Weapon Systems. Kuwait: advanced aerial defense system ~$2.5 billion. Any weapons US companies make for other countries mean less for the United States. The production timeline — years, not months — means these weapons will not arrive before any resumption of hostilities.

The Chutel piece — “U.S. to Pull 5,000 Troops From Germany. But Plenty More Will Remain.” — documents Trump converting a personal grievance about Chancellor Merz’s “humiliation” comment into military policy. 5,000 troops withdrawn from a 36,000-strong presence — returning Europe to pre-Ukraine-invasion levels. Germany hosts five of nine US Army garrisons in Europe. US European Command and US Africa Command both headquartered in Stuttgart. Landstuhl Medical Center — treating personnel wounded in the Iran war — is the largest American hospital outside the continental US. Trump suggested he might do the same to Italy and Spain, which have both distanced themselves from the war.

VII. The Gaza Model in Lebanon

The Granados/Dahir/Varghese satellite imagery piece — “Israel Said It’s Applying the Gaza Model in Lebanon. This Is What the Devastation Looks Like.” — is the accountability document of the week. Defense minister Katz explicitly said Israel’s approach was modeled on tactics used in Gaza. At least two dozen towns and villages near the Lebanese border flattened. Amnesty International visual investigator Marcolini: “This is basically the same pattern that we documented back in Gaza, then in southern Lebanon. And now it’s southern Lebanon again. It is a strategy that they have, and they have been doing this consistently throughout the region.” Satellite imagery shows stark sectarian differentiation: majority-Shiite villages Aita al Shaab and Hanine appear as expanses of gray rubble. The nearby predominantly Christian village of Rmeish shows far less damage. Israel told some Christians and Druse they could stay if they expelled Shiite Muslims from southern towns.

Fatima Abdallah, 46, in a tent in a Beirut stadium, her home of two decades demolished in Houla: “Our home was the fruit of our lives’ work.” She used the Lebanese phrase “with stones, not with people” — the home gone, the family alive. “Only Allah can compensate us.”

VIII. What the War Has Made

The Abdulrahim/Sweis piece — “Jordanians Struggle as Mideast Wars Scare Tourists Away” — documents one of the war’s most quietly devastating regional consequences. Petra, a UNESCO World Heritage site: tourism is “1 percent of what it was,” per camel keeper Hussein al-Budool. The camels Alyan and Sharhan are sleeping in the sand. Petra commissioner: “The war began on Saturday. By Monday, we had 100 percent cancellations.” European visitor arrivals down 27% in March. Jordan imports 95% of its fuel — higher oil prices cost the government $200 million in March alone. Tourism provides up to 18% of Jordan’s government revenue. 85% of Petra’s 40,000 local residents depend on it. The Dead Sea: empty loungers, no swimmers. Easter week — normally packed with Christian pilgrims — deserted.

The Travelli/Raj piece — “’City of Glass’ Is Under Threat From Iran War” — brings the war to its most improbable address: Firozabad, Uttar Pradesh, 21 miles from the Taj Mahal. A million people depend on glassmaking. The factories have been barred from coal-fired furnaces since 1996 to protect the Taj’s white marble. They run on imported natural gas from the Persian Gulf. Since March, mountains of broken glass have piled up untouched — melting them has become too expensive. Glass cutter Saddam Hussein, 32, supporting wife and three children on $6 a day, found only four or five days of work in the past month. “The war is over there, but we’re getting killed here. When I don’t get work, my family goes hungry.” Factory owner Mukesh Bansal: “We’re not part of the war, but we’re bearing the brunt of it.”

Richard Nephew, former US deputy special envoy for Iran: “The longer this goes on, the higher the likelihood that countries will look to protect their own economic interests and cut deals with the Iranians, even if that triggers the wrath of the U.S.”
