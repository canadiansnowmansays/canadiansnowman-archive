On 8 November 2026 Hindus, Sikhs and Jains around the world are lighting lamps for Diwali. Canadians of every faith are eating the sweets that arrive at the office. In the United States, the festival has lately acquired a second tradition, which is telling the people who celebrate it to leave.

The custom took hold last year. In October 2025 Kash Patel, the Director of the Federal Bureau of Investigation, posted a Diwali greeting on X. Ellie Quinlan Houghtaling of The New Republic collected some of the replies. “This is a Christian Nation,” wrote one self-described unapologetic Christian American. “Check your foreigner stuff at the gate.” A pastor in Ogden, Utah, offered two words: “Go back.” Another account needed only one: “Deport.”

Patel was not alone. Carl Samson of NextShark reported that Nikki Haley, the former governor of South Carolina, was told to “Go home with your BS. Straight way to India.” Vivek Ramaswamy was told, “You will never be an American to me.” Tulsi Gabbard, the Director of National Intelligence, was told to go back to India as well. Gabbard is a practising Hindu with no Indian heritage at all. Bigotry has never been strong on geography.

That same week, on 21 October 2025, Donald Trump stood in the White House and lit the lamps himself. According to the Khaleej Times, he described the diya as a symbol of the victory of light over darkness and of knowledge over ignorance. It was a generous remark. Some of the people replying to his FBI director do not appear to have been listening.

Go Back to Cincinnati

Vivek Ramaswamy was born in Cincinnati. This has been of limited use to him.

He has spent his political life as the Republican Party’s favourite answer to the charge of racism: a Hindu son of immigrants, a biotechnology millionaire, and the author of a book against wokeness. In November 2025, after Democrats won in New Jersey, Virginia and New York City, he told his party, as The Tribune reported, to “cut out identity politics. It doesn’t suit Republicans. It’s the woke left’s game.” He added: “We don’t care about the colour of your skin or religion; we care about the content of your character.”

Some of his own side had not received the memo. As Hannah Knowles reported in The Washington Post in May 2026, his Republican primary opponent for the Ohio governorship, Casey Putsch, called him an “Indian boy” who “cosplays an American,” used the Nazi-derived language of “blood and soil,” shared an image of him as a blue Hindu deity, and invited him to a game of cowboys versus Indians. Putsch took about 18 per cent of the vote. A 19-year-old admirer told the Post that Ramaswamy had not “integrated” because he is Hindu. At one town hall a questioner asked why a non-Christian should be supported, and Ramaswamy, a man with a Yale law degree and a net worth in the hundreds of millions, recited the Ten Commandments to the room to establish his fitness to govern Ohio.

He deserves credit for what came next. He told a Turning Point USA conference that such people “have no place in the future of the conservative movement.” In remarks and an essay reported by Pooja Salhotra of The New York Times, he said that “the idea that a ‘heritage American’ is more American than another American is un-American at its core.” He said his feeds were “littered with hundreds of slurs.” He also warned older Republicans who doubted that the blood-and-soil view was spreading that they “should think again.”

Fairness requires two further sentences. Ramaswamy has said that he did not hear “a single bigoted remark” from an actual voter, and he won his primary comfortably. And when he lost to Amy Acton on 3 November 2026, by something like 51 per cent to 45, according to the Press Trust of India, he lost in a national wave that took down plenty of Republicans named Marshall and Graham. Nobody has shown that his religion decided it, and this essay does not claim so.

The contradiction lies elsewhere. Here was a man who took the party’s promise at face value. It said it did not care about the colour of your skin or your religion. Then it sent him to a town hall to recite Exodus.

The Vice-President’s Household

There is one more Hindu in this story, and she has not asked to be in it.

Usha Vance is the daughter of Indian immigrants and the wife of the Vice-President. She holds no office, and what she believes is her own affair. In October 2025 JD Vance told a Turning Point audience that he hoped she would one day be “somehow moved by the same thing that I was moved by in church,” adding that if she was not, “God says everybody has free will.” When criticism followed, he wrote, as The Week reported, that she “is not a Christian and has no plans to convert.” A man’s hopes for his marriage are not this series’ business.

The arithmetic is. Apply the rule proposed in those Diwali replies, “Check your foreigner stuff at the gate,” and it reaches the front door of the Vice-President’s residence. A movement cannot tell Hindus that they do not belong in a Christian nation and also ask them to host its receptions. It has been attempting both for some time.

The Average Man Knows Perfectly Well

Readers of this series will recognise the tune, because it has been played before, and the Supreme Court once wrote down the notes.

Bhagat Singh Thind came to the United States from Punjab and enlisted in the American Army in 1917. According to the National Park Service, he was the first soldier to wear a turban in the American military. He was honourably discharged in 1918, and in November 1920 an Oregon judge granted him citizenship, partly because of that service.

The government went to court to take it back. The law at the time reserved naturalisation for “free white persons,” and on 19 February 1923 a unanimous Supreme Court ruled that Thind was not one. Justice George Sutherland granted in the opinion that “the blond Scandinavian and the brown Hindu” might share “a common ancestor in the dim reaches of antiquity.” But “the average man knows perfectly well that there are unmistakable and profound differences between them today.”

Thind was a Sikh. The court called him a Hindu anyway. A century later a practising Hindu from a Pacific island is being told to go back to India, so the average man has kept up his standards.

Dozens of Indian Americans then lost their citizenship. One was Vaishno Das Bagai, a San Francisco merchant naturalised in 1921. The government stripped him of his citizenship, California’s Alien Land Law cost his family their property, and the United States refused him a passport. He died by suicide in San Jose on 16 March 1928. In a letter published by the San Francisco Examiner, he had written: “I came to America thinking, dreaming and hoping to make this land my home.” Then: “But they now come to me and say, I am no longer an American citizen.”

Told he did not belong, he was also denied the means of leaving. That is what “go back” has tended to mean in practice.

Congress reversed the ruling with the Luce-Celler Act, signed by Harry Truman on 2 July 1946. Three years later a Punjabi-born farmer named Dalip Singh Saund became a citizen. In 1956 the voters of Imperial and Riverside counties in California sent him to Congress, the first Asian American and the first Sikh ever elected to it. That is how quickly the unassimilable become the congressman, and it is how this series has found it to work for the Irish, the Chinese, the Jews and everyone else.

A Note From the Neighbours

A Canadian should mention the following before someone else does.

On 23 May 1914 the Komagata Maru sailed into Vancouver harbour with 376 passengers from British India, most of them Sikhs. Canada kept nearly all of them aboard for two months and sent the ship away on 23 July. When it reached Budge Budge, near Calcutta, troops opened fire and about 20 passengers were killed. It took until 18 May 2016 for a Canadian prime minister to apologise in the House of Commons, as The Canadian Press recorded. Canada’s Diwali sweets taste better for the apology, but it was 102 years late.

So this is not a sermon from a country with clean hands. The difference at the moment is one of direction.

Knowledge Over Ignorance

On one day of the year this series could fairly be spelled The American ContraDiction. The movement wants the donors, the doctors, the Director of the FBI and the Second Lady. It wants the photograph of the President with the lamp. It also contains a growing number of people who regard every one of them as a foreigner who has been insufficiently told so, and it has not found a way to choose.

Ramaswamy, to his credit, has asked it to choose. He calls blood-and-soil thinking un-American, and he is right, though he might notice that the Supreme Court of the United States was once of the other opinion, and that the country’s history is largely a record of the argument between the two.

Donald Trump said it best, with the taper in his hand. The diya stands for the victory of knowledge over ignorance. In the present case, knowledge would begin with knowing where Cincinnati is.
