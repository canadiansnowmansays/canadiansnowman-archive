by Stephen Gordon

Essay 1,605 is the first double this series has ever drawn: a year that produced, in one twelve-month span, the most famous failed conspiracy in history and the most famous successful novel. In January of 1605, in Madrid, a one-handed veteran and disgraced grain clerk published a book about a gentleman who read too many romances and rode out to fight giants that were windmills. In November of the same year, in London, a soldier of fortune was discovered in a cellar beneath the House of Lords with thirty-six barrels of gunpowder and a slow match. One of these men was at war with reality. History has decided it was the one with the lance, and history, I will argue by the end of this essay, got it backwards.

The plot first, because it comes with the better numbers.

The scheme, run by Robert Catesby with Guy Fawkes as its man in the cellar, was to blow up the State Opening of Parliament on November 5, 1605 — king, lords, bishops, and government, subtracted in one entry — and raise a rising in the ashes. The quantity of powder is usually given as thirty-six barrels, and I note with professional respect that the plotters had done their arithmetic; the estimates run to a blast that would have levelled the building and much of the neighbourhood. The counting was sound. What undid the plot was — and regular readers of this series may want to sit down, though they will have seen it coming — a publication. In late October, an anonymous letter reached Lord Monteagle, a Catholic peer, advising him, in terms just vague enough to be deniable and just clear enough to be fatal, to stay away from the opening of Parliament. Monteagle took it to the government; the government, with a showman’s patience, waited; and around midnight before the appointed day, searchers found Fawkes in the cellar with the powder and the fuses. The conspiracy that had held its secret across eighteen months and a dozen mouths was broken by a single page of writing that went where writing goes. Luther, essay 1,517, changed the world by publishing. The Gunpowder Plot was destroyed by it. The press and the pen sit on both sides of every ledger, and this series keeps finding them there.

The plotters died as seventeenth-century plotters died, and I will not linger; the Numbers Game does not round men, and it does not dwell on them either when the century’s justice gets theatrical. What interests me is what happened to the date. Parliament, within weeks, passed an act appointing November 5 a day of annual thanksgiving, and the anniversary took root — bonfires, bells, effigies — and somewhere along the way the date acquired the rhyme: Remember, remember, the fifth of November. Sit with that as a piece of information technology. The plot failed absolutely; not a barrel was lit; and yet it owns a calendar square in perpetuity, four centuries of fires, because its date was set to metre. I can tell you from fifteen hundred essays of trying that this is the hardest trick in the trade. Empires fall and cannot get a couplet. The equinox drifted for sixteen centuries, essay 1,582, and no one wrote it a rhyme. But strap a failed cellar to a rhythm and a number, and every child in England can recite it before they can spell conspiracy. The rhyme is the most durable container ever built for a number. The plot is the proof: it is remembered not because it succeeded, and not even because it nearly succeeded, but because its date scans.

Two footnotes to the cellar, and I promise both earn their place. The first is that the Monteagle letter — the single page that broke the conspiracy — has never been attributed. Suspicion has settled for four centuries on Francis Tresham, a late-recruited plotter and Monteagle’s brother-in-law, but nothing was ever proven, and Tresham died in the Tower before the question could be put to him with the era’s usual thoroughness. Which means the most consequential piece of writing in the history of English conspiracy is an anonymous publication — unsigned, undated, its author withheld from the record permanently. This series spent essay 1,519 on a man who signed everything and published nothing. Here is his inverse: a writer who published one page, signed nothing, and saved a government. The ledger of authorship has entries missing in both directions.

The second footnote is linguistic, and it is my favourite fact in this essay. The bonfires needed effigies; the effigies were of Fawkes; the effigies were called guys; and the word, over the centuries, slid from the burned figure to any oddly dressed fellow, then to any fellow at all — until today, four hundred years on, half the English-speaking world addresses its friends, its colleagues, and its group chats with the name of the man in the cellar. Hey, guys. The plot that failed to remove one government inserted its triggerman into every casual sentence in the language. No successful conspirator has ever achieved a fraction of that distribution. It is the strangest afterlife in this series: the rhyme kept his date, and the crowd kept his name, and neither one remembers they are doing it.

Now Madrid, and the better man.

Miguel de Cervantes came to the novel by way of the most thoroughly documented failure in Spanish literature, and the documentation is the point, because his failures were failures of counting. A soldier at Lepanto, where his left hand was maimed; five years a captive in Algiers, ransomed at ruinous cost; and then — here the Numbers Game leans forward — a career in accounts. In the late 1580s Cervantes worked as a royal commissary, requisitioning grain and oil from unwilling villages for the provisioning of the great fleet then assembling. I will let the readers of essay 1,588 arrive at it themselves: yes. That fleet. The Most Fortunate Armada sailed, in some fraction, on wheat that Miguel de Cervantes had counted into the royal stores. The greatest novelist of the age spent the 1580s as a purchasing agent for the most expensive adjective in naval history, and when the adjective sank, he moved on to tax collecting, and the tax collecting went the way of everything else: entrusted funds lodged with a merchant who failed, books that would not reconcile, and at least one spell in a Seville jail over the discrepancies. Tradition — with the standing tongs, for it rests on his own sly preface — holds that Don Quixote was engendered in that jail. I choose to believe it on the grounds that it is exactly how this series would write it: the age’s most honest imagination, imprisoned by its ledgers, dreaming up a man who refuses to count.

Because that is what Don Quixote is, read from where I sit. The famous scene — thirty or forty windmills on the plain, which the knight declares to be giants and charges — is always filed as madness, and it is madness, but attend to the bookkeeping of it. Sancho Panza gives his master the correct inventory: those are windmills, they have sails, the sails turn in the wind. The count is accurate and it is delivered in good faith by the most reliable auditor in the book. And the knight overrides the count — not by disputing the arithmetic, but by appeal to a higher ledger in which he has already been enrolled as a knight, the plain as a battlefield, and the windmills as giants, and in which the entries were made by the romances he read before he ever rode out. Quixote is the man for whom the story has been posted first, so that reality arrives as a discrepancy to be explained. The wizard Frestón, he decides, transformed the giants at the last moment to cheat him of the victory. Note the move. The count was wrong because an enemy tampered with it. I write about that move under other datelines, weekly, filed from a country where the windmills have lawyers.

And here is why I said history got the two men backwards. Guy Fawkes was perfectly sane. His count was accurate, his barrels sufficient, his cause coherent within its own cruel arithmetic; he was at war with a government. Quixote was at war with the inventory itself — and Cervantes, who had been jailed by inventories, knew precisely what he was writing: the first full-length study of a mind that prefers its enrolled story to the delivered count, written by a man whom the delivered count had personally ruined. The plot got the rhyme. The novel got everything else — the second part in 1615, the imitators, the word quixotic lodged in every language — because Cervantes had found the subject that does not expire. Cellars are searched and emptied. The other thing is never empty. Four hundred and twenty-one years on, I can report from the field that the supply of men charging windmills, certain the giants were swapped out by their enemies at the last moment, remains fully provisioned. Cervantes counted that correctly, and it is the only account he ever balanced.

Dior encountered a windmill once — a decorative one, on a lawn in the Beaches, turning briskly — and her response deserves entry in the record: she stopped, assessed it for four seconds, catalogued it as neither food, friend, nor squirrel, and moved on without adjusting her opinion of the neighbourhood. It is the whole of sanity in four seconds. Sancho would have hired her on the spot, and she would have declined, Dior having no interest in governing an island, the household she governs being trouble enough.

The count moves on, as it does. The next landmark is essay 1,609, and it concerns a professor of mathematics in Padua who heard a rumour about a Dutch toy, built a better one in weeks, sold it to the Venetian Senate as a naval early-warning system at a handsome raise — and then, having secured the salary, made the mistake of pointing the instrument up. What he counted there could not be uncounted, and the century, which opened with a rhyme and a windmill, will spend its middle decades learning what that costs.

The plot failed and got the rhyme. The novel succeeded and got the language. The windmills, for the record, are still turning, and they have never once been giants.

See you at 1,609, when a man points a tube at Jupiter and the inventory grows by four.
