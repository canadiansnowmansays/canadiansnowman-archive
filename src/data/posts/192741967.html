Vladimir Putin addresses the Munich Security Conference, February 10, 2007. “The unipolar world… did not take place,” he declared. The speech was dismissed as posturing. It was, in reality, an early warning that the post-Cold War order was already eroding from within.

On February 10, 2007, Vladimir Putin stood at the podium of the Munich Security Conference — the annual gathering of defense ministers, foreign policy officials, and strategic thinkers from across the Western alliance — and delivered a speech that should have ended all illusions about the post-Cold War order.

“The unipolar world that had been proposed after the Cold War did not take place,” Putin said. He described the United States as having overstepped its national borders in every sphere — economic, political, cultural, and educational — imposing its norms on other states and defining itself as the center of the world. He warned against NATO expansion. He criticized American missile defense plans in Eastern Europe. He argued for a multipolar world in which Russia’s interests would be respected.

The speech was received in Munich with visible discomfort. Condoleezza Rice, then Secretary of State, described it as “disappointing.” Senator John McCain, who was also present, called it “the posturing of a 20th century leader trying to look tough.” Western commentators largely dismissed it as nostalgia for Soviet greatness — the grievances of a declining power that had not accepted its diminishment.

Nobody said: he has a point.

They should have. Not because Putin’s argument was correct in all its dimensions — his characterization of American behavior was self-serving and selective, his vision of Russian primacy was as aggressive as anything he was criticizing in American policy, and his subsequent actions in Georgia in 2008, Ukraine in 2014, and Syria from 2015 demonstrated that his concern for international norms was entirely instrumental.

But the structural argument was accurate. The unipolar moment had produced exactly the overconfidence, the norm-violation, and the legitimacy damage that its critics had predicted. The Iraq War had demonstrated that American power could be exercised outside the rules-based order with impunity. If that was the case, the question for every other significant power was not whether to accept the order but whether to build alternatives to it.

Both Russia and China drew that conclusion, on their own timelines and through their own mechanisms. The story of how they did it — and what it means for the world that is now coming into being — is the story this chapter tells.

Russia: The Revanchist

Putin’s path from KGB officer to authoritarian ruler to revanchist imperialist is well documented and less interesting, for our purposes, than the structural conditions that made his project possible.

Russia in the 1990s was a catastrophe. The shock therapy that Western economists prescribed for the transition from Soviet central planning to market capitalism produced, in practice, a decade of economic collapse, hyperinflation, institutional disintegration, and the transfer of state assets to a small group of well-connected insiders who became the oligarchs. GDP fell by roughly 40 percent between 1990 and 1998. Life expectancy declined. The demographic crisis that has haunted Russia ever since — low birthrates, high mortality, declining population — accelerated.

The humiliation was as much psychological as economic. A country that had been a superpower, that had launched Sputnik and Gagarin, that had fought the Nazi Wehrmacht to a standstill and then to destruction, that had maintained a global empire for decades — this country watched its currency collapse, its military disintegrate, and its former satellites join the alliance it had spent the Cold War opposing. The West’s triumphalism was not imagined. It was real, and it was rubbed in.

Putin offered Russians a narrative of restoration. He brought order to the chaos of the Yeltsin years. He channeled the windfall of rising oil prices in the 2000s into state investment, military modernization, and the reassertion of Russian influence in the former Soviet space. He told Russians that their humiliation was temporary and reversible, that the greatness of their civilization would be restored, that the post-Cold War order had been imposed on them rather than negotiated with them.

This narrative was, in significant part, manufactured. But it was not manufactured from nothing. The grievances it mobilized were real. The NATO expansion that George Kennan had warned about was real. The dismissiveness of Western officials toward Russian security concerns was real. The 1999 bombing of Serbia — a Slavic Orthodox country with which Russia had traditional ties — without UN authorization was a real precedent for the kind of unilateralism that Russia would subsequently practice.

None of this excuses Putin’s decisions. The invasion of Georgia in 2008, the annexation of Crimea in 2014, the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 — these were choices made by a specific individual within a specific institutional framework, and they could have been made differently. The structural conditions that made them possible do not determine them.

But understanding the structural conditions is essential to understanding why the post-war order proved more fragile than its architects had hoped. The order depended on the buy-in of major powers — on the calculation by significant states that the order’s benefits outweighed the costs of participation. When the United States demonstrated, through Iraq and through NATO expansion, that the order’s rules applied to the weak but not to the strong, it undermined the legitimacy argument on which buy-in depended.

Russia’s conclusion was not that the order needed reform. It was that the order was a racket — a system designed to lock in American dominance under the guise of universal rules — and that Russia’s interests required building alternatives, or at minimum, demonstrating that the system’s rules could be violated by others as well as by Americans.

The invasion of Ukraine in 2022 was, among other things, this demonstration made explicit. Russia was applying the Iraq doctrine — the assertion that a major power could ignore the UN Security Council, invade a sovereign country on the basis of contested claims about security threats, and conduct a war of regime change — to its own neighborhood. The fact that Russia was wrong and the United States was wrong does not mean the parallel is invalid. It means the precedent was dangerous.

China: The Patient Builder

China’s high-speed rail and massive container ports embodied the extraordinary economic transformation that lifted 800 million people out of poverty. Patient, disciplined, and strategic, this rise occurred inside the existing international order — until China grew strong enough to begin reshaping it.

China’s rise is a different story, told on a different timeline, through different mechanisms. Where Russia’s response to the post-Cold War order was essentially reactive — grievance translated into aggression — China’s was essentially proactive: the patient accumulation of the economic, technological, and institutional power that would eventually make the existing order negotiable on Chinese terms.

Deng Xiaoping’s famous instruction to China’s leadership in the 1990s — “hide your strength, bide your time” — was the strategic framework for this project. China would participate in the existing international order, benefit from its institutions, and use its access to the global economy to build the foundation for eventual primacy. It would not challenge the order frontally — it would grow within it until it was strong enough to reshape it.

The results were extraordinary. China’s GDP grew at approximately 10 percent annually for three decades, lifting 800 million people out of poverty in the largest reduction in human deprivation in history. By the early 2020s China was the world’s largest economy by purchasing power parity, the world’s largest manufacturer, the world’s largest trading nation, and the world’s largest holder of US Treasury bonds.

This economic foundation was converted, with systematic determination, into political and military power. The People’s Liberation Army was modernized and professionalized. China’s naval capacity expanded dramatically, with aircraft carriers, advanced submarines, and anti-ship missile systems explicitly designed to deny American naval dominance in the Western Pacific — the A2/AD strategy, area denial and anti-access, that has fundamentally changed the strategic calculus of any potential conflict over Taiwan.

The Belt and Road Initiative, launched in 2013, extended Chinese economic relationships across Asia, Africa, and parts of Europe through infrastructure investment on a scale no single country had attempted since the Marshall Plan. Chinese technology companies — Huawei, Alibaba, Tencent, TikTok — achieved global scale in ways that made the question of technological standards a geopolitical contest rather than a commercial one.

Colombo Port City, Sri Lanka — one of the flagship projects of China’s Belt and Road Initiative. What began as economic outreach became the physical backbone of an alternative institutional architecture, built while the United States watched.

China also built alternative international institutions. The Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, established in 2015 with fifty-seven founding members including American allies like Britain, Germany, France, and Australia — over explicit American objections — was the most significant Chinese institutional creation since the People’s Republic itself. The Shanghai Cooperation Organisation, the BRICS grouping, the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership — each represented a piece of the alternative institutional architecture that China was constructing alongside, and in eventual competition with, the American-designed frameworks of the post-war order.

Throughout this process, China maintained studied ambiguity about its long-term intentions. Was it seeking to reform the existing order or replace it? Were its institutional alternatives supplements to the existing framework or alternatives to it? The ambiguity was strategic — it allowed China to benefit from the existing order while building the capacity to reshape it, maintaining access to American technology and markets while developing the alternatives that would eventually reduce that dependence.

Xi Jinping’s accession to power in 2012 marked the end of strategic ambiguity. His vision of the “China Dream” — the great rejuvenation of the Chinese nation, the restoration of China’s historical centrality in Asian and global affairs — was explicitly revisionist. The existing order had been built by others, in China’s period of weakness. It would be revised when China was strong enough to revise it.

The Convergence

The most consequential development of the 2010s — the development whose implications were least appreciated in Washington — was the gradual convergence of Russian and Chinese strategic interests.

Russia and China had been rivals and, briefly, enemies throughout most of the Cold War. The Sino-Soviet split of the 1960s, the border clashes of 1969, Nixon’s opening to China — which was explicitly designed as a counterweight to Soviet power — all reflected the structural competition between the two Eurasian powers that the post-war order had assumed would continue.

But the Iraq War, NATO expansion, American democracy promotion, Western sanctions, and the general assertiveness of unipolar American power had given Russia and China a shared grievance: the sense that the existing international order was designed to perpetuate American dominance at the expense of other major powers. Their specific interests diverged on many issues. But their interest in building alternatives to American-dominated institutions, in weakening the dollar’s reserve currency status, in reducing American military reach, and in establishing the principle that major powers had zones of influence that Western liberal norms did not override — these interests converged sufficiently to produce a strategic alignment without formal alliance.

The Putin-Xi “no limits” partnership announced in February 2022 — days before Russia’s invasion of Ukraine — was the formalization of a convergence that had been building for two decades. It was not an alliance in the NATO sense, with specific mutual defense commitments. It was something more flexible and in some ways more durable: a shared commitment to the multipolarity that both countries defined as the alternative to American hegemony, expressed through economic cooperation, diplomatic coordination, and the quiet undermining of American-dominated institutions.

Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping announce their “no limits” strategic partnership in Beijing, February 4, 2022 — just weeks before Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine. The alignment was not a formal alliance but a durable convergence against American-dominated order.

In the Iran war of 2026, this convergence is visible in every dimension. Russia is providing Iran with satellite imagery and advanced drone capabilities. China is ensuring its oil shipments through a strait that has been closed to everyone else. Pakistan — formally nonaligned but increasingly within the Chinese orbit — is the primary diplomatic mediator, conducting shuttle diplomacy between Washington and Tehran with Chinese backing. The Islamabad talks framework that Pakistan is proposing requires Chinese support to succeed. The dollar’s reserve currency status is being tested at exactly the chokepoint — the Strait of Hormuz — that the petrodollar system requires to be open.

This is what the convergence of Russia and China looks like in practice: not a frontal military challenge to American power but a systematic erosion of the conditions on which American power depends.

The American Response: Too Little, Too Late

The United States was not unaware of China’s rise or Russia’s revanchism. The Obama administration’s “pivot to Asia” in 2011 was an explicit recognition that the strategic center of gravity was shifting from the Middle East — where America had been enmired for a decade — to the Pacific, where China’s growing power represented the most significant long-term challenge to American primacy.

The pivot was real in its rhetoric and inadequate in its execution. The Trans-Pacific Partnership — the trade agreement that was supposed to be the economic pillar of the pivot, binding Pacific nations to an American-centered trading framework that excluded China — was negotiated, signed, and then abandoned by the Trump administration in January 2017. The military reorientation toward the Pacific that the pivot required was impeded by the continuing demands of the Middle East, where the ISIS crisis pulled resources and attention back toward a region America had been trying to exit.

The Obama administration also failed to respond adequately to Russia’s annexation of Crimea in 2014 — a failure that emboldened the subsequent escalation. The sanctions imposed were real but insufficient. The military support to Ukraine was deliberately limited to avoid “provoking” Russia — the exact logic that Putin had been counting on, that NATO’s response to Russian aggression would be calibrated to avoid escalation rather than to deter it.

By the time Trump took office in January 2017, the window for managing China’s rise within the existing order had largely closed. China was already the world’s largest economy by purchasing power parity. Its military modernization had already fundamentally altered the strategic balance in the Western Pacific. The Belt and Road had already created economic relationships across the developing world that would be difficult and expensive to counter.

Hugh Keenleyside died in 1992, the year the Soviet Union dissolved — the same year that many in Washington concluded that the hard work of institution-building was finished, that history had ended, that the rivals had been defeated. He had spent his career understanding that rivals were never finished, that the work of building legitimate international order required constant maintenance, and that the vacuum American power left when it overreached or withdrew would always be filled by something. He did not live to see China’s rise or Putin’s revanchism. He would not have been surprised by either.
