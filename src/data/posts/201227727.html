America’s 200th birthday, in 1976, was a party with a point.

The Bicentennial was not without its critics — it was produced during the Ford administration, in the long shadow of Watergate and Vietnam, and the gap between the celebratory rhetoric and the national mood was, at times, visible from orbit. There was a substantial counter-celebration organized by people who thought the official festivities were whitewashing a history that deserved reckoning rather than bunting. There were arguments, in the press and in Congress, about what the birthday was supposed to mean.

But there was a birthday. There was a keynote. There was an argument being made — however contested, however incomplete — about what America was and why its 200th year was worth marking. Tall ships sailed into New York Harbor. Philadelphia hosted a world’s fair. The Liberty Bell was moved so the public could see it up close. The National Mall hosted celebrations that drew millions of people. The occasion had content. It had intention. It had, at minimum, a theory of what was being commemorated.

Fifty years later, the 250th anniversary of the same republic has produced: a mixed-martial-arts bout on the South Lawn of the White House. A Formula 1 race through the streets of Washington, D.C. A proposal for a colossal archway near the entrance to Arlington National Cemetery. A National Garden of American Heroes, featuring 250 historic figures chosen according to criteria that have not been publicly explained and appear to owe more to fame than to any coherent argument about what makes an American heroic.

And a question that the occasion’s planners have conspicuously declined to answer: what, exactly, are we celebrating?

I. The Semiquincentennial Commission and the Punt

The planning for the 250th anniversary began, formally, during the Biden administration. The federal Semiquincentennial Commission had been established by Congress, and its mandate was to plan national celebrations worthy of the occasion.

What the Biden administration produced was, in its way, an honest response to an impossible problem. Having watched the history wars consume every attempt to establish a common national narrative, having seen the 1619 Project debate and the 1776 Commission counter-debate play out in real time, the Biden team made a decision that was politically understandable and civically catastrophic: they punted.

The commission invited people to record their personal history, on the theory that “your story is America’s story.” Local institutions were encouraged to plan events that would speak to their particular communities. The 250th birthday would not have a keynote. It would not have an argument. It would have a million individual stories, each valid, none connected to any other by a common thread.

This is a generous impulse. Personal history matters. Local community matters. The recognition that the American story is not one story but many is an important corrective to the consensus historians’ flattering single narrative. And yet.

A nation that cannot tell a common story on its 250th birthday — that can only point to the aggregate of its individual stories and say: all of this, somehow, is us — is not a creedal nation with a functioning creed. It is a geography with a flag. The individual stories are real and valuable. But they are not, by themselves, a creed. They do not answer the question of what the proposition is or why it matters or what it asks of anyone. They are the raw material for a story, not the story itself.

The Biden administration looked at the raw material and decided it was enough. It was not.

II. What Trump Did Instead

Donald Trump, who returned to office in 2025 with considerable enthusiasm for putting his stamp on the semiquincentennial, had no interest in the Biden approach. He wanted spectacle. He wanted celebrity. He wanted his name, in the most literal sense possible, on the occasion.

What he produced is, as a diagnostic document, extraordinarily revealing.

The UFC bout on the South Lawn is the clearest expression of the Trump theory of American greatness: raw physical competition, staged for maximum entertainment value, held on the grounds of the republic’s most symbolically charged address. It is not a celebration of American ideals. It is a celebration of American spectacle — of the country’s unparalleled capacity to produce entertainment, to commodify conflict, to turn anything, including its own birthday, into a television event.

The Formula 1 race through Washington — a European sport, it is worth noting, run through streets named for the Founders — is similarly diagnostic. It is globally prestigious. It is visually spectacular. It has absolutely nothing to say about what America is or what it has meant to the world. It is a product placement, not a commemoration.

The visual evidence compounds the diagnostic. The Reflecting Pool in front of the Lincoln Memorial has, this spring, gone green with algae, untended and highly visible to the tourists arriving for the birthday. The East Wing of the White House has been demolished. There is a planned triumphal arch sized to interrupt the sightline between the Lincoln Memorial and Arlington National Cemetery — the conversation, as Jeffrey Goldberg described it on The Atlantic’s David Frum Show, between a martyred president and the war dead buried on Robert E. Lee’s former plantation. And inside the White House itself, the Ionic columns — civilian in association, tied to learning and republican restraint — are reportedly giving way to Corinthian ones, the order the Romans reserved for temples and imperial luxury. None of this is subtle. A nation that cannot articulate what its birthday is celebrating has, apparently, decided to redecorate instead.

Mark Twain would have recognized the type of celebration before he recognized the century. As Ron Chernow observed in The Atlantic, Twain — who coined the term "Gilded Age" and reserved his sharpest contempt for the plutocrats who mistook spectacle for substance — would likely have found in the ballroom, the arch, and the gold-plated vanity projects of this anniversary exactly the kind of humbug he spent a career skewering. He is on record admiring showmen. He is also on record loathing the confusion of showmanship with achievement, and there is no reason to think this anniversary would have earned an exception.

Stephen Leacock — my own lodestar, as this series has already confessed — would have recognized something gentler and perhaps more damning: not corruption dressed up as celebration, but a town, in this case an entire capital, mistaking its own bunting for substance. Leacock spent a career mapping Mariposa and Plutoria Avenue, his fictional stand-ins for small-town Ontario and new-money Toronto, and both were built on the same observation: the civic body convinced of its own cosmic importance, the wealthy man who erects a monument to himself and calls it public spirit, the boosters who cannot tell a parade from an achievement. He would not have needed Twain’s fury for the UFC bout or the arch. He would have needed only his usual patient irony — the kind he spent on Mariposa’s council meetings and Plutoria Avenue’s dinner parties — to note that a nation celebrating itself with a cage match and a Formula One race is behaving exactly like his fictional Mariposa, a town so pleased with its own importance that it could turn even a hotel fire into a civic triumph, provided the band kept playing and enough people were watching. Twain raged at humbug. Leacock smiled at it, sadly, and kept writing — which may be the more useful posture for a Canadian trying to get through this particular July.

The ground-level evidence bears this out with a precision no essayist could invent. As Alexandra Petri documented in The Atlantic after a day at the Great American State Fair on the National Mall, the semiquincentennial's actual texture includes a $6 energy drink called Screamin' Freedom, a James Madison impersonator who spends his allotted stage time talking about Thomas Jefferson instead, and a Department of Education booth raffling off a "copy" of the original draft of the Declaration of Independence. A nearby chart, from an organization calling itself the American Journey Experience, informed fairgoers that 34 percent of 15,000 founding-era political documents cited the Bible — a statistic offered, apparently, as self-evident. This is not satire. This is the government-adjacent programming actually produced for the occasion. The Garden of Heroes is not an aberration. It is the theme, repeated at every booth.

The National Garden of American Heroes is the most revealing document of all. As Appelbaum observes, the list of 250 honorees bears less resemblance to a pantheon collectively embodying the American idea than to the setup of a bad joke: Davy Crockett, Julia Child, and Kobe Bryant walk into a bar. These are real Americans, all of them — Crockett the frontiersman, Child the chef, Bryant the athlete. They are famous. They are, in their various ways, admirable. They have essentially nothing in common beyond their nationality and their celebrity, and their juxtaposition suggests that the people who compiled the list were not asking what these figures represent about the American idea — but simply who American audiences would recognize.

A national pantheon that is organized by recognition rather than meaning is not a celebration of American ideals. It is a Gallup poll of American fame.

III. The Executive Order and Its Logic

In March 2026, the Trump administration issued an executive order on “restoring truth and sanity to American history.” The order instructed the National Park Service to remove signs that “inappropriately disparage” historical figures, and to focus instead on the “greatness” of American achievements.

The consequences were immediate. A display on George Washington’s enslaved people came down at Independence Hall in Philadelphia. An exhibit at the Grand Canyon was stripped of a passage noting that federal officials had “pushed tribes off their land” to establish the park. Museums and historic sites across the country began reviewing their interpretive materials for anything that might be construed as disparagement.

I want to be precise about what this order is and is not.

It is not an argument about historical accuracy. No one in the administration has claimed that the removed information was false. The enslaved people at Mount Vernon were real. The displacement of Indigenous peoples to create the Grand Canyon National Park is documented history. The order does not dispute these facts. It instructs that they be removed from public display not because they are wrong but because they are inconvenient — because they complicate the preferred narrative of greatness and achievement.

This is censorship. Not in the casual political-rhetoric sense of the word, but in its precise meaning: the suppression of true information by a government authority because the information does not serve the authority’s preferred story. And it reveals, more clearly than anything else the administration has done around the semiquincentennial, the theory of patriotism that underlies the spectacle.

The theory is this: Americans will love their country more if they know less about it. The way to produce patriotism is to suppress the facts that might complicate it. The way to honor the republic is to protect its official story from the evidence of its actual history.

This theory has a name and a genealogy, and it is older and stranger than a single executive order. As Jim Rasenberger documents in The Atlantic, the belief that America is not merely a good country but a divinely chosen one runs from Winthrop's "city upon a hill" through Jefferson's talk of an "overruling providence," and it has always come paired with John Adams's cold rejoinder: "We are not a chosen People, that I know of. We must and We Shall, go the Way of all the Earth." The current administration has revived the chosen-nation strain with unusual literalism. The president has described himself as saved by God after the 2024 assassination attempt, posted images of himself as Jesus, and dusted off "manifest destiny" as a rationale for annexing territory. His defense secretary has invoked providence to justify killing Iranians, in language explicit enough to draw an apparent rebuke from the pope. Suppressing inconvenient history is not merely a public-relations strategy in this framework. It is a theological necessity: a chosen people cannot be shown evidence that complicates their chosenness without the whole edifice wobbling. Adams saw the danger in this two centuries ago. "Instead of the most enlightened people," he warned, "I fear we Americans shall soon have the character of the silliest people under Heaven."

This theory is empirically wrong. As Appelbaum cites, large majorities of Americans prefer history that challenges what they know over accounts that reinforce it. By an almost nine-to-one margin, they think it is at least as important to learn about other racial and ethnic communities as about their own. They overwhelmingly agree on the importance of learning about past harms, even when doing so makes them uncomfortable. Nikole Hannah-Jones, who has spent years travelling to every kind of American community discussing the 1619 Project, reports that she has never had someone walk away saying they hate the country. They say they are ashamed of some things the country has done. They say they want it to be better. They do not say the story is not worth knowing.

The administration’s theory of patriotism — that it is produced by ignorance rather than by honest reckoning — is contradicted by everything the evidence shows about how Americans actually relate to their history.

IV. What a Real Semiquincentennial Would Require

I want to be fair to the difficulty of the task.

Planning a 250th birthday for a nation of 335 million people, in the most politically polarized period since the Civil War, is not easy. There is no version of a semiquincentennial that pleases everyone. There is no speech that can be written that both the post-American left and the hyper-American right would endorse. There is no pantheon of heroes that would not be contested. There is no narrative that does not require choices about emphasis, about inclusion, about what the story is and who it belongs to.

The 1976 Bicentennial was contested too. The tall ships and the world’s fair were accompanied by protests, by counter-celebrations, by serious arguments about whether the occasion was being used to paper over the Nixon years and the Vietnam catastrophe and the ongoing struggle for Black civil rights. The celebration had critics. It also had content.

A real semiquincentennial — one adequate to the occasion and to the moment — would require, at minimum, an attempt to answer the question that the Biden administration declined to answer and the Trump administration has answered with spectacle: what is being celebrated?

The answer that the evidence of 250 years supports is not a simple one. What is being celebrated is the ongoing argument about whether the creedal bargain is real — the 250-year record of people demanding that the proposition mean what it says, and the record of resistance to that demand, and the imperfect, incomplete, still-unfinished process by which the creed has been extended, generation by generation, to people who were told it did not apply to them.

That is not a comfortable story. It is not a simple story. It cannot be reduced to a UFC bout or a Formula 1 race or a garden of famous people. It requires that the audience know enough history to understand what is being marked — which, given that fourteen percent of American students are proficient in the subject, is itself a problem the celebration cannot solve.

But the attempt matters. The attempt is itself a claim — that the story is coherent enough to be told, that the argument is worth making, that the 250th year of the republic is an occasion that deserves more than spectacle.

The attempt was not made.

Or, to be fair, not made where the cameras were pointed.

A few blocks from the South Lawn, inside a 180-year-old building actively under construction, the Smithsonian mounted an exhibition it calls “American Aspirations”: Jefferson’s portable writing desk, Harriet Tubman’s book of hymns, Roberto Clemente’s Pirates jersey, the Medal of Honor awarded to a Black Navy sailor named James Mifflin about whom almost nothing else is known. As the Smithsonian’s Lonnie Bunch III described the project in The Atlantic, its curators organized the objects not by decade but by aspiration — pursuit of freedom, pursuit of progress, pursuit of hope — precisely because a chronological arrangement would flatten the thing they were actually trying to show: a nation still being made, still working to attain what its founding document promised and did not deliver. This is, almost exactly, the mainstream-school argument this series has been making for eight parts. It exists. It was built, seriously and at real institutional cost, in the same season as the algae and the arch. It is simply not what will be remembered from this particular July, because it is not what got the UFC’s production budget or the president’s attention. The attempt was made. It just wasn’t the headline.

V. The Canadian View from the Street

I watch the American birthday from a particular vantage point — one that is close enough to feel the occasion and far enough away to see its shape.

Canada does not have a single founding moment with the symbolic clarity of July 4, 1776. Confederation in 1867 was a political negotiation, not a revolution. We do not celebrate it with the intensity or the mythology that Americans bring to their Independence Day. We do not, frankly, bring much mythology to it at all. Canada Day is a pleasant summer holiday with fireworks and some flag-waving, and the national mood toward it is, depending on the province and the year, somewhere between affectionate and indifferent.

I have always thought that Americans were fortunate to have a founding moment as clean and dramatic as July 4 — a single day, a single document, a single set of claims about what the country was for. It gives the birthday an argument built in. You do not have to invent the occasion. You can simply ask: are we living up to what we said on this day?

The tragedy of the 250th anniversary is that the political system has been unable to ask that question seriously — either because it is afraid of the honest answer, or because it has given up on the idea that there is a shared “we” capable of asking it together.

The UFC bout is, in its way, the answer. We do not know what we are celebrating, so we will celebrate the thing we know how to do: produce entertainment. We do not know what we stand for, so we will stand for spectacle. We do not know whose story it is, so we will tell no story — only host an event.

Davy Crockett, Julia Child, and Kobe Bryant walk into a bar. The bartender asks what they’re celebrating. None of them can answer.

At 250, that is the semiquincentennial. It is not enough. The country deserves better, and — this is the thing I keep coming back to — the evidence suggests that most Americans know it.

The hunger for the real story is there. The supply is the problem.
