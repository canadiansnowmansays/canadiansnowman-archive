Saskatchewan Prairie — Vast, flat, wind-punished southern prairies. This is where the Haldeman story begins.

The story of the most powerful private citizen in the modern world begins in Herbert, Saskatchewan, population 759, where a Minnesota-born chiropractor named Joshua Norman Haldeman arrived in 1907 with his family and settled into the flat, wind-punished landscape of the southern prairies.

Haldeman was not a quiet man. He farmed 5,000 acres, studied at Moose Jaw College where he met his wife Winnifred, rode broncs, organized one of Canada’s first rodeos, earned his pilot’s licence, and built a chiropractic practice. He also threw himself into politics with a fervour that the prairies have always been generous in producing and Confederation has always been ill-equipped to absorb.

The Great Depression took his farm. The loss radicalised him. Haldeman never stopped raging at what he perceived as the systemic forces behind the catastrophe — banks, bureaucracies, and what he came to describe as an “Invisible Government” carrying out the objectives of an international conspiracy. He became the research director for Technocracy Incorporated in Canada, a movement founded in New York in 1933 that proposed replacing elected politicians with scientists and engineers who would manage society according to scientific principles. The movement’s ultimate vision was a “Technate” — a unified continental entity stretching from Panama to the North Pole, governed by technical expertise rather than democratic politics.

Pause on that for a moment. Elon Musk’s maternal grandfather led a Canadian political movement whose central thesis was that scientists and engineers should run everything, that politicians were an impediment to progress, and that a continent-spanning technical civilization was the optimal form of human organisation. In 2019, Musk tweeted that he was “accelerating Starship development to build the Martian Technocracy.” The apple did not fall far from the tree. It fell on a different planet.

Technocracy was banned by Ottawa in 1940 under the Defence of Canada Act as part of a wartime sweep of organisations considered subversive. Haldeman was arrested and charged in a Regina courtroom with stirring up disloyalty to the King. He was convicted. He placed an ad in the Regina Leader-Post calling the government’s move an “unjustified political blunder.” He was not a man who accepted authority gracefully — another trait that would express itself, amplified by several orders of magnitude, in his grandson.

After Technocracy, Haldeman founded his own political party — Total War and Defence — which advocated conscripting every employable person between sixteen and sixty, along with all natural resources, industrial equipment, and private bank holdings. The movement did not catch on. He moved to the Social Credit Party, served as its national chairman, and ran for Parliament three times: losing to the Co-operative Commonwealth Federation’s candidate in 1945 (the same riding where William Lyon Mackenzie King also lost), losing to Tommy Douglas in the 1948 Saskatchewan election, and losing to John Diefenbaker in 1949. Three elections. Three losses. Three future prime ministers or equivalent national figures among his opponents.

By 1950, Haldeman was done with Canada. He emigrated to South Africa with his wife, his children, his chiropractic credentials, and his grievances. His motivation was not subtle. Errol Musk — Haldeman’s future son-in-law — later said the family was “very fanatical in favour of apartheid.” Haldeman told the Afrikaner nationalist newspaper Die Transvaler that the government’s racial attitudes had “encouraged me to come and settle here.” In 1951, writing for the Regina Leader-Post — the same newspaper where he had once defended Technocracy — he described Black South Africans as “very primitive” who “must not be taken seriously.”

This is uncomfortable material for a series about Elon Musk, and it is essential material for a series about Elon Musk. The man who now controls the world’s dominant satellite network, a major social media platform, an AI encyclopedia, and a semiconductor fabrication facility is the grandson of a man who left Canada for apartheid South Africa because he preferred the politics. The man who tweets about building civilization on Mars carries the DNA — intellectual if not necessarily ideological — of a man who believed scientists and engineers should replace democratic governance, that international conspiracies controlled the banks and media, and that the appropriate response to political failure was to emigrate to a country more hospitable to your views.

I am not arguing that Elon Musk is his grandfather. I am observing that the patterns rhyme. The distrust of institutions. The conviction that technical expertise should override democratic process. The willingness to leave when a country disappoints you. The scale of ambition untethered from the modesty that most people — most Canadians, certainly — would apply to their own capabilities. Joshua Haldeman believed he was right and that the systems around him were broken. His grandson believes the same thing, with considerably more resources to act on it.

Pretoria / Childhood section — Pretoria skyline, unmistakably South African contrast to the Canadian plains.

In Pretoria, Haldeman’s daughter Maye grew up, became a model, married an electromechanical engineer named Errol Musk, and on June 28, 1971, gave birth to a son they named Elon. The marriage was unhappy and eventually abusive. The childhood was difficult. Musk was bullied severely at school — beaten badly enough to be hospitalised on at least one occasion. He retreated into books. Science fiction, primarily. Isaac Asimov. Robert Heinlein. And Douglas Adams, whose Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy gave a lonely, beaten-up kid in Pretoria a vision of a universe that was absurd, vast, and navigable if you had the right reference book and didn’t take authority too seriously.

Musk has said that the Hitchhiker’s Guide taught him that the hardest part of finding the answer is knowing what question to ask. “42” — Adams’s joke about the futility of seeking the meaning of life through computation — became a reference point Musk returns to repeatedly. The irony, which Adams would have savoured, is that Musk appears to have taken the satire literally. Adams wrote the Guide as a comedy about an unreliable reference book in an uncaring universe. Musk is building the actual Guide — Grokipedia — and deploying it worldwide through his actual satellite network, which is considerably more reliable than Adams’s Sub-Etha Net but serves an eerily similar function.

By seventeen, Musk had decided South Africa held nothing for him. Mandatory military service loomed — service that would have meant enforcing the apartheid system his grandfather had emigrated to support. The irony is precise: Joshua Haldeman came to South Africa because he approved of its politics. His grandson left because he didn’t want to serve them.

The escape route was Canadian. Maye Haldeman Musk had been born in Regina, Saskatchewan. She had never renounced her Canadian citizenship. Her son — seventeen, bookish, bruised from years of bullying, and armed with the kind of determination that comes from having read enough science fiction to believe the universe owes you a better story — went to the Canadian consulate in Pretoria on his own, obtained passport application forms, and filled them out not only for himself but for his mother, brother, and sister. Not for his father. The applications were approved in late May 1989.

He arrived in Canada in June 1989 with roughly $4,000 — $2,000 in traveller’s cheques from his father, $2,000 from his mother’s stock account, originally funded by beauty pageant winnings. He had a list of relatives he had never met. He planned to call his mother’s uncle in Montreal but discovered the man had left. He went to a youth hostel, shared a room with five strangers, and slept on his backpack.

“I was used to South Africa, where people will just rob and kill you,” he later said. “So I slept on my backpack until I realized that not everyone was a murderer.”

He wandered Montreal marvelling that people did not have bars on their windows.

This is worth sitting with. The person who would become the wealthiest human being in history arrived in Canada at seventeen with $4,000, a backpack, a list of strangers, and the slowly dawning realisation that not every country requires you to fortify your home against violence. Canada was safe. Canada was open. Canada was the first place Elon Musk lived where the default human interaction was not threat.

He bought a $100 Greyhound Discovery Pass — unlimited bus travel anywhere in Canada for six months — and headed west to Saskatchewan, where he connected with a second cousin named Mark Teulon who lived on a farm near Waldeck, outside Swift Current. He worked on the farm. He cleaned the boiler room at a lumber mill. He shovelled grain. These were not internships at Stanford. These were the kinds of jobs that seventeen-year-olds in rural Saskatchewan do because there are no other jobs, and the fact that Elon Musk did them does not make them romantic. It makes him, for a few months in 1989, a Canadian labourer — one of millions of people who have done the same work for the same wages in the same wind.

Queen’s University — Grant Hall, the iconic landmark every Queen’s student knows

In the fall of 1990, Musk enrolled at Queen’s University in Kingston, Ontario. He had considered Waterloo — “definitely better for engineering” — but chose Queen’s because it had more women. “I didn’t want to spend my undergraduate time with a bunch of dudes,” he later explained, with the kind of candour that has characterised his public statements ever since, for better and considerably worse.

At Queen’s, he was placed on the international floor of his dormitory, where he met Navaid Farooq, who became what Musk has described as his first real and lasting friendship outside his family. He also met Justine Wilson, a Canadian writer who would become his first wife. They married in 2000 and had six children together — including twins in 2004 (one of whom, originally named Xavier, would later become Vivian Jenna Wilson, a transition that profoundly affected Musk’s political trajectory, a subject this series will address directly in a later essay).

Queen’s gave Musk something South Africa never had: a social environment where intelligence was currency rather than a target. The bullied kid from Pretoria found that Canadian university culture rewarded exactly the kind of relentless intellectual intensity that had gotten him beaten up in school. He researched Latin American debt instruments for a bank, concluded that Brady Bonds were undervalued, and called the Goldman Sachs trading desk in New York to ask about purchasing five million dollars’ worth — putting on a deep voice to sound older. He was nineteen.

The research he did at Queen’s on Latin American debt is a minor biographical detail and a major psychological one. Musk did not look at the Brady Bond market and think: this is interesting, I should write a paper about it. He thought: this is mispriced, I should buy it. The gap between observing an opportunity and acting on it — the gap that stops most people — did not exist for Musk at nineteen. It does not exist for him now. Whether this quality is called vision, recklessness, or first principles thinking depends entirely on whether the bet works.

After two years at Queen’s, Musk transferred to the University of Pennsylvania on a scholarship. Queen’s was the springboard. Penn gave him the dual degrees — physics and economics from Wharton — that would provide the intellectual framework for everything that followed. But it was Canada that gave him the passport, the university admission, the first friendships, the first wife, and the foundational experience of living in a society that did not require him to sleep on his backpack.

Here is the Canadian ledger.

Canada gave Elon Musk: citizenship, a passport, safe passage out of apartheid South Africa, relatives who housed and fed him, manual labour that taught him what work felt like, Queen’s University, his first real friendships, his first wife, two years of post-secondary education that led to a scholarship at an Ivy League university, and the fundamental experience of living in a country where people don’t put bars on their windows.

Elon Musk gave Canada: a departure. He left in 1992 and has never returned in any meaningful sense. He does not manufacture in Canada. He does not employ significant numbers of Canadians. He does not invest in Canadian ventures. He does not advocate for Canadian interests. His satellite network operates over Canadian territory. His electric vehicles drive on Canadian roads. His social media platform shapes Canadian political discourse. But Canada is not a market Musk thinks about. Canada is a door he walked through thirty-four years ago, and the door is behind him.

I have written throughout my work about Hugh Keenleyside — the Canadian diplomat who helped build the international institutions of the postwar order and whose contributions were absorbed into larger narratives and credited to others. Canada’s relationship with Musk is the Keenleyside problem at personal scale. We provided something foundational. We received no ongoing benefit. We hold no leverage over what was built with our contribution.

This would be merely ironic if Musk’s enterprises did not directly affect Canadian sovereignty. Starlink operates in Canadian telecommunications space under CRTC licensing. Tesla vehicles represent a significant share of the Canadian EV market. X shapes Canadian political discourse. xAI’s models will process Canadian data. TeraFab’s chips will power systems that operate in Canadian airspace, on Canadian roads, and through Canadian networks. The teenager who arrived in Montreal in 1989 and marvelled at the absence of window bars now controls infrastructure that touches virtually every aspect of Canadian daily life.

Canada opened the door. What walked through it now sits in Austin, Texas, controlling more of the world’s strategic infrastructure than any private citizen in history, and Canada has no seat at any table where decisions about that infrastructure are made.

Part III picks up where the Canadian passport delivered him: Silicon Valley, 1995. The first money. And the first principles bet that would define everything after.
