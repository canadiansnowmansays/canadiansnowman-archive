There is a particular kind of strategy that works best when nobody can see all of it at once.

For most of this year, the Trump administration’s campaign to take hold of the midterm elections has arrived the way weather arrives: a front here, a squall there, each one fought on its own terms. Lawsuits demanding state voter rolls. A citizenship database switched on and off by the courts. A Postal Service rule for screening mail ballots. A list of “noncitizens” in Nevada that shrank from 15,903 to 185 to zero. Each storm passed through a different courtroom under a different legal theory, and each, taken alone, looked like a bureaucratic dispute. This series has spent fifty-seven parts arguing that together they are a friction strategy: the point is not to win any one case but to keep enough in motion that election officials spend October fighting Washington instead of running elections.

On October 21, 2026, someone finally filed a lawsuit that says the same thing.

As Zach Montague reported for the New York Times on October 21, 2026, a coalition of Democratic lawmakers and campaign committees asked a federal judge in Washington to halt, in one order and nationally, the administration’s efforts to control how states run their elections. The suit names several federal agencies and argues they have been working in concert to spread falsehoods about voter fraud as a pretext for new demands on the states. Its summary of the legal position is four words long: “None of this is lawful.”

What makes this filing different from the dozens that came before it is not its argument. Courts have been agreeing with that argument all year. It is its shape. It tries to fold thirty fights into one.

The week that prompted it

The lawsuit came out of California, and out of one fortnight in October.

Early in the week of October 12, federal authorities delivered a thumb drive to election officials in Los Angeles County. On it, they said, were roughly 20,000 names of people registered to vote in California whom the Department of Homeland Security considered “likely noncitizens.” The county was given 48 hours to act.

Los Angeles County did not open it. Its registrar, Dean Logan, explained that plugging an unsolicited federal storage device into an election system would breach the county’s own data-security protocols, which is the sort of sentence that would have been unremarkable in any year in which election officials were not also being threatened with prison. California’s secretary of state, Shirley Weber, wrote back that the data “lacks both the detail and function to be of any investigative use,” and that Washington had never explained why it suspected any of the listed people of anything.

Then came a letter from Trent McCotter, a senior Justice Department official, which the Times described as highly unusual. It demanded that California not count any ballot cast by anyone on the list until the state could be certain each of them was a citizen. Failing that, it suggested, state election officials might themselves be investigated or prosecuted for aiding and abetting voter fraud. The President, never one to leave a suggestion as a suggestion, posted that the officials would all go to jail for a long time, with three exclamation marks for emphasis.

On October 15, as Devlin Barrett and Laurel Rosenhall reported for the New York Times, the Justice Department sued California and a Los Angeles County official, asking a court to block the counting of mail ballots from the 20,000 until their citizenship could be established. Attorney General Todd Blanche said California had been given its chance to self-correct and had refused. David Becker of the Center for Election Innovation and Research, a nonpartisan group that advises election officials, said the suit would have serious problems and questioned the timing. He noted that about six million ballots had already been cast in early voting across the country, without incident.

On October 19, as Devlin Barrett reported for the New York Times, the Justice Department announced it had charged 28 California residents with voting-related crimes. All 28 were green card holders, lawful permanent residents. Eleven had been arrested. All 28, McCotter said, were on the list of 20,000. And he held them up as proof that the list was sound.

Two days later, the Democrats went to court.

Twenty-eight out of twenty thousand

It is worth slowing down on McCotter’s arithmetic, because the whole week rests on it.

The claim is that 28 charges vindicate 20,000 names. Run it the other way. If every one of the 28 is guilty, the list has produced charges against 0.14 per cent of the people on it. The other 19,972 remain names on a thumb drive that the state’s chief election officer says cannot be investigated, attached to a demand that their ballots be set aside before anyone has checked.

The department’s own lawsuit describes how the list was built, and the description is not reassuring. Homeland Security officials run voter data through third-party software that sorts matches into high, medium and low confidence. Staff at U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services then check the “high confidence” matches against their own records for an alien number and for any evidence of citizenship, along with State Department and Social Security data. What survives goes to Homeland Security Investigations, an arm of ICE, for a further manual review. That is a funnel, and a funnel is only as good as what comes out the bottom of it. What has come out of this one so far is 28 people.

California’s officials, meanwhile, have been doing the checking Washington skipped. In sworn statements cited in the Democratic suit, they said they had reviewed some of the flagged voters and found errors throughout, including a high percentage of people who had been born in the United States. A citizen by birth cannot be naturalised and cannot be a noncitizen. A list that contains many of them is not a list of likely noncitizens. It is a list.

None of this means the 28 are innocent, and nobody serious is saying it. According to the department, 21 of them had cast ballots in past federal elections, which, if proved, is a crime. One of those charged, a 72-year-old man from Belize living in Commerce, California, is accused of voting in 2020, 2022 and 2024 and of lying on a naturalisation application. California’s attorney general, Rob Bonta, agreed that voter fraud should be prosecuted, and offered his own example: an Orange County woman, a Republican by his account, who registered her dog and voted in its name, pleaded guilty and paid for billboards announcing that voter fraud is wrong. The state did not need a thumb drive from Washington to find her.

The proportion is the point. California had 16 million voters in 2024 and has about 23 million registered. Twenty-one past illegal votes, if every one is proved, needs a run of zeros after the decimal point to express as a percentage. Nationally, the Justice Department has charged 113 people in 20 states in two months, which legal experts told the Times is more than prior years but still a tiny fraction of a per cent of all voting. The President has claimed hundreds of thousands of illegal votes. The government’s own prosecutors have found a little over a hundred.

So the charges do two things at once. They are real cases and will be tried as such. They are also a press conference, held fifteen days before Election Day, at which a senior official used them to vouch for a list the people who run California’s elections say is riddled with errors. Janice Hahn of the Los Angeles County Board of Supervisors called the arrests a tool for painting a picture of fraud that does not exist. McCotter, at the same press conference, said the department’s door was open.

Readers of this series will recognise the pattern. Threaten (prosecution for counting ballots). Accommodate (our door is open). Obfuscate (twenty-eight charges as proof of twenty thousand). The only letter of TACO missing from the week is the capitulation, and that, historically, is the one that comes from the courts.

The shape of the friction

Here is why the Democratic suit matters, and why it is a gamble.

Every piece of the administration’s campaign has, in isolation, been losing. The Times tallied more than 30 Justice Department lawsuits seeking to compel individual states to hand over their voter rolls; federal judges have rejected the government’s arguments or thrown the suits out more than two dozen times. Courts have enjoined the President’s executive orders on elections. The Supreme Court, as Part XLIX recorded, blocked his attempt to nationalise mail-ballot rules by a vote of seven to two. The Postal Service rule was struck down by two judges on two different theories. The Nevada list evaporated.

And yet the campaign continues, because losing was never the risk. The risk to the administration was running out of new fronts, and it has not run out. When the voter-roll suits failed, there was the citizenship database. When the mail-ballot rule failed, there was the thumb drive. When the thumb drive went unopened, there was the lawsuit to segregate ballots. When that lawsuit looked like a long shot, as the Times itself described it, there were the arrests. Each new front costs the government a filing fee and a press release. Each one costs state election officials staff time, legal fees and public trust, in the weeks when they can least spare any of the three.

That is the friction strategy at its most efficient: it needs no courtroom win, because the cost of defending falls on the defenders. As Part LI argued about the Fulton County ballot seizure, an investigation needs no court’s blessing to start and no resolution to keep running. A threat of prosecution works the same way. It does not have to be carried out to make an official think twice about counting a ballot.

The Democratic suit attacks the structure rather than the symptoms. It asks for an order forbidding the federal government from directing any state to disqualify individual voters, from directing any state to set aside and ultimately not count ballots from registered voters, and from threatening to prosecute state or local officials for routine election administration. In the suit’s telling, these are not separate initiatives but one project: the President’s decade-long fixation on discredited fraud claims, with the Justice Department and Homeland Security enlisted to carry it out. Its legal foundation is the oldest one in American election law. The Constitution gives the running of elections to the states.

If it works, the administration’s advantage disappears. There would be no next front, because every next front would already be covered by the order.

Why it might not work

Three obstacles stand between the coalition and that result, and the honest version of this essay names them.

The first is time. The suit was filed on October 21. Election Day is November 3. Even an emergency motion moving at the fastest pace a federal court allows leaves almost no room for briefing, a hearing and a ruling, never mind an appeal. The administration has shown all year that it knows how to use the emergency docket, filing, withdrawing and refiling at the Supreme Court as Part XLVII documented. A ruling against it on, say, October 30 could be stayed on October 31.

The second is scope. The suit is trying to secure what amounts to nationwide relief. The Supreme Court’s 2025 decision in Trump v. CASA sharply limited the power of lower federal courts to issue universal injunctions that protect people who are not parties to the case. The coalition is made up of lawmakers and campaign committees, not fifty states. How far an order in their favour could reach, and whether it would protect a county registrar in Arizona who never joined the suit, is exactly the sort of question the administration will press, and press quickly.

The third is the bench itself. The case went to Chief Judge James E. Boasberg of the federal district court in Washington, who, as the Times noted, has clashed with this Justice Department before. That cuts both ways. Boasberg knows the department’s habits better than almost any judge in the country, and any ruling from him in the coalition’s favour will arrive pre-labelled by the administration as partisan. That matters less on appeal than in the press, but the press is where the friction strategy lives.

The department’s response was brief. A spokesman said election integrity is a top priority and everything it had done was grounded in law and being litigated in open court. Litigated, yes. It has lost, in open court, more than two dozen times on voter rolls alone. That is not a defence of the strategy. It is a description of it.

What a single case can and cannot do

Even if the coalition loses on timing or scope, the filing does something the previous thirty cases could not. It puts the whole pattern in one document, under oath and on the record, before a single judge.

Until now, the connection between these initiatives has lived in essays like this one and in the observations of election administrators who could see the whole field from their desks: obvious, and unproven. A court asked whether the Justice Department, Homeland Security and ICE’s investigators are working on one project will have to look at that connection directly.

And there is the problem Part LVII raised. The fight does not end when the polls close. Certification, recounts and the seating of the new House on January 3, 2027 all lie beyond Election Day, and the threats aimed at California’s officials this month would work just as well against canvassing boards in December. An order forbidding federal threats against officials doing routine administration may matter more after November 3 than before it. A ruling too late for the vote could still arrive in time for the count.

A judge, a thumb drive and an open door

There is something almost Leacockian about the image at the centre of all this: a federal government that wants to remove 20,000 people’s votes, delivering its evidence on a thumb drive that the county is too sensible to plug in, while its senior official assures everyone that the door is open.

The serious version is less funny. The administration does not need the thumb drive to be accurate, the California suit to succeed or the 28 prosecutions to prove anything about the other 19,972. It needs election officials to wonder whether counting a lawful ballot might cost them their liberty. It needs voters on the list, citizens by birth among them, to wonder whether their ballot will count. It needs the public to see arrests, hear “fraud” and not do the division.

The Democratic suit may be too late, too broad or too easily stayed. But for fifty-seven parts this series has watched the administration win by keeping the fights small and numerous. On October 21, the other side finally tried making it one fight.

We will know within days whether a single judge can do what thirty separate courtrooms could not: end the war, instead of winning the battle in front of it.
