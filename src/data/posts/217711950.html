There’s a specific kind of institutional self-knowledge that governments sometimes produce entirely for their own internal use, never intending it to reach the public it describes — and the Pentagon Papers are perhaps the single most consequential example of that phenomenon in twentieth-century American history. Commissioned in 1967 by Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara, the man who had presided over much of the very escalation this series described in Part IX, the study that would become known as the Pentagon Papers was a study of U.S. involvement in Vietnam stretching back across four presidential administrations, undertaken because McNamara wanted to understand how U.S. decision-making in Indochina had led to a war that increasingly seemed unwinnable. That a sitting secretary of defense would commission, in secret, a historical accounting of his own department’s decisions on the assumption the war might already be unwinnable, tells you something important about the gap between private assessment and public confidence that this series traced through the Johnson administration’s Tet-era credibility collapse in Part X. The Pentagon Papers are what happens when that gap gets written down, filed away as classified, and then, four years later, gets out anyway.

What McNamara Commissioned, and Why

The study, officially titled Report of the Office of the Secretary of Defense Vietnam Task Force and formally covering the United States’ political and military involvement in Vietnam from 1945 to 1968, was assembled by a team of analysts working under a project informally called the Vietnam Study Task Force. Subtitled “History of U.S. Decision-making in Vietnam, 1945-68,” the study was completed in late 1968 — meaning it was finished just as the Johnson administration itself was winding down in the aftermath of Tet, the withdrawal announcement, and the approaching 1968 election. The 47-volume history, consisting of approximately 3,000 pages of narrative and 4,000 pages of appended documents, took 18 months to complete, and once finished, it simply sat in classified storage. After all that effort and expense, the top-secret history languished in the safes of a few officials as the war in Vietnam ground on — an institutional artefact produced at enormous cost, containing precisely the kind of unvarnished internal accounting that might have reshaped public debate about the war years earlier, deliberately kept from the public it concerned.

Among the analysts who worked on the study was Daniel Ellsberg, a former U.S. Marine Corps officer who had gone on to become a strategic analyst at the RAND Corporation. Ellsberg’s own background made him an unusually well-positioned contributor: he had written a dissertation on decision-making under conditions of uncertainty for his PhD at Harvard, and had recently returned from two years in Vietnam studying counter-insurgency for the State Department. Crucially, and this is a detail worth sitting with given how central it becomes to the rest of this story, Ellsberg had originally been an early supporter of U.S. involvement in Indochina before his direct experience of the war and his work compiling its internal history left him seriously opposed to U.S. involvement by the time the study was finished. This wasn’t a critic looking to confirm an existing bias — it was an insider whose views were changed by what the documents themselves revealed.

What the Papers Actually Showed

The substance of the Pentagon Papers, once it finally reached the public, confirmed and considerably deepened what Tet had already suggested to much of the American public: that official government statements about the war, across four successive presidential administrations, had diverged sharply and repeatedly from what those same administrations privately understood to be true. A 1996 New York Times retrospective noted that the Pentagon Papers had demonstrated, among other things, that Lyndon Johnson’s administration had systematically lied, not only to the public but also to Congress. The documents revealed that the U.S. had secretly enlarged the scope of its actions in the Vietnam War through coastal raids on North Vietnam and Marine Corps attacks, none of which had been reported in the mainstream media at the time they occurred — providing documentary confirmation, years after the fact, of exactly the kind of undisclosed escalation this series traced through Part IX’s account of the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution and its shaky factual foundation.

Perhaps most damning of all was a detail that later reporting on the papers would repeatedly emphasise: internal government assessments had, at points, been remarkably candid about what the war was actually understood to be for, in terms starkly disconnected from the idealistic public rhetoric of defending democracy and containing communism. One internal 1965 document, cited in later retrospectives on the papers, put the government’s own weighting of its war aims bluntly — roughly 70 per cent of the American objective, by this internal accounting, was simply to avoid a humiliating American defeat, with a further goal being to emerge from the crisis without unacceptable damage from the methods used to pursue it. That kind of internal candour — the war understood, at the highest levels of the Pentagon, primarily as a matter of face-saving rather than any deeper strategic necessity — is precisely the accountability the American public had been denied for years, exactly the accountability this series’ broader project of chronicling American credibility politics over Vietnamese strategy has been building toward since Part IX.

Ellsberg’s Decision to Leak

By 1969, having worked closely with the study’s findings and having grown convinced the war was both unwinnable and fundamentally premised on decades of official deception, Ellsberg made repeated efforts to bring the material to public attention through conventional channels before ever approaching a journalist. He approached several members of Congress, hoping an anti-war senator might enter the study into the Congressional Record or hold hearings on its findings, and none took action. Only after this avenue proved fruitless did Ellsberg turn to the press, specifically to Neil Sheehan, a New York Times reporter whom Ellsberg had first met while covering the war in Vietnam. Ellsberg had recently read an article Sheehan had written suggesting American military actions in Vietnam likely constituted war crimes, and concluded Sheehan was the right journalist, and the Times the right outlet, to bring the material into public view.

The mechanics of how the material actually made it into print are worth recounting briefly, since they capture something of the pressured, improvised nature of the whole episode. Ellsberg initially intended only to let Sheehan take notes on the documents in his apartment, but Sheehan disobeyed this instruction, secretly copying the documents across numerous print shops in the Boston area with his wife’s help, before flying the copies to Washington, where Times editors began the work of organising and verifying the material in a hotel room. Ellsberg gave 43 of the study’s volumes to Sheehan on March 2, 1971, and the Times spent the following months quietly preparing its coverage.

Publication, Injunction, and the Supreme Court

On June 13, 1971, the New York Times began publishing parts of the Pentagon Papers, in what became a series of front-page articles drawing directly on the leaked study — with the first instalment, in a detail that has become one of the more darkly amusing footnotes of the whole affair, appearing on the Times’ front page right next to a story covering the wedding of President Nixon’s daughter. Nixon himself, according to later accounts, claimed not to have read the initial article at all — an oddly incurious response to a story that would, within days, consume his administration’s attention entirely, driven in substantial part by the recognition that even though the material covered the Kennedy and Johnson years rather than his own administration’s conduct of the war, the precedent of a major newspaper successfully publishing leaked classified national security material posed a direct threat to his own administration’s ability to control its own narrative and prevent future leaks.

The Nixon administration’s response escalated quickly. After the Times published its early instalments, the Department of Justice sought a federal court injunction to impose prior restraint on further publication — an extraordinary legal step, since this marked the first time since the country’s founding that the federal government had attempted to stop a newspaper from publishing material it already possessed. A temporary restraining order succeeded in halting the Times’ publication for roughly two weeks, but Ellsberg, anticipating exactly this kind of legal countermeasure, had already provided a duplicate set of documents to Ben Bagdikian, an editor at the Washington Post, and on June 18, 1971, the Post began publishing its own series of articles based on the papers, with additional newspapers picking up coverage as the legal battle over the Times’ injunction continued to unfold.

The resulting case, New York Times Co. v. United States, moved with unusual speed through the federal court system and reached the Supreme Court within days. On June 30, 1971, in a 6-3 decision widely regarded as one of the most significant prior-restraint rulings in American constitutional history, the Court found that the government had failed to justify restraining publication, allowing the Times and the Post to resume printing the material freely. The ruling remains, to this day, a foundational precedent establishing the extraordinarily high bar the American government must clear to prevent a newspaper from publishing material already in its possession, regardless of the material’s classification status — a legal victory for press freedom that emerged directly from a war whose conduct the same press freedom had, for years, struggled to accurately convey to the public consuming it.

The Fallout for Ellsberg, and the Road to Watergate

Ellsberg’s own legal exposure was severe. Indicted within weeks of publication, he went to trial in January 1973 facing charges under the Espionage Act of 1917 along with additional charges of theft and conspiracy, carrying a maximum possible sentence of 115 years — an almost unimaginable personal legal jeopardy for a man who, by his own account, understood exactly what he was risking when he made the material public. What ultimately saved Ellsberg from that exposure wasn’t a legal argument about the underlying merits of his actions, but rather the Nixon administration’s own subsequent misconduct in trying to discredit him. Furious at the leak and determined to prevent further disclosures, Nixon aides created a covert unit nicknamed the “White House Plumbers,” under the supervision of John Ehrlichman, tasked specifically with plugging leaks and, more aggressively, discrediting Ellsberg personally. This unit, in September 1971, carried out a break-in at the office of Ellsberg’s psychiatrist, hoping to find damaging personal information that could be used to undermine his credibility.

That break-in, along with a range of other illegal surveillance the government had conducted against Ellsberg, came to light during his 1973 trial, and Judge William Matthew Byrne Jr. dismissed all charges against Ellsberg in May 1973 as a result of this governmental misconduct and illegal evidence-gathering. The significance of this episode extends well beyond Ellsberg’s own personal legal fate, because the same “Plumbers” unit assembled to target him would go on, in June 1972, to carry out the break-in at the Democratic National Committee’s headquarters in the Watergate complex — the episode that would eventually force Nixon’s resignation in August 1974. In this sense, the Pentagon Papers episode isn’t merely a chapter in the history of the Vietnam War’s own unravelling public credibility — it’s also, quite directly, the origin point of the separate but intimately connected chain of events that would end Nixon’s presidency entirely, an irony given that the Papers themselves concerned decisions made almost entirely by his predecessors rather than by Nixon’s own administration.

Reading the Papers Against This Series’ Recurring Theme

I want to close this essay, as I’ve done at several earlier points in this series, by connecting the Pentagon Papers back to the broader argument this series has been building since Part IX: that American conduct of the Vietnam War was shaped overwhelmingly by credibility politics rather than clear strategic assessment, and that the growing gap between official confidence and internal private doubt was, itself, one of the most consequential features of the entire American engagement. The Pentagon Papers are the documentary proof of exactly that gap — a 7,000-page internal government history demonstrating, in the government’s own words, across four administrations, that private assessments of the war’s prospects were frequently far more pessimistic, and far more nakedly focused on avoiding embarrassment rather than achieving any coherent strategic objective, than anything the American public was being told at the time.

It’s worth noting, too, the peculiar historical justice in how the material finally reached the public: not through any formal accountability mechanism — not through congressional oversight, not through an official declassification process, not through any of the channels democratic governance is supposed to provide for exactly this kind of after-the-fact reckoning — but through a single analyst’s decision to break the law rather than continue watching a war he had come to believe was being fought, in significant part, on a foundation of sustained public deception. That’s a form of accountability considerably more chaotic, legally fraught, and personally costly than the kind this series’ broader project — and my own ongoing work chronicling accountability journalism in other contexts — would generally hope for. But it was, in 1971, the only form of accountability available, and it arrived only because Ellsberg was willing to risk what amounted to the rest of his life in prison to provide it.
