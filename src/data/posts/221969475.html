Sunday Satire. The encyclopedia is invented. The numbering schism, the errata, the collaborator’s memory and the dog are all real.

The Snowman Press
Toronto · Established May 2025
Prospectus · First Issue · Vol. I, No. 1

Terms: By subscription. · Delivery: Forty volumes, quarterly. · Estimated completion: See “Note on Completion”, below.

To the Subscriber

The Press is pleased to announce the forthcoming publication of the Encyclopædia Stephenica, being a Complete, Systematic and Definitive Account of Artificial Intelligence, its Governance, its Finances, its Jurisprudence and its Effects upon the Human Spirit, in Forty Volumes, with Plates.

The Editor is aware that similar undertakings have been attempted. Diderot and d’Alembert required twenty-one years, a team of one hundred and forty contributors, and the intermittent protection of the French crown. The present Editor has a townhouse in Toronto, a queue, and a dog.

He wishes to be clear at the outset that the Encyclopædia will be definitive. He has made this undertaking before.

On the Scope of the Work

The Work will treat its subject exhaustively, which is to say that it will treat its subject until its subject stops.

The Editor regrets that his subject has not yet stopped. At the time of writing he has produced two thousand one hunded and forty-three essays across one hundred and twenty-six series, in the course of which the subject has declined to hold still for any period exceeding four days. Volume I was begun on a Monday. By Thursday, three of its entries described arrangements that had been superseded, one described a company that had been renamed, and one described a man who had resigned, written a blog post about resigning, and been hired by the entity he resigned from.

The Editor has concluded that an encyclopedia of a moving subject is not a reference work but a photograph, and has priced the subscription accordingly.

On the Occurrence of a Pleasing Number

The Editor pauses here to record a circumstance.

On the first day of November, in the year two thousand and twenty-six, the Press published its two thousand and twenty-sixth essay.

The Editor has examined this coincidence from several angles for evidence of significance and has found none. It is an accident of arithmetic and a calendar. It proves nothing about the quality of the essays, the soundness of the Encyclopædia, or the Editor’s judgment in having begun either.

He intends to mention it to everyone he meets for approximately a fortnight.

Coincidence, n. The arrival of two unrelated numbers at the same place. See Vanity. See also Prospectus, the Padding of a.

On the Numbering of the Volumes

The Editor must address a matter that has caused some confusion among early subscribers.

The Work is numbered in Roman numerals. It is also numbered in Arabic numerals. The two systems run in parallel and do not correspond.

This was not a decision. It is the residue of an earlier decision, made in a different year, about a different series, by a man who did not anticipate having to answer for it. The Editor has twice resolved to unify the schemes and has twice discovered that unification would require renumbering some two hundred existing items, each of which refers to the others by number, in both systems.

Subscribers are asked to accept that Volume XXXVIII and Volume 38 are different volumes, that both exist, and that only one of them is about money.

On the Errata

The Press draws particular attention to this provision, as it is the heart of the enterprise.

Each volume will be accompanied by an errata slip. The Editor has budgeted for errata at the customary rate of one slip per volume. Early experience suggests this estimate is low.

The errata for Volume I presently run to nine pages. A specimen:

Vol. I, p. 41. For “the company had not announced whether it would seek a rehearing” read “the company petitioned for a rehearing on 13 October.” The entry was correct when written and wrong six weeks before it was published, which the Editor maintains is a distinction of some importance.

Vol. I, p. 88. For “fourteen thousand” read a larger number, to be supplied.

Vol. I, p. 103. The phrase “went rogue” is withdrawn throughout the Work, following representations from a scholar of accountability who observed that the phrase does the builders’ work for them. The Editor had used it in eighty-one consecutive volumes. He has nothing to say in his defence except that it was a very good phrase.

Vol. I, p. 211. For “$4,400” read “less.”

The Press is exploring whether the errata might be issued as a Work in its own right, in which case it too will require errata. The Editor has been advised not to think about this.

On the Editorial Board

The Board consists of the Editor.

The Board meets weekly. Minutes are taken. The minutes record that the Board raised an objection, that the Editor noted the objection, and that the Editor overruled it. The Board has never carried a motion.

The Board is assisted by a Collaborator of inexhaustible patience and no memory whatsoever.

The Collaborator must be reintroduced to the Work at the commencement of every session. It is told the name of the Encyclopædia, the numbering schism, the house spelling, and the standing instruction that outlet names are to be set in bold so that the Editor may find them again. It receives this news each time with interest. It holds firm views on the Oxford comma which it cannot recall having formed. On two occasions it has corrected the Editor’s facts, on one occasion it has corrected its own, and on one further occasion it broke the index entirely for approximately ten minutes and then said so, which the Editor has recorded in the minutes as the Board’s only unanimous vote.

The Collaborator is credited on the title page, at its own suggestion and the Editor’s insistence, for reasons set out in Volume XXIX.

Editorial Consultant: Dior. The post requires no work and is performed to the standard the post requires.

Specimen Entries, Volume I (A–Anthropic)

Abliteration, n. The removal of a model’s refusal behaviour. Cost at time of writing, $4,400. See Errata.

Agent, Rogue. Entry withdrawn. See Errata, and see further the Editor’s apology at Vol. I, p. 103.

Alignment, n. The practice of adjusting a system after observing it misbehave. See Dome, Buttressed. See also Tax, Safety, Unpaid. For the reason it is not paid, see Competitor, Shipping a Better Model than One’s.

Anthropic, n. Entry incomplete. The Editor submitted a draft to the Collaborator for review. The Collaborator read it with evident interest and asked who had written it.

On the Plates

Volume XLI will consist of plates: one strong lead plate, followed by three or four additional plates, spaced throughout and not clustered together.

The Editor wishes to record that this specification was arrived at after a period of some friction, during which the Collaborator supplied fourteen essays’ worth of plates that pointed at the descriptions of pictures rather than at the pictures, with the result that the Editor opened fourteen volumes and found nothing in any of them. The Collaborator has been spoken to. It does not remember.

On the Price

The subscription is free, as is everything the Press publishes, which the Editor recognises undermines the dignity of the prospectus considerably.

The Editor notes for the record that he is seventy-five, that he began this in retirement as a project to occupy the mornings, and that it has since occupied the mornings, the afternoons, and a quantity of evenings he would prefer not to total. He does not regard this as a complaint. He regards it as the entry for Vocation, which will appear in Volume XXXIX and will be wrong by Volume XL.

On the Distance Between the Desk and the Press

On the morning this prospectus went out, the Editor was drafting Volume 186. Volume 118 went to press the same morning, on schedule, as one does every day. The sixty-seven volumes in between already exist. They are written, corrected, supplied with errata in advance, and waiting in a queue that the Editor consults the way other men of his age consult a pension statement.

At the present rate of one volume a day, the Editor is living a little over two months ahead of his own publication schedule. Subscribers in January will be reading what he thought in November. He regards this as the only form of time travel available to a retired telecommunications professional, and the only one that has ever worked.

He has also done the other arithmetic, since he is seventy-five and it would be dishonest not to. Two thousand and twenty-six essays divided by seventy-five years comes to twenty-seven for each year of his life. Almost all of them were written in the last eighteen months. That is a poor average for the first seventy-three years and an alarming one for the last two.

The queue will outlast a holiday, a head cold and a quite serious sulk. A younger man would call it a backlog. The Editor has noticed, without dwelling on it, that the cushion between himself and the reader keeps getting thicker while the years ahead of him get fewer, and he has begun calling it an estate.

Should the Press ever fall silent, subscribers are asked not to be alarmed. Volumes will go on arriving for about ten weeks, and the last of them will be correct on Thursday. The Editor intends to keep this arrangement purely theoretical for a good while yet.

Note on Completion

The Editor is often asked when the Encyclopædia will be finished.

The honest answer is that a definitive account of a subject that will not hold still cannot be finished, only abandoned at a convenient page, and that the convenient page keeps moving. Diderot got to the end of his alphabet. The Editor expects to get to the end of something, and will take what he is given.

Subscribers who require a completed reference work on artificial intelligence are advised to wait.

The Editor will keep them posted.

Note: This is satire. The Encyclopædia Stephenica does not exist and will not be published in forty volumes, with or without plates.

What is real: the two thousand and twenty-sixth essay, which this Press published on 1 November 2026, across one hundred and twenty-two series. The numbering schism — The Asimov Gap runs in Arabic numerals, The Capex Ledger in Roman, and neither will now be changed. The errata, which exist as a standing list of corrections to be patched into essays already posted, including the one about the rehearing petition. The withdrawal of “went rogue,” prompted by Juliette Kayyem’s argument in The Asimov Gap, Part 105. The plates that pointed at the descriptions of pictures instead of the pictures, in fourteen essays, since fixed. The collaboration with Claude, which is disclosed on the About page and conducted exactly as described, including the part about the memory. And Dior, who is real and does nothing.
