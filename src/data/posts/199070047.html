There is a certain kind of man who, upon hearing that his adversary has left the stage, immediately sits down to compose a gracious note. There is another kind who fires up the AI machine and asks it to generate a video of himself throwing the departing fellow into a dumpster.

And then there is a third kind — the rarest — who takes the dumpster video, reverses it, relabels the dumpster, and posts the result before breakfast.

On Thursday, May 21, Stephen Colbert signed off from the Ed Sullivan Theatre for the last time. Paul McCartney played “Hello, Goodbye.” The lights went dark on a thirty-three-year-old American institution — cancelled not because it was failing (it led its time slot in the ratings) but because its host had committed the unpardonable sin of mocking his parent company’s $16 million settlement with the President of the United States. CBS called it a financial decision. The Ellison family, Paramount’s Trump-allied owners, were presumably unavailable for comment, having never been asked.

What followed was a masterclass in what the political scientists call “escalation dominance,” except that every single escalation made the escalator look worse.

Act I: The Fantasy

Within an hour of Colbert’s final sign-off, Trump was on Truth Social. “Colbert is finally finished at CBS. Amazing that he lasted so long! No talent, no ratings, no life. He was like a dead person.”

But Trump was not done. He is never done. That is both his superpower and his clinical diagnosis.

By Friday evening, the official White House accounts — on Truth Social, on X, on Instagram, because apparently the machinery of the republic had nothing more pressing — posted a twenty-two-second AI-generated video. In it, a computer-rendered Trump walks onto the Ed Sullivan stage, approaches Colbert from behind, grabs him by the shoulders, and hurls him into a green dumpster. He then closes the lid and performs his trademark upper-body convulsion to the Village People’s “Y.M.C.A.”

The official version: approach from behind, dispose of comedian, dance alone. Note the empty space around the dancing figure in Panel 3. Even the AI couldn't fill it. (Source: White House AI video / Truth Social)

The caption, from the official communications apparatus of the world’s most powerful nation: “Bye-bye 👋.”

Two hundred and five thousand likes. Four and a half million views. Your tax dollars at work.

Let us pause on the dance. Anyone who has watched the real Trump perform the YMCA at a rally — and there is footage from Des Moines, and Tulsa, and Butler, and roughly four hundred other venues — will recognize the move: fists clenched at approximately chest height, pumping alternately as though attempting to open two very stubborn jars of pickles while suffering a mild electrical current. The feet do not move. The hips do not move. Nothing below the sternum participates. It is less a dance than a neurological event.

The real thing, Des Moines, Iowa, October 2020. Same fists. Same posture. Same solitude. The AI didn't exaggerate the dance — it faithfully reproduced its loneliness. (Source: Campaign rally footage)

And it is always performed alone.

This is the detail the AI faithfully preserved. In Trump’s fantasy of ultimate triumph — the enemy vanquished, the stage claimed, the crowd cheering — the hero still dances by himself. Nobody joins him. Nobody tries. Even in a video where every pixel was engineered to flatter him, the algorithm could not imagine another human being who wanted to dance with him.

If you want to understand a man, watch what he does when he is the author of his own fantasy. Trump’s fantasy is solitude disguised as victory.

Act II: The Reversal

The internet, as it does, required approximately twelve hours to respond. And the response, courtesy of the account @PauleyTicks, was a work of structural perfection.

The video begins identically: the Ed Sullivan stage, the AI Colbert at his desk, the AI Trump approaching from behind. If you have seen the 2016 presidential debate — the one where Trump physically stalked Hillary Clinton across the stage, looming behind her like a man who had learned about personal space from nature documentaries about bears — the visual grammar is immediately familiar. The approach from behind. The dominance display. The assumption that physical presence equals power.

The internet's twelve-hour rebuttal: same setup, opposite ending. Note the revised label on the dumpster. Two words. Thirty-four files. (Source: @PaulleyTicks / X)

But in this version, the script flips. AI Colbert turns, seizes AI Trump, and manhandles him toward the dumpster. There is a struggle — brief, decisive — and then Trump goes in. The lid closes. The audience erupts.

And here the detail that elevates the whole enterprise from parody to indictment: the dumpster, in the original Trump video, was unmarked. In the reversal, it reads, in large white stenciled letters, “PEDO TRASH.”

Two words. Thirty-four Epstein files. A lifetime of photographs the President has never been able to explain.

AI Colbert stands beside the closed dumpster, clapping, grinning, while AI Trump peers out from inside like a man who has just discovered that the trash compactor has opinions about the company he kept. The audience — even the AI-generated one — is delighted.

This is what Trump never understood about Colbert, and never will: comedy is a collaborative weapon. You don’t win it by escalating. You win it by turning the escalation around and handing it back with a bow. Trump started an AI war and lost it in one remix. He built the dumpster. Someone else just changed the label.

Act III: The Real Dance

And now we arrive at what was actually happening while the President of the United States was supervising the production of cartoon violence against a comedian.

At a venue near the Ed Sullivan Theatre, in New York City, Stephen Colbert was at his “Fired & Festive!” afterparty. The dress code, you will note, was the name of the thing Trump did to him — turned into an invitation. That is what comedians do with pain. They put it on the marquee and charge admission.

The photographs — real photographs, taken by real cameras in the presence of real photons — show a man in a blazer, drink in hand, on a dance floor bathed in purple and green light, grinning like someone who has just been liberated rather than defeated. Around him: actual humans. Behind him: people filming on their phones, not because they were ordered to, but because what was happening was worth recording.

Meanwhile, at the "Fired & Festive!" afterparty: a man who knows how to dance, how to hold a drink, and how to be in a room with people who are there voluntarily. None of these images were generated by artificial intelligence. All of them were generated by an actual life. (Source: Brian Krassenstein / X)

And beside him — close enough to touch, close enough to be touched — was his wife, Evelyn. She is facing him. She is dancing with him. She is in the same frame voluntarily, under no contractual obligation, at no prescribed distance, with no visible Secret Service detail managing the choreography.

This is where the comparison becomes not just amusing but anatomical.

Donald Trump dances alone at rallies, surrounded by thousands of people who are watching him. Stephen Colbert dances with his wife at a party, surrounded by people who are dancing with each other. Trump performs at an audience. Colbert moves with a partner.

One man holds his fists at chest height and pumps them in place, feet cemented to the floor. The other man has an arm raised, is pivoting on his heels, is making eye contact with another human being who appears — and this is the devastating part — to be enjoying herself.

And this, if we are being honest, is the real reason Donald Trump hates Stephen Colbert. Not the jokes. Not the monologues. Not even the ratings. Trump has endured mockery from sharper minds and survived insults from more powerful enemies. What he cannot endure — what no amount of AI engineering can fix — is the sight of a man who is a better dancer than he is, who knows how to be in a room full of people without dominating it, who can hold a drink and a conversation and a rhythm simultaneously, and whose wife crosses a dance floor toward him rather than maintaining the diplomatic distance of a woman fulfilling the terms of an agreement. Colbert doesn’t just know how to party. He knows how to party with real people. And for a man who has spent his entire life performing to crowds he never joins, that is the one talent no amount of money, power, or generative artificial intelligence can purchase.

Melania, for the record, was not in the AI video either.

Coda: The Three Screens

So there it was, Friday night in America: three screens, three stories.

On Screen One, an AI-generated President throws an AI-generated comedian into a dumpster and dances alone to a song written by a gay man, posted from the official White House account with the emotional vocabulary of a kindergartner leaving daycare. Bye-bye. Wave emoji. In fairness, the President may have been short on time: he was also, according to his own account, in the final stages of negotiating an end to the war he started in Iran on February 28 — a war he initially promised would be over in days but which has now required a fragile ceasefire, Pakistani mediators, a Qatari delegation, and almost three months of killing to reach the “largely negotiated” stage. One assumes the Nobel Peace Prize nomination letter was being drafted simultaneously, though knowing the President’s long history with pseudonyms, it was probably signed “John Barron” — the fictitious publicist Trump invented in the 1980s to call reporters and plant flattering stories about himself, and whose name he later bestowed upon his youngest son, because even Trump’s children are branded content.

On Screen Two, the internet’s instant remix puts Trump himself in the dumpster, relabeled with the two words his legal team has spent years trying to keep out of the conversation. The fantasy reversed in twelve hours. The weapon returned to sender.

On Screen Three, the real one — the only one that matters — a sixty-two-year-old man dances with his wife at a party full of friends, celebrating the end of something he built with his own talent and the beginning of whatever comes next, which includes, improbably, co-writing a Lord of the Rings movie with Peter Jackson, because the universe has a sense of humour even when the President does not.

Trump needed a team of engineers and several hundred thousand dollars’ worth of computing power to generate a fantasy in which he is cool. Colbert just showed up and was.

The satire, as they say, writes itself.

Though apparently the President needs a computer to write his.

The blur is the point. Real joy doesn't render at 4K.
