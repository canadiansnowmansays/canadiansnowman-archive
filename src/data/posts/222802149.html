SEASONAL GUIDANCE No. HHS-2026-1031
Subject: Trick-or-Treating in the Natural Era
Issued: Friday, October 30, 2026
Effective: Upon dusk, Saturday, October 31, 2026
Distribution: Parents, Guardians, Porch-Light Operators, and Any Adult Currently Holding a Pillowcase

1. PURPOSE

On April 22, 2025, the Secretary announced that the nation would phase out petroleum-based synthetic dyes from its food supply by the end of 2026. The Secretary described these dyes as poisonous compounds. The deadline is now sixty-two days away. Halloween 2026 is therefore the last Halloween of the Petroleum Era and the first Halloween of the Natural Era. It is also the night on which the most Americans will be eating the most dye at the same time.

This Guidance helps families tell the difference between a poisonous treat and a natural one. It also explains why the natural one is mostly insects, and why that is good news.

2. BACKGROUND

For decades, American candy got its colours from substances refined out of petroleum. Red 40, Yellow 5, Yellow 6, Blue 1, Blue 2 and Green 3 were tested, certified and approved by the Food and Drug Administration. The FDA kept saying they were safe. The Department has found that this does not settle the matter.

In their place, the Department has encouraged manufacturers to use colours from nature. Nature, the Department regrets to report, turned out to be a crowded place.

3. CLASSIFICATION OF COMMON CONFECTIONERY INGREDIENTS

Under the Natural Era framework, every ingredient falls into one of two categories. Families should consult the table below before releasing any treat to a child.

The governing principle is simple. If it came out of the ground, it is poison. If it came out of a bug, it is wholesome.

4. A NOTE ON THE LAC BUG

The Department wishes to recognise the female lac bug, a small and dedicated public servant.

She pierces a tree branch with her mouthparts, drinks its sap, and spends her whole life secreting a resin from a gland on her abdomen. The resin hardens into a protective shell around her. She lays her eggs inside it and, if all goes well, dies there. About 100,000 of her colleagues are needed to produce a single pound of shellac. That shellac then gives a Milk Dud its shine and a jelly bean its snap.

She asks for nothing and has never once testified before the Senate.

The male lac bug mates and dies within about four days. The Department has no further questions about the male lac bug.

5. FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Q: My child’s candy corn contains bug secretions. Should I be concerned?

A: No. As The Atlantic’s Tove Danovich reported on October 30, 2026, the glaze makes up only about a quarter of one per cent of a shiny candy. The Department considers this a modest serving of nature in an otherwise unnatural product. Your child is also eating gelatin made from animal ligaments, which the Department has no objection to.

Q: Shellac is also used in furniture polish, nail polish and hair spray. Is my child eating furniture polish?

A: Your child is eating the same natural substance that is also used in furniture polish. That is a completely different thing. A shared ingredient does not make two products the same, in the same way that a petroleum dye and the candy it colours are completely different things, except when the Department says otherwise.

Q: Isn’t shellac also used to coat pills?

A: Shellac coats many medications to mask bitter flavours and slow how fast the stomach absorbs them. The Department is still reviewing whether the medications themselves are natural. The shellac has been cleared.

Q: The FDA permits up to 60 insect fragments and one rodent hair per 100 grams of chocolate. Is this a defect?

A: As of this Guidance, the FDA’s Food Defect Action Levels are renamed the Food Fortification Action Levels. Insect fragments are unplanned protein, and the Department has long favoured protein. The rodent hair is under separate review. As one candy scientist told The Atlantic, “A bug leg is just a bit of protein that will go through you anyway.”

Q: Is there a vegan alternative to shellac?

A: Zein, a protein from corn, can be used as a coating. Food scientists report that it does not seal out moisture as well as shellac. They also report that it smells like a wet dog during manufacturing. The Department offers no opinion on what anything smells like during manufacturing, having been around a few manufacturing processes of its own.

Q: My family keeps kosher (or halal, or vegetarian). Carmine is none of those things. What should we do?

A: The Natural Era is natural for everyone. It is not edible for everyone. These are separate policy areas.

Q: Some leading scientists say the better choice is simply to give children a strawberry. Is that guidance?

A: Professor Joe Schwarcz of McGill University’s Office for Science and Society has made this argument. McGill is in Canada. The Department will not be taking nutritional advice from Canada at this time.

Q: Hasn’t Canada been using natural colours for years? Isn’t this Canada’s standard?

A: The FDA Commissioner did note, on April 22, 2025, that companies already use natural ingredients in Europe and Canada. This was an observation and not a concession. Any similarity between American candy after December 31, 2026 and Canadian candy before it is coincidental. That includes the noticeably paler Canadian Froot Loops. American Froot Loops will be pale for American reasons.

Q: My neighbour gave out full-size bars with Red 40 in them. Should I report her?

A: The phase-out is voluntary. The Department relies on manufacturers acting in good faith, the way it relies on parents, porch lights and bears.

6. SPECIAL NOTE REGARDING THE SECRETARY’S QUALIFICATIONS

Some members of the public have asked whether the Secretary is the right official to lecture families about the provenance of what they eat. The Department notes that the Secretary has a long personal record of engaging with natural foods at the source. In 2014 he collected a bear cub that had been killed on a roadside and intended to take it home. Few officials in the history of the Department have shown such willingness to meet nature on its own terms. The Department regards a little lac-bug resin on a Whopper as well within the Secretary’s demonstrated comfort zone.

7. COMPLIANCE CHECKLIST FOR HALLOWEEN NIGHT

Before your child eats a treat, parents should:

  1. Check the label for petroleum. If petroleum is found, confiscate the treat and eat it yourself, out of sight of the child, for disposal purposes.

  2. Check the label for “confectioner’s glaze,” “cochineal,” “carmine,” “E120” or “gelatin.” If found, congratulate your child on choosing nature.

  3. If the treat glows under a black light, consult Section 3 before you panic.

  4. If the treat contains a whole, intact insect rather than secretions or fragments, it is not confectionery. It is a costume. Return it to its owner.

  5. If your child asks what a lac bug is, tell them she is a small public servant who works very hard and should be thanked.

8. EFFECTIVE PERIOD

This Guidance remains in force until 11:59 p.m. on December 31, 2026, when the Petroleum Era formally ends. After that, all American candy will be natural, slightly duller in colour, more expensive, and coated in the life’s work of 100,000 insects per pound.

The Department wishes every family a safe, natural and well-glazed Halloween.

— Office of Retroactive Justification, Division of Natural Provenance
