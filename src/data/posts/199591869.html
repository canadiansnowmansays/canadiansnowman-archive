India elected a woman prime minister in 1966 — eighteen years before the United Kingdom did, thirty-eight years before Germany did, and at a time when the United States still has not. India has had a woman president, women chief ministers, women Supreme Court justices, women astronauts, women Nobel laureates, and women who have led corporations, armies, and social movements of historic consequence.

India also has one of the worst gender gaps in the world. It ranks 127th out of 146 countries on the World Economic Forum’s Global Gender Gap Index. It has a female labour force participation rate of roughly 24 percent — one of the lowest in the world, lower than Saudi Arabia, lower than Bangladesh. It reports over 30,000 rapes per year, a figure that every expert agrees is a catastrophic undercount. It has a skewed sex ratio — approximately 940 women per 1,000 men — that reflects decades of sex-selective abortion, infanticide, and the preference for sons that pervades Indian society across caste, class, and religious lines. Crimes against women — acid attacks, dowry murders, honour killings, trafficking — are endemic, systemic, and inadequately prosecuted.

Both paragraphs above are true. Both describe the same country. And the gap between them — between India’s extraordinary women and India’s ordinary violence against women — is the most damning contradiction this series has examined.

I saw it for the first time in 1975, long before I understood what I was seeing.

One of the first Indian men I got to know well was Vinod Gupta, my Camera Department Manager at the Woolco store in Toronto’s Dufferin Mall. Woolco — a Canadian division of Woolworths, eventually bought out by Walmart, all 120-plus locations transforming overnight — was where I was learning retail management. Vinod was where I was learning India, though I didn’t know it yet. He married the year before I did, in 1975, and was eager to have me meet his wife and socialize in their home. We had a delicious vegetarian meal. What was odd for me as a young Westerner sitting in an Indian home for the first time was not the food. It was the architecture of the evening. His wife ate separately from us, in the kitchen. Whenever Vinod wanted something — more rice, more water, more anything — he literally snapped his fingers and called out to her. She responded as if in servitude, attending to his beck and call with a practised efficiency that told me this was not a performance for guests. This was the arrangement.

I remember telling my fiancée — soon to be my first wife — what I had witnessed. Her reply was immediate: “Don’t get any ideas.”

Vinod was not a cruel man. He was, by the standards of his generation and his culture, entirely normal. That is precisely the point. The treatment of women in Indian domestic life is not primarily a story of individual cruelty. It is a story of structural normalcy — patterns so deeply embedded that they are invisible to the men who benefit from them and inescapable for the women who live within them. What I saw in Vinod’s dining room in 1975 was the domestic expression of a civilization-wide arrangement, and it was the first time I understood that India’s diversity included a diversity of experience so vast that two people sitting in the same house could be living in different centuries.

I. The Constitution and the Reality

The Indian Constitution guarantees equality of the sexes. Article 14 provides equality before the law. Article 15 prohibits discrimination on the basis of sex. Article 16 guarantees equal opportunity in public employment. Article 39 directs the state to ensure equal pay for equal work. The 73rd and 74th Amendments, passed in 1992, reserve one-third of seats in local government — panchayats and municipal bodies — for women, creating what is arguably the largest affirmative action program for women in political representation anywhere in the world.

The legal architecture is, on paper, among the most progressive in the developing world. The gap between the paper and the pavement is where the story lives.

Indian women’s legal rights are undermined by a system of patriarchal social norms that the law has not displaced and that, in many cases, the institutions responsible for enforcing the law actively reinforce. Police stations are notorious for dismissing or discouraging women who report sexual assault. Judicial proceedings are slow, victim-hostile, and frequently compromised by caste and class dynamics. The “two-finger test” — a medically discredited method of determining whether a rape victim is “habituated to sexual intercourse” — was formally banned by the Supreme Court in 2013 but continued to be administered in practice for years afterward.

The family law system maintains separate personal law codes for Hindus, Muslims, Christians, and Parsis — a colonial inheritance that the BJP’s Uniform Civil Code proposal seeks to replace. Each code contains provisions that disadvantage women in matters of marriage, divorce, inheritance, and custody. Hindu personal law was substantially reformed by the Hindu Code Bills of the 1950s — themselves a political battle that Ambedkar championed and that nearly brought down Nehru’s government. Muslim personal law remains largely unreformed, and the practice of triple talaq — instant divorce by a husband’s verbal declaration — was not struck down by the Supreme Court until 2017.

The legal progress is real. The enforcement gap is also real. And the enforcement gap is a function of something deeper than law: the cultural infrastructure of patriarchy that treats women’s bodies, women’s labour, and women’s autonomy as community property rather than individual rights.

II. The Violence

The 2012 Delhi gang rape — the assault and murder of Jyoti Singh, a 23-year-old physiotherapy student, on a moving bus — was the event that forced India to confront its gender crisis on a national and international stage.

The details of the crime were of a brutality that tested the limits of reportage. The response was unprecedented: mass protests in Delhi and across the country, an outpouring of public grief and fury, and the appointment of the Verma Committee, which produced sweeping recommendations for reform of India’s sexual assault laws. The Criminal Law (Amendment) Act of 2013, passed in response, broadened the definition of sexual assault, increased penalties, and created new categories of offence including stalking, voyeurism, and acid attacks.

The 2012 case was not an anomaly. It was a symptom — a particularly horrific instance of violence against women that was distinguished from the daily reality not by its nature but by its visibility. The bus was in the capital. The victim was middle-class. The international media was watching. Thousands of rapes occur in India every year in circumstances that attract no media attention, no protests, and no legal reform — in rural areas, against Dalit women, against tribal women, against women whose caste and class render them invisible to the public conscience.

The intersection of gender and caste is particularly devastating. Dalit women are subjected to sexual violence at rates that dramatically exceed the national average, and the violence is often perpetrated by upper-caste men with impunity. Rape is used as a caste enforcement mechanism — a punishment for perceived transgressions of the caste boundary, a reminder of hierarchy inscribed on the body. The legal system, staffed disproportionately by upper-caste men, has historically treated these cases with indifference or complicity.

The Hathras case of 2020 — in which a 19-year-old Dalit woman was gang-raped by upper-caste men in Uttar Pradesh, died of her injuries, and was cremated by police in the middle of the night without her family’s consent — crystallized the intersection of caste and gender violence in a single horrifying sequence. The police’s behaviour — destroying evidence, preventing the family from accessing the body, initially denying that a sexual assault had occurred — was not aberrant. It was diagnostic.

III. The Missing Women

The economist Amartya Sen, in a landmark 1990 essay, estimated that over 100 million women were “missing” from the global population — women who should have been alive based on normal demographic ratios but who were not, due to sex-selective abortion, female infanticide, neglect of girls’ health and nutrition, and maternal mortality. A disproportionate number of these missing women were Indian.

The preference for sons in Indian society is not limited to any single community. It cuts across Hindu, Muslim, Sikh, and Christian families. It cuts across caste and class — indeed, the sex ratio is often worse among wealthier, more educated families who have access to prenatal sex determination technology. The Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques Act, passed in 1994 and amended in 2003, prohibits sex-selective abortion and the disclosure of fetal sex during ultrasound. It has not worked. India’s sex ratio at birth remains skewed, and the availability of ultrasound technology in every small town and many villages has made enforcement effectively impossible.

The consequences of the missing women are demographic, social, and political. In states like Haryana and Punjab — the agricultural heartland, Gurneet’s region — the sex ratio is so skewed that marriage markets have broken down. Men in these states import brides from eastern India, from tribal communities, from Bangladesh — women who are often economically vulnerable, linguistically isolated, and susceptible to exploitation. The trafficking of women for marriage is a direct consequence of the preference for sons carried to its demographic conclusion.

IV. The Labour Question

India’s female labour force participation rate — approximately 24 percent, by some measures even lower — is one of the great puzzles of Indian development economics. In most countries, economic growth increases women’s participation in the formal labour force. In India, the opposite has happened: as GDP has grown, female labour force participation has declined.

The explanations are multiple and overlapping. Rising household income allows families to withdraw women from the labour force — in a society where a woman’s working outside the home carries stigma, the ability to afford not to work is a mark of status. The decline of agriculture — where women’s labour was essential though unrecognized — has not been matched by a corresponding increase in formal-sector jobs available to women. Safety concerns — the real and reasonable fear of sexual harassment and assault in public spaces and workplaces — deter women from working outside the home. The burden of unpaid domestic labour — childcare, eldercare, cooking, cleaning — falls almost entirely on women, consuming hours that are unavailable for paid employment.

The economic cost is staggering. McKinsey estimated in 2015 that advancing women’s equality in India could add $700 billion to India’s GDP by 2025. The World Bank has repeatedly identified female labour force participation as the single greatest untapped driver of Indian economic growth. The talent is there. The barriers are structural, cultural, and political — and no major Indian political party has made women’s economic participation a central policy priority.

V. The Leaders

Against this backdrop of violence, discrimination, and structural exclusion, Indian women have led and continue to lead with a force that commands attention.

Indira Gandhi governed India for fifteen years. Whatever one thinks of the Emergency, and this series has been critica, the fact that a woman wielded that much power in a patriarchal society was itself a disruption. She was not a feminist in any programmatic sense. She was a dynast who happened to be female. But her example, the proof that a woman could govern the world’s largest democracy, command an army, win wars, and dominate a political landscape populated by men who underestimated her, left a mark that has not faded.

Arundhati Roy, the novelist and activist, has used her platform to challenge every orthodoxy Indian public life offers — from the Hindu nationalist project to the environmental destruction of dam-building to the treatment of Kashmiris and Dalits. She has been charged with sedition. She has not been silenced.

Medha Patkar led the Narmada Bachao Andolan — the Save the Narmada movement — against the construction of large dams that displaced tribal and rural communities. Irom Sharmila fasted for sixteen years — the longest hunger strike in recorded history — to protest the Armed Forces Special Powers Act in Manipur. Kiran Bedi became India’s first female IPS officer. Tessy Thomas led the development of the Agni-V ballistic missile. Sudha Murty, the philanthropist and writer, has built one of India’s most significant charitable foundations. Rikesh’s mother Asmita spent a career as a school principal, shaping generations of students — an act of leadership that no headline captures but that a society runs on.

The women of India are not waiting for permission. They are leading in spite of a system that is designed, at nearly every level, to prevent them from doing so. The question is not whether Indian women are capable. The question is whether India is capable of matching their capacity with opportunity.

VI. The Unfinished Revolution

Ambedkar, when he presented the constitution to the Constituent Assembly, warned that India was entering a life of contradictions: political equality coexisting with social and economic inequality. The contradiction he identified has no sharper expression than the gap between what the Indian Constitution promises women and what Indian society delivers.

The revolution that began in 1950 — the constitutional revolution, the legal revolution, the revolution of rights on paper — is unfinished. It will remain unfinished until the cultural infrastructure of patriarchy is dismantled with the same systematic effort that built it. Laws are necessary. They are not sufficient. What is required is what Ambedkar called social democracy — not merely the right to vote but the right to live with dignity, to work with safety, to move through public space without fear, and to be valued not as someone’s daughter, wife, or mother but as a citizen.

The Indian women’s movement — vibrant, diverse, increasingly intersectional, increasingly willing to confront caste and religion alongside gender — is fighting for this. It is fighting in the courts, on the streets, in the universities, in the media, and in the kitchen. The fight is not over. In a country of 700 million women, it has barely begun.

The impossible democracy is impossible in part because it demands that 700 million women be treated as full citizens in a society that has spent millennia treating them as property. If that demand is met — genuinely, structurally, not merely in law but in life — the Indian democratic experiment will have achieved something that no democracy in history has achieved: equality at scale. If it is not met, then the promise of the constitution remains what Ambedkar feared it might become: a magnificent lie.

The revolution is unfinished. The women are not waiting.

For every woman who was never asked what she wanted.
