Ask a Canadian at a hockey rink whether Quebec or Alberta could actually leave, and you will usually get one of two confident answers. The first is that a province can go whenever half its voters plus one say so, the way Britain left Europe. The second, more common in English Canada, is that Ottawa can simply stop it, whatever the count, because the Constitution says so and the army is ours.

Both answers are wrong. The second is wrong in a more interesting way.

Since August 20, 1998, Canada has had something few countries have: a judicial account of how part of it might lawfully leave. It does not say secession is forbidden. It says secession is negotiable. Everything since, from a federal statute in 2000 to a Quebec counter-statute that same year, from a prime minister’s remark in May 2026 to an Alberta courtroom that same month, has been an argument over who sets the terms before anyone sits down at the table.

Three Questions for the Court

The 1995 referendum, as Part III recounted, ended with 50.58 per cent voting No and a federal government that had come within about 54,000 votes of discovering it had no plan. Jean Chrétien’s cabinet decided it would not be caught that way twice. On September 30, 1996, it approved an order in council putting three questions to the Supreme Court of Canada. Could Quebec secede unilaterally under the Constitution? Did international law give it a right to do so? And if the two conflicted, which prevailed?

Lucien Bouchard’s government refused to take part. The Court appointed an amicus curiae to argue the case Quebec would not, then took nearly two years to write an opinion both sides would claim as a victory, which may be the most Canadian thing a court has ever done.

The judgment in Reference re Secession of Quebec, [1998] 2 S.C.R. 217 was delivered unsigned, “by the Court,” the device the justices use when they want the country to hear one voice.

No, Quebec could not secede unilaterally under the Constitution. Secession would require a constitutional amendment, and constitutional amendments are not something one province does alone.

No, international law did not help. Self-determination, the Court held, ordinarily means pursuing development within an existing state. A right to break away arises for colonial or occupied peoples, or perhaps those denied meaningful participation in government. Quebecers, who had supplied prime ministers for most of the previous three decades, did not fit. With no conflict between the two bodies of law, the third question answered itself.

Had the Court stopped there, the federalists would have had their prohibition. It did not stop there.

The Four Principles and the Duty

The Court built its answer on four unwritten principles underlying the Constitution: federalism, democracy, constitutionalism and the rule of law, and the protection of minorities. None could be applied in isolation.

From them it drew its famous conclusion. If a clear majority of Quebecers, on a clear question, voted to secede, the result would carry a democratic legitimacy the rest of Canada must recognize. Ottawa and the other provinces would have a constitutional duty to negotiate. The duty ran both ways: Quebec could not simply dictate terms, and neither side had a right to any particular outcome. A party that refused to negotiate in good faith, the Court added, would damage its standing internationally.

That was quietly radical. The Court acknowledged that an illegal unilateral secession might still succeed in fact, if the seceding government established effective control and other countries recognized it. The world would be watching how each side behaved.

Then came the part that mattered most. What counted as a “clear majority” on a “clear question” was for political actors to decide, not judges. The Court had drawn the field and declined to referee the game.

Bouchard welcomed the opinion as recognition that Quebec’s status was a political question. Chrétien’s government welcomed it as confirmation that a unilateral declaration was illegal. Both were right. Neither mentioned that the Court had handed them a blank space to fight over.

Stéphane Dion Fills in the Blank

Ottawa moved first. Stéphane Dion, a Université de Montréal political scientist whom Chrétien had recruited in January 1996 as minister of intergovernmental affairs, steered a short bill through Parliament. It received royal assent on June 29, 2000, mercifully known as the Clarity Act.

The text of the Clarity Act on the federal Justice Laws website is shorter than most Canadians assume and more pointed. Within 30 days of a province officially releasing a referendum question on secession, the House of Commons must consider it and decide, by resolution, whether it is clear. A question that “merely focus[es] on a mandate to negotiate” is deemed unclear. So is one that envisages other possibilities, such as economic or political arrangements with Canada, that obscure a direct expression of whether the province should cease to be part of Canada.

The 1980 question sought a mandate to negotiate sovereignty-association; the 1995 question tied sovereignty to an offer of a new economic and political partnership. Under the Clarity Act, both would have failed before a ballot was cast.

After a vote, the House must also decide whether there has been a clear expression of will by a clear majority, taking into account the size of the majority, the percentage of eligible voters who turned out, and “any other matters or circumstances it considers to be relevant.” The Act sets no number. If the House finds either the question or the majority unclear, the Government of Canada shall not enter into negotiations on secession.

The Act then lists what negotiations must address before any constitutional amendment, which it says secession requires: the division of assets and liabilities, changes to borders, the rights, interests and territorial claims of the Aboriginal peoples of Canada, and the protection of minority rights.

Here is the shape of the thing. Ottawa cannot lawfully refuse to negotiate after a clear vote. But Ottawa’s own House of Commons decides whether the vote was clear, on criteria it largely defines, after the fact.

Quebec Answers Back

Quebec’s reply came from the Parti Québécois government of Lucien Bouchard, through Joseph Facal, the minister for Canadian intergovernmental affairs. Bill 99 was introduced in the National Assembly on December 15, 1999, days after the federal bill appeared, passed on December 7, 2000, by a vote of 69 to 41, and received assent on December 13, 2000, according to the National Assembly’s own legislative record for Bill 99.

Its key clauses are blunt. Section 4 of the Act, as published on LégisQuébec, provides that the winning option in a referendum is the one that obtains a majority of the valid votes cast, “namely 50% of the valid votes cast plus one.” Section 13 declares that no other parliament or government may reduce the powers, authority, sovereignty or legitimacy of the National Assembly, or constrain the democratic will of the Québec people to determine its own future.

Two statutes, then, passed the same year, each claiming to implement the same opinion. One says the House of Commons decides what a clear majority is. The other says it is 50 per cent plus one, and that Ottawa has no say.

Keith Henderson Goes to Court

Keith Henderson, former leader of the anglophone-rights Equality Party, challenged Bill 99 soon after it passed. The case took nearly two decades to reach a verdict. In April 2018, Justice Claude Dallaire of the Quebec Superior Court upheld the law, finding, as Jonathan Montpetit reported for CBC News in “Contentious Parti Québécois law from 2000 not sovereignist plot, court rules”, that it did not authorize a unilateral declaration of independence and did not create new powers.

On April 9, 2021, the Quebec Court of Appeal rejected Henderson’s challenge. Justice Robert Mainville, writing for a panel with Justices Allan Hilton and Stéphane Sansfaçon, found no live dispute to decide, since no referendum had been held in more than 25 years. The court read the law as consistent with the Secession Reference, under which secession without prior negotiation would remain unlawful, as summarized in the Centre de recherche en droit’s “Sommaire de Cour d’appel: Henderson c. Procureur général du Québec, 2021 QCCA 565”. It did, however, strike the trial judge’s added declaration that the provisions were valid, warning it could breed confusion. As Selena Ross reported for CTV News in “Both sides declare a win after appeal ruling on Bill 99 and Quebec self-determination” (April 10, 2021), sovereigntists and federalists each claimed victory. Twenty-three years after the Reference, the pattern held.

So Bill 99 stands, but on a reading that makes its 50-per-cent-plus-one clause a statement of what wins a Quebec referendum, not of what obliges Ottawa to agree. Both laws survive. They simply do not meet.

The Formula Nobody Has Chosen

Suppose a clear majority votes Yes on a clear question, the House agrees, and negotiations succeed. The Clarity Act says secession then requires a constitutional amendment. Which one?

Here the law runs out. The Supreme Court declined to say. The general formula in section 38 of the Constitution Act, 1982 requires Parliament plus seven provincial legislatures representing at least half the population, the so-called 7/50 rule. Section 41 requires unanimity of Parliament and all ten legislatures for matters including the office of the Queen and the lieutenant governors, the composition of the Supreme Court, and the use of English and French.

Scholars have argued about this for decades. In a 1997 article in the McGill Law Journal, “The Legality of a Unilateral Declaration of Independence under Canadian Law”, Jeremy Webber argued that because secession necessarily abolishes or transforms the office of a province’s lieutenant governor, section 41 and its unanimity requirement would apply. Others contend the 7/50 formula would suffice. Nobody knows, and it matters: under unanimity, Prince Edward Island could in theory block a deal that every other legislature had approved.

Then come the questions the Clarity Act lists but does not answer. What share of the federal debt would a departing province carry? Would it keep the Canadian dollar, and who would set its interest rates? Are its borders today’s provincial lines, or could regions that voted No, or Indigenous nations, claim to stay? Part VI will take up the Crees and Inuit, who answered that last question themselves in 1995.

Carney’s Line, and Alberta’s Courtroom

For a quarter-century, federal politicians mostly avoided saying aloud what the Clarity Act implied. In the last week of May 2026, with Alberta’s petition in the news and the Parti Québécois leading Quebec polls, Mark Carney said that a 50 per cent plus one vote does not constitute a clear majority under the Clarity Act. The reaction in Quebec City was unanimous, which is itself a rare event. As Thomas Laberge of The Canadian Press reported on May 27, 2026, in “Quebec legislature members denounce Carney’s comments on Clarity Act”, members of the Coalition Avenir Québec, the Liberals, the Parti Québécois and Québec solidaire all rejected the statement. Justice Minister Simon Jolin-Barrette said the Quebec legal framework applies, “period.” Even the federalist Liberal leader, Charles Milliard, said Law 99 is very clear: 50 per cent plus one.

As Catherine Lévesque reported in the National Post on May 25, 2026, in “Bloc slams Carney for potential use of ‘anti-democratic’ Clarity Act in Alberta”, Carney had said his government had an obligation to examine any Alberta question for consistency with the Act. He has also said the Act will not apply to the question Albertans will actually face on October 19, as The Globe and Mail reported under the headline “Clarity Act will not apply to Alberta’s planned sovereignty vote, Carney says.”

That question exists because of a courtroom in Edmonton. On May 13, 2026, Justice Shaina Leonard of the Court of King’s Bench quashed the citizen-led separation petition in a challenge brought by the Athabasca Chipewyan First Nation and the Blackfoot Confederacy, as CP24 reported in “Alberta separation petition overturned as judge rules in favour of First Nations”. Alberta’s amendments to its referendum law the previous December, she found, had triggered a duty to consult Treaty First Nations that the province never fulfilled. Secession, she wrote, would self-evidently affect Treaties 7 and 8.

It was the second time in 2026 the petition had been struck down; in January, as JURIST reported in “Alberta to vote on whether to initiate independence referendum after adverse court rulings”, Justice Colin Feasby had quashed an earlier version for failing to guarantee constitutional protections. Premier Danielle Smith called the Leonard decision “anti-democratic,” promised an appeal, and on May 22 announced her own question for October 19: remain a province, or have the government commence the legal process toward a binding separation referendum. Her argument is that a question committing to nothing triggers no consultation. The First Nations reply that treaties were signed with the Crown, not with a premier’s choice of verbs.

Notice what the Alberta ruling adds to the 1998 architecture. The Supreme Court spoke of minorities and Aboriginal interests as matters for negotiation after a vote. Justice Leonard placed treaty obligations before the vote, at the moment a province changes the rules for asking. The barrier is moving upstream.

What the Shrug Gets Right and Wrong

The man in the Tim Hortons who looks up from his double-double at each new referendum and says, “Again? Must be Tuesday,” believes, roughly, that Ottawa will never let it happen. He is wrong about the mechanism and closer to right about the outcome.

Ottawa cannot veto a clear result. The Supreme Court said so 28 years ago. What Ottawa can do is decide, through the House of Commons, whether the question was clear and the majority sufficient; insist that debt, borders, minorities and Indigenous claims be settled before any amendment; and wait for a constitutional formula that may require the consent of every province. Quebec, for its part, has written into law that 50 per cent plus one is enough, and every party in its legislature agrees. The two positions have coexisted since 2000 precisely because no one has tested them.

The real barrier to leaving Canada, then, is not prohibition. It is negotiation: long, expensive, uncertain, conducted with a partner that writes the rules for judging your victory. It is also, not coincidentally, the perfect environment for leverage. A province that can credibly threaten to begin that negotiation has something to trade. A province that actually begins it discovers how much it will cost.

Quebec voted on October 5, 2026, and elected a Parti Québécois minority promising a referendum once Donald Trump leaves office. Alberta votes on October 19. Neither ballot, whatever it says, will settle the question the Supreme Court left open in 1998. It will only decide who gets to argue about it next.
