By Stephen Gordon | CanadianSnowmanSays

Seven days.

That is how long it took Tennessee to go from the Supreme Court handing down Louisiana v. Callais to Republican legislators unveiling a map that slices Memphis — a majority-Black city — into three pieces and absorbs them into rural, conservative districts stretching deep into the Tennessee countryside.

Seven days from ruling to map. The vote is expected Thursday.

In Part XX of this series, I wrote that the redistricting war had entered a new phase. In Part XXI, I called it a cascade. I want to retire the metaphor now, because what is happening in Tennessee, Louisiana, Alabama, and South Carolina simultaneously is no longer a cascade — a natural phenomenon, water following gravity. It is a coordinated campaign, moving at a pace that is itself the point. Speed is a strategy. Move faster than the courts can respond. Move faster than the public can process. Move faster than the opposition can organise.

Seven days.

What the Map Does

The current Tennessee congressional map has one Democratic district — the Ninth, centred on Memphis. It is majority-Black. It has been represented since 2007 by Steve Cohen, a white Democrat who has repeatedly maintained the support of Black voters through nineteen years of service. Cohen is currently facing a primary challenge from State Representative Justin J. Pearson, a young Black Democrat who became nationally known in 2023 when he was expelled from the Tennessee House for his role in a gun control protest — and then immediately reappointed by his constituents.

Under the proposed map, Shelby County — which encompasses Memphis — is split into three districts. One runs along the state’s western border before extending east to include part of Williamson County, a wealthy suburban county outside Nashville. Two others share parts of Shelby County with rural, conservative communities. The result: Memphis is divided. Its Democratic voters are diluted into three separate red districts. District 9 becomes a different shape, stretching east along the Tennessee-Mississippi border into deep-red territory.

The blue pocket disappears. The map turns uniformly red.

Speaker Cameron Sexton justified the new map with a statement that is worth preserving in full, because it is the fight-club rule stated more openly than we have heard it stated anywhere in this redistricting cycle: “The Supreme Court has opined that redistricting, like the judicial system, should be colorblind — the decision indicated states like Tennessee can redistrict based on partisan politics. Tennessee’s redistricting will reduce the risk of future legal challenges while promoting sound and strategic conservatism.”

Read that carefully. Sexton is using the Court’s own colorblind language to justify eliminating the one district where Black voters had the power to elect representatives of their choice. The circular logic is complete: the colorblind standard enables the elimination of Black representation, which is then described as colorblindness in action. Race had nothing to do with it. It was purely partisan. The fact that Black voters in Memphis vote Democratic is incidental. The fact that eliminating their district eliminates Black representation is incidental.

The Supreme Court has now ruled that this argument is legally sufficient. Speaker Sexton has absorbed the ruling in seven days.

The Cohen-Pearson Paradox

There is a detail in the Tennessee story that deserves its own paragraph, because it illuminates something the seat-count coverage cannot capture.

Steve Cohen and Justin Pearson are opponents. Cohen is the white nineteen-year incumbent defending his seat against a Black primary challenger who has significant national support and a compelling story. This is, in the normal course of democratic politics, a contested primary between two Democrats — the kind of intraparty competition that produces accountability and occasionally produces generational change.

Both men joined the same rally against the new map on Tuesday.

The redistricting has scrambled their primary so thoroughly that their individual contest has become secondary to the shared threat. The proposed map does not just endanger Cohen’s seat or Pearson’s path to Congress. It potentially eliminates both — by making the district they are competing for unwinnable for either of them.

This is what Glynda Carr meant in Part XXI when she asked “what do the next ten years look like if we disrupt the pipeline?” The pipeline disruption is not abstract. It looks like Steve Cohen and Justin Pearson standing at the same rally, united by the map that erases the seat they were competing to hold.

What Raumesh Akbari Said

State Senator Raumesh Akbari is a Democrat from Memphis. On Tuesday, on the Senate floor, she delivered what may be the most direct statement made by any elected official in this entire redistricting cycle.

“History will not look back kindly on you when you had an opportunity to do what was right and you chose to do something else.”

She was speaking to her Republican colleagues. She was speaking to a chamber where her party’s opposition means nothing — Republicans hold a supermajority, and some amount of Republican defection will not matter.

She said it anyway.

Hundreds of protesters gathered outside the State Capitol on Tuesday. Their chants and boos reverberated through the halls. Once inside, some were removed from the gallery. The House approved a strict rules package banning anyone removed for disorderly conduct from returning for the remainder of the session.

The legislature is moving. The protesters are outside. Senator Akbari is on the floor, speaking to colleagues who have already decided. She speaks anyway, because the record matters, because history is listening, because “as long as I have breath” is not just Spencer Deery’s phrase — it is the phrase of everyone who stands in a chamber that has stopped listening and speaks anyway.

The Simultaneous Fronts

Tennessee is not the only front moving this week. The cascade has become a multi-theatre operation.

Louisiana — Multiple lawsuits were filed this week against Governor Jeff Landry over his order suspending the state’s House primary. Voting rights groups and voters argued he had overstepped his executive authority by delaying the election to give legislators time to draw a new map. Thousands of absentee ballots had already been cast before the suspension was announced. The litigation is now running on a parallel track to the redistricting — courts being asked to restore a primary while the legislature draws the map that will replace it.

Alabama — Governor Kay Ivey, who said on Wednesday that Alabama’s court battles precluded new redistricting, reversed herself on Friday and called a special legislative session. Alabama is currently under a court order barring new maps until after the 2030 census. The special session’s immediate goal is not to draw a new map — it is to delay certain House primaries to create time for the state to get the court order lifted. Alabama is trying to redistrict its way around an injunction that exists specifically to prevent it from redistricting.

South Carolina — The legislature faces a deadline of May 14th, when its session ends. Conservative pressure is building. The question of whether South Carolina can move fast enough — and whether Republican leaders want to — is unresolved as of this writing.

Three states, three simultaneous legal and legislative battles, all triggered by a single Supreme Court ruling one week ago. The speed is not incidental. The speed is the strategy.

What Has to Be Repealed

There is one detail in the Tennessee story that has received insufficient attention, and it is the most clarifying detail of the week.

To pass a new congressional map, Tennessee’s legislature must first repeal a state law banning redistricting between census years.

The Republican supermajority is about to repeal its own state law — a law that exists specifically to prevent what it is about to do — in order to do it. The law was passed by Republicans. The repeal will be passed by Republicans. The justification is that the Supreme Court ruling creates new circumstances that supersede the state’s own prohibition.

This is the redistricting war at its most structurally honest: the rules that constrained the machine are being removed by the machine, one by one, as needed, to allow the machine to operate without constraint. The Indiana senators who said no to Trump’s redistricting demand were defending, among other things, the principle that states should govern their own processes. In Tennessee, that principle is being repealed by the same party that claims to hold it.

The vote is expected Thursday.

The Speed and What It Means

Seven days from ruling to map. One week from Callais to Memphis divided into three.

I want to be precise about what this speed tells us, because it is easy to read it simply as political efficiency — Republicans moving quickly to take advantage of a ruling that went their way. That reading is not wrong. But it misses something.

The speed tells us the maps were ready. Before the ruling was handed down, before the ink was dry, before the legal implications were fully parsed — someone had drawn these lines. DeSantis drew Florida’s map in anticipation of the ruling. Tennessee Republicans are moving within seven days because the work was already done, waiting for the legal clearance the Supreme Court provided on Wednesday.

This is not opportunism in the usual sense. Opportunism responds to unexpected events. What is happening in Tennessee and Florida and Louisiana and Alabama is the execution of a plan that was made before the opportunity arrived — a plan that assumed the ruling would come, calculated what would be legally permissible afterward, and prepared accordingly.

The preparation is the tell. The tell is the speed.

The View From Here

Part XX of this series opened with a single sentence: “Eight days ago, Virginia voted.” The redistricting war had arrived at a rough draw. I said so.

I was wrong within 48 hours. The Texas ruling on Monday. Callais on Wednesday. Florida on Wednesday afternoon. Louisiana’s primaries suspended Thursday. Alabama reversed Friday. Tennessee’s map unveiled Wednesday of the following week.

That is twelve days from Virginia’s rough draw to Tennessee’s Memphis being sliced into three pieces. Twelve days. In political time, twelve days is usually nothing. In redistricting time, in the spring of 2026, twelve days is the distance between equilibrium and a new map that eliminates a majority-Black district in the birthplace of the civil rights movement.

Senator Akbari said history will not look back kindly. She is right. She said it to a chamber that had already decided. That is what it looks like when democracy fights back inside an institution that has stopped fighting fair — you speak the record into existence, you make history listen even when the room is not listening, you stand at a microphone in Nashville and say the true thing to people who have chosen to ignore it.

The vote is Thursday. The lawsuits will follow. The cascade continues.
