Every institution this old accumulates folklore, and the United Nations has accumulated more than most, in part because its Assembly floor is one of the very few rooms on earth where the leaders of adversarial states are guaranteed to sit within a few dozen metres of one another at least once a year. Some of what follows is exactly as dramatic as its reputation suggests. Some of it is not what people remember it as. Getting the distinction right matters for a series built on the premise, stated at the outset, that this history should be reckoned with honestly rather than mythologised in either direction.

The shoe that (probably) never hit the desk

The single most famous image in UN General Assembly history may not have happened at all. On 12 October 1960, during a heated Assembly debate over decolonisation and Soviet conduct in Eastern Europe, the Philippine delegate Lorenzo Sumulong rose to accuse the Soviet Union of practising precisely the colonial domination it claimed to oppose, telling the chamber that the peoples of Eastern Europe “have been deprived of the free exercise of their civil and political rights and which have been swallowed up, so to speak, by the Soviet Union.” Nikita Khrushchev, leading the Soviet delegation, was furious, banging his fist on the desk and shouting objections through the Assembly president’s attempts to restore order. Somewhere in the retelling of that afternoon, the fist became a shoe — a story that has circulated for more than sixty years, illustrated by a single widely reproduced photograph that has since been established, by UN Audiovisual Library researchers who examined the available film and photographic record, to be a composite: a shoe pasted into the image after the fact. No contemporary photographer or news film actually captured Khrushchev removing and banging a shoe, and the balance of eyewitness testimony from journalists present that day leans toward fist-pounding and fierce interruption rather than a shoe ever leaving his foot. What is not in dispute is that the confrontation happened, that Khrushchev was genuinely enraged, and that the same fall session saw the Assembly president’s own gavel break under the strain of maintaining order — a detail somehow less famous than the shoe that likely never was.

The confrontation did not occur in a vacuum. The autumn 1960 Assembly session was unusually charged even by Cold War standards: seventeen newly independent African states had joined the UN that year alone, the Assembly was in the middle of debating what would become, that December, the Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples — a landmark resolution formally condemning colonialism that the Soviet Union had pushed hard to associate itself with, positioning Moscow as the anti-colonial world’s natural ally against the Western powers still holding onto empire. Khrushchev’s fury at Sumulong’s counter-accusation landed, in other words, squarely on the rhetorical ground the Soviet delegation had spent the whole session trying to claim for itself, which goes some way toward explaining why the reaction was as theatrical as it was, shoe or no shoe.

The same 1960 session produced a genuine record still standing today: on 26 September, Fidel Castro delivered a General Assembly address lasting 269 minutes — four hours and twenty-nine minutes — the longest speech in the Assembly’s history, denouncing colonialism and American policy toward Cuba at a length that has never been approached since, by any delegate, on any subject. Castro’s marathon and Khrushchev’s genuine, if shoeless, outburst arrived within days of one another, at a session that also saw New York’s hotels initially refuse to house the Cuban delegation, prompting Castro to decamp to a hotel in Harlem where he met with Malcolm X — a footnote to the same eventful fortnight that rarely makes it into the shoe story at all. Khrushchev himself paid Castro a visit at that same Harlem hotel during the session, embracing him warmly in front of assembled photographers, a scene that did more to cement the Soviet-Cuban alignment in the public imagination than either man’s formal remarks to the Assembly managed to do.

Olive branch, freedom fighter’s gun

Fourteen years later, on 13 November 1974, Yasser Arafat became the first representative of a non-state national liberation movement to address the General Assembly, arriving by helicopter as chairman of the Palestine Liberation Organisation and closing his address with a line that has outlived almost everything else said from that podium in the twentieth century: “Today I have come bearing an olive branch and a freedom fighter’s gun. Do not let the olive branch fall from my hand.” Arafat wore a holstered pistol into the chamber, a detail that produced considerable alarm among UN security staff and became, almost immediately, as much a part of the story as the speech itself — though contemporary accounts differ on whether the weapon was actually visible to delegates on the floor or remained concealed beneath his jacket throughout, one of the smaller uncertainties this essay’s title is built around. What is not uncertain is the substance of what the speech accomplished: it was the moment the Assembly, by a large majority, formally recognised the Palestinian people’s right to self-determination and granted the PLO observer status at the UN, a diplomatic achievement that outlasted every detail of the theatre surrounding its delivery.

The diplomatic consequences of that session ran further than the observer-status vote alone, and they continued to reverberate through the Assembly for years afterward. The following autumn, on 10 November 1975, the Assembly passed Resolution 3379, determining by a vote of 72 to 35 with 32 abstentions that “zionism is a form of racism and racial discrimination” — a resolution the US ambassador Daniel Patrick Moynihan denounced from the floor that same day, telling the Assembly the United States “will never acquiesce in this infamous act.” The resolution stood on the books for sixteen years until the Assembly voted, 111 to 25 with 13 abstentions, to revoke it on 16 December 1991, after Israel made the revocation a condition of its participation in the Madrid peace conference that had opened weeks earlier — to date, the only resolution the General Assembly has ever formally repealed. The 1974 and 1975 sessions, taken together, mark the high-water point of the Assembly’s use as a forum for reshaping the international legal and political status of the Israeli-Palestinian conflict through sheer weight of numbers — a use of the body’s genuine power that had nothing to do with the Security Council’s veto-bound paralysis and everything to do with the Assembly’s own, much less constrained, one-state-one-vote arithmetic. It is a reminder, arriving in this essay about theatre, that the Assembly floor’s real influence has always run on two tracks simultaneously: the spectacle that gets remembered, and the resolutions, votes, and legal determinations that quietly do the actual work of shifting how the world talks about a conflict, whether or not anyone was watching closely enough to notice at the time.

A claim made at Columbia, not at the UN

Not every widely remembered “UN moment” actually happened at the UN, and one of the most cited examples deserves a direct correction, because the misattribution has calcified into received wisdom over nearly two decades. In September 2007, Iranian President Mahmoud Ahmadinejad travelled to New York during the UN General Assembly’s general debate, as heads of state and government routinely do, and delivered his own address to the Assembly on 25 September. The remark most people associate with that trip — “In Iran, we don’t have homosexuals like in your country” — was made the previous day, on 24 September, not on the Assembly floor at all but during a question-and-answer session at Columbia University, where Ahmadinejad had been invited to speak as part of the university’s World Leaders Forum, introduced by university president Lee Bollinger with a pointed public rebuke of Iran’s human rights record. The Columbia remark drew far more press coverage at the time than Ahmadinejad’s actual UN speech the following day, and the two events have since blurred together in popular memory into a single “UN moment” that never technically occurred at the UN. The distinction matters for reasons beyond pedantry: it is a small, specific example of how the Assembly’s genuine reputation for producing extraordinary moments makes it a magnet for misattributed ones too, and a series built on getting the historical record right owes its readers the correction even when the more dramatic version of the story is the one everyone already knows.

The Assembly returned to the Arafat story once more in 1988, in a coda that is itself a small piece of institutional theatre worth including here. When the United States denied Arafat a visa to attend that year’s General Assembly session in New York, citing his organisation’s links to terrorism, the Assembly responded by voting to relocate its entire session to Geneva for a single meeting — the first and, to date, only time the General Assembly has moved its proceedings outside UN headquarters specifically to accommodate one speaker the host country had refused to admit. Arafat opened his remarks in Geneva by noting, with evident satisfaction, that he had not expected his second appearance before the Assembly since 1974 to take place in “the hospitable city” rather than New York — a pointed, understated dig at Washington that the American delegation could do nothing about, since the Assembly’s power to relocate its own sessions falls, unlike Security Council enforcement action, entirely outside any single member state’s veto.

Triple sabotage

The most recent addition to this catalogue needs no correction, because it happened exactly as reported, on camera, in front of the assembled press corps of the entire world. On 23 September 2025, President Trump arrived at the General Assembly to address the 80th session and found, in short order, an escalator that stopped abruptly partway up with the president and First Lady Melania Trump aboard, and a teleprompter that went dark as he began his prepared remarks. Trump opened his address by noting he was “delivering it without the use of a teleprompter, because the teleprompter is not working,” joked that “whoever’s operating this teleprompter is in big trouble,” and later posted on social media that he had been the victim of “three very sinister events” — the escalator, the teleprompter, and a sound system he said had also failed — demanding a Secret Service investigation into what he called deliberate sabotage. Karoline Leavitt, the White House press secretary, posted publicly that anyone found to have deliberately halted the escalator “need[ed] to be fired and investigated immediately,” and the newly installed US ambassador to the UN formally demanded a full accounting of the teleprompter failure’s “root cause” along with assurances against any repeat. UN officials, in response, said the escalator’s built-in safety mechanism had most likely been triggered accidentally by a member of Trump’s own delegation filming the arrival, and that the teleprompter in question had been operated by the White House’s own equipment rather than the UN’s, which they said had functioned normally throughout. Whatever actually malfunctioned and why, the episode became instant, self-generating theatre in a way that would have been entirely legible to Khrushchev or Castro: a head of state, a global audience, and a technical mishap transformed within hours into a story about institutional hostility rather than institutional accident.

What the spectacle usually covers for

There is a thread connecting all five of these episodes worth naming directly, because it is easy to enjoy them as anecdotes and miss what they have in common structurally. Each one arrived at a moment of genuine substance — a decolonisation debate that would reshape UN membership through the 1960s, the first formal recognition of Palestinian self-determination, a human-rights confrontation over a government’s own record, an American president’s address to a body he has repeatedly threatened to defund and delegitimise — and in each case, the theatrical detail crowded out, in public memory, the substantive event it accompanied. Nobody outside specialists remembers what the Assembly actually voted on the day Arafat spoke; everyone remembers the holster. Nobody remembers that Ahmadinejad’s actual UN speech the day after Columbia was, by most accounts, a comparatively unremarkable recitation of familiar Iranian grievances against Western power; everyone remembers a line he never delivered from that podium at all.

This is not a criticism of the people present at the time, who could hardly control what cameras chose to capture or what stories editors chose to run — nor is it a claim that the theatre is somehow beneath the institution’s dignity or unworthy of a place in its history. A body that brings together the leaders of mutually hostile states in one room, year after year, is always going to produce moments that read better as drama than as diplomacy, and pretending otherwise would be its own kind of dishonesty. Security protocols at the UN have themselves absorbed some of these lessons quietly over the decades — visitor screening and weapons policy tightened considerably in the years after 1974, in ways few outside UN security staff ever notice, precisely because Arafat’s holster made an impression that outlasted the speech itself. It is, instead, a caution for the rest of this series, because the chapters that follow deal with substance far more consequential than theatre — Korea’s accidental loophole, Rwanda’s abandonment, Iraq’s unauthorised invasion, Syria’s vetoed ceasefires — and none of it comes with a shoe, a holster, or a broken escalator to make it memorable. It will have to earn the reader’s attention on the strength of what actually happened instead, which is, on balance, a considerably harder and more important thing for an institution to be judged on than whether its diplomats behaved themselves in front of the cameras.
