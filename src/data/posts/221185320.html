On September 17, 2026, Mark Carney stood in the hemicycle of the European Parliament in Strasbourg and told a story about acorns. In 1917, amid the wreckage of Vimy Ridge, a young Canadian lieutenant named Leslie Miller scooped a handful of them out of the shattered ground and carried them home to Ontario. They grew into oaks. A century later, grafts from those Canadian oaks were sent back to France and replanted on the ridge where their parents had stood. As the BNN Bloomberg transcript of the address shows, Carney offered this as the essence of the partnership between Canada and Europe.

It is a lovely story, and it is perfectly chosen. It says that Canada’s roots are tangled with Europe’s, that what was carried west can be carried back, and that a relationship can survive being uprooted.

What it does not say is that the acorns travelled on a troopship, and that for most of the century since, Canada’s military has travelled on American infrastructure. Oaks can be regrafted. Plumbing is harder.

This series has so far looked at Carney’s diplomatic language (Part I), his economic record (Part II), his quiet preparations for the “extreme tail risk” of American aggression (Part III), the political clock he is running against (Part IV), and Chrystia Freeland’s argument that only strength works with this president (Part V). This instalment looks at the part of the sovereignty project that is hardest to see and most expensive to change: the armed forces, and the seventy years of American pipework running through them.

Two essays from The Atlantic, published four weeks apart, frame the problem. Read together, they disagree about the one question that matters most.

I. The Pipework

Philippe Lagassé, who holds the Barton Chair at Carleton University’s Norman Paterson School of International Affairs and sits on the Independent Review Panel for Defence Acquisition, put the matter bluntly in The Atlantic on October 19, 2026: no American ally has wound its own self-defence more tightly around the U.S. military than Canada has.

The winding began early. In August 1940, with France fallen and Britain alone, Franklin Roosevelt and William Lyon Mackenzie King met at Ogdensburg, New York, and created the Permanent Joint Board on Defence, the first institution of continental military cooperation between the two countries. In 1957 came the North American Aerospace Defense Command, NORAD, with a Canadian deputy commander and a binational chain of command built to watch for Soviet bombers coming over the pole.

From there, Lagassé argues, the Canadian Armed Forces were shaped to deploy alongside American forces and to slot into American systems without friction. That was not an accident or a failure of nerve. It was a bargain, and for decades it was a good one. A small, chronically underfunded military could make contributions that mattered, in both countries’ interest, at a fraction of what genuine independence would cost.

The bargain was renewed as recently as the Trudeau government, which contracted for American-made surveillance aircraft, an American combat system for Canada’s new warships, and 88 F-35 fighters. Each purchase made sense on its own terms. Each also tightened the coupling. As Lagassé notes, Canada cannot mount a major operation overseas without American support, and like most NATO allies it depends on the United States for communications, command and control networks, and intelligence.

None of this mattered much while the United States was what Lagassé calls a benign ally. You do not inspect the plumbing in a house where the water has always run clean. You inspect it when the landlord starts muttering about converting the place into a 51st unit.

II. One Hand on the Wheel, One on the Door

What Carney has done in response is best described as two policies running at once.

The first is reassurance. Canada’s defence spending reached NATO’s 2 percent of GDP benchmark, confirmed by the alliance in March 2026. Carney signed on to the new NATO pledge of 5 percent by 2035, with 3.5 percent earmarked for core military capability. His government has said it is in talks about joining Golden Dome, Trump’s continental missile shield. It has floated “Fortress North America” as a joint answer to Chinese economic power. And according to Lagassé, Canada’s defence minister reportedly assured Pete Hegseth that Canada was moving in lockstep with the United States on continental defence.

The second is departure. In February 2026, Carney released Canada’s first-ever defence industrial strategy. As Axios summarised it at the time, the plan pledges to raise the share of defence acquisitions awarded to Canadian firms to 70 percent, increase defence exports by half, and create 125,000 jobs, with Carney promising to double defence spending by the end of the decade. The Defense Post put the ten-year investment figure at CA$500 billion. Today, by Lagassé’s account, more than 70 percent of the military capital budget goes to American equipment. The strategy proposes to flip that ratio within ten years.

The departures are already visible in the hardware. Canada chose Sweden’s Saab for its new airborne early-warning aircraft instead of an American platform. The F-35 order is under review, with Saab’s Gripen fighter in the frame for at least part of the fleet, a question first raised in Part III and still open. This month the government also paused its purchase of U.S.-made Black Hawk helicopters because of trade and diplomatic friction, according to CBC News and Radio-Canada.

Is it contradictory to swear lockstep with Hegseth while routing seven of every ten procurement dollars away from American contractors? Of course it is. It is also the only sensible thing a country in Canada’s position can do. The reassurance buys time. The departure uses it. The trick is that the first track has to be convincing enough to keep Washington calm while the second is real enough to matter if Washington stops being calm.

Washington has noticed both tracks, and it did not take long to show which one it prefers.

III. The Board That Stopped Meeting

On May 18, 2026, Under Secretary of Defense for Policy Elbridge Colby announced on X that the Pentagon was “pausing” American participation in the Permanent Joint Board on Defence, the same body Roosevelt and King had founded at Ogdensburg 86 years earlier. As UPI reported, Colby said Canada had failed to make credible progress on its defence commitments and that the Pentagon would reassess what the forum was worth.

Lagassé, writing on his own Substack at the time, pointed out that Colby tied the pause not only to spending but to Carney’s speech at Davos in January 2026, the one in which Carney declared the rules-based order to be fading and urged middle powers to stand together. Spending, Lagassé argued, was not the real grievance. The speech was.

Carney shrugged the pause off. The board had not met since 2024, his government noted, and the real machinery of cooperation, NORAD modernisation, exercises, intelligence sharing, carried on untouched. True, but beside the point. The Permanent Joint Board was the institution in which Canada was formally the equal of the United States. Suspending it was a way of saying that the equality was a courtesy, revocable at will, and that it had just been revoked for a speech.

Even some Republicans winced. Representative Don Bacon of Nebraska, as Al Jazeera reported, called for cooler and wiser heads to preserve the alliance.

Here is Lagassé’s real worry, and it is the sharpest point in his essay. If the larger partner in continental defence decides that defending the continent requires American equipment, the F-35, American surveillance aircraft, American networks, Canada’s freedom to buy elsewhere may turn out to be theoretical. You can choose a Swedish airplane. You cannot choose a Swedish NORAD.

IV. The European Door

Which is why the other half of Carney’s strategy runs through Brussels and Strasbourg.

On December 1, 2025, Canada became the first non-European country to join SAFE, the European Union’s €150 billion Security Action for Europe defence-financing instrument. As Al Jazeera reported at the time, Carney said participation would fill capability gaps, open markets for Canadian suppliers and draw European defence investment into Canada. The Council of the EU gave its final approval in June 2026. Canada is, so far, the only non-European member.

Then, on September 16, 2026, came the larger gesture. In her State of the Union address in Strasbourg, with Carney seated in the front row, Ursula von der Leyen proposed opening the door for Canada to become the EU’s first “associate member.” As MercoPress noted, the status does not exist in the EU’s treaties and nobody has yet defined what it would mean. Trump called the idea laughable. Pierre Poilievre said Canada would be neither the 51st American state nor the 28th European one.

Carney’s speech the next day was the acorn speech. He welcomed the offer, proposed an alliance covering defence, energy, critical minerals and artificial intelligence, ruled out full membership, and, as Euronews reported from his press conference afterwards, promised that any final arrangement would be put to a vote in Canada’s own Parliament.

It is worth being precise about what the European door offers and what it does not. It offers markets, money, partners and a second customer for Canadian defence firms that cannot live on Ottawa’s orders alone. It does not offer a replacement for the American intelligence feed, the American satellites or the American command networks that Lagassé lists. Europe is building those for itself, from a standing start, on a 2030 timetable it is not sure it will meet. Canada can share in that work. It cannot borrow what does not yet exist.

V. Who Pays

Lagassé puts core defence spending at roughly $64 billion in 2025 and projects it at $160 billion by 2035. That is a two-and-a-half-fold increase in a decade, and he is right to call it a level of peacetime spending that would ordinarily be unthinkable.

He is also right about the uncomfortable corollary. Carney has not said how he will pay for it. The options are the ones they always are: new taxes, cuts to social programmes, or a lot more debt. So far, Canadians have been asked to sacrifice nothing. Part II of this series showed a Canadian fiscal position that is healthier than the American one, but healthier is not the same as limitless, and Part IV found Carney already juggling stimulus against inflation with a two-year lag before his big projects pay off. Defence will be competing for the same dollars as Churchill Falls, housing and health care, and it will be competing at a time when the trade war is cutting into the revenue that pays for all of them.

Then there is the industrial base itself. Canada’s defence industry is small and, as Lagassé notes, not self-sufficient. Firms cannot build factories on the strength of speeches. They need long-term contracts, signed and funded, before they hire and tool up. The 70 percent target is a promise about 2036. The contracts that would make it true have to be signed in 2026 and 2027.

VI. Weather or Climate

This brings us to the disagreement.

Near the end of his essay, Lagassé reports that most of the Canadian military leaders he has spoken with assume the relationship will largely recover once Trump has left office. Intelligence sharing, exchanges and NORAD operations continue as normal. Planners on both sides understand that cooperation has to outlast tariffs and insults. On this view, Trump is weather: a severe storm, damaging, but a storm, and it will pass.

That confidence may already be fraying. Two days after Lagassé’s essay, Isaac Stanley-Becker reported in The Atlantic on October 21, 2026 that Canada has struggled to find senior officers willing to take postings at NORAD and other U.S. commands. They fear the career damage, the shrinking access and the families who no longer want to cross the border. “The day-to-day relationship has deteriorated,” the Ottawa defence analyst David Perry told him. The next instalment of this series takes up that story.

Vivian Salama, in The Atlantic on September 22, 2026, reported the opposite view from the people whose job is to read Washington for a living. Foreign officials told her that allies have stopped waiting Trump out. They now assume American reliability will not snap back, whoever wins in 2028. One senior European official described Trump as an earthquake and said it would be foolish not to prepare for the aftershocks.

Their reasons are worth listing. They look past Trump to J.D. Vance, whom they regard as no friend of Europe. They hear calls on the Democratic left to defund the Pentagon. They note that Joe Biden kept most of Trump’s first-term tariffs, and that progressive Democrats have embraced protectionism of their own. Katherine Tai, who was Biden’s trade representative, told Salama that after Trump’s first term she and other officials were sent abroad to reassure allies that America was back, and that she doubts America gets to make that declaration a second time. On this view, Trump is climate.

The Canadian public seems to have reached its own verdict. A Pew survey cited by Salama found 44 percent of Canadians viewing China favourably against 33 percent for the United States, a figure that would have been unimaginable a decade ago and says less about Beijing’s charms than about Washington’s behaviour. And in August 2026 the administration imposed 50 percent tariffs on roughly $20 billion of Canadian goods, which is not how a passing storm behaves.

Where does Carney stand? Part III gave the answer. In his September 23, 2026 interview with the New York Times, he confirmed he had spent a year preparing for the extreme tail risk of American military action against Canada. A prime minister who thought Trump was weather would not be doing that. Carney is building on Salama’s assumption while his officers, for now, continue to work on Lagassé’s. It is a strange arrangement, but it may be the right one. The officers keep the pipes running, because the pipes are all there is for now. The prime minister builds the second system, slowly and expensively, in case the first is shut off at the main.

Trump, for his part, seems to have noticed. Salama quotes him telling reporters that Canada, not China, is now the toughest country to deal with, because Canada “has felt emboldened.” Coming from him, that is close to a compliment.

VII. Sleeping Next to the Elephant

Freeland, whose essay anchored Part V, recalls Pierre Trudeau’s line that sharing a border with the United States is like sleeping next to an elephant: however friendly the animal, every twitch and grunt is felt. For seventy years Canada handled the elephant by building its bed into the elephant’s. It was warm, it was cheap, and it was safe for as long as the elephant was in a good mood.

What Carney is attempting is to build a bed of its own without leaving the room. That is the honest summary of the defence file. Canada will not leave NORAD. It will not stop sharing intelligence. Lagassé is surely right that the defence of North America will always be a joint project led from Washington. But it is buying Swedish aircraft, borrowing European money, building Canadian factories and signing up for an alliance with Brussels that does not yet have a name, so that if the elephant ever rolls over, there is somewhere to go.

Carney said it plainly in 2025, weeks after taking office: the old relationship of steadily deepening integration with the United States is over, and while that is a tragedy, it is also the new reality. Salama’s reporting suggests most of America’s allies have reached the same conclusion.

The acorns Leslie Miller carried home from Vimy took a century to make the return trip. Canada does not have a century. It has about ten years, a $160 billion bill nobody has yet been asked to pay, and a set of pipes that still runs south. The oaks are a fine metaphor. The plumbing is the job.
