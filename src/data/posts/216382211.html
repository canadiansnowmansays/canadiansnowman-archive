A note on this essay: Epilogue I covered what was threatened. Epilogue II covered what was warned. This one covers what the drafted series assumed would happen next — the “Wednesday meeting” and the hemispheric “Donroe Doctrine” endgame of the original Parts 11 and 12 — measured against what actually happened between January and September 2026. It closes the series.

I. The Meeting That Actually Happened

The Wednesday meeting the earlier drafts kept building toward did take place, on January 14, 2026, when Vice President JD Vance and Secretary of State Marco Rubio hosted Danish Foreign Minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen and Greenlandic Foreign Minister Vivian Motzfeldt at the White House. It lasted several hours. Rasmussen emerged calling the talks “frank” and “constructive” — diplomatic language for confrontational without becoming explosive — and gave the line that ended up defining the meeting: “We have a fundamental disagreement, but we also agreed to disagree. We will, however, continue to talk.”

What the meeting actually produced was a working group: the three governments agreed to keep negotiating toward something that could accommodate Trump’s stated security concerns, respect Danish territorial integrity, and preserve Greenlandic self-determination, all at once. What it did not produce was any compromise on sovereignty, any agreed security framework, or any softening of the American position. Rasmussen confirmed, when asked directly, that American officials offered no apology for the threats and no retreat from Trump’s ownership demands.

The meeting also surfaced something that never got the attention it deserved at the time: a direct intelligence dispute. Rasmussen told reporters plainly that Danish intelligence hadn’t recorded a Chinese warship near Greenland “for a decade or so” — directly contradicting the security rationale Trump had been citing publicly. Independent Arctic security analysts backed Denmark’s account. If the stated justification for the entire campaign was Russian and Chinese activity in Greenlandic waters, the country actually responsible for monitoring those waters was, on the record, saying that threat wasn’t current. It was the closest thing to on-the-record proof that the security argument had never really been the argument, offered by the one government with the intelligence to know.

Read at the time, this looked like exactly what the earlier drafts called it: theater. A working group is what two sides agree to when neither is prepared to concede and neither is prepared to walk away — process standing in for progress, buying time without changing the underlying arithmetic. Denmark offered everything short of sovereignty. Trump wanted sovereignty specifically. A working group cannot reconcile those positions; it can only postpone the moment when someone has to choose between them. Denmark’s own actions in the days after the meeting read as preparation rather than confidence: additional Danish forces deployed to Greenland, Swedish troops arriving at Denmark’s request. That is not the posture of a government that believes talking has solved the problem.

II. How Close It Actually Got, and the Stand-Down That Wasn't Quite One

What Isaac Stanley-Becker reported for The Atlantic on September 18 adds, and what the drafted essays never had access to, is confirmation of just how far preparations went before anyone stood down. Danish armed forces developed detailed contingency plans to destroy Greenlandic airfields rather than let them fall into American hands, and flew emergency blood supplies and explosives to the island to prepare for a possible U.S. attack. Frederiksen herself warned that an American invasion would mean “everything ends” for NATO — not weakens, not strains: ends. That is not the language of a government treating the threat as posturing to be waited out. It’s the language of a government that ran the invasion scenario seriously enough to move munitions.

Then, on January 21 — one week after the White House meeting, and the day after Part 1 of this series published — Trump stood at Davos and stepped back, proclaiming what he called the “framework of a future deal.” It should have been, by any normal reading, the end of the crisis this series was built to document. It wasn’t, quite. In the months that followed, per the same Atlantic account, Trump kept returning to the subject “in fits of pique,” at one point arguing Greenland “should be controlled by the United States, not Denmark.” That line was more fully quoted in a New York Times explainer by Maya Tekeli, Lynsey Chutel, and Amelia Nierenberg published September 19: at a NATO summit in July, Trump told reporters, “Greenland is a big problem for us. We need it for protection of the world, not just the United States, and it’s very important. It doesn’t help Denmark.” Even after the framework existed, the pressure campaign this series spent ten essays documenting didn’t fully stop. It just stopped escalating toward force.

It is worth sitting with how little coverage the actual turning point got, relative to the threats that preceded it. The invasion rhetoric had been front-page news for weeks. The Davos reversal, delivered at an economic forum alongside dozens of other headlines, registered as a footnote to most outlets already moving on to the next crisis — which, as this series noted at the very start, is exactly the pattern Trump-era news cycles tend to follow. A story escalates loudly and de-escalates quietly, in fits and starts rather than a single clean turning point, and the quiet part rarely gets the retrospective explainer it deserves.

It should have. The Davos framework, however incomplete and however often Trump wandered back toward the old rhetoric afterward, is the actual hinge point of this entire story — the moment the crisis this series was built to document stopped escalating toward force and started, slowly and with almost no public accounting, turning into something else. Parts 3 through 12 of the original series were drafted on the assumption that the crisis was still live through February. It wasn’t, not in the form those essays assumed, though it also wasn’t fully resolved for another eight months. What followed the Davos framework wasn’t a straight line to an agreement. It was closed-door negotiations — Washington, roughly once a month, for the better part of a year — punctuated by exactly the kind of rhetorical relapses that kept Greenlanders from ever quite believing it was over.

III. What the Donroe Doctrine Predicted

The final drafted essay in this series, Part 12, never got to make its argument in public, but it deserves to be stated plainly here, because it was largely right about the shape of things even where it was wrong about the ending. It opened with the same image this series’ Introduction described: a map that had circulated in MAGA circles the previous summer, showing American territory stretched from the Arctic to Mexico — Canada, Greenland, the Panama Canal, reaching toward Venezuela — reposted at one point by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, which was how it first came to wider attention rather than staying confined to fringe social accounts. At the time it first circulated, the map read as fantasy, the kind of thing easy to dismiss as online noise rather than policy. Part 12’s argument was that Venezuela had already proven it wasn’t fantasy, and that each territory on that map served a specific, identifiable purpose in the same acquisitive logic: Greenland for Arctic control and missile defence, positioned as a symbolic victory proving allied territory could be taken without catastrophic consequence; Venezuela’s oil reserves and the elimination of a government Washington had wanted gone for years; the Panama Canal as both a critical shipping chokepoint and a symbolic reversal of what Trump has long called a historical giveaway; Canadian resources — water, timber, minerals — folded into what the essay called the psychological completion of manifest destiny; Mexico, where drug-war justifications provided the pretext for border-security and economic-subordination arguments that had little to do with drugs specifically.

The essay named the whole pattern the “Donroe Doctrine” — Monroe’s 1823 principle that the Western Hemisphere was closed to European colonization, inverted into something closer to American recolonization of the same hemisphere Monroe had meant to protect. “Monroe’s Western Hemisphere shielded from European interference becomes Trump’s Western Hemisphere subject to American domination,” the draft argued. “Monroe protected sovereignty. Trump demands surrender. Monroe excluded colonizers. Trump becomes colonizer.” The essay was explicit that this wasn’t a revival of the original doctrine so much as its inversion, dressed in familiar rhetoric — hemispheric security, American leadership — that made the reversal easy to miss if you weren’t looking for it.

Measured against Venezuela — an operation that had already happened by the time this series began, resulting in American forces effectively controlling the country’s government — the Donroe Doctrine framing was not hyperbole. It was description. Measured against Greenland specifically, it captured the logic driving the threat with real precision: Trump’s stated need for ownership, not access; the doctrine Miller articulated on cable news; the psychological rather than strategic character of the demand. Where the doctrine’s forecast diverged from what actually happened was in the method, not the appetite. The essay assumed the acquisitive logic would keep escalating toward something closer to Venezuela’s outcome — coercion, pressure, and eventually force, applied to an ally the same way it had just been applied to an adversary. What actually happened was that the same administration that produced the Miller Doctrine and the Donroe Doctrine framing also, eight months later, signed a security agreement that left Danish sovereignty and Greenlandic self-determination formally intact.

That is either evidence the doctrine was never going to survive contact with an actual NATO ally the way it survived contact with Venezuela — the Article 5 crisis analyzed in Epilogue I doing real deterrent work after all, even without ever being formally invoked — or evidence that the doctrine got most of what it wanted anyway, just through a slower and less legible route than conquest. Both readings are defensible. Neither is fully satisfying.

IV. What Actually Got Signed

On September 18, 2026, Trump announced on social media that the United States had reached an agreement with Denmark and Greenland giving the U.S. “permanent control over security, and all other needs” in Greenland — an “Infinite Life” agreement under which Washington would “FOREVER have the complete ability to do what is necessary in Greenland in order to secure and defend the security of Greenland.” He said the U.S. would “immediately begin the process of developing a large Military presence in the appropriate part of Greenland, of which there are many,” and that no U.S. adversary could “EVER have a base in Greenland, have a military presence in Greenland, or make sensitive investments in Greenland, without our express written approval.” (Zolan Kanno-Youngs, Jeffrey Gettleman, and Anton Troianovski reported the announcement for the New York Times on September 18.)

Denmark and Greenland’s own statements, released the same evening, described something narrower than Trump’s version. Frederiksen said the agreement “recognizes the sovereignty and territorial integrity of the Kingdom and the right of the Greenlandic people to self-determination” — language that, read against Trump’s “permanent control over... all other needs,” describes two different agreements more than it describes two framings of the same one. Nielsen, Greenland’s premier, called it an accord that “recognizes Greenland’s interests and our place in international cooperation.” Neither leader used the word “control.” Both used the word “sovereignty.”

The gap between those two versions turns out to matter in specifics, not just tone. Officials from Denmark and Greenland did not confirm that the United States had actually received the veto power over adversary investment that Trump described — a demand American negotiators had pushed hard for in the closed-door talks and that Greenlandic and Danish officials had strongly resisted. Two people close to the negotiations told Kanno-Youngs, Gettleman, and Troianovski that Trump’s “permanent control” language overstated the actual new powers the U.S. would receive; a U.S. military official said the American military would be working alongside its Danish and Greenlandic counterparts under the agreement, not asserting control over them. What the deal does appear to do, without real dispute: bar non-NATO countries from building bases or stationing troops on Greenland, restrict adversary investment in sensitive sectors, and let the U.S. expand its existing military footprint — reopening old Cold War-era sites (American soldiers were spotted the week of September 14 surveying the runway at Narsarsuaq in southern Greenland) and building new installations, on top of the Pituffik Space Base that has been the sole surviving American installation since the Cold War drawdown reduced what was once more than a dozen sites to one.

Two analysts, on the record, made the same point from different angles: that this may be less new than Trump’s language implies. Mikkel Runge Olesen of the Danish Institute for International Studies, quoted by Jeffrey Gettleman and Maya Tekeli in a September 19 New York Times piece on Greenlandic reaction, noted that under the existing 1951 treaty, the U.S. already had sweeping rights to build, operate, and control military installations across Greenland — rights Greenlanders and Danes had never contested — and that since Greenland is NATO territory, “there had never been a realistic possibility that China or Russia would put troops there” in the first place. “Is there anything here that one could not have achieved without this crisis?” he asked. Imran Bayoumi of the Atlantic Council’s GeoStrategy Initiative, quoted in the Kanno-Youngs/Gettleman/Troianovski piece, was blunter: the agreement offers “little in the way of new content,” and Washington “could have achieved many of these outcomes through negotiations with Denmark and Greenland directly, without worsening the relationship.” Both agreed it still hands the U.S. a stronger formal footprint in an increasingly contested Arctic. Neither thought that required ten months of threatening to annex an ally to obtain.

The deal is set to be formally signed by all three governments during the week of September 22, at the United Nations General Assembly, and — this detail matters, given everything else here — it still requires parliamentary approval in both Denmark and Greenland before it takes legal effect. As of September 19, the specifics Trump described on the evening of September 18 hadn’t been made public in full. The story this series set out to document isn’t entirely finished even as this Epilogue goes to press.

V. What Greenlanders Are Actually Saying

If the point of Senator Kelly’s question — “aren’t we supposed to be the good guys here?” — was ever going to get tested against the people it was actually about, this is where that happens, and the record so far is not reassuring. News of the deal reached Nuuk on September 19, and the reaction Gettleman and Tekeli found on the ground for the Times was relief mixed with something closer to disorientation than celebration.

“My immediate reaction is that it makes me nervous and skeptical,” said Ellen Frederiksen, a retired teacher in the southern town of Narsaq. “The way Trump presents it, it sounds as if he is being given a monopoly over Greenland. I sincerely hope that isn’t the case.” Aka Hansen, a filmmaker and opposition politician in Nuuk, had been told earlier in 2026 to prepare for the worst, and stored five days’ worth of water in case American forces actually landed. “Now, suddenly, everything is fine,” she said. “What happened in between?” It is, honestly, the single best question anyone has asked about this entire episode, and this series — for all its research into Pentagon offices and NATO treaty clauses and Trump’s real-estate psychology — cannot fully answer it either. Something changed between January’s invasion planning and September’s signing ceremony. Nobody involved has yet explained what, in public, in terms that add up.

Not everyone was unsettled. Naaja Nathanielsen, one of two Greenlanders sitting in Denmark’s Parliament, called the prospective agreement a good thing outright: “it will create all the conditions needed to restore more normal relations.” But even the more hopeful voices sat alongside residents like Aana Uulat Bach, a retired teacher in Nuuk, who worried less about sovereignty on paper and more about what an expanded American military footprint would mean for daily life on an island whose culture — part Scandinavian, part Indigenous Inuit, built around fishing and seal hunting across some of the most environmentally sensitive terrain on Earth — was never really party to any of the negotiations conducted about it in Washington and Copenhagen. “If it becomes a daily routine for them to fly around here near us, then it will be a complete change,” she said. “We are not soldiers. We are people of nature, and we want to preserve that.”

The Danish ambassador in Washington had been telling guests on September 16, at a reception two days before the announcement, that the outcome would depend on whether one person could be swayed — leaving no doubt who he meant. He was right, in the narrow sense that Trump did, eventually, agree to something short of annexation. What the ambassador’s calm couldn’t fully paper over, and what Stanley-Becker’s Atlantic reporting from the same period captured better than any official statement did, is the cost of getting there: “Washington may have achieved its security objectives,” the piece concluded. “But it has lost something else: trust among its hosts.” One Nuuk resident, asked for a reaction, offered two words: “Goodbye, U.S.A.”

VI. Measuring the Prediction Against the Outcome

So: was Kelly right? Was the Miller Doctrine vindicated or defeated? The honest answer is neither, cleanly.

Kelly’s specific warning — that seizing Greenland by force would trigger a cascade toward Taiwan, Kazakhstan, and eventual great-power conflict — did not happen, because the seizure it depended on did not happen. Denmark’s sovereignty and Greenland’s self-determination, the two things Kelly’s entire warning was built around protecting, both survived, at least in the language Frederiksen and Nielsen chose for their statements, and apparently in more than language: the reporting on what was and wasn’t actually conceded suggests Trump’s “permanent control” framing oversold what Denmark and Greenland actually gave up. Article 5’s credibility, dented by ten months of an ally openly preparing to blow up its own airfields rather than let its alliance leader occupy them, was never actually tested by the thing that would have broken it. NATO did not dissolve. In that narrow but real sense, the worst-case scenario this series was built to document didn’t arrive, and the essays warning about it — however well-argued, however necessary at the time — describe a catastrophe that, this time, the world avoided.

But “avoided” is doing real work in that sentence, and it shouldn’t be allowed to smooth over what actually happened along the way, or what it cost. An ally spent months making contingency plans to destroy its own infrastructure rather than surrender it. Its soldiers carried explosives and its government flew in blood supplies, preparing for an attack from the country that had promised, since 1951, to protect it. Whatever Washington ultimately received on paper — and even that, per Olesen and Bayoumi, may amount to less than advertised, largely restating access rights Denmark had never actually contested — the price was ten months of an ally not knowing whether it needed to defend itself from its own protector, and a population that, per September 19 reporting from Nuuk, still doesn’t fully trust that the story is over. “What happened in between?” is not a question a government asks about an ally behaving normally. It is the question people ask after living through something they were never given an honest account of.

Whether that’s a story about restraint winning out, or about a threat that cost an ally its sense of safety while delivering the threatening party comparatively little it couldn’t already claim, is the question this whole series was actually asking, stated more precisely than “aren’t we supposed to be the good guys here?” ever quite managed to on its own. Senator Kelly’s question doesn’t have a clean yes or no answer in the record this series leaves behind. What it has instead is the one the Atlantic’s reporting closed on, plainer than anything this series could improve on: America may have achieved its security objectives. It lost something else along the way. One resident of Nuuk needed only two words for it. Readers can decide for themselves whether those words settle the account, or just open it.
