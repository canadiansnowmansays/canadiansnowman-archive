Mahatma Gandhi leading the Salt March to Dandi, March 1930. The 240-mile march became the defining image of satyagraha — disciplined, strategic nonviolent confrontation.

The West has a version of Gandhi that fits comfortably on a poster. He is a small man in a loincloth. He is peaceful. He is wise. He defeated the British Empire through love and nonviolence, like a political saint who wandered out of the Sermon on the Mount and into the twentieth century. Martin Luther King Jr. studied him. The United Nations named his birthday the International Day of Non-Violence. He is safe.

The real Gandhi was not safe. He was a radical, a political genius, a deeply flawed human being, and the most consequential figure in the history of anti-colonial resistance. He transformed the Indian independence movement from an elite debating society into a mass movement of hundreds of millions. He drove the British out of India not by persuading them but by making India ungovernable. And the method he used — which he called satyagraha, truth-force — was not passive, not submissive, and not the gentle moral suasion the Western hagiography implies. It was confrontation. Disciplined, strategic, morally grounded confrontation — but confrontation nonetheless. Gandhi understood power at least as well as any general, and he deployed nonviolence as a weapon, not merely a principle.

He was also wrong about several important things, some of which contributed directly to the catastrophe of Partition. This essay attempts to hold both truths simultaneously, which is, as we are learning, the Indian way.

I. South Africa

Gandhi in South Africa, 1895 — the young lawyer in a suit, years before he adopted the loincloth and developed the techniques of satyagraha

Gandhi arrived in South Africa in 1893, a 23-year-old lawyer in a suit, travelling first class. He was thrown off a train at Pietermaritzburg because a white passenger objected to sharing a compartment with a man of colour. The story is famous. What is less often told is what Gandhi did with the experience.

He stayed in South Africa for twenty-one years. He built a political movement among the Indian diaspora there — not the African population, a distinction that matters — and developed the techniques of nonviolent resistance through campaigns against discriminatory laws targeting Indians. The Natal Indian Congress, which he founded in 1894, was explicitly modelled on the Indian National Congress back home.

It was in South Africa that Gandhi shed his suits and began wearing Indian cloth. It was there that he developed the concept of satyagraha — a word he coined from the Sanskrit satya (truth) and agraha (holding firmly). It was there that he first went to jail for civil disobedience. And it was there that he displayed the attitudes toward black Africans that his admirers least want to discuss.

The historical record is clear: the young Gandhi used racial slurs to describe black South Africans. He argued for the separation of Indians from Africans in political and social life. He objected to Indians being classified alongside Africans, which he considered degrading — not because the classification system was unjust, but because Indians, in his view, deserved a higher status within it. He eventually moderated these views, but “eventually” is doing a great deal of work in that sentence.

I raise this not to diminish Gandhi but to insist on the complexity that a serious series demands. The man who would become the Mahatma — the Great Soul — began his political career as a man of his time and his class, carrying the prejudices of upper-caste Hindu society into a colonial context that multiplied them. His growth was real. His early positions were also real. Both things are true. The poster version of Gandhi cannot hold this. The actual man can.

II. Return to India

Gandhi spinning khadi. The spinning wheel was both a daily spiritual practice and a powerful political symbol of self-reliance and resistance to British industrial goods.

Gandhi returned to India in 1915 at the age of 45, already famous, and spent a year travelling the country by train — third class, deliberately — before entering politics. What he saw transformed his approach. The India he returned to was not the India of the English-speaking lawyers who ran the Congress. It was a country of 250 million villagers who spoke dozens of languages, lived under the caste system, worked land they did not own, and had no connection to the constitutional debates happening in colonial meeting halls.

Gandhi understood, with the political instinct of a genius, that independence would never be won by petitioning the British. It would be won by mobilizing the Indian masses — and that required a leader who looked like them, spoke like them, and lived like them. The suits disappeared permanently. The spinning wheel became his symbol and his daily practice — not merely an emblem of self-sufficiency but a deliberate statement that India’s freedom would be built from the village up, not from the courthouse down.

His first major Indian campaigns were local: the Champaran satyagraha of 1917, supporting indigo farmers exploited by British planters; the Kheda satyagraha of 1918, supporting peasants facing tax demands during a famine. Both were successful. Both demonstrated that nonviolent resistance could work against specific, concrete injustices. Both established Gandhi’s method: identify the grievance, frame it in moral terms, organize the affected population, confront the authority, accept the punishment, and force the oppressor to reveal its own violence.

The method was not spontaneous. It was engineered. Gandhi was a strategist of extraordinary sophistication who chose his battles carefully, timed his campaigns for maximum political impact, and managed his public image with a skill that modern political consultants would envy. The loincloth was a costume. The spinning wheel was a brand. The fasts were leverage. None of this makes them insincere — Gandhi genuinely believed in simplicity, self-sufficiency, and the moral power of suffering — but it does make them strategic.

III. Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience

The massacre at Jallianwala Bagh on April 13, 1919, was the event that ended any possibility of gradual, negotiated reform within the colonial framework. At Amritsar, Brigadier General Reginald Dyer ordered his troops to open fire on an unarmed crowd gathered in an enclosed garden. The firing continued for approximately ten minutes. Official British figures counted 379 dead and over 1,200 wounded. Indian estimates were far higher. Dyer later testified that he would have used artillery if he could have gotten it through the narrow entrance.

The Jallianwala Bagh massacre did not create the independence movement. But it destroyed the illusion that the movement could succeed through cooperation with the Raj. Gandhi, who had previously supported Indian participation in the British war effort during World War I, pivoted to total non-cooperation.

The Non-Cooperation Movement of 1920–1922 was the first all-India mass political movement in history. Gandhi called on Indians to boycott British goods, British courts, British schools, and British government positions. Thousands returned imperial honours. Students left colonial colleges. Lawyers abandoned their practices. The movement spread across the subcontinent with a speed that alarmed the British and astonished the Congress leadership, many of whom had not anticipated that Gandhi could actually mobilize the masses he claimed to speak for.

Gandhi suspended the movement in February 1922 after a mob in Chauri Chaura set fire to a police station and killed 22 officers. The decision was agonizing and controversial — many within the Congress believed the movement was on the verge of success and that Gandhi had sacrificed a historic opportunity to maintain a principle. Gandhi was unapologetic. Nonviolence was not a tactic for him. It was the point. A movement that achieved independence through violence would produce a violent nation. He was willing to delay freedom to protect the means by which it was achieved.

Whether he was right is one of the great counterfactual questions of the twentieth century. What is certain is that the decision was consistent. Gandhi suspended or withdrew from campaigns repeatedly when violence erupted. He controlled the movement’s tempo like a conductor, and when the orchestra went off-key, he stopped the music.

IV. The Salt March

The campaign that crystallized Gandhi’s genius — and that demonstrated the method at its purest — was the Salt March of 1930.

The British had maintained a monopoly on salt production and taxation since the Raj’s early decades. Salt was essential to life in a tropical country. Every Indian used it. Every Indian paid the tax. Gandhi chose salt because it was universal, because the injustice was tangible and personal, and because defending a salt monopoly would make the British look ridiculous.

On March 12, 1930, Gandhi left his ashram at Sabarmati with 78 followers and walked 240 miles to the coastal village of Dandi, where he intended to make salt from seawater in defiance of British law. The march took 24 days. By the time he reached the coast, thousands had joined. The world’s press was watching. On April 6, Gandhi picked up a lump of natural salt from the mud and declared the British salt laws broken.

Gandhi and followers during the Salt March, 1930. By the time he reached Dandi, the march had become a global spectacle that exposed the moral bankruptcy of British rule.

The British response was mass arrest. Over 60,000 people were jailed. At the Dharasana Salt Works, a column of satyagrahis marched toward the gates and were beaten by police with steel-tipped lathis. They did not resist. They did not run. Row after row walked forward and were struck down. The American journalist Webb Miller’s account of the scene — the marchers advancing, the clubs falling, the bodies crumpling — was published worldwide and turned international opinion decisively against the Raj.

This was the method. Force the oppressor to choose: concede, or reveal itself. The British chose to reveal themselves. And the world saw.

V. The Failures

Gandhi’s limitations were as consequential as his achievements, and three of them bear directly on the story this series is telling.

The first was caste. Gandhi opposed untouchability — he renamed the Untouchables Harijans, “children of God,” and campaigned for their inclusion in Hindu temples and public life. But he did not oppose the caste system itself. He regarded varna — the four-fold division of society into Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, and Shudras — as a legitimate, even divinely ordained, social order. He simply wanted it purged of its cruelties. For Ambedkar, who experienced caste not as an abstraction but as a daily humiliation, this was insufficient to the point of insult. The Poona Pact of 1932 — a compromise between Gandhi and Ambedkar over separate electorates for Untouchables — was achieved only after Gandhi undertook a fast unto death that Ambedkar interpreted as moral blackmail: agree to my terms or be responsible for the Mahatma’s death.

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar and Mahatma Gandhi. Two towering figures with sharply different visions for India’s social order and the future of Dalits

Ambedkar never forgave this. He regarded Gandhi’s approach to caste as cosmetic — a reform of manners, not of structures. The tension between them is one of the defining fault lines of modern India, and it has not been resolved. Gandhi wanted to reform Hinduism from within. Ambedkar concluded that Hinduism could not be reformed and converted to Buddhism. Both men were right about different things. Neither was right about everything.

The second failure was Partition. Gandhi opposed the division of India with every fibre of his being. He offered Jinnah — the leader of the Muslim League and the architect of Pakistan — the prime ministership of a united India. He proposed that Congress accept Muslim League governance over the entire country rather than accept division. These offers were rejected, and when Partition came, Gandhi called it a “spiritual tragedy.” But his inability to prevent it reflected a deeper limitation: Gandhi’s vision of India was, at its core, Hindu. His language, his symbols, his references, his daily practices — the spinning wheel, the ashram, the fasting, the prayer meetings — were drawn from the Hindu tradition. His inclusiveness was genuine, but it operated within a framework that many Muslims experienced as assimilationist rather than pluralist. Come into our home, the framework said. But it is our home.

Muhammad Ali Jinnah’s demand for Pakistan was many things — cynical, strategic, born of legitimate fear — but at its root it was a response to a question Gandhi could never adequately answer: In an independent India governed by Hindu majority rule, what guarantee do Muslims have? Gandhi’s answer was moral: trust, goodwill, the shared bonds of Indian civilization. Jinnah’s answer was political: none. And so the subcontinent was divided.

The third failure was assassination, which is a strange word to apply to a failure of the victim rather than the perpetrator. But Gandhi’s death on January 30, 1948 — shot by Nathuram Godse, a Hindu nationalist who believed Gandhi had been too accommodating to Muslims — revealed the force that Gandhi had spent his life trying to contain and that India has never fully defeated. Hindu nationalism killed the Mahatma. The ideology of Godse is, in its essentials, the ideology of the RSS. The RSS is the parent organization of the BJP. The BJP governs India today.

I state this not as polemic but as genealogy. The line is direct.

VI. What Remains

Gandhi in his later years. The man who gave India a moral language of resistance could not prevent Partition or the rise of the forces that would eventually kill him

Gandhi is everywhere in India and nowhere. His face is on the currency. His birthday is a national holiday. His statue stands in every city. Politicians of every party invoke his name. And the country he built has moved, with increasing velocity, away from nearly everything he stood for.

Gandhi wanted village self-sufficiency. India chose industrialization. Gandhi wanted simplicity. India’s billionaire class is among the most extravagant on earth. Gandhi wanted Hindu-Muslim unity. India has a governing party that defines Indian identity in explicitly Hindu terms. Gandhi wanted nonviolence. India is a nuclear power.

The gap between Gandhi’s India and the India that exists is not hypocrisy. It is history. Nations are not built by saints. They are built by politicians, bureaucrats, soldiers, and businessmen who take what the saints gave them and do what they can with it. What Gandhi gave India was a founding mythology of moral courage, a technique of resistance that has been adopted on every continent, and a demonstration — visible, repeated, undeniable — that the powerless can defeat the powerful if they are willing to suffer for it.

What he could not give India was a way to hold together after the British left. That task fell to other men — to Nehru, to Ambedkar, to Patel — and it required something Gandhi distrusted: a state. A constitution. Institutions. Laws enforced by courts backed by police. The machinery of governance that Gandhi regarded as necessary evils at best and spiritual corruption at worst.

The story of Indian democracy is, in some sense, the story of what happened after Gandhi — what happened when the Mahatma’s moral vision collided with the practical necessities of governing 350 million people across a subcontinent that had just been torn in half.

That collision happened at midnight on August 14, 1947. And it is the subject of our next essay.
