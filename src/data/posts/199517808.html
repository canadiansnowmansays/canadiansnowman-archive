There are five languages spoken in my Toronto townhouse on any given morning.

My husband Ugyen, who is from Bhutan — a Buddhist kingdom wedged between India and Tibet that most Canadians couldn’t find on a map — speaks Dzongkha, the national language of his homeland. He also speaks Hindi and English. Rikesh, who has been my roommate for ten years — a decade, which tells you something about either his patience or mine — is from the northwest of India and speaks Gujarati. Gurneet is Sikh and speaks Punjabi. Both men speak Hindi. Both speak English. All of us manage to negotiate the coffee maker without an interpreter, though the spice shelf requires occasional diplomacy.

Five languages. One kitchen. Three countries of origin. Four men whose lives intersect daily in a narrow townhouse in North York, each carrying a civilizational inheritance that the others understand only partially and that I, the Canadian in the room, am still learning to read.

I mention this not as a quirky domestic detail but as an empirical observation that has been accumulating in my kitchen for years. Many Indian men have passed through this house before Rikesh and Gurneet, from different states, different language groups, different religions, different castes, different dietary codes. The lesson has been consistent and unavoidable: India is not one thing.

Most people outside the subcontinent don’t know this. They carry a mental image of India that is roughly: the Taj Mahal, Bollywood, call centres, cricket, curry, and — depending on their politics — either the world’s largest democracy or Modi. This is the equivalent of thinking you understand Europe because you’ve seen the Eiffel Tower and eaten a pizza. India has 28 states and 8 union territories. It has 22 officially scheduled languages and something approaching 19,500 documented dialects. The distance between Kerala and Kashmir — cultural, economic, linguistic, religious — is greater than the distance between Portugal and Finland. A Tamil Brahmin and a Bihari Muslim are both Indian citizens, both inheritors of the same constitution, and both occupants of worlds so different they might as well be on separate continents.

And yet India holds together. Noisily, imperfectly, sometimes violently — but it holds together.

That is the central puzzle of this series, and I am not sure the world has fully reckoned with either its scale or its implications.

The question this series asks is simple: How?

How does a country with more internal diversity than all of Europe combined sustain the largest democratic experiment in human history? How did a civilization that existed for five thousand years before anyone tried to make it a nation-state become one — and remain one — through Partition, through assassination, through religious violence, through an actual declared state of emergency in which democracy was suspended by a democrat’s daughter? How does it hold together now, under a Hindu nationalist government that is quietly rewriting the terms of the secular compact on which the modern Indian state was built?

And what does its answer — or its failure to answer — tell the rest of us about what democracy can and cannot survive?

I should say plainly what I am and what I am not.

I am a 74-year-old retired Canadian businessman and investigative journalist who lives in Toronto and publishes on Substack. I am not an India scholar. I do not speak Hindi, or Gujarati, or Punjabi, or Tamil, or any of the other languages that make India the most linguistically complex democracy on earth. My formal credentials for writing about the subcontinent consist of sharing a kitchen with Indian men for the better part of two decades, reading voraciously, and possessing the peculiar Canadian habit of watching other countries’ politics with more attention than we pay to our own.

But I have learned — from the men who have lived in my house, from the conversations that happen when the workday is done and the chai is poured — things that no textbook delivers with the same force. I have learned that a man from Gujarat and a man from Punjab can disagree about nearly everything and still share a deep, unshakeable identity as Indian. I have learned that caste is not a relic but a living architecture. I have learned that the word “secular” means something different in India than it does in Canada or France — not the absence of religion from public life, but the equal embrace of all religions by the state, which is a far more ambitious and fragile project.

I have also learned that when I told Rikesh and Gurneet I was writing a series about Afghanistan, they didn’t just express polite interest. They leaned in. Afghanistan is in their neighbourhood. The Partition that created Pakistan — the country that sits between India and Afghanistan — is not history to them. It is family memory. The geopolitics of Central and South Asia is not an abstraction in my kitchen. It is personal.

Rikesh, for his part, is the man who first introduced me to artificial intelligence. He works in IT, and it was he who pointed me toward ChatGPT and opened a door I have not since closed. He has been nothing but encouraging, though I suspect he occasionally wonders whether the volume of essays emerging from my office constitutes a reasonable use of the technology. His mother Asmita, a retired school principal visiting from India, is less convinced. She questioned the use of AI in writing with the polite but unmistakable skepticism of a woman who spent a career teaching people to think for themselves.

This series is written from that vantage point — not above, not inside, but alongside. The Canadian outsider who has been given a window, and who is trying to describe what he sees through it with honesty and care.

A young man on a London sidewalk

In June 1977, I was twenty-five years old and sleeping on the pavement near St. Paul’s Cathedral in London, England. I had staked out my spot overnight to secure a front-row view of Queen Elizabeth’s Silver Jubilee procession — the twenty-fifth anniversary of her accession, celebrated with a thanksgiving service and a parade of Commonwealth leaders through the streets of a city that had once governed a quarter of the earth.

The Gold State Coach, a historic vehicle built in 1762, as I witnessed in 1977

I watched the limousines pass. I watched Pierre Trudeau — the only head of state with his window rolled down. We yelled. He acknowledged the yell and the mad waving, because that was Pierre. I watched India’s Prime Minister pass — Morarji Desai, the man who had defeated Indira Gandhi in elections held just months earlier, ending the Emergency that had suspended Indian democracy for nearly two years. I didn’t know any of that at the time. I was a kid from London, Ontario, standing on a sidewalk in London, England, watching important people drive by in expensive cars.

I know it now. Desai had been in a prison cell two years before that procession, locked up in solitary confinement by the woman whose office he now held. He had campaigned on a platform of restoring human rights and freedom. He had won the most crushing defeat in the history of the Indian National Congress. And there he was, riding through London in the back of a limousine, representing the world’s largest democracy at the court of its former colonizer — living proof that the Indian democratic experiment could break and then repair itself.

I didn’t understand what I was watching in 1977. I do now. And that understanding — assembled over decades, sharpened by the men who have lived in my house and by the history I should have learned much earlier — is what this series attempts to share.

What this series will cover

We will begin where India begins — not with the British, but with a civilization that was ancient when Rome was young. The Indus Valley. The Maurya Empire. Ashoka — the emperor who chose nonviolence two millennia before Gandhi. The Mughal period, when Hindu-Muslim coexistence was real and complicated and nothing like the simplistic narrative that either Hindu nationalists or Western orientalists would later impose upon it.

We will then turn to the British — first the East India Company, which may be the most consequential corporation in human history, and then the Raj itself, with its railways, its famines, its racial architecture, and its ultimately fatal presumption that a quarter of humanity could be governed from Whitehall.

We will spend time with Gandhi, whose legacy is both more radical and more problematic than the Western hagiography allows. We will spend more time with Partition — August 1947, the greatest mass migration in human history, fifteen million people moving, one to two million dying, Nehru’s “tryst with destiny” delivered while trains full of corpses crossed the new border. This is the birth wound, and it has never healed.

We will examine Nehru’s gamble — the decision to build a democracy when every post-colonial precedent pointed toward authoritarianism — and Ambedkar’s constitution, written by an Untouchable, the most radical democratic document of the twentieth century. We will examine Indira Gandhi’s Emergency, when democracy was suspended, and the miracle of the 1977 election, when it self-corrected — the election that put Morarji Desai in the limousine I watched pass on a London street without understanding what I was seeing.

Prime Minister Shri Morarji Desai

We will examine modern India’s extraordinary internal diversity — language, caste, religion, region — and the economic liberalization of 1991 that created the India my roommates come from: ambitious, globally mobile, technologically fluent, and still rooted in family, caste, and community in ways the West doesn’t see. We will examine the rise of Hindu nationalism, the RSS, the demolition of the Babri Masjid, the Gujarat riots, and Modi — a figure I will analyze using the same TACO framework (Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate) that has structured my analyses of Trump, Xi, and the other strongmen of our era.

We will examine India’s geopolitical position — the world’s swing state, courted by Washington and Moscow, managing a live border with China, indispensable in every global forum, and maddeningly noncommittal about which side it is on. Because India has never been on anyone’s side but its own, and that is not a bug. It is a feature. It is, in fact, the central lesson of Indian foreign policy since Nehru, and one the West has never fully absorbed.

And we will end where we began. In the kitchen. Where the real education happens.
