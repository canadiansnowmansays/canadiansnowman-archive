On Sunday, November 1, two days before the American midterm elections, the President told a rally in Iowa why the war with Iran was not over. “The only reason we’re not finished with Iran is I want to be nice — I want to try and save all those lives if I can,” he said. “But at some point we’re going to have to give them the hammer.”

The next morning, Iran’s foreign ministry said it was ready to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within two days if Washington met its terms, as Erika Solomon and Sanam Mahoozi reported for the New York Times. Brent crude fell 4 per cent on the news, to about $109 a barrel.

Both statements were made to the same audience, and that audience voted on Tuesday.

That same Monday, the United States bombed Iran. American aircraft struck Qeshm Island, in the Strait of Hormuz, in what officials described to Helene Cooper, Tyler Pager and Jonathan Swan of the New York Times as a small-scale defensive measure. That evening the President told Sean Hannity on Fox News: “This isn’t like a war that’s going on. There’s no war raging. It could happen if they don’t make a deal, but they want to make a deal very badly, and there’s a very good chance we make a deal.”

On Tuesday the voters answered. The Republicans lost the House and the Senate, and by Wednesday the Vice-President was saying the war was why. By Sunday he had still not decided whether to give Iran the hammer. His advisers, the Times reported, still cannot agree on what the war is meant to achieve.

The Peace Calendar

Readers of this diary have watched the war acquire an election calendar since Part XCIX. In the final fortnight before the vote, the peace process acquired one too.

On Sunday, October 25, nine days before polling day, Qatari mediators delivered a letter from Vice-President Vance to Mohsen Rezaei, Iran’s security chief, proposing direct talks. On Friday, October 30, a Qatari delegation arrived in Tehran. On Saturday, October 31, Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi called the letter “a positive step,” and the same day Rezaei called its changed tone “a good sign.” On Monday, November 2, Tehran offered its two-day plan, the President posted that an agreement “could happen,” and Vice-President Vance said the two sides had made “progress” and that he and the President felt “pretty good” about a deal. The mediators pushed for a face-to-face meeting “later this week.”

Over the same fortnight, the strike plans kept the same date. The options Pete Hegseth, General Dan Caine and Admiral Brad Cooper presented to the President, as this diary reported last week, were for the week after the election, and his own advisers described them as ways “to create the conditions for Trump to declare victory.” The Atlantic’s reporters judged that a heavy defeat on Tuesday would not reduce the chance of new strikes, and pointed to 2006, when President Bush lost both houses over Iraq, fired his defence secretary and then sent more troops.

Peace was being discussed on a schedule that ran out on Tuesday. The war was planned for the week that began on Wednesday. This diary’s view is that the talk of peace was aimed at voters as much as at Tehran. Seen that way, the President’s line in Iowa was a promise that the pause would end, not a change of policy.

The Quiet Strikes

The pause was not quite a pause. The Qeshm strike, first reported by the Wall Street Journal, was one of several small American strikes inside Iran over the past few weeks, and neither government acknowledged any of them publicly. One official told the Times that earlier strikes had hit empty cruise-missile launchers and that their purpose was to stop Iran attacking ships in the strait. Another said Monday’s strike followed the Revolutionary Guards shooting down an American drone and firing on, and missing, two American fighter jets over the strait. They are the first American bombs on Iranian soil since two days of strikes at the beginning of September, when the United States also destroyed five Iranian crude carriers after Iran fired missiles at two American warships in the Arabian Sea, and missed.

Officials called Monday’s action limited and said there were no immediate plans to escalate. This diary takes them at their word, and notes that a drone shot down and two jets fired on are real provocations that any commander would answer. It also notes the timing. The President had said he would not attack before the midterms. On the eve of the vote, American aircraft struck Iran while he told a national television audience there was no war. Officials in his own administration, the Times reported, say they no longer know what his next step will be.

The talk of peace was public, and the strikes were kept quiet. That is a sharper version of this diary’s thesis than the one it started the week with.

The Offer

Tehran’s offer was also aimed at an audience. “If Iran’s legitimate demands are accepted and implemented, it would be possible to put the entire plan into operation within two days, restore security to the Strait of Hormuz, and initiate the negotiation process,” said Esmail Baghaei, the foreign ministry spokesman. “The realization of this depends entirely on Washington’s will and decision.”

The plan is the phased sequence Iran first proposed in September, in which each side makes concessions in turn. Qatar has compressed it, first to five days and now to two. The demands have not been compressed. Washington would have to lift sanctions, release billions of dollars in frozen assets and, according to Araghchi, recognise a deal between Iran and Oman to administer passage through the strait in future. The toll-booth thesis this diary has followed since the summer, that the strait is taxed rather than closed, now has a formal version. Rezaei repeated that nuclear negotiations could begin only after Washington met those terms.

Iran’s leaders are also now discussing in public a withdrawal from the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty. Baghaei reminded reporters that the United States and Israel attacked Iran in June 2025, shortly after the two sides had held talks. Tehran has been negotiated with and then bombed before, and it is saying so.

Fairness requires this diary to give the other side of its own argument. The Vance letter was not a stunt. The President approved it, it put the nuclear programme on the table, and it was written by the one senior official known to have opposed the war. Tehran’s terms, for their part, were drawn up knowing Washington was unlikely to accept them. The plainest reading is that both capitals made offers designed to be refused, and wanted the refusal on the record before the vote. Anwar Gargash, the diplomatic adviser to the president of the United Arab Emirates, put it more politely at the Manama Dialogue: “The rules of logic tell us that the Iranian stand and U.S. stand are far apart. If these positions are not bridged, then we have to worry about the worst.”

The Vote

The midterms, as David E. Sanger wrote for the New York Times, “laid bare the deep unpopularity of the war of choice that President Trump started and is now struggling to end.” The Republicans lost both chambers of Congress.

The administration’s rifts, barely hidden since February, broke into the open as soon as the results came in. “The reason we lost is the war,” Vice-President Vance wrote on social media, adding that the President had reached the same conclusion. “If you’d like to argue it was worth it, that losing the Senate for the next 6 years was worth it, then do so.” A few hours later, giving reporters a tour of his unfinished White House ballroom, the President did so. “I can tell you as far as that war is concerned, I would have done nothing different.” By Thursday he was calling Vance’s verdict “largely true” while still defending the decision to attack.

Last week this diary followed The Atlantic’s report that the President was looking for someone to blame, and expected his Defense Secretary or his chairman of the Joint Chiefs to be the one. The first public verdict came instead from his Vice-President, and it named the war itself. Pete Hegseth now faces a Congress controlled by Democrats who have said they intend to investigate him and possibly impeach him. Hegseth answered the Vice-President with a defiant video urging the administration to “fight, fight, fight.” The war’s two most senior civilian voices are now arguing with each other in public.

The 2006 precedent this diary has been tracking now applies in full. Sanger recalls it too: President Bush lost both houses over Iraq, replaced Donald Rumsfeld, changed strategy and sent more troops. “Past presidents, suddenly plunged into lame-duck status, have often reached for their military options,” Sanger writes, “partly to demonstrate that their role as commander in chief is undiminished.” Bush, though, had a new strategy. “If the president has a new strategy for prosecuting the war, he and his aides have been silent about it.”

The Decision

The forces are in place. More than 50,000 troops in the region, in Europe and at home are assigned to the Iran mission, with more than a dozen warships, two aircraft carriers in the region and a third about two weeks away, and long-range B-1 and B-2 bombers on call in the United States. American and Israeli aircraft have been rehearsing strikes over Iraq. Personnel have been withdrawn from bases in the region, the forces that remain have practised for mass casualties, and the United States has sent Israel another Patriot missile-defence battery. The President’s favourite target is Pickaxe Mountain, the deeply buried site Iranians call Kooh Kolang, believed to hold advanced centrifuges but, as far as anyone can tell, little or no nuclear fuel. He has talked of dropping the 22,000-pound MOAB on it. Weapons specialists point out that the MOAB explodes above ground and that Pickaxe is built into granite. The nuclear material itself still lies untouched under the rubble of the June 2025 strikes.

David E. Sanger and Eric Schmitt of the New York Times set out the four choices in front of him. The first is a short, intense campaign of several days against Iran’s energy, nuclear, missile and drone sites. Officials do not expect it to bring down the government, and Iran would hit back at the Gulf, its data centres and its undersea cables. “The US has the forces to start big, but munitions are likely to keep it short,” said Kelly Grieco of the Stimson Center. “And if the US starts another round, it doesn’t get to decide when it ends. Iran gets a vote.” The second is a pared-back strike on military and nuclear sites only, sparing energy targets to limit Iranian retaliation. It would let the President declare victory and begin leaving, but officials concede it would achieve none of the goals he set in February. The third is to let Vance negotiate. The fourth is to muddle along: keep the blockade, escort the 11 to 18 million barrels of other countries’ oil that get through each day, perhaps settle for a narrow deal on the strait, and leave the rest to the Gulf states and to the next president. The reporters add one detail that explains the week. Several times in recent months the President has approved a return to major combat, and each time he has pulled back hours before it was to begin.

The diplomacy has moved too. Five officials briefed on the talks, including an American and two Iranians, told Adam Rasgon, Vivian Nereim, Tyler Pager and Farnaz Fassihi of the New York Times that Iran is now reviewing “concrete proposals” from the mediators on the future of its nuclear programme, after weeks of refusing to discuss it. Qatar has held two rounds of talks in Tehran and a third in Doha, and has also met Iranian officials in Armenia. In public, Mohsen Rezaei still says there will be no nuclear talks until Washington meets Iran’s terms. In private, the officials say, he has been more open. The main sticking point remains Iran’s stockpile of highly enriched uranium, about 440 kilograms by the International Atomic Energy Agency’s last estimate, enough for a small number of bombs. The President calls it “nuclear dust” and wants it given up. Tehran will not ship it abroad but has discussed diluting it at home under international inspection. Vance has become the leading supporter of the Qatari effort, with Pakistan, Turkey and Saudi Arabia also carrying messages. By the weekend, though, some American negotiators had turned more pessimistic, fearing that any deal could be undercut by hard-liners in Tehran. The core dispute is sequencing. Washington wants the uranium dealt with first, before frozen funds are released or sanctions lifted. Tehran wants to keep the uranium until the end, as insurance. Many experts think the President would be lucky to get limits like those of the 2015 deal, under which Iran shipped 97 per cent of its stockpile abroad. He tore that deal up in 2018.

Analysts read the vote as having settled the question, if not the answer. “The elections decided the fate of the war,” said Danny Citrinowicz, a former Israeli intelligence officer who spent two decades on Iran. “The only question is how it will end: with a bang, a peaceful, negotiated but likely unsatisfactory solution, or Trump saying, ‘JD Vance was right, and goodbye.’” Daniel Shapiro, the Pentagon’s senior Middle East official under President Biden, said renewed strikes made “little sense” when even Republicans blamed the war for their defeat, “but Trump is erratic, unstable and hates to be seen as a loser.” Iran, he added, has little reason to give the President a deal he can call a victory, so “his choices are to escalate and then claim victory — with the risk that he cannot control the escalation — or essentially walk away.” Ryan Crocker, six times an American ambassador in the region, hoped the result would “reinforce the president’s gut instincts toward caution,” and warned that Iran’s leaders have a high tolerance for pain: “We will be playing into their strong suit if we restart kinetic activity.”

Fairness requires this diary to note one argument for striking soon. If the President believes more bombing will make Iran concede, waiting does not help him. The money is running out, the new Congress will not add more, and in less than two weeks he travels to the Philippines and China for summit meetings where he will want to look undiminished. The argument against is the one Sanger makes: 38 days of continuous attacks in the spring did not bring down the government, recover the nuclear fuel, destroy the missiles or produce the “unconditional surrender” the President once promised. It is not clear why a shorter campaign would.

Israel’s half of the equation also changed this week. On Saturday, President Isaac Herzog gave Gadi Eisenkot, not Benjamin Netanyahu, the first chance to form a government, as Ephrat Livni and Aaron Boxerman reported for the New York Times. Lawmakers holding 67 of Parliament’s 120 seats recommended Eisenkot, against 46 for Netanyahu, after one of his own ultra-Orthodox allies declined to back him. Eisenkot has up to six weeks, and the path is narrow. Under Israeli law, though, Netanyahu remains caretaker prime minister until a new government is sworn in, which means through the whole of the American strike window. He is now a caretaker whom a majority of Parliament has refused to recommend. He called a government relying on Arab votes “national suicide,” and analysts say new elections would suit him. Last week this diary reported that he had pressed for a strike on Iran before the vote and been refused by his own chief of staff. His incentive to want a crisis has not shrunk.

On Sunday the President told CNN it was “highly unlikely” the United States would need to strike again if the negotiations succeeded. “They’re withering away financially,” he said. “We’ve hit them militarily. We could finish them off.” It was the same message as the Iowa rally a week earlier, peace on offer with the hammer held behind it, only now it came from a president who had lost both houses of Congress. William Wechsler of the Atlantic Council described the risk in that position: “The concern is that Trump will conclude that he is facing his worst nightmare for the next two years: going down in history as a loser. The only way out of it, he may therefore conclude, is to win the war.”

As this diary went out on Sunday night, no order had been given. Before the vote, the talk of peace was for the voters. After it, the question is whether the peace talks are real, or whether they are what the President offers while he works out how to avoid looking like he lost.

The Gulf Bill

For the people of the Gulf, the American election has become a countdown. “They know that before the midterms are over, they can reasonably expect their lives to continue as normal,” Vivian Nereim wrote for the New York Times after a road trip from Riyadh to Manama and Doha. “After then, nobody knows what will happen.” Residents are stockpiling water and cash, packing go-bags, and cutting trips short so they are not stranded abroad if the airspace closes. Gulf governments are still repairing energy plants and radar damaged in the spring, and a global shortage of interceptor missiles limits how far they can defend against another round. American and Israeli planners have drawn up an expanded target list that includes Iranian energy infrastructure and other sites vital to its economy. Iran has promised to answer by hitting the region’s critical infrastructure and its undersea cables.

“The scary part this time is that the war is going to be worse than before,” said Khalid Al-Jaber of the Middle East Council on Global Affairs in Qatar. “It’s going to be bigger. It’s going to be much harder for everyone.” Egypt’s foreign minister, Badr Abdelatty, told the Manama Dialogue that “the second round will be more dangerous, more destructive, and it will lead to nowhere.” Donald Blome, the American assistant secretary of state for the region, told the same audience that diplomatic channels with Tehran were still open, while the United States was “prepared to go in another direction.” When the conference ended on Sunday, many of those attending left the region as fast as they could.

The war is now in its ninth month, and the Gulf states that sought to stay out of it are paying for it, as Humza Jilani reported for the New York Times. The International Monetary Fund expects the economies of the Gulf Cooperation Council to shrink by 5 per cent this year. Kuwait, Bahrain and Qatar, which depend almost entirely on the strait to export their energy, face double-digit contractions. Saudi Arabia’s economy shrank 3.8 per cent in the third quarter, with oil activity down 20.4 per cent.

The damage goes beyond oil. Riyadh Air, the kingdom’s new luxury carrier, is busing international passengers to and from an alternative airport four and a half hours from its damaged hub. The Saudi investment conference has been postponed to next year. Darren Woods, chief executive of Exxon Mobil, skipped this week’s ADIPEC energy conference in Abu Dhabi over security concerns. The region’s data centres, struck by Iranian drone swarms early in the war and named in Iran’s threats last month, may now be dispersed or built underground.

The only Gulf economy expected to grow this year is Oman’s, forecast at more than 4 per cent, because its long Arabian Sea coastline lets it ship around the strait. Oman is also the partner Tehran wants Washington to accept in running the strait. The Emirates, with almost $3 trillion in sovereign assets, have promised to cut their dependence on Hormuz to “zero.” The IMF forecasts a strong recovery in 2027, 7.9 per cent in Saudi Arabia and 18.8 per cent in Qatar, on the assumption that the fighting tapers off.

Yemen

The war that none of the President’s options addresses had one of its deadliest days this week. More than 50 people were killed in Yemen in 24 hours, as Shuaib Almosawa and Ismaeel Naar reported for the New York Times. On Saturday, airstrikes on the village of al-Shariya, east of Sanaa, killed at least 27 people and wounded 30, most of them women and children, according to the Houthi-run health ministry, which blamed Saudi warplanes. Riyadh did not respond. A day earlier, strikes on a crowded market in Taiz province killed at least 30, according to a Houthi-run news agency. The government in Taiz blamed the Houthis, and the Houthis blamed Saudi Arabia. Casualties are hard to verify: the Houthis restrict access to strike sites, and Riyadh says little about its campaign. This diary gives the figures as each side reports them.

The World Health Organization counted almost 1,800 dead by the end of October and more than 300,000 displaced, and hospitals are running short of supplies. The United Nations warns the fighting is returning to the levels of a decade ago. The Saudi-led bombing has not dislodged the Houthis from the north or from the Red Sea coast they seized this year, and the Houthis keep firing at the kingdom: on Saturday the coalition said it had shot down a cruise missile aimed at the Riyadh region. Their military spokesman promised revenge “against the killer of children and women.”

The Cost Ledger

Brent crude fell to about $109 on November 2 on talk of a deal, down from more than $112 on October 22. Oil fell sharply after the midterm results on hopes the President would end the war. More than 35 ships were attacked around the strait in October, more than in any other month of the war. The Pentagon has now told Congress the war has cost $47 billion, up from $43.6 billion in September, not counting damage to American bases, several of which Pentagon officials concede will probably never be rebuilt. Congress has never passed extra money for the war, and the new Democratic Congress is unlikely to start. Any new campaign would further deplete munitions stocks already called “dangerously low.” The Gulf’s economies have shrunk by about 5 per cent, and consumers everywhere have paid hundreds of billions of dollars more for fuel. The voters sent their own bill on Tuesday.
