This ledger has, over its run, traced the ways the artificial-intelligence build-out reaches the household budget of people who never asked for it. Part IX followed it through property-tax fights. Part X followed it through Treasury yields and, from there, mortgage rates. Part XII followed it through the young people organising against the whole thing, and Part XV followed it through San Francisco rents. Each was a transmission channel: a route by which capital poured into data centres arrives, eventually, as a number on somebody’s monthly statement.

Here is a fifth, and it may be the one that hits the widest. On September 24, 2026, Reed Abelson and Teddy Rosenbluth reported in The New York Times that hospitals and health insurers have turned artificial intelligence on each other, and that the crossfire is pushing medical costs up. This is a companion to the Code Blue series’ long argument about American health-care dysfunction, but its place is here, in the ledger, because the mechanism is pure Capex: the same technology whose financing this series has been auditing is now being deployed on both sides of a billing war whose costs land on patients and employers.

The billion-dollar coding war

Start with the headline number, because it frames everything. A group of insurers released a report on September 24 claiming that hospitals’ use of A.I. was responsible for nearly one billion dollars in additional expenses over two years.

The mechanism is worth understanding, because it is not fraud in the ordinary sense. Using A.I. tools, hospitals gather more information about each patient and submit bigger, more detailed claims. According to the analysis by the Blue Cross Blue Shield Association, hospitals in 2024 and 2025 described their patients’ illnesses as more complex than in 2023 — adding secondary diagnoses like anaemia or low sodium — despite no evidence that the patients received any different treatment. By describing patients as sicker, the hospitals were paid an average of nearly 12,000 dollars more per case. Luke Chalker of the association put it simply: coding is changing because of this technology.

The fuller figures came out in the days that followed. Medical Daily reported on September 25, 2026 that the association put the added cost to Blue Cross plans at 942 million dollars across 2024 and 2025, from more than 55,000 claims moved into higher-paying complexity categories, and that complex inpatient cases rose from 37 per cent to 40 per cent of claims between early 2023 and late 2025. Chalker, the association’s senior vice president of product and data science, framed the finding: “The disconnect between diagnoses and treatment suggests that AI is identifying more billable conditions, not sicker patients.” Dr. Razia Hashmi, its vice president of clinical affairs, allowed for some honest catch-up: “There may be an element of correct coding there, but the likelihood that this is technology-enabled upcoding is higher, in my view.” The American Hospital Association answered that inpatients really are older and sicker, pointing to a rise of about 5 per cent in the standard measure of case complexity between 2019 and 2024, and said the tools help hospitals document care accurately and answer denials.

The hospitals’ machines, in other words, have learned to read a chart and find every billable condition lurking in it. Dave Mazurkiewicz, chief financial officer of Michigan’s McLaren Health Care, described the tool doing exactly that — reminding a physician to record the severity of a patient’s kidney disease to justify a higher payment, or flagging that a diagnosis left undocumented would be unbillable. McLaren buys the technology from a company called SmarterDx, which takes a cut of the extra revenue it generates, and by Mazurkiewicz’s account the system has lifted the hospital’s revenue by a million dollars a month.

Read that arrangement again. A vendor sells a hospital an A.I. tool, and is paid a percentage of the additional billings the tool extracts from insurers. The incentive is not to document care accurately. It is to document it expensively. This is the revenue-optimising version of A.I. that Amber Nigam of Basys.ai, whose own firm advises insurers and Medicare on spotting abuses, said he is really, really concerned about — tools skilled at packaging information, even stitching together conditions from different years, to justify an expensive procedure. A payment request might fold in physical therapy from years earlier to justify a back surgery today.

The other side does it too

If the story ended there it would be a Code Blue essay about hospitals gaming insurers. It belongs in the ledger because the insurers have their own machines, pointed the other way.

Just as providers use A.I. to argue that patients are sicker, insurers have been accused of using it to delay or deny care, scanning charts for claims to reject. Mazurkiewicz described the symmetry with grim economy: all the insurers are using A.I. to scan our charts looking for claims to deny, and for the same reason, we are now looking at the same charts. A new Medicare pilot programme that leans on sophisticated technology to decide whether certain care should be covered has already drawn fire from lawmakers.

That pilot has a name, WISeR, short for Wasteful and Inappropriate Service Reduction, and a record. It began in January 2026 in six states, New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, Texas, Arizona and Washington, requiring prior approval for services such as skin substitutes and epidural steroid injections, and is set to run through 2031. On July 16, 2026, the Senate voted 46 to 50 along party lines to defeat a Democratic attempt to end it. Senator Ron Wyden described patients whose “care their doctor has recommended for them has been slowed or halted by a shadowy, AI-driven third party.” The House Appropriations Committee had already voted unanimously to add language to the 2027 health spending bill blocking money for the programme. Then, STAT reported on September 15, 2026, more than a thousand pages of documents pried loose by a Freedom of Information Act lawsuit from the Electronic Frontier Foundation showed a rollout that was hasty and error-ridden, including a warning from one of the pilot’s own vendors that the agency’s launch timeline was “unrealistic,” and delays in patient care as a result. The denial machine, in other words, now has a government edition, and it was rushed. It is also paid on the same principle as the hospitals’ tools, in reverse. As Ars Technica reported on September 26, 2026, from records covering the pilot’s first three months, WISeR pays its contractors a share of the “averted expenditures,” the care they decline to approve. One contractor, Virtix, denied more than half of the 6,000-plus requests it decided in Washington state, and the agency demanded a corrective action plan; the contractor handling Arizona denied fewer than 20 per cent. SmarterDx is paid for the billings it adds. WISeR’s vendors are paid for the care they subtract.

And because the marginal cost of another round is almost nothing, the rounds multiply. Caroline Pearson of the Peterson Health Technology Institute explained that both sides are willing to keep going because it’s cheap. The A.I. tools also enable a far higher volume of appeals when insurers deny a claim, and insurers report a flood of them bearing the unmistakable fingerprints of a large language model. Providers who once gave up too easily now appeal too aggressively. Machine against machine, at a penny a round.

The dystopia everyone can see coming

What makes this entry unusual is that the people building the technology describe its endpoint in the language of nightmare, unprompted and on the record.

Shiv Rao, a cardiologist who founded the documentation firm Abridge, named the danger directly: bots fighting bots, agents fighting agents, a horrible dystopic future nobody wants to live in. And at a New York event where executives from the insurer Cigna and Atlanta’s Emory Healthcare appeared together to discuss a truce, the chief executive of Emory Healthcare, Joon S. Lee, offered the image that gives this ledger entry its spine. His nightmare, he said, is bots on the hospital’s side and bots on the insurer’s side that simply keep talking to each other — with a whole data centre occupied doing nothing but that.

Sit with that sentence in the context of everything this ledger has documented. A whole data centre — the kind whose financing Part III called circular, whose off-balance-sheet leases Part II put at 662 billion dollars, whose bonds Part X showed lifting your mortgage rate, whose gas turbines Part XVII showed fouling the air near Homer City and El Paso — occupied entirely by two artificial intelligences arguing over whether a patient’s anaemia was worth 12,000 dollars. That is the A.I. build-out’s logic followed to its purest absurdity: enormous capital and enormous energy consumed to move money from one corporate ledger to another, producing no additional health, no additional care, nothing but heat and a higher bill.

Where the bill lands

And it is a higher bill, which is the whole reason this belongs in the ledger rather than merely in a health series.

Benefit consultants and insurers cite A.I.-driven billing friction as one of the factors that could push costs into double digits in 2027. A survey of employers suggested costs could rise as much as 11 per cent unless they cut back on benefits. The annual employer survey Marsh released on September 2, 2026, found the same 11 per cent figure as the increase employers would face if they did nothing, and put the expected rise after their cost-cutting at 8.2 per cent per employee, the highest since 2003 and the fifth straight year of elevated growth, and named one of the causes in so many words: “the rapid adoption of AI-enabled billing software that assists physicians with claims submission,” which has meant more claims, and higher-level claims, filed for payment. About 59 per cent of employers said they planned changes to cut costs, including higher deductibles. That is where the 11 per cent goes when it is trimmed to 8.2: onto the employee. Eric Miller of Segal, which advises companies on benefits, noted that we are still in the early period of this. The channel, in other words, is only beginning to open.

There is a dissent worth recording, in keeping with this ledger’s practice of giving the other side its full weight. Some economists are less gloomy. David Cutler of Harvard argued that A.I. is likely to save money by replacing the armies of people who currently process claims, freeing up a large share of administrative cost, and may even force a long-overdue conversation about simplifying how doctors and hospitals are paid. David Brailer, who oversaw the George W. Bush administration’s push for electronic health records, offered the more pointed historical rhyme: hospitals seized on that earlier technology to bill more, too, by documenting their care more thoroughly, and he sees nothing intrinsic in A.I. that will escape the same market forces. His prediction, published in Health Affairs, is that A.I. will most likely increase overall health spending — the sceptical read, but arrived at through the same logic Cutler uses to reach the hopeful one.

The honest verdict is that both can be true at once. A.I. may cut the administrative headcount and raise the total bill, because the savings accrue to the firms and the extra billings accrue against the patient. That is the pattern this entire ledger keeps finding: efficiency for the balance sheet, cost for the household.

The ledger’s entry

So the fifth transmission channel goes into the book alongside the property taxes, the Treasury yields, the youth backlash and the rents. This one is different in a way that ought to unsettle even readers numb to the others. The tax and yield channels at least fund the construction of something — data centres that, whatever their costs, compute. This channel funds an argument. The capital and the electricity go into machines whose entire output is a dispute with other machines over a bill that a human being will ultimately pay, plus 11 per cent.

Blue Cross’s Chalker called the situation, from the insurers’ point of view, not a war but a completely one-sided blood bath. He would say that. But strip away the question of which corporate giant is currently winning, and the citizen’s-eye view is simpler and worse. On one side, a machine insisting you were very sick. On the other, a machine insisting you were fine. Between them, a data centre running hot, and a premium going up.

Nobody voted for the bots. Everybody pays for the argument.
