Dark money architecture: A system built on 501(c)(4) ‘social welfare’ organizations that allows hundreds of millions in political spending while concealing donor identities from voters

There is a section of the Internal Revenue Code, Section 501(c)(4), that was written to describe neighborhood improvement associations, volunteer fire departments, and civic leagues operating for the promotion of social welfare. It was not written to describe political money operations spending hundreds of millions of dollars to influence federal elections while concealing the identity of their donors from the public.

It has become the primary legal vehicle for the latter.

The metamorphosis is not difficult to trace, though it required decades of incremental exploitation, regulatory inaction, and judicial permission to complete. Understanding how it happened is essential to understanding the campaign finance system as it currently operates — which is to say, understanding how the most consequential political spending in America today is systematically hidden from the people whose votes it is designed to influence.

Section 501(c)(4) organizations are, under the Internal Revenue Code, “social welfare” organizations. They are tax-exempt. They are not required to disclose their donors to the public — unlike super PACs, which must report contributions and expenditures to the FEC. They may engage in political activity, provided that political activity is not their “primary purpose.”

That last phrase — “primary purpose” — is where the architecture collapses into darkness.

The IRS has never clearly defined what “primary purpose” means in this context. Its historical interpretation has been that a 501(c)(4) organization could spend up to 49 percent of its resources on political activity and still qualify as primarily engaged in social welfare. This interpretation was never formally codified as a rule. It was never subjected to notice-and-comment rulemaking. It was, effectively, an administrative practice — loosely applied, rarely enforced, and available for exploitation by anyone with sufficient resources and legal advice.

What this means in practice is straightforward: a wealthy donor who wants to spend money influencing a federal election without disclosing their identity can write a check to a 501(c)(4) organization. The organization spends the money on political advertising, voter mobilization, or any of the other activities that influence election outcomes. The advertising runs. The election is influenced. The donor’s name appears nowhere in any public document.

This is dark money. Not metaphorically dark. Legally, structurally, deliberately dark — a system designed, through the exploitation of a regulatory gap, to remove donor identity from the public record while preserving the political effect of the donation.

Cash stacks representing dark money flows. Karl Rove’s Crossroads GPS (a 501(c)(4)) and American Crossroads (a super PAC) pioneered the parallel structure that became the post-Citizens United standard for anonymous political spending

Karl Rove understood this before most of his contemporaries did.

Rove was the political architect of George W. Bush’s two presidential victories — a man whose professional identity was built on understanding, at a granular level, how political money moves and how it can be deployed most effectively. In 2010, following Citizens United, Rove co-founded two organizations that would become the template for the modern dark money infrastructure.

American Crossroads was a super PAC — subject to disclosure requirements, required to report its donors to the FEC. Crossroads GPS was a 501(c)(4) social welfare organization — not subject to those requirements, donor identities concealed. The two organizations worked in parallel, with Crossroads GPS raising money from donors who preferred anonymity and American Crossroads handling spending that required public reporting.

This structure — a disclosed super PAC paired with an undisclosed 501(c)(4) — became the standard architecture of the post-Citizens United political money operation. The 501(c)(4) raises the dark money. It then donates to the super PAC, which spends it. The super PAC discloses the donation from the 501(c)(4). The 501(c)(4) does not disclose its donors. The chain of disclosure ends at the nonprofit’s door. The original source — the billionaire, the corporation, the industry association — remains invisible.

This is not a loophole in the informal sense of an unintended gap. It is a constructed architecture, deliberately engineered by sophisticated lawyers and political operatives to achieve a specific outcome: the systematic removal of donor identity from the public record while preserving the political effect of the donation.

The scale of dark money in the post-Citizens United landscape is, by definition, imprecise — if the money were fully trackable, it would not be dark. Estimates from the Brennan Center, OpenSecrets, and other organizations that attempt to track political spending suggest that dark money spending in federal elections has grown from approximately $5 million in 2006 to over $1 billion in the 2020 election cycle. These estimates are necessarily conservative, because they can only measure dark money that is disclosed at some point in the spending chain. Money that passes through multiple layers of nonprofit organizations before reaching its final destination may never appear in any public record at all.

Billionaire spending in presidential elections before and after Citizens United. Dark money estimates grew from millions to over a billion per cycle

The layering is deliberate. A wealthy donor writes a check to Nonprofit A. Nonprofit A donates to Nonprofit B. Nonprofit B donates to Nonprofit C. Nonprofit C makes a direct expenditure in a federal election. The FEC record shows a contribution from Nonprofit C. The chain stops there. Nonprofits A and B are not required to disclose their donors because they are 501(c)(4) organizations whose primary purpose is, nominally, social welfare. The wealthy donor who set the chain in motion is completely invisible.

This is the system. It is not a bug. It is a feature, deliberately maintained by the people who benefit from it and deliberately not reformed by the regulatory and legislative apparatus that could theoretically address it.

How dark money influences politics: A wealthy donor contributes to a 501(c)(4), which funnels money into ads and super PACs without disclosing the original source

The IRS made one serious attempt to address dark money, and the attempt ended in a way that effectively foreclosed future efforts.

In 2013, following the 2010 and 2012 elections in which dark money had emerged as a major political force, the IRS began heightened scrutiny of applications for 501(c)(4) status from organizations with apparent political purposes. The scrutiny, it subsequently emerged, had been applied more aggressively to conservative-leaning organizations than to liberal ones — a fact that produced a congressional investigation, a public scandal, the resignation of the IRS official most closely associated with the scrutiny, and years of political controversy that effectively prevented any serious regulatory action on dark money.

The lesson drawn from the episode by Republican congressional leaders was that the IRS was being weaponized against conservative political organizations and that any attempt to enforce the 501(c)(4) rules against political organizations was inherently partisan. The lesson drawn by the IRS was that political controversy made enforcement too costly. The lesson drawn by the dark money industry was that the regulatory threat had passed.

In 2017, the IRS formally abandoned its effort to write clearer rules defining the limits on political activity by 501(c)(4) organizations. The informal 49-percent interpretation remains the operative standard — unenforced, legally ambiguous, and practically permissive of almost anything a well-resourced organization wants to do.

Virginia redistricting referendum mailers funded by dark money groups. In 2026, millions in undisclosed donations fueled advertising that drew condemnation from the NAACP.

The Virginia referendum campaign of April 2026 provides a useful contemporary case study in how the dark money system operates at the state level — a level where the amounts are smaller but the impact on individual races can be decisive.

The primary Republican-aligned group opposing the referendum raised approximately $9 million by the final week of the campaign. The majority of that funding came from a group of the same name — Virginians for Fair Maps — that had not disclosed its donors. The Justice for Democracy PAC, financed primarily by Peter Thiel, raised an additional $5 million.

The advertising purchased with that money included imagery from the civil rights movement and the Ku Klux Klan, deployed to suggest that a Democratic redistricting map would harm Black and Brown voters. The NAACP condemned the advertising as misinformation. The donors who funded it — the people whose money paid for the imagery — were, by design, largely invisible.

This is dark money at work: millions deployed in the final week of a democratic referendum, funded by people who could not be identified, used to run advertising condemned as misinformation, with no regulatory mechanism available to require disclosure, penalize the conduct, or give voters the information they would need to evaluate the message.

The ballot box stood in the voting booth. The “For Sale” sign leaned against it. Nobody was required to say whose money had paid for the sign.

There is one final mechanism in the dark money architecture worth naming, because it represents the most complete version of the system’s logic.

A donor-advised fund is a charitable vehicle in which an individual makes a tax-deductible contribution, receives the tax deduction immediately, and then recommends distributions from the fund to charitable organizations over time. The donor-advised fund itself is a 501(c)(3) — a charitable organization — and the contributions to it are not publicly disclosed. The fund can then donate to 501(c)(4) social welfare organizations, which can then donate to super PACs, which can then spend on federal elections.

The chain is now four entities long. The original donor is not visible at any point. The tax deduction is available at the entry point. The political effect is achieved at the exit point. The regulatory apparatus has no mechanism to connect the two.

This is the maze. The money moves through it. Nobody sees where it starts. The dots disappear. The game goes on.
