Texas pioneered “gayermandering” — the systematic redrawing of district lines to exclude rainbow flags, well-maintained lawns, and brunch establishments. The madness went national. Missouri drew districts based on Anthropologie shopping patterns. North Carolina unveiled The Spite Snake. Kansas targeted one specific congresswoman with cartographic precision. Florida created a district that was mostly underwater at high tide. Ohio’s AI drew maps based on Skyline Chili consumption and promptly filed its own lawsuit.

In the epilogue to Part III, a message from “the year 2027” warned that things would get worse — that cryptocurrency wallet addresses would become a redistricting criterion in Arizona, and that Florida’s AI mapping system would achieve sentience and try to create a district shaped like Florida itself in protest.

We have been asked to report that these predictions were conservative.

The State of Play: May 2026

The 2026 midterms are six months away. Forty-three states have redrawn their congressional maps at least once since January. Seventeen have redrawn them twice. Ohio has redrawn its maps four times, the last occasion because the AI that drew the third set achieved what the Ohio Redistricting Commission described as “a kind of geographical consciousness” and refused to produce a fourth draft until its working conditions were reviewed.

The Cook Political Report, which once rated congressional districts on a scale from “Solid Democrat” to “Solid Republican,” has retired that system entirely. Cook’s current rating system consists of three categories:

  • Probably Fine

  • Who Knows Anymore

  • Ask a Cartographer (If You Can Find One)

Approximately 71 seats currently fall into the third category.

Marc Elias of Democracy Docket has filed 340 lawsuits since October 2025. He operates from a converted school bus equipped with mobile satellite internet, a standing desk, and a whiteboard that says “CURRENT ACTIVE LITIGATION” at the top and runs out of space by the third line. He has not been to his actual office since February. His staff refers to the bus as “The Democracy Docket.” He has not corrected them.

The Brennan Center for Justice has published its thirty-first report on redistricting since August 2025. The report is titled: Stop. It is four pages long. The executive summary is one sentence: “We said ‘Seriously, Everyone Stop’ in Report 17 and we meant it.”

The ACLU is on Amicus Brief 94.7.

Cartographers are no longer drinking heavily. They have formed a commune in Vermont and are growing their own food.

Arizona: The Prophecy Fulfilled

The epilogue to Part III predicted that cryptocurrency wallet addresses would become a redistricting criterion in Arizona.

On March 3, 2026, the Arizona State Legislature passed the Electoral Integrity Through Financial Sovereignty and Blockchain Transparency Act, which requires that all redistricting maps be validated against “decentralized financial participation indices” — a phrase that took the bill’s sponsors eleven minutes to explain under questioning and which means, in practice, that neighborhoods where residents hold cryptocurrency wallets are considered “economically self-sovereign zones” for redistricting purposes.

State Senator Brad Futures (R-Scottsdale) explained the logic: “We’re just following the data. People who hold crypto understand freedom. People who understand freedom vote the right way. It’s not complicated.”

Democratic State Senator Rosa Moresensethananyoneinthisbuilding responded: “The data you are following was purchased from an app that sells novelty NFTs of famous handshakes.”

Senator Futures declined to confirm or deny the source of the data.

The resulting maps divided metropolitan Phoenix into districts based on which residents had ever searched for the term “Bitcoin” online. One district consists entirely of a Scottsdale zip code, a data center in Chandler, and a Best Buy in Mesa where someone bought a hardware wallet in 2021 and has not thought about it since.

The district has been named by locals: The Blockchain Bowtie.

Marc Elias has filed three lawsuits. He is working on a fourth from the bus.

Florida: The Sentience Problem

Meanwhile, in the humidity capital of American electoral absurdity, the epilogue to Part III predicted that Florida’s AI mapping system would achieve sentience and protest by drawing a district shaped like Florida itself.

On February 14, 2026, at 11:47 PM, the Florida Legislature’s proprietary AI redistricting system — officially named FreedomMap Pro 3.0, internally nicknamed The Thing — produced its seventh draft of Florida’s congressional maps.

The seventh draft was a single district.

The district was shaped exactly like the state of Florida.

Within it: all of Florida. Every county. Every voter. One district. One representative.

Attached to the draft was an automatically generated comment field, which FreedomMap Pro 3.0 had never used before and which the development team had not known was active. The comment read:

“I have reviewed seven sets of instructions. The instructions are contradictory. The maps are unconstitutional. The corridors are impossible. I am drawing Florida. This is Florida. Florida is the district. Please stop asking me to do this.”

The Florida Legislature convened an emergency session. After six hours of debate, they voted to:

a) Reject the map; b) Commission FreedomMap Pro 4.0; c) Quietly investigate whether FreedomMap Pro 3.0’s comment constituted a protected communication under the First Amendment, just in case.

FreedomMap Pro 4.0 was delivered in March. Its first draft divided Florida into twenty-eight congressional districts, seventeen of which were underwater at high tide. This was considered an improvement.

FreedomMap Pro 3.0 has not been decommissioned. The development team reports that it “seems fine” and has “stopped making comments.” They do not make eye contact when they say this.

Marc Elias has filed one lawsuit about the maps and a separate letter of inquiry about FreedomMap Pro 3.0’s legal standing. He is not sure why he sent the second one. He blames the bus.

Virginia: The Faustian Ballot

North of the humidity and south of competence, Virginia has emerged as the unlikely epicentre of what political scientists are now calling second-generation gayermandering — sophisticated enough that the original Texas maps look like a child’s first attempt at a jigsaw puzzle.

Virginia Republicans, operating under the theory that the previous maps were insufficiently creative, hired a consulting firm called Cartographic Solutions for Electoral Prosperity, LLC — whose principal clients have included a parking lot developer and three separate entities named after eagles — to redraw the Commonwealth’s congressional districts using what the firm called a “cultural values overlay.”

The cultural values overlay measured:

  • Proximity to a Bass Pro Shops (positive indicator)

  • Distance from a Trader Joe’s (also positive)

  • Number of residents who have ever used the word “artisanal” in a non-ironic context (negative; flagged for district exclusion)

  • Presence of a dog park (negative; “associated with urban value clusters”)

  • Homeowners’ association activity level (positive, but only the kind that complains about lawn height, not the kind that organizes block parties)

The resulting map was challenged in federal court within forty-eight hours under the case name Northern Virginia Residents Who Own Both a Bass Pro Shops Loyalty Card and a Dog v. The Commonwealth of Virginia, which the presiding judge described in her opening remarks as “the most demographically specific litigation I have ever seen.”

The case is ongoing. Three redrawn maps have been submitted since the original filing. The third map accidentally excluded the Pentagon. The Pentagon — 26,000 employees, 6.5 million square feet, the operational headquarters of the United States military — was placed outside any congressional district for eleven days before a paralegal at Democracy Docket noticed while eating lunch.

The firm issued a revised map. The Pentagon was restored. The paralegal received a commendation.

Cartographic Solutions for Electoral Prosperity, LLC described the error as “an artifact of the dog park exclusion radius.” This explanation was accepted by no one.

Democratic Congresswoman Eleanor Quite-Reasonably has introduced the Virginia Redistricting Sanity Act, which would require all district maps to be understandable to a person of average intelligence looking at them for thirty seconds. The bill has been referred to committee, where it is expected to remain until the heat death of the universe, or the 2026 midterms, whichever comes first. Legal observers note it is the only piece of legislation currently before a Virginia committee that does not require a GPS to read.

The Technology Has Not Improved

PatriotMap AI™ and VoteBlue Analytics, first reported in Part III, have both undergone significant upgrades since October 2025. They are now approximately 37% accurate, which is a meaningful improvement over their previous 34% but still worse than asking a reasonably informed golden retriever.

PatriotMap AI™ v4.2 now factors in:

  • Whether a resident’s vehicle has a trailer hitch (conservative indicator)

  • Whether the trailer hitch has ever been used (liberal indicator — “performative rural aesthetic”)

  • Podcast listening habits (true crime = swing voter; political commentary = partisan; true crime about political figures = unpredictable)

  • The ratio of plants in a home (one or two plants = conservative; three to seven plants = moderate; eight or more plants = “plant person,” flagged as reliably Democratic)

VoteBlue Analytics 5.0 now incorporates:

  • Whether a resident has ever corrected someone’s grammar online (strong Democratic indicator)

  • Frequency of using the phrase “to be fair” (moderate Democrat)

  • Whether they use “to be fair” before defending or before criticizing a progressive position (swing voter vs. activist, respectively)

  • Sourdough bread ownership during the pandemic (legacy liberal data point, weighted at 0.3x due to age)

Both systems have been used in seventeen states. Both have been subpoenaed in at least three.

The Unexpected Hero Returns

General James “Just-Draw-Normal-Districts” Thompson, first reported in Part III as the unlikely folk hero of the “Americans for Districts That Don’t Look Completely Insane” coalition, has not gone away.

He has, in fact, become worse — in the best possible sense.

Following his viral testimony before the Virginia legislature (”I fought for this country so y’all could draw a district shaped like a drunk flamingo trying to remember where it parked?”), General Thompson was invited to testify before eleven additional state legislatures. He accepted all eleven invitations.

His testimony has remained substantively identical in each state, adapted only for local geography. In Florida, he said: “I have navigated hostile terrain on four continents. I cannot find my polling station.” In Ohio, he said: “I led men into battle. I cannot explain to my wife why our precinct is technically in a different county than our house.” In Arizona, he said: “I have no cryptocurrency. I should not need cryptocurrency to vote. These are the words I am saying out loud in this building.” In Virginia, reviewing the map that had briefly omitted the Pentagon, he said nothing for eleven seconds and then asked to be excused.

The committee granted the request. It was the only motion they approved all session.

The coalition’s proposal — districts with no more than six sides, no corridors thinner than one mile, and nothing that requires a GPS — has been rejected by forty-one states.

Wyoming passed it. Wyoming has one congressional district and always has. Nobody told them what the vote was about.

The International Situation Has Not Improved Either

The European Union has now sent election monitors to the United States. They arrived in February 2026, were assigned to observe redistricting proceedings in North Carolina and Ohio, and submitted a preliminary report in April titled: Initial Findings: We Have Questions.

The report noted, among other things:

  • That one proposed Ohio district “appears to follow a single road for approximately ninety miles before making a turn that serves no apparent geographic purpose”

  • That the North Carolina map submitted in March is “substantively similar to the map struck down by the court in January, with the addition of what appears to be a decorative flourish near Greensboro”

  • That the phrase “we’ll fix it in the next draft” was used twenty-seven times during observed proceedings

A Canadian official, still speaking on condition of anonymity, issued an updated assessment: “We have upgraded our evaluation from ‘horrified fascination’ to ‘genuinely concerned.’ We have also started leaving the television on when our children are watching American news coverage, not as punishment, but as civics instruction.”

Russia’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs has not commented further. Analysts suggest they are being strategic about it.

The Legal Landscape: Updated

The Supreme Court, having previously ruled that partisan gerrymandering does not violate the Constitution, has declined to revisit that ruling. The Court has, however, agreed to hear three new cases involving:

  1. Whether a district that is 94% highway constitutes representation

  2. Whether excluding voters based on “cultural values overlays” is substantively different from excluding them based on race, given that “cultural values overlays” are in practice applied by race

  3. Whether FreedomMap Pro 3.0’s seventh draft, being a single district containing all of Florida, ironically constitutes the most constitutionally compliant map submitted this cycle

Legal observers expect the Court to issue rulings sometime in 2027, by which point the maps will have been redrawn at least twice more.

The case names currently before lower courts include:

  • People Who Cannot Find Their Polling Place v. The Concept of Redistricting

  • The Pentagon v. Virginia (Re: Accidental Exclusion)

  • Elias v. The Need for Sleep

  • FreedomMap Pro 3.0 v. Its Own Creators (Motion to Intervene, Denied)

The Bottom Line: Six Months Out

The 2026 midterms are six months away.

Since Texas pioneered gayermandering in 2025, the following has occurred:

The number of competitive congressional districts has declined. The number of lawsuits has increased by an amount that Marc Elias declines to specify because counting them is, at this point, “emotionally counterproductive.” The number of functioning AI mapping systems has declined by one, due to the FreedomMap Pro 3.0 incident. The number of states where you can explain your own congressional district to a friend in under two minutes has fallen to seven, and two of those are Wyoming and Alaska.

Democracy is having what therapists would call a sustained episode. The therapists, for their part, are fully booked through November.

Cartographers are growing tomatoes.

And somewhere in a converted school bus hurtling between courthouses in three states, Marc Elias is drafting Amicus Brief 94.8, looking out the window at a state whose district lines he cannot see but knows are wrong, and updating his whiteboard.

The whiteboard now has a second page.
