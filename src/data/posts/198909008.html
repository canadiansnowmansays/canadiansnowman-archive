Stephen Gordon May 23, 2026

This is an addendum to “The Rise and Disappearance of Tulsi Gabbard,” published April 29, 2026.

Three and a half weeks ago, I wrote that Tulsi Gabbard had not been fired, not resigned, not protested, and was not visible. That she had simply disappeared — the nation’s top intelligence official rendered decorative during the biggest American military engagement in a generation.

Yesterday, she made it official. She resigned.

And even the resignation disappeared.

I.

The letter is dated May 22, 2026. It is addressed to the President. It is brief, gracious, and heartbreaking in exactly one respect: Gabbard’s husband, Abraham Williams, has been diagnosed with an extremely rare form of bone cancer. She will leave office on June 30 to be by his side.

I want to pause here and do something this series rarely does — step entirely outside the analysis. Abraham Williams’s diagnosis is not a political story. It is a human one. Whatever Gabbard’s tenure was or wasn’t, a family is facing the kind of news that reorganizes everything. That deserves silence before it deserves commentary.

Silence observed.

II.

Now. Let’s talk about everything else.

On the same day that Gabbard submitted her resignation, Shane Harris of The Atlantic published a comprehensive accounting of her tenure under the headline “Tulsi Gabbard Takes the Exit Ramp.” The New York Times ran a detailed breakdown confirming what the original essay argued three weeks ago: she had been sidelined from the Iran war planning, sidelined from the Venezuela operation, and replaced in practice by CIA Director John Ratcliffe, who had assumed the role of the President’s de facto intelligence adviser.

She is the fifth cabinet-level official to depart Trump’s second term, following Pam Bondi, Kristi Noem, Lori Chavez-DeRemer, and — depending on how you classify the role — Michael Waltz. All four cabinet secretaries who have left are women. Make of that what you will.

The Times reports that Trump did not force her out. He didn’t need to. You don’t fire someone who isn’t doing anything. You just stop noticing they’re there, which is exactly what the original essay described: not a fall, but a vanishing.

Trump’s response on Truth Social was four words of substance: “Tulsi has done an incredible job.” This is the same man who, last July, praised her by saying she was “the hottest one in the room” — weeks after privately excoriating her for a self-promotional video filmed during a trip to Asia. The relationship, such as it was, ran on exactly two fuels: flattery when she performed his obsessions, and contempt when she didn’t.

III.

Harris’s Atlantic piece contains an observation that deserves serious attention, because it inverts the thesis of the original essay in a way I did not anticipate.

Harris argues that Gabbard’s exclusion from the administration’s worst decisions — the Iran war, the Venezuela raid — may turn out to be the best thing that ever happened to her career. She can’t be blamed for the disasters because she wasn’t in the room when they were planned. She retains credibility among the anti-interventionist wing of the MAGA coalition. Joe Rogan — who called Trump’s war on Iran “nuts” — recently described Gabbard as “amazing” and “cool as fuck.”

In other words: the disappearance may have been the strategy all along. Or, more precisely, the disappearance created an opening that a sufficiently ambitious politician could exploit after the fact. Harris’s sources describe Gabbard as the most ambitious person they’d ever met in Washington.

The woman who sold T-shirts opposing a war with Iran stayed silent during a war with Iran — and may run for president on the strength of that silence.

I confess I did not see that coming. The original essay treated Gabbard’s invisibility as the final indignity. It may have been the exit ramp to something larger.

IV.

But I want to resist the temptation to rewrite the story as a tale of secret genius.

Harris also documents what Gabbard actually did during her fifteen months: she accused former officials of a “yearslong coup” against Trump. She amplified baseless claims about the 2016 Russia investigation. She declassified intelligence material over CIA objections and publicly misrepresented what the documents said. She fired two senior analysts who wrote an assessment that contradicted Trump’s effort to link Venezuela’s president to a criminal gang — an assessment that helped justify the very military intervention she supposedly opposed.

She stripped security clearances. She gutted the Foreign Malign Influence Center. She slashed her own agency at Trump’s direction with what the Times called vigour, which is a polite word for enthusiasm applied to demolition.

This is not the record of someone who was quietly preserving her principles for a future campaign. This is the record of someone who did everything she could think of to earn a seat at the grown-ups’ table and was told, repeatedly, to sit back down.

The anti-war Democrat from Hawaii spent fifteen months helping a wartime president punish his enemies, distort intelligence, and weaken the institutions she was sworn to lead. Then she went to the beach.

V.

The resignation letter closes: “With love and aloha.”

Three weeks ago, this essay ended with a T-shirt in a Goodwill bin in Honolulu. Today it ends with a letter from Hawaii, where the original Tulsi Gabbard — the one who went to Syria, who endorsed Bernie Sanders, who opposed regime-change wars with something that looked like conviction — was manufactured and, apparently, retired.

Her deputy, Aaron Lukas, will serve as acting director. The intelligence community will continue to function as it has for the past fifteen months, which is to say: without her.

Somewhere in Honolulu, that T-shirt is still in the bin.

Its original owner has finally been reached for comment. She sends her love and aloha.
