In Pinar del Río, a fifty-five-year-old farmer named Humberto Palmero taught himself, through YouTube videos, how to distill waste plastic into gasoline and diesel. As CNN has reported, in Havana, on August 3rd, the national grid collapsed for the sixth time this year, and thunderstorms moved in the next morning to disrupt the repair crews before they could finish. In Langley, sometime in the weeks before that, someone stood up a new internal structure — doctors, financial analysts, counterintelligence officers, the whole apparatus of a modern intelligence bureaucracy — and gave it a mandate: find the cracks in the Cuban political elite, and widen them.

This series concluded in April. It said, at the time, that it was concluding honestly — that the negotiations were unresolved, the blockade was ongoing, and the only fair thing a writer could do was stop and let events answer the question the series had been asking. Four months later, events have answered it by refusing to stop asking. The Ocean Mariner never really made port. So here is the coda nobody asked for and Cuba’s ten million residents didn’t get a vote on: what happened after “The Last Word.”

What the Task Force Actually Is

As the New York Times has reported, the task force will allow the agency to quickly direct more financial, human, and technical resources at the island, with the explicit goal of creating fissures among the Cuban political elite. The framing matters, because the CIA has done this exact thing before under a very different name. In 1961, in the humiliating aftermath of the Bay of Pigs, Washington did not abandon the project of removing Fidel Castro — it professionalized it. Robert Kennedy’s Cuban Task Force met at the White House in the fall of that year and settled on a strategy that should sound uncomfortably familiar to anyone who has read this series in order: covert operations directed at “targets important to the economy,” refineries and plants singled out for destruction, trade surveillance designed to strangle Cuban commerce with third countries, all of it run through the principle that paramilitary pressure should look like a homegrown Cuban opposition rather than an American hand. That effort had a name too. It was called Operation Mongoose, and it consumed more CIA officers and more money than any covert program in the agency’s history up to that point, and it failed.

Sixty-five years later, the language in the current reporting is almost a paraphrase of Kennedy’s own memo. The tools have changed. The target has not. What Robert Kennedy tried to do with sabotage teams and exile brigades, this administration is attempting with sanctions architecture and a starvation blockade dressed up, since May, as diplomacy — CIA Director John Ratcliffe’s very public flight into José Martí International — as Peter Kornbluh reported for The Nation — delivering what his own agency did not bother to disguise as an ultimatum, invoking the 32 dead Venezuelan security men in Caracas as Exhibit A of what happens to governments that test Washington’s patience.

The difference this time is that nobody is pretending the CIA isn’t involved. The 1961 task force operated in near-total secrecy; this one announces its director’s travel on social media. That shift — from covert shame to overt leverage — is itself a measure of how much has changed about what Washington is willing to be seen doing to a small island ninety miles from Florida.

Regime Alteration, Not Regime Change

As Tyler Pager, Julian E. Barnes, and Frances Robles reported for The New York Times on August 7th, the task force’s actual mandate is narrower, and more revealing, than “create fissures” suggests. Washington is searching for a Cuban analog to Delcy Rodríguez — the Venezuelan official who stepped seamlessly into the presidency after U.S. forces captured Nicolás Maduro in January — someone pragmatic enough to take over and cooperative enough to work with. It is regime alteration dressed as reform, the theory being that decapitation only pays off if there’s a ready hand to catch the reins.

The trouble, per the same reporting, is that American intelligence has concluded Cuba resembles Iran far more than Venezuela on this particular point: thin on pragmatists, thick on hardliners waiting in the wings. The officials named as potential successors are Oscar Pérez-Oliva Fraga, a grand-nephew of Fidel Castro currently being elevated on state television; Raúl Castro’s own grandson, Raúl G. Rodríguez Castro, known within Cuba as “El Cangrejo”; and Interior Minister Lázaro Álvarez Casas — the latter two having reportedly met directly with Ratcliffe on his May visit. None of them reads as Havana’s answer to Delcy Rodríguez. The Justice Department has meanwhile unsealed an indictment against the 95-year-old Raúl Castro, echoing the legal groundwork laid before the Maduro raid — except Castro, unlike Maduro, holds no formal office, so his capture would not automatically hand power to anyone at all. Washington may have built the machinery of a decapitation strike without identifying a head to replace the one it removes.

The Parallel Task Force Nobody Authorized

While one task force works to fracture Cuba’s elite from above, a different and unfunded one has been operating from below for years, and it has nothing to do with regime change. It has to do with a toaster.

As Cuba Headlines has documented, drawing on the same New York Times reporting, Cubans have spent 2025 and 2026 building a parallel country’s worth of infrastructure by hand. Electric mototaxis, most of them Chinese-made, have been quietly replacing the fleet of 1950s American cars that were never built for a fuel crisis like this one. Imported lithium batteries, rigged up to run fans and televisions through the blackout windows, have become a small underground economy of their own. Solar panels have gone up on rooftops one household at a time, with no state program behind them. And when the gas ran out for cooking, the country’s ingenuity turned domestic: rice cookers gutted and rebuilt as charcoal stoves, pressure cookers repurposed the same way, spare fire extinguisher canisters turned into stove bodies. The Food Monitor Program puts the number of Cubans now cooking under these improvised, precarious conditions above nine million — better than a third of them relying on wood or charcoal because there is nothing else to rely on.

Mr. Palmero’s YouTube-taught plastic-to-diesel operation is the extreme end of the same phenomenon, but the pattern underneath all of it is consistent: none of this came from the state. Havana’s government has offered rhetoric — Miguel Díaz-Canel has taken to describing the resourcefulness of ordinary Cubans as evidence of national “creativity and spirit,” which is one way to describe nine million people improvising survival because the grid cannot be trusted to stay on past the next thunderstorm. What the government calls resilience, its own people are living as obligation. There is no meaningful policy behind any of it. It is simply what happens when a population concludes, correctly, that no one above them is going to fix this, and gets on with fixing it themselves.

Two Task Forces, One Country

Put the two of these next to each other and something clarifies. Washington has assembled the doctors, the counterintelligence officers, the financial analysts — trained professionals with a budget and a headquarters — for the express purpose of finding weaknesses to exploit in a government of ten million people. Meanwhile, that same ten million has assembled its own improvised task force, with no budget, no headquarters, and no professional training beyond what a mechanic in Cienfuegos can find on the internet, for the express purpose of keeping the lights on, the food cooked, and the water pumped, one household at a time.

One of these efforts is well-funded, deliberate, sanctioned by an executive order, and aimed at accelerating Cuban suffering as leverage. The other is improvised, desperate, entirely unfunded, and aimed at surviving it. Both are, in their own ways, working: the blockade continues to cause real damage to a government whose legitimacy was already fraying, while the plastic-to-diesel operations and the gutted rice cookers continue to keep Cubans alive despite it. Neither is a solution. The CIA’s task force cannot make Cuba’s government collapse on command any more than Operation Mongoose could in 1962, and no number of solar panels bolted to a Havana rooftop is a substitute for a functioning national grid.

What both task forces confirm, from opposite directions, is the argument this series made in its conclusion four months ago: that the people of this island have been left, again, to absorb the consequences of a contest being waged over their heads. Langley is trying to fracture the elite. Ordinary Cubans are trying to fix the toaster. Only one of those projects has anyone’s wellbeing as its actual objective, and it isn’t the one with the budget.
