A Supreme Court that spent June deregulating the flow of money into politics — the physical architecture of how campaigns are now funded

Two instalments ago, in the piece I called The Both-Ways King, I wrote that the machinery built to pre-decide American elections was failing — that a president who had to endorse both South Carolina candidates to keep from backing a loser, and a lobby that spent into a New York headwind and watched three of its targets win anyway, were evidence of the same happy verdict: the apparatus designed to settle elections before the votes arrive was meeting an electorate that had remembered it gets the last word.

That was the optimistic instalment. This is its correction.

Because three days after Louisiana completed the execution of Bill Cassidy — the June 27 runoff that closed Part XL — the Supreme Court of the United States sat in Washington and set about repairing the machinery, on Tuesday, June 30. While the party had just finished punishing its last disloyal Louisianan for the crime of a conscience vote, the Court removed the last meaningful cap on how lavishly that same party may now reward the loyal, everywhere, all at once. The timing was a coincidence. The symmetry was not.

What Actually Fell

Let me state the ruling plainly, because the plainness is the whole of the danger.

For decades, federal law capped how much a political party could spend in coordination with its own candidates — money spent hand-in-glove with a campaign, as opposed to the “independent” expenditures Citizens United unleashed in 2010. Those caps were not trivial. For the 2026 cycle they ran from roughly sixty-five thousand dollars in coordination with a House candidate up to something over four million for a Senate candidate in the largest states. The number scaled with the size of the prize. But it was a ceiling, and the ceiling meant something: a party could not simply become a candidate’s second bank account.

On Tuesday, in National Republican Senatorial Committee v. Federal Election Commission, the Court struck that ceiling down. The vote was 6–3, along the lines everyone can now recite in their sleep. Justice Kavanaugh wrote for the majority, holding that the coordinated-spending limits — enacted by Congress in the long shadow of Watergate — violated the First Amendment. The 2001 precedent that had upheld them for a quarter-century, Colorado II, was, in Kavanaugh’s economical phrase, overruled. It was the second precedent the Court had overturned in as many days.

There is a detail here that this series exists to notice, and I will not let it slide past. When the case reached the Court, the Trump Justice Department — charged with defending the laws of the United States — declined to defend this one. It had already stopped enforcing the limits. So the Court had to appoint an outside lawyer, a private attorney named Roman Martinez, to stand up and argue for the statute that the government itself would no longer defend. Sit with that arrangement for a moment. The law was still on the books. The executive branch sworn to enforce it simply walked away, and a stranger had to be conscripted to speak for it. That is not how a healthy system defends its own rules. It is how a system dismantles them while maintaining the appearance of a fair fight.

The Fingerprint

For readers who have followed this newsletter’s long documentation of who, exactly, has been rebuilding the American machine, the origin of this case will not surprise you.

NRSC v. FEC did not fall from the sky in June 2026. It began in 2022, as a lawsuit filed by a group of Republicans that included one then-Senate candidate from Ohio named JD Vance. The man who is now Vice President of the United States spent the intervening years shepherding toward the Court the precise ruling that will now flood his party’s coffers in the first midterm of the administration he serves. He backed the suit as a candidate; he reaps it as the sitting Vice President. The through-line I have traced across forty instalments — the patient, institutional, entirely-within-the-rules reconstruction of how power is acquired and kept — has rarely offered a cleaner single fingerprint. Vance did not tweet this into being. He litigated it, over four years, and won.

Kagan’s Phrase

If you want the danger of this ruling compressed into a single image, you do not have to reach for one. The dissent supplies it.

Writing for the three dissenters, Justice Kagan warned that the decision jettisons a rule needed to protect the integrity of the democracy, and that it ushers back in the very opportunities for quid-pro-quo corruption the limits were built to check. And then the phrase I have borrowed for the title of this instalment, because nothing I could invent would improve on it: the ruling, she wrote, will let a political party serve as an alternative checking account for a campaign.

An alternative checking account. Consider what that means in practice. An individual donor still faces a legal limit on what she may give directly to a candidate. But a donor who gives instead to the party — which can now spend without limit in coordination with that same candidate — has found a channel around the wall. The money that could not go in the front door of the campaign now flows through the party and out into the candidate’s ad buys, his polling, his direct mail, coordinated down to the timing and the message. The cap on the front door still stands. The Court has simply removed the wall around back.

This is the structural story this series was built to tell. A gerrymander steals an outcome, but only until the next map. The Cassidy purge removed one senator, but the seat was already red. What the Court did on Tuesday is more durable than either, because it does not steal a single election — it rewires the plumbing of all of them, in a direction that runs one way: toward whoever already has the most money to move.

The Money Was Already Loaded

And here is why the timing is not academic. The gun the Court just unlocked was already loaded, and pointed at November.

Going into June, the Republican National Committee sat on more than a hundred and twenty-five million dollars in available cash, with no unpaid bills. That war chest existed before the ruling; the ruling simply told the party it could now aim all of it, in coordination, at the specific races that will decide control of Congress. The effect lands first and hardest in exactly the battlegrounds this newsletter has been tracking all week. North Carolina — Roy Cooper’s race, the one I wrote about only days ago as the Democrats’ strongest pickup in the six-state poll — was already projected to become the first billion-dollar Senate race in American history. Cooper’s own verdict on the ruling was that the Court had handed megadonors and shady corporate money the power to drown out millions of small-dollar voices. He would say that; he is the candidate about to be outspent. That does not make him wrong.

Where Democracy Fights Back

So where, in a ruling this sweeping and this clean, does democracy fight back at all?

I will be honest, as this series tries always to be at its close: this is a hard one to end in the major key. The Court did not merely permit a bad outcome the way a gerrymander does — it reached back and dismantled a guardrail that had held for a quarter of a century, and it did so cleanly, permanently, and inside the rules. There is no appellate walk-around coming for a Supreme Court decision. This wall did not get bent. It got demolished by the highest authority there is.

But the fight-back beat is real, and it is worth naming precisely, because it is the same beat that closed Part XXXIX. Several Democrats in high-profile Senate races have already moved to challenge the parallel piece of this machine — the Trump FCC’s quiet guidance handing coordinated party ads the cheapest available broadcast rates, which would stretch every one of those new party dollars further. That fight is live.

And beneath the litigation is the deeper answer, the one this whole series keeps circling back to. The Court has just handed the parties an unlimited checking account. But money in politics has always been a bet on the proposition that spending decides outcomes — and June was one long rebuke of exactly that proposition. AIPAC entered its New York primaries with ninety-six million dollars and lost three of them. A self-funder spent better than a hundred million of his own money and, yes, won Georgia’s governorship — but the President’s endorsement could not save his pick in Iowa, and had to hedge itself to two candidates in South Carolina to keep from losing outright. The correlation between the biggest checkbook and the winning candidate has been fraying in plain sight all season.

The Court reloaded the gun. What it cannot legislate, from any bench, is that the voters flinch when it goes off. That is the one variable no ruling reaches — the same variable that took down Cassidy’s executioners’ preferred candidates in state after state this spring, the same variable that turned AIPAC’s nine figures into an embarrassment. Democracy fights back here not by keeping the money out — that battle, for now, is lost — but by continuing to be the one thing all this money is spent to purchase and can never quite guarantee: a room full of people who have not yet decided, and who will decide for their own reasons, on their own Tuesday, in a booth no checking account can follow them into.

The wall came down. The voters are still standing where the wall used to be. For now, in this grim season, that will have to be the good news: not that the money has been stopped, but that the thing it is chasing still refuses, election after election, to be bought outright.
