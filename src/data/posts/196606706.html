In August 2017, Elon Musk, Greg Brockman, and Ilya Sutskever met at a party house Musk had recently purchased just south of Seattle. There was confetti on the floor and plastic cups from the night before. Amber Heard — whom Musk was dating at the time — was there. So was Shivon Zilis, Musk’s adviser and chief of staff. Musk had just given Tesla electric cars as gifts to his fellow OpenAI co-founders. Sutskever, in return, had brought Musk a painting of a Tesla he had made himself.

The topic of the meeting was equity. How would a proposed for-profit arm of OpenAI distribute shares among its founders? Brockman and Sutskever said they would not agree to give Musk full majority control. Musk paused. He stood up. He walked around the table toward Brockman.

“I truly thought he was going to physically attack me,” Brockman testified Tuesday in Oakland federal court. “I thought he was going to hit me.”

Before leaving the room, Musk delivered his verdict: “When will you be departing OpenAI? I will withhold funding until you decide what you are going to do.”

That is the founding feud, in a room full of confetti, with a Tesla painting on the table.

What Brockman’s Two Days Produced

Greg Brockman spent two days on the witness stand — Monday and Tuesday — and his testimony systematically dismantled the narrative Musk had spent three days building the previous week.

Musk’s central argument is that he founded OpenAI as a nonprofit, committed to open sourcing its technology, and watched Altman and Brockman betray those commitments in pursuit of commercial gain. Brockman addressed each element directly.

On the nonprofit: Brockman read a 2017 text he sent to Zilis detailing a meeting with Musk that July. “He said nonprofit was def the right one early on, may not be the right one now,” the text read — referring to Musk. The man suing OpenAI for abandoning its nonprofit status was telling his own chief of staff, in writing, that the nonprofit structure might no longer be appropriate, in the same year the for-profit discussions began.

On open sourcing: Musk repeatedly claimed from the stand that freely sharing OpenAI’s technology with the world was a core founding commitment. Brockman was asked directly. “Honestly, it was not a topic of conversation,” he said. Musk never formally required open sourcing of the nonprofit, never expressed interest in it in their discussions, and never moved to make it a structural requirement.

On Musk’s contribution: Musk testified he was indispensable — that he recruited the key people, raised the funding, and drove OpenAI’s early success. Brockman described a different reality. Musk was generally not very available for meetings or conversations, he said. He relied on Zilis and Sam Teller as proxies. The driving force in OpenAI’s day-to-day operations was Altman and Brockman, not Musk.

Elon Musk has sued OpenAI, accusing Mr. Brockman and its chief executive, Sam Altman, of breaching the A.I. lab’s founding contract by putting commercial gain over the public good.Credit...Jason Henry for The New York Times

On the for-profit conversion: Brockman confirmed that Musk spent several months in 2017 actively working with the other founders to design a for-profit structure. The Dota victory — when OpenAI’s AI beat professional players at an international tournament — was Musk’s “triggering event.” It was Musk who said it was “time to make the next step for OpenAI.” The for-profit discussions that followed were not a betrayal of Musk’s vision. They were, in substantial part, his idea, contingent on his having majority control.

On his own wealth: Under questioning from Musk’s lawyer Steven Molo, Brockman acknowledged that his stake in OpenAI’s for-profit subsidiary is now worth approximately $30 billion — placing him in the 80s or 90s on the Forbes list of the world’s wealthiest people. Molo pressed him on why he had not donated the bulk of that equity back to the nonprofit foundation. Brockman did not have a straightforward answer. He also acknowledged that he had promised to personally donate $100,000 to OpenAI in fundraising emails to potential donors — including a 2014 email to Marissa Mayer — and never followed through. “I did not end up donating, that is true,” he said. His explanation: he had asked Altman when he should donate, and Altman said he would let him know.

Musk’s lawyers also surfaced a 2017 journal entry: “Financially, what will take me to $1B?” Brockman did not dispute writing it.

The Tesla Autopilot Disclosure

The most unexpected revelation of Brockman’s testimony was not about equity or governance. It was about labour.

Brockman testified that Musk enlisted several OpenAI employees to do months of free work for him at Tesla in 2017. The work involved overhauling Tesla’s approach to self-driving technology — specifically the Autopilot team. OpenAI employees, funded by OpenAI’s nonprofit, were redirected to work on Musk’s for-profit electric car company without compensation to OpenAI.

This detail sits uneasily alongside Musk’s portrait of himself as the selfless philanthropist whose charitable resources were misused by Altman and Brockman. The man suing for misuse of charitable resources was, simultaneously, redirecting those resources to his own commercial enterprise.

The City on Mars

Brockman also testified that Musk told him he wanted control of OpenAI’s proposed for-profit arm partly because building a city on Mars required approximately $80 billion — and he needed the financial resources that a majority stake in a frontier AI company would provide.

SpaceX, which now owns xAI — OpenAI’s direct competitor — is currently preparing for a public offering that would value the company at up to $1.5 trillion. The city on Mars is still a work in progress. The AI company that was supposed to fund it is now suing the one Musk left behind.

Before the Trial: The Settlement Text

Before Monday’s proceedings began, a weekend filing surfaced a pre-trial communication that reframes the entire week. Two days before the trial opened, Musk texted Brockman to gauge his interest in a settlement. Brockman suggested both sides drop their respective claims. Musk’s response: “By the end of this week, you and Sam will be the most hated men in America. If you insist, so it will be.”

OpenAI’s lawyers moved to enter the text into evidence, arguing it proves “motive and bias — in particular, that Mr. Musk’s motivation in pursuing this lawsuit is to attack a competitor and its principals.” Judge Gonzalez Rogers denied the request, ruling the text should have been submitted during Musk’s testimony. It is in the public record but not in evidence.

The distinction matters legally. It does not matter analytically. A man who two days before trial was threatening to make his co-founders “the most hated men in America” — and who subsequently took the stand to describe himself as a selfless guardian of nonprofit principle — has a credibility problem that no evidentiary ruling can fully contain.

Sam Altman, it emerged, had worried before the trial that Musk might drop the case at the last minute, leaving OpenAI without a chance to tell its side of the story. He said so on a podcast last month. That fear was apparently well-founded.

The Expert Witness

Monday’s first witness was Stuart Russell, a computer science professor at UC Berkeley, hired by Musk’s team as an AI expert witness. The most memorable part of his testimony was not his analysis of OpenAI’s mission. It was his compensation. Russell disclosed he was being paid $5,000 per hour for 40 hours of preparatory work — $235,000 in total, approximately 20 percent of his annual income. The payment came from Excession LLC, Musk’s family office. Standard expert witness rates in high-profile cases typically run between $500 and $1,000 per hour. Russell is being paid five to ten times the going rate.

Trial Structure — A Clarification

Before Monday’s proceedings, Judge Gonzalez Rogers made a structural ruling that has been underreported. She split the trial into two phases: a liability phase to determine whether any wrongdoing occurred, and a remedies phase to decide outcomes if wrongdoing is found. The liability phase is expected to conclude by May 21.

The jury’s verdict in the liability phase will be advisory only. Judge Gonzalez Rogers makes the final binding decision. The nine people who have been watching Musk testify for three days and Brockman for two will render an opinion. The judge decides.

This is the governance argument in miniature. Even the trial’s accountability mechanism is not quite what it appears to be.

What This Confirms

Cross-references to The Asimov Gap

The founding feud is now sworn testimony. The party house in Seattle, the confetti, the Amber Heard detail, the physical intimidation, the funding threat — all of it is now in the trial record under oath. The AI boom’s founding schism is no longer background. It is evidence. The Musk-Brockman confrontation over majority control is the structural origin of everything examined in Parts 28, 30, and 31: the governance vacuum, the for-profit conversion, the race to the bottom. It happened in a room full of party debris, with a Tesla painting on the table, because one man would not accept anything less than control.

The open sourcing claim is directly contradicted. Musk’s repeated assertion that freely sharing OpenAI’s technology was a founding commitment has now been contradicted under oath by the co-founder who was in the room. “Honestly, it was not a topic of conversation.” If the jury believes Brockman over Musk on this point — and the documentary record supports Brockman — Musk’s moral framing loses its most sympathetic element.

The Tesla Autopilot labour transfer is a governance problem, not just a trial detail. OpenAI employees doing free work for Tesla’s Autopilot team in 2017 is a misuse of nonprofit resources by the plaintiff. The man suing for misuse of charitable resources was simultaneously redirecting those resources to his own commercial enterprise. This has not been adequately covered in trial reporting.

The advisory jury structure matters for the governance argument. The jury renders an opinion. The judge decides. The most consequential corporate governance case in AI history is, at its core, being decided by one person. That is a feature of the American legal system, not a failure. But it is worth naming: the accountability moment the AI era has been waiting for is, structurally, a bench trial wearing a jury’s clothes.

Watch For Wednesday

Shivon Zilis takes the stand. She is simultaneously a former OpenAI board member, mother of at least four of Musk’s children, his self-described romantic partner, and — according to OpenAI — the person Musk used to keep tabs on the company after he left. She was in the room in Seattle in 2017 when Brockman feared Musk would hit him. Her deposition has already stated she is in a romantic relationship with Musk.

There is no precedent in AI governance history for a witness with this profile. There is also no precedent for a trial that requires one.
