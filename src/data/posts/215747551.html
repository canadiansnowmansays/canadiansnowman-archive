Stephen Gordon Sep 14, 2026

At 7:52 p.m. Eastern on Monday, September 14, 2026, the question this series has been tracking since June resolved itself in a single unsigned paragraph. The Supreme Court blocked the Trump administration’s plan to restrict mail-in voting ahead of the midterm elections, ruling that the government was “unlikely to succeed on the merits” of its challenge to the lower-court injunctions that had kept the Postal Service’s new ballot rules frozen since late August. Only Justices Samuel Alito and Clarence Thomas dissented. Everyone else — including two of the president’s own appointees — voted no.

This is worth stating plainly, because Part XLVIII, written that same morning, was built on the premise that the Court’s silence might be the only verdict this fight would get before the ballots went out. That premise held for almost exactly nine hours. The Court did not let the clock run out. It ruled, and it ruled decisively, and the fact that it did changes the shape of the story in ways worth working through rather than papering over.

What the Ruling Actually Says

The order itself is thin, as emergency rulings typically are: one paragraph, unsigned, no vote count beyond the two noted dissents. But Justice Brett Kavanaugh, joining the majority, wrote a concurrence that matters more than the order it accompanies. As Ann Marimow reported for The New York Times, Kavanaugh said there is “at least a fair prospect” that the Postal Service has the legal authority to eventually carry out rules like these — but that applying them to the 2026 election specifically would be “arbitrary and capricious,” because state and local election officials “do not have sufficient time to reasonably implement the rule before the elections.”

Read carefully, that is not a ruling that the Postal Service overstepped its authority. It is a ruling that the Postal Service overstepped the calendar. Kavanaugh’s vote was available to the administration in principle and withheld in practice, for a reason that has nothing to do with constitutional first principles and everything to do with fifty days, forty design analysts, and envelopes already printed in Madison and Weber County and King County. It is the most concise judicial acknowledgment yet of the argument this series has made since Part XLV: that the rule’s problem was never only that it was unconstitutional, but that it was, on this timeline, undoable.

Three Judges, Three Theories, One Destination

What makes Monday’s result more than a single data point is what it joins. By the time the Supreme Court ruled, two different district judges — appointed by two different presidents, reasoning from two different bodies of law — had already reached the same conclusion.

Judge Indira Talwani, an Obama appointee sitting in Boston, found that the rule likely violated the Elections Clause: that it stripped states of their constitutional authority to run their own elections, and that the administration’s argument that this was somehow not an “election rule” rang, in her words, hollow.

Judge Carl J. Nichols, a Trump appointee sitting in Washington, found something narrower and in its own way more damaging: that no federal statute anywhere gives the Postal Service the authority to build a voter-data collection system in the first place. As Zach Montague reported for The New York Times, Nichols wrote that “the Postal Service could issue (and enforce) the rule only if Congress gave it the authority to do so, but here no statute grants the Postal Service the power to issue key parts of the rule.” He noted the agency was on track to handle more than fifty million pieces of federal ballot mail this cycle, and that the plan created “an increased risk that a significant number of otherwise appropriate absentee or mail-in ballots will ultimately not be counted.”

And now Kavanaugh, writing for the Supreme Court’s majority, arrived by a third route entirely: not the Elections Clause, not statutory authority, but sheer operational impossibility inside the time remaining.

Three judges. Three legal theories — constitutional, statutory, and administrative-procedural. One destination. When that happens — when jurists who disagree about almost everything else, appointed by presidents who disagree about almost everything else, look at the same set of facts and arrive at the same place by different roads — it stops looking like partisan litigation and starts looking like what it is: a rule that could not survive contact with its own deadline, examined from every direction the law makes available and found wanting from each one.

Testing the Friction Thesis Against an Actual Loss

Part XLVIII argued that the administration had already won the mail-voting conversation regardless of how the emergency applications were eventually resolved — that ambient uncertainty, once created, does not evaporate on the strength of a ruling issued this late. That argument was written to survive either outcome. It is worth checking it now against the outcome that actually happened, because a clean 7-2 defeat is a different test than an indefinite delay would have been.

The uncertainty was real, and it was expensive, and it landed before Monday’s ruling could undo any of it. As Eileen Sullivan and Adam Sella reported for The New York Times, election officials in Madison, Wisconsin, mailed eighteen thousand ballots more than a week ahead of schedule simply to get them out the door before the rules could change again. Officials in Weber County, Utah, drew up plans for backup distribution centres to bypass the Postal Service entirely. Lisa Deeley, the Democratic vice chair of the Philadelphia City Commissioners, put it as bluntly as an election official is likely to put anything on the record: “We don’t even really have a way to prepare because we don’t know when that ruling is going to come down... It’s just chaos.” Seven Republican secretaries of state broke with the administration’s own coalition to file a brief warning the Court that implementing the rule now would almost certainly produce “mistakes, delays and confusion for both voters and election officials” — a rare instance of the administration’s own political allies telling the Supreme Court, in effect, that the emergency was manufactured rather than found. None of that chaos was hypothetical, and none of it un-happened when the gavel came down Monday evening.

But here is where the friction thesis needs a genuine amendment rather than a restatement. A rule that simply expires from delay leaves behind an ambiguous legacy — voters and officials are left to guess whether the government quietly gave up or is merely regrouping, and that ambiguity is itself useful to an administration that profits from doubt. A rule that loses 7-2, with the losing side unable to muster more than two votes and with one of the administration’s own appointees writing separately to explain exactly why the government was wrong on the timing, leaves behind something much harder to spin as unfinished business. Stuart Holmes, Washington State’s elections director, said as much within minutes of the ruling: he was “pleased and relieved” that vote-by-mail could continue as planned, because the state had built contingency plans it would have been “very costly to shift.” That is the sound of an official who no longer has to hedge.

So the honest accounting is this: the uncertainty did its work on voter behaviour in August and September, exactly as this series predicted, and that damage is sunk regardless of what the Court did next. But the manner of the loss matters more than Part XLVIII gave it credit for. A 7-2 defeat, joined by a Trump appointee on explicitly practical grounds, is a harder foundation to build a “the fight isn’t over” narrative on than a case that simply timed out. The Times’ own live coverage captured this tension without resolving it, noting that the ruling was “a resounding win for Democratic-led states and voting rights groups” even as “the weekslong legal tussle over the issue may have sown its own kind of confusion, a victory of sorts for Mr. Trump.” Both things are true. But they are not equally weighted, and Monday evening tilted the scale further toward the first than this series expected it to by this point in the year.

What the Ruling Does Not Settle

Kavanaugh’s “fair prospect” language is doing quiet, important work, and it should not be read past. The Court did not say the Postal Service lacks the underlying authority to regulate ballot mail design or request voter data from willing states. It said this specific rule, applied on this specific timeline, was arbitrary and capricious because officials could not reasonably comply in time. That is a ruling about September 2026, not about the general question of how much control the federal government can eventually exert over the mechanics of mail voting. The merits case — the slower-moving question of whether the executive order itself, and the statutory authority USPS claims under it, survive full briefing and argument — continues. It will likely stretch well past November, exactly as Part XLVIII anticipated, and its outcome will matter far more for 2028 than anything that happened this week matters for 2026.

There is also the matter of what twelve states did while this litigation played out. Missouri and eleven others joined the Postal Service in defending the rule in court and told the justices they would be able to comply if given the green light. Some of that compliance work — the printers, the portal integrations some counties began preparing — does not simply disappear because the Supreme Court ruled against implementation this cycle. The infrastructure argument that doomed the rule for 2026, that there was not enough time, is not an infrastructure argument that will still be true in 2028. Richard Hasen, the UCLA election law scholar quoted in the Sullivan and Sella reporting, noted that the government never demonstrated the rule would actually prevent fraud or that it could even be implemented — a two-part failure. The Court’s ruling addressed only the second half of that failure. The first half remains entirely unadjudicated, and it is the half a future administration, with more lead time and a friendlier calendar, could plausibly try again.

The Pattern, Revisited

Part XLV’s TACO framework — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — predicted a fourth act in which, having exhausted the tools of any given fight, the administration would seek a new battlefield rather than accept the loss as final. Nothing about Monday’s ruling forecloses that fourth act; if anything, the merits case is exactly the new battlefield the framework predicted, already scheduled, already underway, already positioned to outlast this news cycle entirely. What Monday’s ruling changes is the terrain on which that next act will be fought. The administration goes into the merits case having lost 7-2 on an emergency application joined by its own appointee, having been told by name, in writing, that its own rushed timeline was the problem. That is a weaker starting position than “the courts never got around to ruling” would have been, and it is worth the administration’s opponents noting that distinction the next time an emergency application lands on the Court’s docket with sixty days to an election.

For now, what actually happens in November is the simplest part of this story. The ballots that Alabama, Delaware, and North Carolina already mailed will be counted under the rules that existed before Executive Order 14399. The portal that never opened will stay closed for this cycle. The forty design analysts will not be needed. And millions of voters who spent August and September wondering whether their mail ballot would count will get an answer that, this time, the Supreme Court gave them directly rather than by default.
