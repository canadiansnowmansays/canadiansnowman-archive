An iconic image from Bhutan: Taktsang Monastery clinging to sheer rock face — vibrant prayer flags framing the misty Himalayan backdrop, a visual of peaceful orientation toward humanity and the planet.

This one is for you Ugyen.

Between the twin climaxes of this series — Norway at number one and the United States at number one — I want to pause for a nation that does not fit neatly onto either list. A nation that, depending on which parameters you examine, is either the most extraordinary contributor on the planet or one of the most troubling underperformers in South Asia.

Bhutan.

If you know Bhutan at all, you probably know two things: it measures Gross National Happiness instead of GDP, and it is the world’s only carbon-negative country. Both of these things are true. They are also radically incomplete.

Bhutan is a kingdom of fewer than 800,000 people tucked into the eastern Himalayas between India and China. It is breathtakingly beautiful, constitutionally committed to environmental preservation, and governed by a philosophy that places happiness, spirituality, and ecological sustainability at the centre of national purpose. It is, by its own framework, a model for what a nation could be.

It is also a nation that expelled roughly one-sixth of its own population in the early 1990s — ethnic Nepalis known as the Lhotshampa, forced from their homes in what human rights organisations have characterised as ethnic cleansing — and has refused to allow a single one of them to return for over three decades. Political prisoners from that era remain in jail today. The UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention has declared their detention illegal. Bhutan’s government has stonewalled every international appeal for their release.

This essay exists because the Contribution Index must be honest about nations that are simultaneously extraordinary and deeply flawed. Bhutan is both. And the tension between the two is, I believe, the most instructive case study this series has produced.

The Scorecard

The Analysis

Human Development: 25 out of 50

Bhutan’s human development tells a story of genuine progress from an extraordinarily low baseline — and a story of who gets counted in that progress.

Life expectancy has risen significantly, reaching approximately 72 years — impressive given that Bhutan was essentially a medieval society within living memory. Healthcare access has expanded through a public system that provides free basic services, but rural coverage remains limited and the infrastructure cannot yet match the ambition. The score of 5 reflects a nation that is climbing, but climbing from a valley.

Educational attainment earns a 5 for similar reasons. Literacy has improved dramatically — from under 20 percent a few decades ago to over 65 percent today — and school enrolment has expanded. But quality is uneven, particularly in rural areas, and tertiary education remains limited. Bhutan sends many of its best students abroad for university, which is both a pragmatic solution and an acknowledgment of domestic limitations.

Poverty reduction earns a 7 — the highest human development score on the card. Bhutan has made steady progress in reducing extreme poverty, and the government’s commitment to equitable development, guided by the GNH framework, has produced measurable improvements in material wellbeing across much of the population.

Democratic governance earns a 5. Bhutan transitioned from an absolute monarchy to a constitutional monarchy with an elected parliament in 2008 — a peaceful, voluntary transfer of power from king to parliament that was, in its way, as remarkable as Costa Rica’s military abolition. The fourth king, Jigme Singye Wangchuck, essentially forced democracy upon a population that had not asked for it, believing that the nation’s long-term stability required it.

But the democracy is young and constrained. Freedom House rates Bhutan as “partly free.” Press freedom has collapsed — from 33rd globally in 2022 to 152nd in 2025, a freefall that suggests the democratic opening is narrowing rather than widening. Political parties operate within limits. Civil society is nascent. And the security establishment retains influence that would be incompatible with full democratic governance.

Gender equality and minority rights earn a 3 — the lowest score on Bhutan’s card, and the one that demands the most honest reckoning.

The Lhotshampa: What Happiness Forgot

Lhotshampa refugees in Nepal. In the early 1990s, Bhutan expelled roughly one-sixth of its population — ethnic Nepalis — in what human rights groups described as ethnic cleansing. Over 100,000 fled. None have been allowed to return in more than three decades. Political prisoners from that era remain jailed. Happiness measured for some, not for all.

In the late 1980s, the Bhutanese government conducted a census that reclassified much of its ethnic Nepali population — the Lhotshampa, or “southerners” — as illegal immigrants. This was followed by a series of discriminatory policies: mandatory adoption of Drukpa cultural dress, the removal of Nepali as a language of instruction in schools, and citizenship laws that retroactively stripped nationality from people whose families had lived in Bhutan for generations.

When the Lhotshampa protested, the government responded with force. Security forces conducted campaigns of harassment, arrest, and violence in the southern districts. Homes were burned. Land was confiscated. Families were coerced into signing “voluntary departure” forms, sometimes at gunpoint, sometimes while being photographed smiling to create the appearance of willing emigration.

By 1996, over 100,000 Lhotshampa — approximately one-sixth of Bhutan’s total population — had fled or been expelled to refugee camps in eastern Nepal. To grasp the proportional scale: this would be equivalent to Canada expelling six million people, or the United States expelling 55 million.

The refugees spent decades in camps. Negotiations between Bhutan and Nepal went nowhere — Bhutan’s government denied that many of the refugees were Bhutanese at all. Beginning in 2007, a third-country resettlement programme began relocating refugees, primarily to the United States, Canada, Australia, and the United Kingdom. Over 113,000 have been resettled. None — not a single person — has been allowed to return to Bhutan.

As recently as 2024, the UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention declared the continued imprisonment of Lhotshampa political prisoners illegal under international law and called for their immediate release. At least 35 remain detained, some for over three decades. Bhutan’s government rejected the recommendation. At its 2024 Universal Periodic Review, Bhutan rejected 68 of 203 human rights recommendations, including ratifying the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights.

The land from which the Lhotshampa were expelled — fertile southern districts — has been redistributed to ethnic Drukpa settlers. Some of this land now falls within the footprint of the Gelephu Mindfulness City, a flagship development project of the current king.

This history does not erase Bhutan’s genuine environmental and philosophical achievements. But it must be held alongside them. A nation that measures Gross National Happiness while maintaining political prisoners from a campaign of ethnic expulsion is not merely imperfect. It is engaged in a form of national self-deception that the Contribution Index is designed to surface.

The Lhotshampa were Bhutanese. They were expelled for being the wrong ethnicity. They were never allowed to return. And the kingdom that expelled them asks the world to celebrate its happiness.

The score of 3 on gender equality and minority rights reflects this history. Some would argue it is generous.

Environmental Stewardship: 47 out of 50

Bhutan — the world’s only carbon-negative country. With over 72% forest cover protected by constitutional mandate, it absorbs more CO₂ than it emits. On environmental stewardship alone, Bhutan scores 47 out of 50 — the highest in the entire series.

And now for the other Bhutan.

Bhutan’s environmental scorecard is not merely strong. It is the highest of any nation assessed in the entire Contribution Index — higher than Costa Rica, higher than Iceland, higher than Norway. On environmental stewardship alone, Bhutan is the best-performing nation on the planet.

Renewable energy earns a 10. Bhutan generates virtually all of its electricity from hydroelectric power, harnessing its Himalayan rivers to produce clean energy that it not only uses domestically but exports to India — effectively reducing regional emissions beyond its own borders.

Emissions per capita earns a 10. Bhutan is the world’s first and only carbon-negative country. Its forests — covering over 72 percent of the national territory, against a constitutional mandate of at least 60 percent — absorb more than four times the carbon dioxide that Bhutan’s economy emits. The kingdom does not merely have a low carbon footprint. It has a negative one. It is actively cleaning the atmosphere.

To put this in context: the United States produces roughly 14 metric tons of CO2 per person per year. Bhutan’s net contribution is negative. The comparison is not between a good performer and a bad one. It is between a nation healing the planet and a nation damaging it.

Biodiversity and habitat protection earn a 10. Bhutan’s biological corridors — connecting protected areas to allow wildlife to move freely — are a model of ecological management. The country contains extraordinary Himalayan biodiversity, including endangered species like the snow leopard and the black-necked crane, and its protected areas are genuinely managed rather than existing as “paper parks.”

Deforestation earns a 9. Forest cover is increasing, not decreasing — the inverse of the global trend. Water and air quality earn an 8 — excellent by any standard, though some pressure exists from development and the growth of Thimphu as an urban centre.

The 47 out of 50 demands emphasis because of what it proves. Bhutan is not a wealthy nation. Its GDP per capita is approximately $3,500. It does not have the sovereign wealth funds of Norway, the technology sector of Estonia, or the institutional capacity of the Nordics. And yet it outperforms all of them on the dimension that may matter most to the long-term survival of the species.

If Costa Rica is the proof that environmental excellence does not require wealth, Bhutan is the proof that it does not require development at all. A nation that was essentially pre-industrial within living memory has achieved something that no industrialised nation has managed: a negative carbon footprint.

Bhutan’s Gross National Happiness framework — a genuine philosophical alternative to GDP that prioritizes wellbeing, culture, and ecology. It has produced extraordinary environmental results.

The GNH framework — whatever its limitations as a governance tool — deserves real credit here. By defining national success as something broader than economic growth, Bhutan created the philosophical space for policies that prioritise forests over factories, rivers over roads, and long-term ecological survival over short-term extraction. The fourth king’s radical proposition — that happiness matters more than GDP — produced, as a direct consequence, the most environmentally sustainable nation on Earth.

Systemic Behaviour: 23 out of 50

Arms exports earn a 9 — Bhutan has no arms industry and contributes nothing to global militarisation. It maintains a small army focused entirely on border defence.

But refugee and humanitarian contribution earns a 1 — and this is where the scorecard becomes excruciating. A nation that created over 100,000 refugees through ethnic expulsion, that has refused their return for over thirty years, that maintains political prisoners from that era, and that rejected international calls for accountability at its 2024 UPR cannot score higher than the minimum on this parameter. This is not about capacity. It is about a deliberate, sustained policy of exclusion that has caused immense human suffering and that the government continues to defend.

Adherence to international law earns a 4. The refugee refusal, the political prisoners, the rejection of ICCPR ratification, and the collapse of press freedom all count against Bhutan here. The nation cooperates with some international frameworks — particularly on environmental agreements — but its human rights posture is one of selective engagement at best and active obstruction at worst.

The Neighbour Test: 23 out of 50

Bhutan’s Neighbour Test presents a unique scoring challenge: a nation whose philosophical framework — Gross National Happiness — embodies the Neighbour Test’s values at a conceptual level, but whose actions on minority rights and political prisoners undercut those values in practice.

Multilateral orientation earns a 5. Bhutan participates modestly in international institutions — it is a UN member, cooperates on environmental agreements, and engages constructively within South Asian regional frameworks. But its international engagement is limited by size, geographic isolation, and dependence on India for security and economic partnership. The 5 reflects genuine but constrained participation.

Response to global crises earns a 4. Bhutan lacks the resources to contribute significantly to international humanitarian or crisis response. The score reflects capacity limitations rather than dispositional failure — but it is still a 4.

Treatment of allies earns a 5. Bhutan’s primary relationship — with India — is one of deep dependency rather than partnership. Its relationship with Nepal has been poisoned for decades by the Lhotshampa refugee crisis. The 5 reflects a nation that does not actively harm its partners but also does not contribute to regional cooperation at scale.

Consistency of values versus interests earns a 3. And here is where the GNH framework meets its harshest test. A nation that measures happiness while maintaining political prisoners for over thirty years, that promotes cultural preservation while having expelled a sixth of its population for belonging to the wrong culture, that teaches environmental mindfulness while refusing to acknowledge the Lhotshampa’s right to return — the inconsistency is not peripheral. It is foundational. GNH measures the happiness of those who remain. It does not measure the suffering of those who were forced to leave.

Bhutan’s philanthropic landscape is modest and largely state-aligned. The Tarayana Foundation, established by the Queen Mother, focuses on rural poverty alleviation. The Loden Foundation supports entrepreneurship. International support, particularly the “Bhutan for Life” programme backed by WWF and private donors, provides long-term environmental funding. But philanthropic activity that might challenge government policy — on, say, the Lhotshampa issue — operates within narrow constraints. In a kingdom where happiness is officially measured, unhappiness is unofficially managed.

Long-term global stewardship earns a 6 — the highest score on Bhutan’s Neighbour Test, reflecting the constitutional mandate to maintain 60 percent forest cover and the genuine intergenerational thinking embedded in the GNH framework. On environmental stewardship for future generations, Bhutan is extraordinary. On human rights stewardship — the obligation to protect the rights and wellbeing of all its people, including those it expelled — the record is catastrophic. The 6 averages an environmental 10 with a human rights 2, and the tension is irreconcilable.

The GNH Question

A note on Gross National Happiness and the “happiest country” reputation.

Bhutan is frequently described as the happiest country in the world. This claim requires qualification. The GNH is Bhutan’s own governance framework — a set of policy priorities developed internally, measuring psychological wellbeing, cultural preservation, ecological sustainability, and good governance. It is not an international ranking.

The World Happiness Report, compiled by the UN Sustainable Development Solutions Network, which does rank nations by surveyed happiness, places Bhutan quite modestly — well below the Scandinavian countries and not in the top twenty. Finland, Denmark, and Iceland consistently rank highest.

The distinction matters because the GNH is a philosophy of governance, not a measurement of citizen wellbeing. It tells us what Bhutan’s government prioritises. It does not tell us that Bhutanese citizens are, in fact, the happiest people on Earth. And it certainly does not account for the happiness — or the misery — of the 100,000-plus former citizens who were expelled and never permitted to return.

Gross National Happiness is a beautiful idea. It has produced extraordinary environmental outcomes. But as a measure of national virtue, it has a hundred thousand asterisks.

The Editorial Conclusion

Bhutan scores 118 out of 200. It does not fit on either the top performer or underperformer list — and that is precisely why it demanded its own essay.

On environmental stewardship, Bhutan is not merely the best nation in this series. It may be the best nation on Earth. A score of 47 out of 50 — achieved by a country with a GDP per capita of $3,500 — is the most powerful single argument this series has produced for the proposition that contribution is a choice, not a consequence of wealth. Bhutan chose its forests. It chose its rivers. It chose a constitutional commitment to ecological sustainability that no wealthy nation has matched.

On human rights, Bhutan carries a wound that three decades have not healed and that its government refuses to acknowledge. The Lhotshampa expulsion was ethnic cleansing. The political prisoners are prisoners of conscience. The refusal to allow return is a sustained injustice. And the collapse of press freedom suggests that the democratic opening of 2008 may be narrowing rather than widening.

The Contribution Index holds both truths simultaneously, because that is what honest assessment requires.

Bhutan is a nation that chose to measure happiness instead of wealth — and then expelled a hundred thousand of its own people for being the wrong ethnicity. It is a nation that maintains the only carbon-negative economy on Earth — and maintains political prisoners who have been in jail for over thirty years. It is a nation that has done more for the planet’s forests than any country in history — and that confiscated the land of its own citizens and gave it to settlers of the preferred ethnic group.

The scorecard captures this: 47 out of 50 on environmental stewardship, 25 out of 50 on human development, 23 out of 50 on systemic behaviour, 23 out of 50 on the Neighbour Test. The extraordinary and the inexcusable, coexisting in a kingdom of fewer than 800,000 people in the shadow of the Himalayas.

If there is a lesson in Bhutan’s scorecard, it is this: environmental virtue is not moral completeness. A nation can be the best ecological citizen on Earth and simultaneously fail its own people in ways that are profound, deliberate, and ongoing. The carbon is negative. The human cost is not.

Costa Rica abolished its army and chose contribution. Norway invested its oil wealth and chose contribution. Both of them did so without expelling a sixth of their population.

Bhutan chose the forests. It has not yet chosen all of its people.

Until it does, the happiness remains incomplete.
