The United States Institute of Peace building on the National Mall in Washington, D.C. — a structure built for the pursuit of peace, later gutted and renamed.

There is a building on Massachusetts Avenue in Washington, a few blocks from the State Department, that opened in 2011 after years of planning and construction and the kind of institutional optimism that attends the opening of a building whose purpose is, explicitly and without irony, the pursuit of peace.

The United States Institute of Peace was created by Congress in 1984, in the final years of the Cold War, as a nonpartisan institution dedicated to conflict prevention, mediation, and the research and practice of peaceful resolution of international disputes. It was not a think tank in the ordinary sense — it was a congressionally chartered independent organization, with a bipartisan board that included the Secretary of Defense, the Secretary of State, and the President of the National Defense University. It worked in conflict zones. It trained mediators. It produced research used by diplomats and military commanders. It was, in the dry vocabulary of its mandate, useful.

Its building was more than useful. Designed by the Washington firm of Hartman-Cox and completed in 2011 at a cost of approximately $180 million — funded, it should be noted, through a combination of congressional appropriations and private donations — the structure sits on the National Mall adjacent to the Lincoln Memorial and the Vietnam Veterans Memorial, at an address whose symbolic weight is not accidental. The building is made of limestone and glass. It is curved, open, full of natural light. It looks, in photographs and in person, like a place designed for people who believe that difficult problems can be solved through careful thought and sustained conversation.

In February 2025, the Trump administration issued an executive order to dismantle it.

Aerial view of the USIP building — open, light-filled architecture symbolizing dialogue and resolution, now bearing a very different name.

The dismantling proceeded with the efficiency that the Department of Government Efficiency — DOGE — brought to all of its early targets. Staff were fired. The president of the institution, a career diplomat named George Moose who had served under presidents of both parties, was removed from the building. The removal was not a letter of dismissal delivered through the customary channels. It was conducted with the assistance of the FBI, the U.S. Attorney’s Office for the District of Columbia, and the Metropolitan Police Department of Washington.

The president of a congressionally chartered institution dedicated to the peaceful resolution of conflict was escorted from his office by federal law enforcement. One pauses on this detail not for rhetorical effect but because it is the kind of detail that, once noticed, is difficult to set aside.

It is also worth pausing on what was actually being dismantled, because the abstract language of “think tank” and “independent agency” and “congressional charter” can obscure what the Institute of Peace actually did with the $50 million a year that the administration’s White House spokesperson would later dismiss as having been blown on delivering “no peace.”

The Institute of Peace had staff embedded in Afghanistan during the peace negotiations of 2019 and 2020, working on the frameworks that American and Taliban negotiators were attempting to build. It had programmes in Iraq that trained judges and lawyers in conflict resolution. It had worked in Colombia, in Myanmar, in the eastern Democratic Republic of Congo — in precisely the African conflict zone where, some months after the DOGE takeover, Trump would host a signing ceremony at the renamed building and present it as evidence of his deal-making genius. Its researchers had spent careers accumulating the contextual knowledge, the local relationships, and the institutional memory that serious conflict resolution requires and that cannot be reconstituted from a standing start when the next crisis arrives.

That expertise walked out of the building — some of it escorted by police — in the first months of 2025. The building remained. The name changed. The work stopped.

This is the human dimension of the Destroy It, Name It pattern that the institutional analysis can sometimes obscure. The Institute of Peace was not a bureaucratic abstraction. It was people — diplomats, researchers, mediators, regional specialists — doing work that was slow and unglamorous and occasionally effective. When DOGE cleared the building, it did not transfer the work to another agency. It ended it. The name on the wall cannot do what the people who left the building were doing. Names do not mediate conflicts. Names do not train judges. Names do not sit across a table from parties who want to kill each other and find language that allows both of them to stop.

The legal challenge came quickly. Former USIP leadership and staff filed suit arguing what seemed, on the face of the 1984 legislation, to be a straightforward case: the Institute of Peace was an independent nongovernmental organization established by Congress, and as such it was not subject to executive branch control. The president could not simply dismantle an institution that Congress had created, any more than he could dismantle the Supreme Court by executive order.

A federal judge agreed. The takeover, the court found, was illegal. The government had no authority to seize the building, fire the staff, or remove the leadership of an institution that existed by act of Congress and operated outside the executive branch.

The government appealed. The enforcement of the lower court’s ruling was stayed pending the appeal, which meant that the administration continued to control the building while the litigation proceeded. “A stay is not permission for the loser of a case to hijack the property of the winning party,” said George Foote, counsel for the former USIP leadership. It was a legally precise observation. It did not change the situation on the ground, where DOGE was in control of the building, almost all of the staff were gone, and the institution’s budget had been zeroed out in the administration’s fiscal year proposals.

And then, in December 2025, with the litigation still active and the lower court’s finding of illegality still on the books and the appeal still unresolved, the Trump administration renamed the building.

The renamed building — “Donald J. Trump United States Institute of Peace” inscribed on the institution that had been dismantled.

The new name, announced by the State Department on December 3, 2025, in preparation for a signing ceremony for a peace agreement between Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of Congo, was the Donald J. Trump United States Institute of Peace.

The State Department’s announcement explained that the renaming was intended “to reflect the greatest dealmaker in our nation’s history.”

A former official of the institution, who had worked there before the DOGE takeover and the firing of nearly all the staff and the police-assisted removal of the president, offered a response that requires no editorial enhancement: “It’s pretty ironic that he put his name on an institution he destroyed.”

Ironic is one word for it. There are others.

The sequence of events is worth stating in the plainest possible terms, without the softening that context sometimes provides, because the plainness is the point. The administration issued an executive order to dismantle a congressionally created institution. DOGE fired the staff. Federal law enforcement removed the president from his office. A federal judge found the takeover illegal. The government appealed, preventing enforcement of the ruling. And then, while the institution lay gutted, its staff gone, its budget eliminated, its president removed by police, its legal status in active litigation — the name went on the wall.

The name went on the wall of the institution he had destroyed.

Not on a new institution he had built. Not on a programme he had created or funded or championed. On the rubble of the one he had taken apart, while the courts were still arguing about whether he had the right to take it apart, while the people who had worked there were still fighting to get it back.

The name went on the wall.

Trump’s name on the Institute of Peace — a monument to the pattern of destroying first, then claiming the shell.

In the taxonomy of naming acts that this series has been examining, the Institute of Peace represents a category distinct from all the others.

The licensed towers — Toronto, Vancouver, the American properties — were commercial transactions. The name was a product. The product could be priced and sold and eventually bought out. We examined this in Parts II, III, and IV.

The Kennedy Center, the arch, the ballroom — these are monument projects. The name is being inscribed on public institutions and public spaces as an act of permanent self-commemoration. We examined the constitutional and democratic dimensions of this in Part V.

The Institute of Peace is something else. It is the naming of a ruin. It is the inscription of a man’s name on the wreckage of a thing he has destroyed, while the courts argue about whether the destruction was legal, while the people whose work he ended are still fighting to restore it.

There is a psychological literature on this specific behaviour — on the impulse to claim ownership of things by destroying them, to assert dominance through negation, to write your name on something by first rendering it incapable of protesting the inscription. It appears in the clinical literature on narcissistic personality organization, where it is sometimes described as the annihilation of the other as a form of self-assertion. If the institution exists independently — if it has its own history, its own mandate, its own staff and leadership and purpose — then it exists outside the control of the namer, and things that exist outside the namer’s control are a kind of reproach. The solution is to remove the independence and then claim the shell.

The shell is now the Donald J. Trump United States Institute of Peace.

Congress created the Institute of Peace in 1984 to work toward the peaceful resolution of conflict. The current administration has zeroed out its budget, fired its staff, removed its leadership with police assistance, and put a president’s name on the building.

The name is on the wall.

The peace work is not being done.

There is a further dimension to the Institute of Peace story that requires examination, because it connects the naming project to a broader pattern that has been visible throughout this series but has not yet been stated directly.

The renaming of the Institute of Peace was announced two days before a signing ceremony at which Trump hosted the presidents of Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of Congo to formalize a peace agreement between their countries. The ceremony was, by any account, a genuine diplomatic achievement — a conflict that had claimed millions of lives over decades was moving, however tentatively, toward resolution, and the United States had played a role in facilitating that movement.

The achievement was real. The question is what the naming of the building had to do with it.

The answer, examined carefully, is: very little. The peace agreement was the product of years of diplomatic work by career officials, regional mediators, and the patient accumulation of pressure and incentive that serious conflict resolution requires. The Trump administration had provided political support and, to its credit, sustained attention to a conflict that previous administrations had sometimes allowed to drift. This is worth acknowledging. Diplomacy is not a partisan activity and genuine achievements deserve recognition regardless of who is in office when they occur.

But the Institute of Peace — the institution whose building now bears Trump’s name — had been gutted before the ceremony. The staff who worked on African conflict resolution, the researchers who understood the regional dynamics, the mediators who had spent careers building the relationships that make agreements possible — they were gone. Fired by DOGE. The institution whose expertise was most relevant to the achievement being celebrated at its renamed headquarters had been dismantled before the celebration.

The name was on the building. The institution was not in the building.

This is not incidental. It is the clearest possible illustration of the difference between commemoration and achievement — between writing your name on a thing and actually doing the thing. The monument project, in its most degenerate form, does not require the achievement. It requires only the surface on which to write the name.

The symbolic weight of institutional dismantling — a visual metaphor for the “Destroy It, Name It” approach applied to a congressionally chartered body dedicated to peace.

Congressman Steve Cohen of Tennessee, speaking from the House floor after the renaming was announced, asked a question that deserves to be recorded here.

“Will his ego stop staining this city?”

It is a rhetorical question, and Congressman Cohen knew it was rhetorical when he asked it. But it contains within it a genuine inquiry about the nature of what is happening in Washington — not just to the Institute of Peace, not just to the Kennedy Center, not just to the currency and the arch and the ballroom and the parks passes and the warship — but to the relationship between a democratic republic and the man who currently leads it.

Democratic republics have a specific and carefully considered relationship to self-commemoration by their leaders. It is, as the historian Jeffrey Engel noted, considered gauche in the Western tradition for a living leader to name things after himself. It is considered more than gauche — it is considered a category error, a confusion between the leader and the state, between the temporary occupant of an office and the permanent institutions of the republic. The monuments are for the republic. The leaders serve the republic. When the leaders begin inscribing their names on the republic’s monuments, the category error has become a political statement.

George Washington understood this without being told. The founders understood it as a principle. Two hundred and fifty years of American democratic practice understood it as a norm — imperfect, sometimes honored in the breach, but consistently understood as the expectation.

The Institute of Peace is what happens when that understanding is set aside.

Not in the heat of an emergency. Not under the pressure of a genuine national crisis. In the careful, deliberate, step-by-step project of a man who looked at a congressionally created institution dedicated to peace, decided he wanted it gone, had it dismantled by his efficiency department and its president removed by police, won the appeal that prevented the courts from stopping him, zeroed out its budget, fired its staff — and then put his name on the building for a photo opportunity.

The name is on the wall.

The peace work is not being done.

And somewhere in the architecture of the republic, something that was built over two hundred and fifty years and was understood, until recently, to be load-bearing, is carrying a weight it was not designed to hold.

Donald Trump has spent a lifetime writing his name on things. Towers. Golf courses. Hotels. A university. A Bible. A perfume called Success. A peace institute he gutted before the paint on the letters dried. A performing arts centre dedicated to a murdered president he has spent his entire career trying to impress.

The name is always the same: gold where possible, large where mandatory, permanent where permitted, and imposed where neither permission nor permanence was ever his to grant.

This series is about what that compulsion reveals — psychologically, politically, historically, and occasionally comically. It is not a flattering portrait.

Then again, neither is a loading dock on Bay Street.
