In 1984, George Orwell gave the totalitarian state four ministries, and — this being Orwell — named each one for the precise opposite of what it did.

The Ministry of Peace waged war. Not to win it, but to keep it going forever, because a nation permanently at war is a nation permanently frightened, permanently rationed, and permanently grateful to whoever promises to protect it.

The Ministry of Plenty managed scarcity. Its task was to announce that the chocolate ration had been generously raised to twenty grams in the very week it was cut to twenty — and to see that the citizens cheered the news.

The Ministry of Love kept order, through the secret police and the torture rooms and the long, patient work of making a man not merely obey Big Brother but love him.

And the Ministry of Truth manufactured lies — the propaganda, the staged news, and, in the part most people forget, the rewriting of the past. Winston Smith’s actual job, eight hours a day, was not writing. It was forgetting. He revised old figures to agree with new claims, dropped the originals down a memory hole, and unpersoned the dead until no surviving record proved they had lived. The publication he corrected was called The Times. The work he did there was erasure.

Four ministries. I have spent the better part of a year writing about three of them without always calling them by name — the perpetual undeclared wars, the affordability crisis announced as a triumph, the machinery of retribution and enforced loyalty. And I write a whole series, The Ministry of Truth, about the fourth.

But the longer I have watched this administration work, the more I have come to think Orwell folded two jobs into one. The Ministry of Truth told the lies. Yet the erasing of the past — the memory hole, the vanished records, the unpersoned dead — was a distinct and, in a sense, prior operation. You cannot install a comfortable falsehood until you have removed the inconvenient truth it replaces. The forgetting comes first. It clears the ground.

In 2026, that clearing operation has grown too large to keep filing under another ministry’s letterhead. It has its own staff now, its own euphemisms, its own steady output. The plaque pried off the wall at Independence Hall. The caption stripped from a portrait at the Smithsonian. The sign about slavery, or climate, or the people who were here first, removed from a national park. The dataset deleted from a government website overnight. The name drilled onto a memorial that Congress dedicated to someone else — and, when a witness remembers too much, the witness sent quietly home.

So I respectfully suggest that Orwell had this in mind as well; he simply left it inside the Ministry of Truth, where the lying took top billing and the forgetting did its work in the back. I am not proposing a ministry he missed. I am giving a name to the one he buried. Orwell drew the map in 1948. I am only labelling the roads that have been built since.

This series will track that labour of forgetting wherever it reaches the custodians of shared memory — the museums, the parks, the monuments, the archives, the honours, the names, and the human witnesses who hold an institution’s past in their heads. It is a companion to two series I already write: The Ministry of Truth, which follows the control of information, and The Name Game, which follows this administration’s compulsion to write the president’s name onto the surfaces of the republic. Where The Name Game watches what gets written on, this series watches what gets rubbed out.

The other three ministries are still out there, and I expect I will get to them in time. But the forgetting was always the first job — the one that cleared the ground for all the rest — and so it is where this begins.

Orwell gave us the principle. It belongs at the head of everything that follows:

“Who controls the past controls the future; who controls the present controls the past.”
