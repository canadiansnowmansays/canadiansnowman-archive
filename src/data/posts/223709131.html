This series has spent more than a hundred parts treating AI risk largely on its own terms, without asking how it should be weighed against the other existential threat humanity was already taking seriously before this series began. This entry asks that question directly, at the exact moment both crises are competing for the same finite share of attention at the same UN gathering this series’ own Part 111 already covered.

Why a hypothetical risk can outrank a proven one

As The New York Times’ Chico Harlan reported on September 22, 2026, the case for treating AI as the graver threat rests entirely on the asymmetry of the worst case rather than on any claim that AI risk is more likely or more immediate. Toby Ord, an Oxford researcher and author of “The Precipice,” makes the comparison explicit: “In terms of things that could be the end of humanity, I think A.I. is a bigger risk” — a judgment that has nothing to do with probability and everything to do with the fact that climate change’s worst plausible outcomes, however severe, generally presuppose a surviving civilization to suffer them, while AI’s worst-case scenarios, on the account this series has documented since Part 93, do not. Ord put numbers on the asymmetry in the book itself, published in 2020: he estimated the chance of an existential catastrophe from unaligned artificial intelligence over the next century at about one in ten, higher than all his other sources of existential risk combined, against roughly one in a thousand for climate change, and the total risk from all sources at one in six. Those are one researcher’s judgements, not measurements, and he presents them as such. But they make plain that his ranking rests on severity multiplied by a guess, which is exactly the kind of figure Stokes’s argument below asks readers to weigh against harm that can already be counted. Seth Baum of the Global Catastrophic Risk Institute supplies the mechanism for how a gradual AI catastrophe could unfold without a single dramatic trigger: “We continue to automate more and more over time. And we end up with something that is fully automated. And something goes wrong” — a slow, cumulative loss of human oversight this series’ own account of RSI in Part 103 and the “situationally aware” model behaviour Daniel Selsam flagged in that same essay would recognise as already underway in miniature.

The counterargument, made by someone who has spent a career on the other threat

Leah Stokes, an environmental politics professor at UC Santa Barbara, supplies the rebuttal this essay needs to take seriously rather than wave past: AI is “eating up all our attention” at the expense of a threat that is, in her words, not hypothetical at all. “Unlike A.I., climate change’s existential threat is not hypothetical. It is killing people now,” she wrote, pointing to the deadly European heat waves of summer 2026 and a glacier collapse in Nepal as concrete, already-realized harm rather than a modelled future scenario. Her complaint isn’t that AI risk is fake — it’s that a proven, ongoing catastrophe is losing ground in public attention to a speculative one, purely because the speculative one comes with a more dramatic ceiling. That is a genuine tension this series has not previously had occasion to examine, since its own coverage has treated AI risk as the subject rather than as one competitor among several for a fixed pool of political and institutional bandwidth.

Katherine Richardson, a University of Copenhagen professor studying planetary boundaries, complicates Stokes’s framing from within climate science itself, in language that converges with this series’ own AI vocabulary almost word for word: nearly all of human civilizational progress has occurred within a narrow temperature band, and “we do not know for certain that we can thrive” outside it. Her closing line — “we can lose control of this process” — is the same phrase this series has used to describe RSI, the Hugging Face incident, and the kill-switch debate in Part 108, applied instead to the climate system. That convergence suggests the two crises may share more structural DNA than either camp’s advocates acknowledge: both stem from systems humans built and are no longer fully able to predict, and both now attract warnings from insiders using nearly identical language about the risk of losing control entirely.

Where the two threats actually intersect, rather than merely compete

The comparison this essay has built so far treats AI and climate as separate risks competing for attention. Harlan’s reporting shows they’re also directly, materially entangled — AI is making the climate problem measurably worse in the short term regardless of its long-term potential to help solve it. Electricity consumption from AI data centers surged 50 percent in 2025, per the International Energy Agency, and the UN University’s water and health institute estimates that by 2030, AI data centers will consume nearly three times the electricity used by Nigeria, Pakistan, and Bangladesh combined — three countries home to roughly 650 million people. The agency’s baseline is worth having alongside that projection. In its “Energy and AI” report, the IEA found that data centres of all kinds used about 415 terawatt-hours in 2024, around 1.5 percent of the world’s electricity, and projected that figure would “more than double to around 945 TWh by 2030”; it estimated that emissions from data centres’ electricity use would grow from 180 million tonnes to 300 million tonnes by 2035 in its base case, and noted that the largest AI-focused data centres then under construction would each consume as much electricity as two million households. That figure sharpens this series’ own Capex Ledger coverage of data-center backlash in material terms rather than purely political ones: the recall elections and moratorium bills this series has tracked since Capex Ledger Part IX aren’t only about property values and local grid strain, but about a genuinely enormous and rapidly growing global energy claim. Simon Stiell, the UN’s climate chief, called the industry “energy guzzling” and said it needs to “respect science and start aligning with global climate efforts — urgently”; Turkish environment minister Murat Kurum, president of COP31, the UN climate conference scheduled to run in Antalya from November 9 to 20, 2026, had already added AI’s “growing energy consumption” to that summit’s agenda by the time of Harlan’s reporting.

The corporate whiplash Harlan documents is its own small case study in how quickly institutional priorities can invert when a more urgent-feeling crisis displaces an older one. Google’s own carbon-neutral pledge, once framed by CEO Sundar Pichai as a “sustainability moonshot” tied to flooding in his own hometown, has given way to rising emissions as the AI race reorders corporate priorities; Microsoft’s carbon-negative promise faces the same pressure. Bill Gates supplies the most vivid individual version of that reordering: in October 2025 he published a 5,000-word essay arguing climate change would “not lead to humanity’s demise,” a conclusion that drew real criticism from climate scientists; in 2026 he published an equally lengthy essay warning specifically about AI risk instead — the same rhetorical energy redirected from one existential threat to a newer one, in roughly the span of twelve months.

What this means for how this series should read its own subject going forward

Trump’s own framing, delivered on social media and quoted directly in Harlan’s reporting, treats the comparison itself as evidence that both warnings are equally dismissible: AI risk warnings come from the “same people that said, just a short time ago, that the World would be extinguished by ‘Climate Change.’” That line collapses a genuine, difficult question — how should finite political attention be allocated across two systemic risks with very different evidence bases — into a rhetorical trick that requires believing neither risk merits serious response. This series has no reason to accept that framing. Ord’s case for AI as the graver risk and Stokes’s case for climate as the more proven one aren’t actually incompatible positions; they’re measuring different things — worst-case severity against demonstrated present harm — and a serious accounting of either crisis, this essay would argue, requires holding both measurements in view simultaneously rather than letting one crowd the other out of the conversation entirely, which is precisely the dynamic Stokes is warning is already happening.
