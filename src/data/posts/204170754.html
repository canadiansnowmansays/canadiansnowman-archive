Erik Brynjolfsson is, by any reasonable measure, the single most credible optimist this series could find on the question of whether AI will be good for ordinary people. He predicted the current moment in 2014, years before ChatGPT existed, when most serious economists thought productivity growth had permanently stalled. He runs the Stanford lab that produces the empirical data — not speculation, not investor decks, actual measured firm-level outcomes — that other academics now build their own work on top of. Dario Amodei and Demis Hassabis eat at his table. Senators call him. He has no product to sell, no IPO pending, no political office to win.

And when Annie Lowrey, writing in The Atlantic, asked him directly what happens next, his answer was not reassurance. “We’re going to be scrambling with chaos,” he said, “and I don’t know how we will handle it.” Asked about the most extreme version of the downside case, he allowed — joking, Lowrey notes, but only somewhat — that a sufficiently powerful AI “could do whatever it wanted, I mean, including get rid of us all.” Could, he was careful to specify. Not would.

This series has spent fifty-nine parts examining warnings from people with reasons to be heard skeptically: industry CEOs with products to sell, politicians with coalitions to hold together, critics with ideological priors. Brynjolfsson has none of those reasons. He is, on the contrary, the person in this entire series with the strongest professional and intellectual investment in AI optimism being correct. His version of the warning deserves to be weighed differently than the others, precisely because it costs him more to give it.

I. What the Optimist Actually Believes

It would misrepresent Brynjolfsson to treat him as a closet doomer performing reluctant honesty for a reporter. His underlying worldview remains genuinely optimistic, built on a specific technical argument that deserves to be stated on its own terms.

AI, in his account, is combinatorial, digital, and exponential in ways that distinguish it from prior technologies — improvements in machine learning compound improvements in chip speed; digital improvements propagate at near-zero cost; and unlike most technologies, AI progress sets up further AI progress rather than running into diminishing returns. Productivity growth, he believes, will follow the same pattern this combination produced before: slow, invisible accumulation for years, then a rapid, perceptible takeoff once the underlying base is large enough. He coined the term “productivity paradox” decades ago precisely to describe this lag — the personal computer took roughly twelve years, by his own account of one composite office worker’s adoption timeline, to move from arriving on a desk to actually improving a firm’s bottom line.

His own empirical research, conducted with Danielle Li and Lindsey Raymond, found generative AI boosting customer-service workers’ output by as much as 30 percent — a real, measured, substantial gain, not a hypothetical one. He does not believe mass technological unemployment is the likely outcome, on the historical pattern that new capacity tends to create new tasks rather than simply eliminating old ones. This is a serious, evidence-based position, held by someone whose career has been built on getting exactly this kind of prediction right before anyone else did.

What makes his position different from the industry’s standard optimism is what he pairs it with: an explicit, unhedged acknowledgment that the same technology could concentrate power and wealth to a degree no prior technology made possible, and that society is not currently moving fast enough to prevent that outcome. The optimism is about capability. The warning is about distribution. He does not treat these as in tension with each other, because they aren’t — a technology can be enormously productive and catastrophically distributed at the same time, and Brynjolfsson’s actual position is that this is the likeliest outcome absent deliberate intervention.

II. The Bet

The clearest empirical test of Brynjolfsson’s optimism is a literal wager, and the fact that it exists at all says something about how contested this question remains even among the economists best equipped to study it.

In 2020, Brynjolfsson made a friendly $400 bet with Robert Gordon of Northwestern, the economist whose own bestselling book had argued that the era of transformative growth was permanently over. The terms: if average annual labor productivity growth over the following decade came in below 1.8 percent, Gordon wins. Above that, Brynjolfsson wins. As of Lowrey’s reporting, Brynjolfsson is ahead — by a few tenths of a percentage point.

Gordon’s response to losing is more analytically interesting than a simple concession would have been. He divides American workers into three categories: industrial workers, people who could work from home whether they do or not, and people who must show up in person. “By far,” he told Lowrey, “the biggest adoption of AI has been in the working-from-home category” — accountants, writers, analysts. Yet, by his accounting, all of the actual productivity improvement of the past eight years has occurred outside that category, among baristas and roofers, the workers with the least AI exposure. Accountants, in his account, have gotten less efficient since ChatGPT arrived, not more.

Gordon’s second point is the more structurally important one for this series. Even granting that AI eventually makes white-collar workers measurably more productive, the national productivity statistic may not move, because of a composition effect: a Google engineer generates something like $215 of labor value per hour; a waiter generates something like $27. If AI causes high-productivity firms to shed workers while low-productivity firms absorb them, the aggregate number can stay flat or fall even while individual workers are, in a narrow sense, more “productive” at their displaced tasks. This is the same warning Part 47 raised about the apprenticeship pipeline, restated in the vocabulary of labor economics: a technology can make remaining workers in a sector more capable while simultaneously destroying the sector’s capacity to train, employ, and advance the next cohort — and the net effect on a country’s actual economic trajectory can be negative even as every individual deployment looks, locally, like a productivity win.

The Stanford-ADP “canaries dashboard” that Brynjolfsson’s own lab helped build supplies the most concrete evidence available that this composition effect is already operating. Workers in their early twenties, the dashboard shows, are already in a measurable hiring recession attributable to AI adoption. This is not a future risk Brynjolfsson is speculating about. It is a current condition his own research infrastructure is currently measuring, in real time, among exactly the population whose career trajectories this series flagged as most exposed in Part 47’s treatment of the missing apprentices.

III. The Argument That Changes Everything Else

Buried in Lowrey’s profile is a single observation from Brynjolfsson that deserves to become part of this series’ permanent analytical vocabulary, the way TACO and the reverse centaur have. It explains, more precisely than anything this series has previously stated, why AI poses a categorically different concentration risk than any prior technology.

“Human skills are inherently decentralized,” Brynjolfsson said. “No matter how smart Bill Gates is, he can’t make all the decisions in Microsoft.” This is, on reflection, a structural fact about every human organization that has ever existed, regardless of how much wealth or authority concentrated at the top: a single human brain has finite attention, finite working memory, finite hours in a day. Even the most centralized human tyranny in history required thousands of human subordinates to actually execute its will, and every one of those subordinates retained some irreducible margin of independent judgment, interpretation, error, and occasionally refusal. This margin is not a flaw in human hierarchy. It is the thing that has made total concentration of control structurally impossible for the entire span of human civilization, regardless of how concentrated wealth or formal authority became.

“But if AI is performing thinking tasks and executing on them,” Brynjolfsson continued, “this limitation disappears.” An AI system does not have Bill Gates’s cognitive bandwidth constraint. It can, in principle, execute a single owner’s will across an arbitrarily large number of simultaneous decisions, with no subordinate layer of independent human judgment standing between the instruction and its execution at scale. This is the precise mechanism by which Piketty’s oligarchy thesis, examined in Part 50 through Doctorow’s reverse centaur framework, acquires a genuinely new technological enabler. Every prior oligarchy in history was bounded by the decentralizing friction of needing human beings to carry out its instructions. AI is, for the first time, a plausible technology for removing that friction entirely.

This is worth stating as starkly as Brynjolfsson’s own framing permits: the thing that has limited the practical reach of concentrated power throughout all of recorded history was never law, and was never morality. It was the simple fact that power needed minds other than its own to act, and those other minds always retained some capacity to slow it down, misunderstand it, or say no. AI is the first technology that credibly threatens to remove that capacity from the equation. Brynjolfsson’s own policy conclusion — that society needs to act now, before this dynamic compounds, because “a lot of people could be made worse off” — is, on this reading, not a generic expression of concern. It is a precise warning about the disappearance of the one structural check that has constrained concentrated power for the entirety of human history.

IV. The Genie Nobody Will Let Out

Lowrey’s reporting on Brynjolfsson generated an unusually substantive set of public responses, and one observation from that discussion deserves inclusion here because it sharpens a question this series has not yet stated precisely enough.

One reader, identified only as 1cjcarpenter, raised a scenario worth sitting with directly: given a well-specified objective like “equal representation under the law,” today’s AI could very plausibly draw congressional districts that satisfy that objective in a demonstrable, auditable way — better, on available evidence, than the partisan gerrymandering processes currently used in most states. “At the same time,” the commenter continued, “I feel pretty certain that vested political interests would never allow AI to do any such thing.” The broader point: “lawyers will not give up their control of the law, doctors will not give up control of medicine, and politicians will not give up control of politics,” regardless of whether an AI system could outperform them on whatever metric the profession claims to value most. “The question,” the commenter concluded, “is whether the lawyers, doctors, politicians and so on can keep the genie in its bottle. But I think we can be sure they will try — if the technology is allowed to become a real threat.”

This is a genuinely useful inversion of the entire concentration-of-power argument this series has built across fifty-seven parts. The dominant fear this series has documented is that AI will be deployed by the powerful to concentrate power further. The 1cjcarpenter scenario describes the opposite failure mode operating simultaneously: AI being deliberately kept out of domains where it could plausibly reduce existing concentrated control — districting, diagnosis, legal interpretation — specifically because the professions and institutions that currently hold that control have no incentive to relinquish it, however demonstrably better the AI’s outputs might be by the institution’s own stated standards. Both dynamics — concentration through AI, and the suppression of AI’s decentralizing potential by entrenched interests — can be true at once, and a series focused only on the first risks missing the second entirely.

V. The Chaos He Cannot Resolve

Brynjolfsson’s own honesty about his limits is, in the end, the most useful thing his profile offers this series. Asked what should be done about the risks he has spent the interview describing, he was direct about where his expertise ends: “The politics that can help get us there are beyond his remit,” Lowrey writes. “Policy isn’t his area of expertise.” His own contribution, as he frames it, is building the body of empirical knowledge and convening the people — academics, executives, students, senators — who might use it to act.

This is an honest answer, and it is also, read against the rest of this series, an unintentional argument for exactly the kind of statutory, democratically accountable governance process this series has spent forty essays calling for and watching fail to materialize. The single most credible empirical voice on AI’s actual effects on workers has concluded that the politics required to handle the chaos he is documenting lies outside what his own discipline can deliver. Measurement alone, however rigorous, does not produce governance. It only produces, at best, a more accurate description of the gap — which is exactly what Brynjolfsson’s “canaries dashboard,” his customer-service productivity study, and his own admission of not knowing how we will handle the coming chaos collectively provide. The data keeps arriving. The governance keeps not arriving with it. That asymmetry, restated now by the field’s most credentialed and least conflicted optimist, is the entire argument of this series, said in someone else’s words.

“The saddest aspect of life right now is that science gathers knowledge faster than society gathers wisdom.” — Isaac Asimov
