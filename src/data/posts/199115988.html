Eleven weeks ago, the President of the United States posted four words on Truth Social: “UNCONDITIONAL SURRENDER!”

The White House issued a clarifying statement hours later, explaining that even if no one in Tehran formally submitted, the president would declare when Iran had essentially given up — or, as he put it, cried “uncle.”

On Sunday, the same president posted that he had “informed my representatives not to rush into a deal” and that “our relationship with Iran is becoming a much more professional and productive one.”

Between those two statements: 37 days of relentless airstrikes. Thousands killed. The Strait of Hormuz closed for nearly three months. The global economy destabilised. Gas at $4.55 a gallon. Brent crude above $100 for twelve consecutive weeks. 1,200 Patriot interceptors fired at $4 million each against $35,000 drones. A jailbreak that failed. A regime that survived. A brotherhood that held. Both chambers of Congress cracking. And a deal that, if it holds, restores the status quo to roughly where it was on February 28th — the day the war began.

David Sanger of the New York Times framed it with the precision that eleven weeks of chaos deserves: “All that does is begin to restore the status quo to roughly where it was on Feb. 28, when Mr. Trump and Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu of Israel launched a war to finally bring Iran’s nuclear and missile programs to an end. So far, they have failed to achieve those goals.”

Aaron David Miller, a former Mideast negotiator now at the Carnegie Endowment, was blunter: “This is what happens when a poorly conceived war of choice turned into a highly flawed ‘peace’ of necessity. Original, unrealizable war aims abandoned, and now little leverage to secure what really matters.”

David Frum, writing in The Atlantic on Saturday, delivered the autopsy: “Trump started the February 28 war for reasons of personality, not strategy. He is on his way to losing the war for the same reasons of personality.” Frum noted the cruelest irony: Trump already fought and won a successful 12-day war against Iran in June 2025. If he had quit while ahead, he could have banked those gains. Instead he went back in February — and lost everything.

The full Truth Social post, May 24, 2026. "THE EXACT OPPOSITE, in fact!" Eleven weeks earlier: "UNCONDITIONAL SURRENDER!" The distance between those two posts is the war.

Here is what the deal appears to contain. The word “appears” is doing more work than any word in this essay, because the United States and Iran are describing different documents.

A senior U.S. official, briefing reporters on Sunday (New York Times), said the two sides had agreed in principle to reopen the Strait of Hormuz and to a commitment from Iran to dispose of its stockpile of highly enriched uranium — 970 pounds enriched to 60 percent, close to bomb grade. The mechanism for disposal would be negotiated in a subsequent round of talks. The deal had not been signed. Final approval by Trump and Iran’s supreme leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, could take days.

Three senior Iranian officials told the Times something different. They said the memorandum of understanding would stop fighting on all fronts, including Lebanon. It would reopen the strait without tolls. It would lift the U.S. naval blockade. And it would release $25 billion in frozen Iranian assets. As for the nuclear programme: the memorandum said nothing about it. Nuclear matters would be negotiated within 30 to 60 days.

The Strait of Hormuz has been a focal point since the start of the conflict.Credit...Reuters

The U.S. says Iran agreed to give up its uranium. Iran says the deal doesn’t mention the nuclear programme. Both statements were made anonymously, by officials not authorised to speak publicly, about a document neither side has released.

The deal does not address Iran’s missile stockpile. It does not stipulate a moratorium on enrichment. It does not limit Iran’s proxy networks. It does not require Hezbollah to disarm. It does not compel Israel to withdraw from southern Lebanon. Those issues — every one of which was cited as a reason for launching the war — would be addressed in future negotiations, the U.S. official said, without a fixed timeline.

Rubio, speaking in New Delhi on Sunday, conceded the point with a sentence that should be carved into the war’s tombstone: “You can’t do a nuclear thing in 72 hours on the back of a napkin” (New York Times).

No. You cannot. Which is why the Obama administration spent nearly two years negotiating a 160-page agreement that suspended most of Iran’s nuclear activity — and under which Iran shipped 97 percent of its enriched stockpile to Russia.

Without a war. Without closing the strait. Without killing thousands. Without $4.55 gas. Without 1,200 Patriots. Without a jailbreak. Without a single congressional vote.

Trump launched a war to get what Obama got with a pen.

Iran knows this. And Iran is not being quiet about it.

Esmail Baghaei, the foreign ministry spokesman, posted an image on social media of a famous relief from an archaeological site in Iran — a Roman emperor bowing in submission to a Sassanid king (New York Times). “In the Roman mind, Rome was the undisputed center of the world,” he wrote. “The Iranians shattered that illusion.”

The relief of Shapur I at Naqsh-e Rostam — the image Iran's foreign ministry spokesman posted as the deal was announced. In 260 AD, the Sassanid king captured the Roman Emperor Valerian and carved the submission into the cliff face for eternity. "In the Roman mind, Rome was the undisputed center of the world," Baghaei wrote. "The Iranians shattered that illusion.

The deputy foreign minister said Iran was “seeking peace with strength, pursuing diplomacy with dignity.” General Ghalibaf, who led the negotiations, warned in an audio address that Iran’s military had been rebuilt during the ceasefire: “We will make the enemy regret any renewed aggression against Iran.”

At a mass wedding ceremony in Tehran, couples rode on military vehicles decorated with flowers and declared that their brides’ dowries would be drones. On state television, a news anchor picked up a machine gun and fired it into a flag of the United Arab Emirates with live bullets.

“You can enter a war with the United States and you will not be completely eliminated,” said Mohammad Ali Shabani, an Iran analyst. “In fact, you could resist. You can resist and impose so much damage on the world economy, so much disruption, that you can compel them to make a deal with you.”

Ellie Geranmayeh of the European Council on Foreign Relations was more measured but no less devastating: “They are now coming out with a stronger geopolitical hand than before given their dominance in the Strait of Hormuz. They also demonstrated that Trump’s nuclear dilemma with Iran won’t be resolved through military might.”

Mojtaba Khamenei, the new supreme leader — injured on the first day of the war, in hiding ever since to avoid assassination — authorised Ghalibaf to make decisions about the agreement and informed the generals how much Iran was willing to concede. A supreme leader the war was supposed to have toppled is now approving the terms of the deal from an undisclosed location.

In Jerusalem, the silence was deafening.

Netanyahu said nothing for 18 hours after Trump’s announcement (New York Times). When he finally spoke, his statement was defensive: he and Trump agreed that Iran could never have nuclear weapons. Israel would maintain freedom of action on all fronts, including Lebanon.

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu of Israel at a ceremony on the eve of Israel’s day of remembrance for fallen soldiers in Jerusalem in April.Credit...Pool photo by Marc Israel Sellem

The analysts were less diplomatic. Michael Herzog, former Israeli ambassador to Washington: “The general sense in Israel was that the outlines of the deal so far represented an insufficient translation of the American and Israeli achievements in the war.” Assaf Orion, a retired Israeli brigadier general: “It’s clear that the United States doesn’t want to escalate. Leverage over Iran would shrink, and that doesn’t augur well for what is to come.”

Netanyahu had said at the outset that the war was “designed to remove the existential threats” to Israel — Iran’s nuclear programme, its ballistic missiles, its proxy networks. The deal as described addresses none of them. Iran keeps its missiles. Hezbollah retains its capacity to fight. Israel is not a party to the talks.

And one of Israel’s deepest fears, as Herzog put it, is that the deal “would not tie Iran’s hands in supporting its proxies, but would tie Israel’s hands in fighting those proxies.”

Opening the strait was never an Israeli war goal. It was Iran’s card, and Iran played it. Israel went to war to end the nuclear threat. What it got was a memorandum of understanding about shipping.

Trump’s own party told him so.

Roger Wicker, chairman of the Senate Armed Services Committee: “The rumored 60-day ceasefire — with the belief that Iran will ever engage in good faith — would be a disaster. Everything accomplished by Operation Epic Fury would be for naught!”

Lindsey Graham: “It makes one wonder why the war started to begin with.”

Thom Tillis: Hegseth assured Congress eleven weeks ago that “they had obliterated Iran’s defenses. Now we’re talking about a posture where we may accept the nuclear material remaining in Iran? How does that make sense at all?”

Ted Cruz: “a disastrous mistake.”

Mike Pompeo, Trump’s first-term secretary of state, was equally dismissive — prompting Steven Cheung, the White House communications director, to declare on social media that Pompeo “should shut his stupid mouth and leave the real work to the professionals.”

Trump fired back at all of them: “Nobody has seen it, or knows what it is. It isn’t even fully negotiated yet. So don’t listen to the losers, who are critical about something they know nothing about.”

Even Michael Flynn, the disgraced former national security adviser, posted a warning: “I know you want to get out of this mess. Give it some thought.” When your own first national security adviser is publicly counselling you to think before acting, the mess is no longer deniable.

The Democrats were no kinder. Cory Booker: “He’s being played as a fool.” Chris Van Hollen: “a return to the prewar status quo.” Ro Khanna: “We would have avoided this war, food prices wouldn’t have gone up, gas prices wouldn’t have gone up.”

Khanna’s sentence is the one that will endure. It is the simplest formulation of the war’s cost-benefit analysis. Everything that the deal proposes to achieve — an open strait, a nuclear negotiation, a ceasefire — existed on February 27th. The war destroyed it. The deal restores it. The distance between February 27th and this memorandum of understanding is measured in thousands of dead, hundreds of billions in economic damage, and a region that now knows the United States can be fought to a standstill.

Tom Nichols of The Atlantic offered the epitaph: “He didn’t know what he was doing when he began it, and now he doesn’t know how to get out of it.” Nichols noted that Trump’s worried sycophants probably understand that the details of an eventual agreement do not matter much at this point. The war has already ended with America’s strategic defeat. “How much Iran will get away with, and how much humiliation the United States will endure, has yet to be ironed out by the negotiators.”

CIA Director John Ratcliffe, Frum reported, told Trump before the war that the prediction of regime collapse was “farcical.” Trump launched the war anyway. Rubio, whom Frum noted Trump had tapped to double as both secretary of state and de facto national security adviser — dooming both jobs not to be done — is now explaining from New Delhi that “you can’t do a nuclear thing in 72 hours on the back of a napkin.” One wonders when that observation first occurred to him.

And yet. The deal is not nothing.

If Iran genuinely disposes of its 970 pounds of near-bomb-grade uranium — ships it to Russia, dilutes it, converts it — that is a meaningful reduction in the immediate nuclear threat. It is what the Obama deal achieved in 2015, but achieved again after Iran had accumulated new stockpiles in the years since Trump withdrew from that agreement in 2018.

The problem is that the mechanism remains unresolved. The timeline is undefined. The 11 tons of lower-enriched uranium are not addressed. The enrichment infrastructure is intact. The centrifuges continue to spin. And the leverage the United States holds — the blockade, the threat of resumed strikes — diminishes every day the strait is open and the oil is flowing.

“No dust, no dollars,” the U.S. official told reporters. Iran gets access to its frozen assets only after a final nuclear deal. That is the incentive structure designed to keep Tehran at the table. But the Iranians have been at negotiating tables before. They sat with Obama for two years. They sat with the IAEA for decades. They are patient in ways that American electoral cycles do not permit. The midterms are in November. The 30 to 60 days of nuclear talks will unfold against a political clock that Iran understands better than anyone.

Rubio said it plainly: “If it doesn’t deliver what we want in 60 days, the president has every option available to him then that he has now.”

Every option except the one that matters: the credibility to threaten force against a country that just proved it could absorb 37 days of American and Israeli strikes and come out the other side dictating terms.

One woman in Tehran captured what the war looked like from the inside.

“We were trying to figure out if we should leave Tehran if bombs fall again, and buying water and batteries,” said Nazanin, 56, an engineer, asking to be identified by only her first name. “I gave a big sigh of relief.”

She is not a general. She is not a strategist. She is not projecting victory or conceding defeat. She is a woman who spent the week buying batteries in case her city was bombed, and who sighed with relief when she learned it might not be.

That is the human residue of a war launched to achieve objectives that a pen achieved in 2015.

Meanwhile, in Gibraltar, the Royal Navy was loading ammunition onto the R.F.A. Lyme Bay and preparing autonomous mine-clearing drones for deployment to the Strait of Hormuz (New York Times). Even if the deal holds — even if the strait “reopens” — it must first be swept. Iran has seabed mines that use magnetic, acoustic, and light sensors. The International Energy Agency estimates a minimum of two to three months to re-establish steady export operations. A Royal Navy commander, asked how many mines Iran has, answered: “How long is a piece of string?”

Between 1,500 and 2,000 ships carrying 20,000 seafarers remain stranded, waiting for safe passage. On Monday, Brent crude fell 6 percent to $94.50 — markets pricing in a deal. Gas dipped to $4.51. But oil remains 30 percent above prewar levels, and analysts warned the supply disruptions will be “prolonged.” The deal opens the door. The mines are still on the floor.

And the toll question is not dead. Baghaei said Monday: “We are not seeking to collect tolls.” Then added: “The services provided and the protection of the environment require the collection of fees.” Last week, Rubio said tolls would render any deal “unfeasible.” Iran is still building the architecture of control, even as the memorandum is being drafted.

And in Lebanon, Israeli strikes continued through the weekend. More than 3,100 people have been killed since March. Over 120 medics dead. Hospitals bombed. Newborns evacuated. Hezbollah’s leader, Naim Qassem, welcomed the potential deal as proof of Iran’s victory — “Iran has managed to humiliate America” — then called on the Lebanese people to take to the streets and bring down their own government. The deal says fighting stops “on all fronts, including Lebanon.” But Netanyahu told Trump that Israel maintains freedom of action on all fronts — including Lebanon. Those two statements cannot both be true. One of them is the deal. The other is the war that continues regardless.
