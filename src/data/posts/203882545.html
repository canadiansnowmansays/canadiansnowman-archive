Carly cried at Waiting for Godot in July, alone in the dark, and wrote about it in her journal but not the Beacon. She was not ready. Three months later she was ready. Norman said: twelve hundred words. Thursday.

Carly Meadows cried at Waiting for Godot in July.

She was alone in the theatre, in a seat she had paid for herself because Norman had not assigned her to review it — he had sent the regular arts correspondent, who had filed a competent eight-hundred-word piece noting the production’s formal rigour and the quality of the lead performances, which Norman had run on page eight. Carly had bought a ticket for the Saturday matinee because she had been at the Beacon for ten months at that point and had covered the Easter egg hunt and the Bieber statue meeting and the tariff address at Rotary Hall and the paddle boat incident and had begun to understand, dimly, that Elsinore was not just the subject of what she was writing but the instrument through which she was learning what writing was.

She had not expected Waiting for Godot to be what it was.

She sat in the dark and watched two men wait for someone who did not come, in a language of such precision and such hopelessness and such persistent small comedy that she felt, by the second act, that she was watching something that had always been true and had simply been waiting for her to be the right age to see it. She was twenty-four. She was, she thought, exactly the right age.

She cried in the way that people cry at things that are not sad — at things that are true in a way that is larger than comfort or consolation. She cried quietly, in the dark, with her notebook on her knee and her pen in her hand, and she did not write anything in the notebook until the lights came up, at which point she wrote one sentence and closed the notebook and went home.

She did not file a piece. Norman had not asked for one. She was not ready.

She was ready in October.

She did not know she was ready until she was in Norman’s office for a different reason entirely — she was filing the final details of the Community Advisory mechanism story, which she had covered from Jim’s application through council approval — and Norman had asked, without looking up from the copy he was reading, whether she had ever written about Waiting for Godot.

She said she had not.

Norman said the production closed on Sunday.

She said she knew.

Norman said the final performance was Saturday night. He said he thought someone should write about what Waiting for Godot meant in Elsinore in 2026, not as a review but as something else, and that he had been trying to determine who should write it and had concluded it should be her.

He said: “You’ve seen it.”

She said she had.

He said: “You have something to say about it.”

It was not a question.

She said yes.

He said: “Twelve hundred words. Thursday.”

She filed it Wednesday afternoon. Twelve hundred and forty words. He ran all of them.

The piece did not follow the conventions of a theatre review. It did not evaluate the performances or the staging or the production design. It described what it felt like to be twenty-four years old and living in Elsinore in 2026 and watching two men wait for someone who was not coming, and to recognise in that waiting something that was not specific to Beckett or to theatre but was specific to the moment — the quality of waiting for things to resolve that had not resolved, for institutions to hold that were not quite holding, for something to arrive that was taking longer than anyone had anticipated.

She wrote about sitting in the dark alone in July and understanding that the comedy in Beckett was not the opposite of the seriousness but the same thing seen from inside the situation rather than outside it. She wrote about covering the Easter egg hunt and the paddle boat incident and the election and the heritage minutes and about how Elsinore had taught her that small things were not small — that a butter tart coupon placed carefully in a park by a volunteer in a grey cardigan was a statement about what fairness meant, and that a Swan Warden on the river at five forty-five every morning was a statement about what showing up meant, and that a filing cabinet behind an editor’s desk was a statement about what trust meant.

She wrote about Vladimír and Estragon in their bowler hats and their boots that didn’t fit and their certainty that Godot would come and their uncertainty about what Godot was and about whether certainty and uncertainty were even different things when you were waiting in the right company.

She wrote about the farmer’s one line per sketch. She wrote about it without naming the sketch or the farmer. She wrote: there are people in every community who speak once per occasion and the once is always the truest thing said, and Waiting for Godot understands this completely, because Beckett understood that the man who speaks least often is not the man who has the least to say.

She did not mention the south. She did not need to. The waiting was there. Anyone reading it in 2026 would know what waiting they were doing.

She ended with the July sentence she had written in the notebook when the lights came up. She had not told anyone about it. She had kept it in the notebook for three months. She put it in the piece and it was the last sentence and it was the right last sentence and she knew it was and Norman knew it was and he did not change a word.

The response to the piece was unlike anything Carly had published in the Beacon.

Norman received fourteen letters to the editor in the week following publication — more than he had received for any single piece in the previous three years. He published eight of them. Six were from Elsinore residents who had seen the production and felt Carly had articulated something they had not been able to articulate themselves. One was from a retired English teacher in Stratford who said it was the finest piece of local arts writing she had read in twenty years of reading local papers. One was from the Festival’s Artistic Director.

Alistair’s letter was four sentences long. It said that he had programmed Waiting for Godot because he believed theatre’s function was to put language around the things people were experiencing but could not yet name, and that Carly’s piece had demonstrated this function more precisely than anything he could have written himself. He said that he hoped she would continue writing. He said that Elsinore was lucky to have her. He said that Norman was lucky to have her too.

Norman published the letter on page two, which was the highest position he had given a letter in eleven years. He did not tell Carly about the letter until the day it ran. He texted her at seven in the morning: Page two today.

She read it at the kitchen table with her coffee. She read it twice. She put her phone down. She looked out the window at Ontario Street, which was not yet awake.

She picked up her notebook. She wrote one sentence.

She got up and went to work.

Norman published Alistair’s letter on page two — the highest position he had given a letter in eleven years. He texted Carly at seven in the morning. She read it at her kitchen table and wrote one sentence in her notebook. Then she went to work.

Gerald read the piece on Sunday morning at his kitchen table. He read it twice. He removed his reading glasses. He put them back on. He cut the piece from the paper — he still received the paper, in print, because someone should — and filed it in a folder he labelled Beacon — Arts Coverage — Notable Pieces — Meadows, C.

He had already started the folder, after her piece about the Constable Brash situation and before her piece about the Community Advisory mechanism. The Godot piece was the third item in it.

He did not send Carly a note. He had not been asked to send a note. He filed the piece and went about his day.

In the afternoon he looked in on the folder again. He thought about what Carly had written about the farmer’s one line per sketch — about the man who speaks least being the man who has the most to say.

He noted, in his personal records: Meadows, C. — developing well. Folder growing.

He filed the note.

Constance Hay had attended the Saturday night final performance.

She had attended every final performance of every Festival season for fifty years. She sat in her seat in the second row, left of centre, and watched Waiting for Godot with the focused attention of a woman who has been watching theatre for half a century and has developed the capacity to hold the play and what the play is about simultaneously without collapsing the distance between them.

She had read Carly’s piece on Friday morning, before the final performance. She had read it with the expression she reserved for things that said something true without oversaying it.

After the performance she went home. She opened her notebook. She wrote one sentence about the play, which she has been doing for fifty years, one sentence per production, a record of what the play said to her on the night she saw it.

Then she wrote a second sentence, which she does not usually do.

The second sentence was about Carly.

She has not shared either sentence. She will not share them until she is ready, which may not be while anyone is watching, because Constance Hay has always understood that the most important things you write are the ones you write only for the record, which is to say for yourself and for the future and for no one who is currently in the room.

A Note for the Record

Waiting for Godot was first performed in Paris in 1953. It has been performed in theatres, prison yards, bombed cities, refugee camps, and small Ontario festival theatres for seventy years. It has been performed in places where the waiting it described was literal, where the people watching it were waiting for things they genuinely did not know were coming or not coming.

In 2026, a twenty-four-year-old reporter in Elsinore, Ontario, sat alone in the dark and recognised in two men in bowler hats something she was experiencing and had not yet named. She wrote about it three months later in twelve hundred and forty words that said what needed saying without saying more than needed saying.

To the south, the cultural institutions that produce and support the kind of work Carly watched and wrote about have been under pressure that is not primarily financial. It is pressure about what culture is for — whether it serves the state or serves the people, whether it asks questions or provides answers, whether the waiting and the uncertainty and the small comedy of two men who cannot leave are permitted or whether the theatre must have a different kind of ending, a cleaner one, an ending in which Godot arrives on schedule and everyone is satisfied and nothing is left unresolved.

In Elsinore, Godot did not arrive. The final performance closed on Sunday. Alistair’s farewell season is nearly complete. The marquee still reads THIS ROUGH MAGIC — 2026 SEASON.

Carly is at the Beacon. She is writing. She is getting better in ways she is only beginning to understand.

Norman knows. He has not told her. He has filed the piece.

In Elsinore, this is the same thing.
