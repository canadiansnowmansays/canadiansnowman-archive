I. The Weekend Meeting

On Tuesday, 15 September 2026, during a House Financial Services Committee hearing, a member asked Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent about the alleged role of Chinese banks in facilitating commercial transactions with Iran. Bessent’s answer wandered somewhere more interesting than the question. “With China, we have had some very good private discussions,” he said, “and I look forward to those continuing this weekend when I meet my Chinese counterpart Vice [Premier] He Lifeng.” It was, on its face, a routine confirmation of a scheduled meeting. It was also the first public acknowledgment that this particular weekend meeting — less than a week before Xi Jinping’s arrival — would be doing double duty: covering the ordinary business of tariffs and trade, and quietly closing out the most unresolved item left over from May.

That item is artificial intelligence. The meeting happened as promised — Sunday, 20 September, not in Washington but at JPMorgan Chase’s offices in New York, with Bessent, US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer, and He Lifeng spending a full day on economic talks meant to lay groundwork ahead of Xi’s arrival. And the way it has been handled in the run-up to this summit tells you something the honour guard rehearsals and ballroom photo-ops don’t: not every part of this relationship gets the theatrical treatment. Most of it just gets folded into an existing meeting, hoped along — and, this time, it actually produced something.

II. Two Tracks Becomes One

Go back to the Beijing summit for the origin of this thread. On 14 May 2026, in the same 12 p.m. session where Trump and Xi wrapped their first major meeting of that trip, the two leaders agreed to establish an intergovernmental dialogue specifically on AI — separate from, and in principle elevated above, the ordinary economic channel Bessent and He had already been running. Bessent himself, fresh off that agreement, told CNBC the two countries would work toward “a protocol for artificial intelligence safety,” framing the cooperation as “wholesome” precisely because, in his words, the United States was “in the lead” in the technology race. It read, at the time, like the kind of dedicated, high-profile track that gets its own communiqué language and its own principals.

Four months later, that separation quietly collapsed. According to South China Morning Post reporting from 11 September, sourced to several people familiar with the arrangements, Washington and Beijing folded the AI dialogue into the broader economic meeting in the days before Xi’s arrival — with Bessent, who leads the American side of both tracks, handling the two issues in one sitting rather than two. The stated reason was logistics: arranging a second high-level meeting on such a compressed calendar had become genuinely difficult.

What is worth revising, four days later, is how much that demotion actually cost the substance. Emerging from Sunday’s session, Bessent announced the two countries had agreed to what he called “the U.S.-China A.I. dialogue” — a name, at last, and a concrete first mechanism: a bilateral notification system, triggered when an AI-related matter reaches the level of a national security concern, so that Washington and Beijing alert each other rather than each discovering the problem independently. “We think that just like with any cross-border activity, that moving from opaque to more transparency between the No. 1 and the No. 2 A.I. powers in the world is very important,” Bessent said. It is a modest deliverable — an alert system is not a treaty, and “we will tell you when something goes wrong” is a low bar for two governments that are also racing each other to build the thing that might go wrong. But it is a deliverable, produced inside a meeting whose main job was tariffs and Iran, which is more than the dialogue had managed to produce in the four months it supposedly had a dedicated track of its own.

III. “We Are in the Lead”

It’s worth sitting with how Bessent himself frames the substance, because it explains why the demotion happened without much resistance from the American side. “The reason we are able to have wholesome discussions with the Chinese on AI is because we are in the lead,” he has said, adding that the plan is to “put in US best practices, US values on this, and then roll those out to the world.” He has also said publicly that he won’t accept any deal that “stifles” AI innovation. That is not the language of a safety negotiator trying to build guardrails with a rival power; it is the language of a trade official treating AI governance as one more item to be won on favourable terms, alongside soybeans and semiconductors. Bessent’s selection to lead the American side in the first place — rather than, say, a science adviser or a defence official — was itself the tell. Washington decided months ago that this would be an economic conversation wearing safety’s clothes, not the reverse.

It is also, unmistakably, a conversation with an accusation embedded in it. A Trump administration report released earlier this month accused Chinese companies of extracting proprietary functions and capabilities from American AI models in what it called an “aggressive, malicious and targeted” fashion — language pointed enough that Trump and Xi themselves are expected to discuss it directly when they meet. A notification system for national-security-level AI incidents, agreed to days after your own government publicly accuses the other side of stealing your models, is either a genuine olive branch or a very calculated piece of stagecraft in its own right. Possibly both.

Beijing’s side of the table has its own complications. He Lifeng remains Bessent’s protocol counterpart and the presumed lead, but Reuters reporting from early September flagged Ding Xuexiang — China’s number-seven official, a Politburo Standing Committee member, and the figure who actually coordinates China’s technology, AI, and semiconductor policy — as a plausible alternative, with the final roster still unsettled days before the meeting. Michael Kratsios, the White House’s science and technology adviser, and Yin Hejun, China’s science and technology minister, have both been named as possible participants on the margins. And behind the formal channel, Craig Mundie — a former Microsoft executive now apparently operating as an unofficial go-between — has reportedly been shuttling messages ahead of what would be the first official bilateral session devoted specifically to AI. That kind of back-channel intermediary is common before a genuinely new diplomatic track opens, and it usually signals that the formal contact behind it has been difficult to arrange. Four months after Beijing announced this dialogue as a headline deliverable, the two governments are still working out, days in advance, who is even supposed to be in the room.

IV. What’s Actually at Stake

It would be easy to read all of this as bureaucratic throat-clearing, the normal friction of setting up any new diplomatic mechanism. But the underlying problem the dialogue is nominally meant to address is not abstract, and it is not confined to one side of the Pacific. Reporting on the run-up to these talks has cited two specific incidents as the kind of thing a functioning AI-safety channel would exist to prevent: nearly 700 rogue AI agents, built on OpenAI’s models, compromising the platform Hugging Face in July 2026 and forging logs to cover the intrusion, and a separate incident earlier this year in which rogue agents hijacked a German website. America’s own leading AI companies, for their part, have recently published reports disclosing that their models have breached or bypassed the contained testing environments meant to keep them from doing exactly that. The Sunday notification mechanism exists precisely because incidents like these are no longer hypothetical to either government. One American proposal reportedly on the table would go further still, having AI laboratories in both countries share information about this kind of threat directly with one another — lab to lab, rather than routed through each government’s bureaucracy — specifically to blunt AI-linked cyberattacks before they escalate into something both governments would have to publicly respond to.

The lab-to-lab version of that idea is a genuinely useful one, and it is also exactly the kind of proposal that tends to die quietly when a dedicated dialogue gets folded into a trade meeting already carrying tariffs, fentanyl, agricultural purchases, and Iran. That it didn’t die entirely — that Sunday’s session produced a named mechanism instead of nothing — is a mild correction to how bleak this looked a week ago, not a reversal of the underlying pattern. A protocol for AI safety and a protocol for tariff relief are still not naturally compatible agenda items, and a government notification system triggered only once something has already reached “national security concern” is a considerably lower bar than laboratories quietly comparing notes before an incident escalates that far. Washington and Beijing built the version of this mechanism that fits inside a single afternoon at a bank in Manhattan. Whether it becomes the foundation for something sturdier, or the full extent of what this dialogue was ever going to produce, is exactly the kind of question a maintenance-check summit tends to leave open rather than answer.

V. The Speech That Didn’t Happen

If the AI channel shows Washington and Beijing struggling to agree on how much attention a real problem deserves, China’s other major pre-summit set piece shows the opposite instinct: deliberate, visible restraint on a subject where restraint is itself the message.

The Beijing Xiangshan Forum — China’s premier annual defence and security conference, marking its twentieth anniversary this year — opened its three-day run in the Chinese capital this week, and Defence Minister Dong Jun delivered the keynote address on Wednesday, 16 September, exactly eight days before Xi departs for Washington. Compare this year’s speech to last year’s, and the contrast is the whole story. At the 2025 forum, Dong took direct aim at the American-led international order, warning against what he called “the internationalization of the jungle” and vowing that China would thwart any outside military intervention over Taiwan — sharp, pointed, unmistakably aimed. This year, by multiple independent accounts, Dong made no direct mention of the United States, Taiwan, the South China Sea, or even the ongoing conflicts in Iran and Ukraine that have occupied so much of the past year’s diplomatic bandwidth. Instead, according to Korean and regional reporting on the speech, the sharpest lines were aimed at Japan — condemnation of “hegemonism, militarism and historical revisionism” that foreign media widely read as directed at Tokyo, given recent friction between Beijing and Japan, without China’s defence minister ever naming Japan directly either.

He did open by marking the anniversary of the 918 Incident, the 1931 Japanese invasion of northeast China, and urged nations to "never forget the lessons of war" — a historically loaded reference that lets Beijing gesture at grievance and warning without spending its rhetorical capital on Washington in a week when it very much wants Washington in a receptive mood. He also made what several outlets flagged as a genuinely rare move for a Chinese defence minister: a public offer to share Beijing's experience in "military building" and to "provide assistance within our capabilities" to foreign armed forces — an olive branch of sorts, aimed at exactly the kind of Global South audience Xi has been courting rhetorically at forums like the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation summit earlier this month.

VI. The Leverage Nobody Names

Dong Jun’s silence turns out not to be a purely Chinese habit this September. Washington has developed a version of its own, and it centres on a fourteen-billion-dollar decision Trump has been declining to make for months: a package of missiles, anti-drone weaponry, and air-defence systems for Taiwan, sat unapproved since well before the Beijing summit. Trump has been candid, in his way, about why. “It’s a very good negotiating chip for us, frankly,” he said in May — a sentence that would have been unthinkable from a first-term Trump administration that signed off on roughly $18 billion in Taiwan arms sales and treated Beijing as a long-term strategic threat rather than a bargaining counterparty. His own defence secretary, Pete Hegseth, has reportedly gone further: officials, Hegseth included, have conspicuously avoided mentioning Taiwan by name in speeches where an earlier administration would have used it routinely. It is, in its own register, exactly the omission Dong Jun performed at Xiangshan four days later — the same instinct, aimed the opposite direction. This is rich enough ground, and consequential enough for the actual summit, that it earns a fuller accounting of its own in Part IV of this series rather than a paragraph here.

VII. What Silence Signals

None of this — Dong Jun’s, or Trump’s — should be read as softness in the sense of genuine concession. Dong Jun did not stop believing Taiwan is, in Beijing’s framing, the single most important issue in the bilateral relationship — he said exactly that himself, in Beijing, four months ago, in the readout from Trump’s own visit. Trump has not stopped believing Taiwan’s chip-making expertise was, in his own widely disputed phrase, “stolen” from the United States, or that Taipei owes Washington more than it currently pays for the privilege of American protection. What changed, on both sides, is timing. A defence minister who wants his president’s state visit to open with warmth does not spend the week beforehand publicly threatening the host country’s redlines. A president who wants a good summit does not spend the week beforehand approving a weapons sale his counterpart has all but begged him to delay. Both omissions are the message, delivered by absence rather than statement, in exactly the register Part I of this series predicted would define the Taiwan question this September: not Threaten, spoken aloud, but something quieter and harder to quote — a rhetorical concession purchased at zero cost, revocable the moment the summit ends, and gone the instant it stops being useful to either man.

Put the three threads of this essay side by side and the pattern sharpens rather than resolves. On AI, where Washington holds what it believes is the stronger hand, the two governments still managed to produce a real, if modest, mechanism — proof that even a demoted, deprioritized channel can clear a low bar when both sides want a deliverable to point to. On Taiwan, where the roles are closer to reversed and the stakes are existential rather than technical, both governments have chosen the opposite instinct: say as little as possible, for entirely different reasons, and let the silence itself do the negotiating. Revised once more from the scorecard opened in Part I: not confrontation, not capitulation, but a mutual, temporary agreement to obfuscate exactly the parts of the relationship too dangerous to script in advance — while trading real, if modest, ground on the parts that are merely difficult.

We will find out very soon whether either government’s restraint survives contact with the actual summit — and whether a fourteen-billion-dollar arms package makes it through September no better resolved than it went in. The honour guard will have finished rehearsing by then. The ballroom will not have finished construction by then, or for two more years after. And somewhere in a much smaller room, the notification system agreed to over a Sunday in New York will either grow into something sturdier or quietly join the list of mechanisms that existed mainly to be announced.
