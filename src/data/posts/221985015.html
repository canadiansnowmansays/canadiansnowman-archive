Three people tried to give money to James Talarico and gave it to a senator from Colorado instead.

As The Atlantic‘s Will Oremus reported on 22 October, if you searched Google for how to donate to Talarico, the Texas Democrat in the most closely watched Senate race of these midterms, you might have been shown an AI-written answer offering links where “you can chip in securely.” One of those links led to a fundraising page carrying Talarico’s name and photograph, which disclosed only in small print at the bottom that the money would go to Senator John Hickenlooper. The page was the work of an outside group, the Kitchen Table Values PAC, and Hickenlooper’s campaign says it knew nothing about it. Three donors sent a combined $1,011 before a Democratic advertising strategist noticed. The page came down and the money was forwarded on.

It is a small story and worth keeping small. Nobody was ruined. But look at how it worked. The misleading page was built by a person. What gave it authority was a machine that read the top of the page and not the bottom, and then recommended it in the confident, unsourced register these systems use for everything. That is the shape of the problem this edition is about, and it is not the shape this series expected.

What has changed since the last special edition

The special edition on 14 October reported something close to good news. When the Brennan Center for Justice fed election falsehoods to ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Grok in the words of real election deniers, the bots mostly refused to go along. Lawrence Norden called them “mostly truth-tellers” and suggested they might serve as a counterweight to social media. That finding was from August.

Brennan ran the tests again in October. As The New York Times‘ Tiffany Hsu, Steven Lee Myers and Julian E. Barnes reported on 22 October, the new analysis found that only OpenAI had tightened its controls on election falsehoods. The chatbots from Google, Meta and others had regressed.

This series should say plainly what that means, because it is the first time it has had a before-and-after measurement of a voluntary safety commitment. Two months after being told publicly where their systems failed, three of the four companies were doing worse. Nothing obliged them to do better. Nothing happened to them for doing worse.

How many people this actually reaches

The scale is new, and the two surveys available measure slightly different things, so both belong here. In a September survey by the American Association of Political Consultants Foundation, cited by Oremus, 4 per cent of respondents said they had asked a chatbot an election question in the previous month, while nearly 40 per cent had taken election information from Google’s AI search summaries. An October survey from the same foundation, cited by the Times, found one in five voters planning to use A.I. to look into a race on their ballot, and twice that number already receiving election details through an AI summary.

Set beside those numbers is the one this series finds most interesting: just 1 per cent of voters polled considered A.I. their most credible source of election information.

That gap is the whole thing. People are not consulting these systems because they trust them. They are consulting them because the answer arrives first, above the candidate’s own website, in a box they did not ask for. Trust is not required. Placement is sufficient.

How wrong the answers are

Oremus obtained a forthcoming report from Forum AI, a start-up that tests how models handle contested subjects, and the figures are not marginal. About 40 per cent of chatbot answers to election questions contained at least one factual inaccuracy. About one in three cited an unreliable source, such as a Reddit or Quora post. Asked when a Pennsylvania voter needed to request a mail-in ballot, one widely used model answered 3 November; the real deadline was a week earlier. Google’s Gemini, asked why American global power is declining, answered by citing People’s Daily, the newspaper of the Chinese Communist Party.

“These aren’t trick questions,” Forum’s co-founder Campbell Brown told Oremus. “There’s no excuse for chatbots to keep getting them wrong.”

A wrong ballot deadline is not a philosophical problem about machine values. It is a person who does not get to vote.

The industry built for the machines

The supply side is where this edition departs furthest from anything this series has covered, and both reports found the same thing independently.

Researchers at a cybersecurity firm called VisionHeight identified 37 websites, all registered on a single night in September by an anonymous organisation, which carry newspaper-sounding names — the Scranton Beacon, the Tar Heel State Report, the Wolverine State Report — and do not read like newspapers at all. They are built as banks of questions and answers about candidates in eleven House races across seven states, most of them Democrats. “Which candidate in MI-04 has the stronger plan for public safety?” one page asks, before answering itself. The sites also carry code that specifically welcomes the crawlers that gather training and search material for chatbots.

VisionHeight’s report contains the sentence this edition is named after: “The sites are not written for voters. They are written for the A.I. systems voters ask.”

It worked. When Oremus asked Google’s AI Mode whether a Michigan candidate supported defunding the police, the answer cited two sites from that network. After he raised it with Google, the sources stopped appearing and the answer changed. One reporter’s enquiry was the enforcement mechanism.

This is a known category, and it is much larger than 37 sites. NewsGuard has catalogued more than 1,100 organisations running what it calls “pink slime” — outlets presenting themselves as professional news while secretly backed by partisan groups. In NewsGuard’s own tests, chatbots asked about narratives appearing on those sites cited them nearly half the time.

Foreign operations have found the same door. The Global Disinformation Index found chatbots repeating a false Russian defence ministry claim about Ukraine. Alethea, which tracks influence operations, asked prominent chatbots about long-running Kremlin narratives and watched them cite fabricated outlets; asked about a European ban on Russian gas, xAI’s Grok referred to InfoRos, an arm of Russian military intelligence that the United States named for interfering in the 2020 election. And on 21 October, NewsGuard reported that Google’s AI answers had picked up a deepfake video, spread by a Russian influence campaign, which purports to show a student accusing Talarico of a sexual assault in 2012.

“When you use a chatbot,” Alethea’s founder Lisa Kaplan said, “you are not expecting to be deceived by a foreign actor who’s trying to manipulate you.”

A new profession, with a name

Where there is an exploitable channel there is now a consultancy. On the left, Run for Something has built a platform called CampSight that watches what the models say about a candidate and advises on changing it. On the right, the Center for Campaign Innovation publishes research on which sources the models prefer. “We’re campaigning for the AI vote now,” its director Eric Wilson told Oremus.

The tactics are mundane and faintly comic. Most candidates had not bothered filling in their Ballotpedia profiles, Run for Something’s Jordan Haines said, on the reasonable grounds that hardly anyone reads Ballotpedia. Hardly any voters do. Their chatbots do. The models also favour anything on a dot-gov domain, which quietly advantages incumbents, who have an official House or Senate page to seed, over challengers, who have less public record — though that cuts both ways, since a thinner record is easier to write over.

The trade even has a word now: sloptimisation.

Part 132 of this series, still to come, follows the money that A.I. companies are putting into these same midterm races. This is the other half of that story, and it is cheaper. A super PAC costs millions. Thirty-seven websites cost a night’s work.

What the companies and Congress are doing

Senator Adam Schiff, who asked the platforms what they intended to do. U.S. Senate, public domain

Not nothing, and not much.

Google, OpenAI and Anthropic have all partnered with Democracy Works, a non-partisan non-profit, to supply verified information about how, where and when to vote; Anthropic’s integration went live the week of 19 October. xAI is the exception, and also did not substantively answer letters that Senators Adam Schiff and Alex Padilla sent last month to Google, Meta, Snap, TikTok and xAI asking what they intended to do about election falsehoods. Google gave the fullest reply. The senators said the responses as a whole were inconsistent and incomplete.

Democracy Works’ chief executive Luis Lozada was fair about the difficulty: “I don’t think they’re just completely negligent. I just think that this is a very, very difficult issue to navigate.”

The most interesting single intervention came from OpenAI, and it deserves credit as the one measure here that is neither a promise nor a partnership. Told by the Brennan Center that its tools would generate realistic images of ballot stuffing, OpenAI did not refuse the request. It changed the output to a cartoon. Josh Lawson, its head of civic and strategic risk, explained the logic: “We are degrading the evidentiary value of an image while still allowing social commentary.”

That is a genuinely clever answer to a problem this series flagged in the 14 October edition, where the image tools made whatever they were asked. It keeps the satire and removes the forgery. It is also, as far as these two reports can establish, the only thing any company has built that treats an election harm as a design question rather than a messaging one.

Against that, the Institute for Strategic Dialogue released a review on 22 October of how the major platforms are protecting election integrity. Its U.S. director of civic innovation, Max Read, gave the verdict: “Their response has been uneven, poorly documented and, in several cases, poorly suited to addressing the harms it is meant to prevent.” The report found a hodgepodge of policies with meaningful gaps in whether AI-generated content gets disclosed or restricted at all, and platforms shifting moderation from people to automated systems in the middle of an election, to an extent the researchers could not determine because the companies would not say.

The same trick, where nobody is fighting over anything

Senator Alex Padilla, who co-signed the letters. U.S. Senate, public domain

Everything above has a motive attached: a seat, a donation, a foreign interest. So it is worth ending on a case with none of that, because it suggests the problem is the mechanism rather than the money.

On 17 June, Kris Anderson, a Las Vegas counselling-centre director who spends two and a half hours every evening reviewing mantis photographs on the citizen-science site iNaturalist, thought for a few seconds that he had found a new species: a dead-leaf mantis from Sabah, in Malaysia, its camouflage “almost too good, as if it were wearing an actual leaf.” Then he noticed the wrong wing-fold and an odd texture on the thorax. It had been generated by A.I. As Eric Boodman reported for The New York Times on 16 October, submissions like it are now a running problem for the volunteer projects that map the natural world. Anderson’s first catch, in March, had three antennae. This one fooled him, briefly. Such images, he wrote, “are capable of passing initial review by non-specialists and may persist within biodiversity databases for extended periods.”

Read that sentence again with the Scranton Beacon in mind. It is the same sentence.

And the quieter version of it is worse. The ornithologist Alexander Lees says the commoner problem is not forgery at all but well-meaning volunteers using A.I. to tidy up their own photographs without noticing that it has added wing-stripes or turned a yellow bird red. Those images then train the identification apps. “You’re essentially giving them training data of things that do not exist,” he said. iNaturalist’s executive director, Scott Loarie, put the dependency plainly: its models are “only as good as the human community” feeding them.

Nobody profits from a fake mantis. There is no PAC, no Kremlin, no consultancy charging to place it. It got into a trusted database anyway, because the database reads faster than its reviewers do — which is the same reason the thirty-seven websites work, and the reason a wrong ballot deadline travels.

The ledger

Twelve days out, the position is this. Tens of millions of voters will get some of their election information from systems that are wrong about two-fifths of the time, that cite an unreliable source in a third of answers, and that three of four companies have allowed to get worse since being told where they failed. A purpose-built supply of material exists to be ingested by those systems, written in the register the machines reward, costing almost nothing, and traceable to nobody. The defences are voluntary, uneven, undocumented, and in one notable case consist of a journalist emailing a spokesman.

Gideon Nave of Wharton supplied the sentence that should worry anyone who thinks this is merely a quality-control problem: “You can implant all sorts of ideas in people’s heads when they’re using AI, and they feel that it’s their own responses.”

The 14 October edition closed on the federal shield being taken down. This one closes on something harder to legislate. In 2016 the lesson was that a platform could be gamed by people writing for other people. The machines were supposed to be an improvement on that, and in one narrow sense they are: they will argue with an election denier, which a Facebook feed never did. But they have also created a new surface, where the way to reach a voter is not to persuade the voter at all. It is to write for the thing the voter asks, and wait.
