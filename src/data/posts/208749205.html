Part V of this series ended with the Sassanid Empire and the Byzantine Empire having exhausted each other in a final catastrophic war between 602 and 628 CE, just in time for a new power to emerge from the Arabian peninsula and reshape the map. What followed was, by any measure of speed, one of the more remarkable episodes of expansion in recorded history: within roughly two decades of the Prophet Muhammad’s death in 632 CE, Arab Muslim forces had taken Syria, Egypt, and Mesopotamia from Byzantium and had destroyed the Sassanid Empire outright, and within a century Islamic rule extended from the Iberian peninsula to the Indus valley and up into Central Asia. For the purposes of a series about the Silk Road, the significant fact is not the speed of the conquest but its geography: the caliphates ended up holding, under a single political and religious authority, essentially the entire western and central portion of the overland route from the Mediterranean to the edge of the Chinese sphere.

This had never happened before. Every previous arrangement this series has described involved multiple mutually hostile powers each controlling a segment and extracting a margin — Han China and then the Tang at the eastern end, Parthia or Persia in the middle, Rome or Byzantium at the western terminus, with Sogdian and Central Asian intermediaries filling the gaps and profiting from the friction between them. Part II described Rome bankrupting itself paying Parthian markups; Part V described Byzantium spending a century trying and failing to route around Sassanid gatekeeping. The Arab conquests eliminated most of that friction across a very large stretch of territory in a single generation, and the commercial consequences were substantial and immediate.

Central Asia entered the Islamic world in stages, and the process was neither quick nor gentle. The Umayyad general Qutayba ibn Muslim conducted the major campaigns across Transoxiana between roughly 705 and 715 CE, taking Bukhara and Samarkand — the latter’s conquest around 712 CE noted in Part III — against determined Sogdian resistance and with substantial destruction. Chinese Tang forces were simultaneously pushing westward, and the two expanding powers eventually collided at the Battle of Talas in 751 CE, a clash near the Talas river in what is now Kyrgyzstan in which Abbasid forces, aided by the defection of Karluk Turkic contingents during the battle, defeated a Tang army under Gao Xianzhi. The battle is frequently described as one of history’s decisive engagements, which somewhat overstates a relatively modest encounter, but the outcome held: Tang expansion westward stopped permanently, the An Lushan rebellion four years later removed any Chinese capacity to try again, and Central Asia’s future belonged to the Islamic world.

Talas also carries the papermaking transmission story, mentioned in Parts III and VIII, in which Chinese prisoners taken at the battle supposedly introduced the technology to Samarkand. Whether or not that specific mechanism is accurate — and there are reasonable grounds for scepticism, since papermaking may well have diffused through ordinary commercial contact somewhat earlier — the outcome is not in dispute. Paper mills operated in Samarkand within decades, in Baghdad by the late eighth century, and subsequently across the Islamic world, and the availability of a cheap, durable writing surface transformed administration and scholarship in ways that parchment, expensive and limited by livestock supply, never could have. It reached Europe considerably later, arriving via Islamic Spain and Sicily, and the roughly six-century lag between Chinese woodblock printing and Gutenberg has a good deal to do with paper arriving in Europe so much later than it arrived in Baghdad.

Baghdad itself is the clearest single expression of what the caliphates did with their inherited position. Founded in 762 CE by the Abbasid caliph al-Mansur as a purpose-built capital — the original round city, laid out with geometric deliberation on the Tigris — it sat at the junction of the overland routes east toward Persia and Central Asia and the river and Gulf routes running south toward the Indian Ocean. Within a century it was plausibly the largest city in the world outside China and a commercial clearinghouse of extraordinary scale, drawing Chinese ceramics, Indian spices, East African ivory and gold, Central Asian slaves and horses, and Baltic amber and furs arriving via Volga trade routes that Scandinavian merchants had opened for the specific purpose of reaching Islamic silver. The scale of that last connection is measurable: hundreds of thousands of Islamic silver dirhams have been recovered from hoards across Scandinavia, Russia, and the Baltic, deposited by Viking traders who had exchanged furs and slaves for Abbasid coinage. A Silk Road series naturally emphasizes the east-west axis, but the caliphates’ commercial reach ran north to the Arctic and south to Zanzibar as well, and Baghdad sat at the centre of all of it.

The intellectual consequence of this position is the part that has outlasted everything else. The Abbasid court sponsored, across the eighth and ninth centuries, a sustained and heavily funded translation programme — the “translation movement” — that rendered Greek, Persian, Syriac, and Sanskrit scientific and philosophical works into Arabic on a scale no previous civilization had attempted. Aristotle, Galen, Euclid, Ptolemy, and Indian mathematical and astronomical treatises entered Arabic, and the scholars who worked with them did not merely preserve the material but extended it. Al-Khwarizmi, working in Baghdad in the early ninth century, produced foundational works on algebra — the discipline takes its name from the title of his book — and on Hindu-Arabic numerals, whose adoption transformed calculation across the Islamic world and, eventually, everywhere else; his own name, Latinized, gives us “algorithm.” Al-Razi and later Ibn Sina produced medical compendia that remained standard references in European universities for centuries after translation into Latin. Al-Biruni wrote on India with an ethnographic seriousness rare in any premodern culture, having learned Sanskrit to do it properly.

The institutional setting for this work is often given as the Bayt al-Hikma, the House of Wisdom, described in popular accounts as a great research academy and library founded by Harun al-Rashid and expanded by al-Ma’mun. Recent scholarship has substantially qualified this picture, arguing that the contemporary sources describe something closer to a palace library and translation office than a university, and that the translation movement was driven less by one institution than by a broad, decentralised, well-funded appetite for the material among caliphs, viziers, wealthy families, and physicians who commissioned translations privately. The revision does not diminish the achievement; if anything it makes it more impressive, since it means the demand for Greek and Indian science was diffused throughout an entire elite rather than confined to a single royal project.

Islamic geography deserves particular mention in a series about trade routes, since the caliphates produced the first body of systematic geographical writing anywhere that was built substantially on commercial rather than military information. Ibn Khordadbeh, working as a postal and intelligence official in the ninth century, compiled the Book of Roads and Kingdoms, an administrative geography listing routes, distances, provinces, and revenues that reads in places like a logistics manual and includes descriptions of trade as far afield as China and the Frankish lands. Al-Muqaddasi in the tenth century travelled extensively and wrote a regional geography grounded explicitly in personal observation, with candid opinions about the cities he visited. Al-Idrisi, working at the Norman court in Sicily in the twelfth century, produced for Roger II a world map and accompanying geographical compendium that synthesised Islamic, Greek, and Norman sources and remained among the most accurate world maps available anywhere for several centuries. These were not abstract exercises. A commercial empire spanning from the Atlantic to the Indus needed to know how far things were and what could be bought where, and the resulting literature is one of the better sources modern historians have for reconstructing the medieval trading world.

None of this was incidental to the trade routes. The translation movement required manuscripts, and manuscripts arrived through the same commercial and diplomatic channels as everything else — Byzantine libraries, Persian archives, Indian courts. It required paper, which arrived from Central Asia by the route described above. It required patrons whose wealth came substantially from commerce, and a capital city positioned to receive scholars from Spain to Bukhara. Al-Biruni knew about India because Islamic rule extended toward it and Indian scholarship travelled to Baghdad. The intellectual flowering was, in a real sense, downstream of the trade network, in exactly the same way the Buddhist transmission described in Part IV was downstream of Han logistics.

One structural feature of the Islamic period deserves attention because it has no real parallel in any of the earlier arrangements this series has described: the hajj. The obligation on every capable Muslim to travel to Mecca once in a lifetime created, as an entirely unintended byproduct of a religious duty, a permanent, self-renewing, continent-spanning system of long-distance travel involving hundreds of thousands of people annually, complete with the infrastructure that such movement requires — roads, wells, waystations, security arrangements, guides, and financing. Pilgrim caravans from Damascus, Cairo, Baghdad, and eventually points as distant as West Africa and Southeast Asia moved along routes that overlapped extensively with commercial ones, and pilgrims routinely financed their journeys by trading along the way, which meant the distinction between a hajj caravan and a trade caravan was frequently academic. The most spectacular documented instance is the 1324 pilgrimage of Mansa Musa of Mali, whose entourage reportedly distributed so much gold in Cairo that contemporary Egyptian sources describe the local gold price depressed for years afterward — an anecdote often told for its extravagance but more interesting as evidence of just how thoroughly the pilgrimage network had integrated West African commerce into the wider Islamic trading world. The hajj is, in this series’ recurring terms, another case of infrastructure built for one purpose carrying something else entirely, with the twist that here the something else was ordinary commerce riding on religious rails, rather than the reverse.

The maritime dimension also expanded dramatically under Islamic rule and deserves noting, since a series organized around an overland route risks understating how much traffic had always moved by sea. Arab and Persian merchants operating out of Gulf ports — Siraf and Basra prominent among them — sailed the monsoon routes to India, Southeast Asia, and directly to China, where they established the substantial resident foreign community at Guangzhou that Part VIII mentioned. The ninth-century Arabic account known as the Akhbar al-Sin wa’l-Hind, compiled from merchant reports, describes the China voyage in practical detail, and the 1998 discovery of a ninth-century shipwreck off Belitung Island in Indonesia — an Arab-style sewn-plank vessel carrying tens of thousands of Chinese ceramic pieces — provided physical confirmation of direct maritime commerce between the Abbasid world and Tang China on a scale that surprised even specialists. The overland and maritime routes were never really alternatives competing for the same traffic so much as complementary systems, and merchants moved between them according to what the goods, the season, and the current political situation made sensible.

The caliphates also formalized commercial practice in ways that outlasted them. Islamic law developed a substantial body of commercial jurisprudence addressing partnership, agency, credit, and contract, driven by the practical needs of merchants operating across enormous distances under a shared legal framework. The qirad or mudaraba partnership — an arrangement in which one party supplies capital and another supplies labour and travel, sharing profit on agreed terms — provided a workable structure for financing long-distance ventures where the investor could not accompany the goods, and the structural resemblance between these arrangements and the commenda contracts that appear in Italian maritime cities somewhat later has generated a long-running scholarly discussion about transmission that remains unresolved. The hawala system of transferring value through networks of trusted correspondents, without physically moving coin, addressed the obvious problem that carrying bullion across bandit country was a poor idea. The prohibition on riba shaped the forms these instruments took, encouraging profit-sharing structures over interest-bearing ones, and the ingenuity applied to that constraint produced a genuinely sophisticated body of commercial technique.

The unity did not last, which is by now the least surprising sentence in this series. The Abbasid caliphate began fragmenting politically within a century of its founding, with regional dynasties asserting effective independence while maintaining nominal allegiance; the Fatimids in Egypt established a rival caliphate outright in the tenth century; Turkic military dynasties, initially recruited as slave soldiers, took increasing control of the actual apparatus of government, with the Seljuks reducing the Abbasid caliph to a religious figurehead by the mid-eleventh century. Baghdad’s destruction by the Mongols under Hulagu in 1258 — the sack, the killing of the last Abbasid caliph, and the destruction of the city’s libraries — is conventionally treated as the definitive end, though the region’s commercial and intellectual life did not simply stop, and the Mongol successor states in Persia would themselves convert to Islam and patronize scholarship within decades.

What the caliphate period contributes to this series is the strongest available counterexample to the pattern the earlier installments established. Everywhere else, the Silk Road’s most consequential effects were accidental — a failed spy’s intelligence report, a religion riding along with merchants, a bacterium in a cargo hold. The Abbasids, uniquely, saw what they had and acted on it deliberately: they held the crossroads, they knew they held it, and they funded a systematic effort to gather, translate, and extend the knowledge that flowed through it. It is the one point in this entire history where somebody looked at the network and formed an intentional plan for what to do with it, and the results were correspondingly durable. Baghdad fell, the caliphate fragmented, and the algebra stayed.

It is worth adding one caution to that framing, since this series has spent ten installments warning against tidy narratives. The Abbasid intellectual achievement is sometimes packaged, in popular accounts, as a kind of civilizational relay race in which the Islamic world dutifully held Greek learning in trust until Europe was ready to collect it — a framing that manages to be both condescending and factually wrong. Al-Khwarizmi was not preserving anything; he was doing original mathematics. Ibn al-Haytham’s work on optics in the eleventh century, built on systematic experiment rather than deference to Aristotle, is a genuine methodological departure from what he inherited. The translation movement mattered enormously, but treating it as the whole story mistakes the loading dock for the factory. What the crossroads position actually provided was raw material and money; what the scholars did with both was their own.
