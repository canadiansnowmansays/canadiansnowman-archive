Capitol insurrection: Tracking the attack 1 year laterOn Jan. 6, 2021, pro-Trump rioters broke into the U.S. Capitol. The attack resulted in deaths, injuries, more than 700 arrests and former President Donald Trump's second impeachment.

There is a particular Washington phrase that sounds like a promise and functions like a warning: these are great people. Donald Trump deployed it on January 6, 2021, when a mob of his supporters was still inside the Capitol. He has deployed it many times since — most recently this week, insisting that the rioters he pardoned on his first day back in office “went there with love” and deserve taxpayer compensation for their suffering.

The evidence filed in American courts over the past several months tells a different story. It is told in three chapters, each released in recent days, each more disquieting than the last.

Chapter One: The Pentagon Appointment

The office of Special Operations and Low Intensity Conflict sits near the top of the Pentagon’s national security hierarchy. It oversees America’s most elite military units — the special forces operators, the counterterrorism planners, the people responsible for defending the homeland against asymmetric attack. It is, by any reasonable measure, among the most sensitive civilian positions in the Defense Department.

It now has a new political appointee: Elias Irizarry, who was nineteen years old when he entered the United States Capitol through a broken window on January 6, 2021, armed with a metal pole.

The New York Times reported on the Irizarry appointment today, noting that the Pentagon’s acting press secretary described him as “a qualified, patriotic young professional.” Political appointees to such positions are typically selected by the office of the defense secretary or the White House.

What the press release did not include was the rest of Irizarry’s file.

Prosecutors had documented that after his March 2021 arrest, Irizarry showed no remorse and instead spent his time trying to identify who had turned him in. He sent text messages to a fellow rioter about joining the Russian military if he could not eventually join the American one. He participated in a group chat titled “Civil War,” in which he discussed using small planes to cross borders undetected.

A former Jan. 6 participant. Texts about the Russian military. A group chat called “Civil War.” These details did not disqualify him. They appear, in the current administration’s calculus, to be irrelevant.

Judge Tanya Chutkan, who sentenced Irizarry to fourteen days and was moved enough by his courtroom remorse to offer him a personal letter of recommendation, told him: “Life is like that. It takes us on strange journeys.” She had no way of knowing that the journey would lead to the Pentagon’s counterterrorism apparatus.

Michael Lumpkin, who ran the Special Operations office under President Obama, put the matter plainly to the Times: “It used to be that any possible negative perception about a hire like this would prevent it from happening. Today, it seems fealty is often more valued than expertise, sound judgment, or a strong moral compass.”

Fealty. That is the currency. The pardon was the down payment. The appointment is the dividend.

Chapter Two: Ninety-Seven and Counting

The numbers have been moving since this series began, and they moved again this week.

Lawfare, the nonprofit legal affairs publication edited by Benjamin Wittes of the Brookings Institution, released a study today documenting that at least ninety-seven people charged in connection with the January 6 Capitol attack have been accused of new crimes since that day — including nineteen cases that occurred after Trump granted blanket clemency on Inauguration Day 2025.

The previous benchmark, established by the Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington, had found forty defendants facing new criminal charges, with twelve of those occurring post-pardon. The Lawfare study more than doubles that figure, and its author, Katherine Pompilio, noted that the real number is almost certainly higher, as her team’s research — mining court documents and calling county clerks’ offices across the country — could not capture every jurisdiction.

The crimes Pompilio documented range across the full spectrum of human transgression: property damage and trespassing at the lower end; grand larceny, stalking, and domestic violence in the middle; and at the serious end, planning to assassinate law enforcement officials and prominent politicians, defrauding government agencies, reckless homicide, and child sexual abuse. At least twenty have been charged with driving under the influence.

Trump’s response, offered at a White House event last autumn when a reporter asked about a pardoned rioter arrested for allegedly threatening to assassinate a congressional leader: “You have thousands of people that we’re dealing with and, you know, if one goes haywire...” — at which point he changed the subject.

Ninety-seven is not one going haywire. Ninety-seven is a pattern. Pompilio’s language was precise: “The pardons closed a chapter for these individuals politically. What I found is that for a significant number of them, the behavior that defined January 6th didn’t stop when they left the Capitol.”

The Lawfare study arrives against the backdrop of a White House that has been aggressive in publicizing crimes committed by undocumented immigrants — posters, press releases, named individuals with offenses listed. The pardoned rioters do not receive that treatment. They receive compensation proposals and political appointments.

And the compensation proposals are not merely rhetorical. Earlier this year, the administration floated a $1.8 billion fund — drawn from the Justice Department’s asset forfeiture accounts, meaning existing public money — to be paid directly to January 6 defendants as restitution for what the White House characterized as political persecution. The fund was eventually scrapped after bipartisan opposition, including from senators who found the legal basis untenable and the optics indefensible. But Trump has not abandoned the principle. As the New York Times reported this week, the president said on Wednesday that the rioters still deserve a payout: “These are great people that were destroyed, their families have been destroyed.” The money may yet find another legislative vehicle.

Which means that every person in the Lawfare study’s ninety-seven — every rioter charged with stalking, with homicide, with domestic violence, with plotting to assassinate lawmakers — is, under the current administration’s stated policy, a potential beneficiary of the American taxpayer. The pardon erased their records. The appointment offered some of them careers. The compensation fund, if it ever passes, would hand them a cheque.

Chapter Three: The Children

Andrew Paul Johnson

This chapter is the hardest to write and cannot be omitted.

Andrew Paul Johnson, forty-five, was a Florida handyman who received a full pardon from President Trump for storming the Capitol on January 6, 2021. On the Capitol grounds, he had described himself on social media as an “American Terrorist” and a “Proud J6er,” and had violated court orders by posing with a gun while his case was ongoing.

Nine months after the pardon, he was arrested in Hernando County, Florida, following a report of a sex offense involving two child victims. Investigators subsequently uncovered a pattern of physical and sexual assaults against children over a period of several months, one victim eleven years old at the time of the abuse.

Johnson attempted to keep one victim quiet with a specific inducement: he claimed he would receive ten million dollars from the Trump administration as restitution for January 6 defendants and would leave a portion in his will to the child. The promised windfall, Johnson apparently believed, was sufficient to buy a child’s silence.

In February 2026, a jury convicted him of five charges, including molesting a child under twelve and another under sixteen, lewd and lascivious exhibition, and transmitting harmful materials to a minor. In March, he was sentenced to life in prison.

Then there is Daniel Tocci, also pardoned for his January 6 conduct, also in the custody of federal authorities — this time for a different order of offense entirely. When investigators pursued his Capitol riot case, they discovered what a Justice Department news release described, with bureaucratic understatement, as an “enormous child pornography collection”: more than one hundred thousand images and videos of child sexual abuse material. He pleaded guilty and was sentenced in late March to four years in federal prison.

The Justice Department’s news release announcing Tocci’s sentence made no mention of his connection to January 6. The department that once prosecuted rioters with institutional determination now apparently preferred not to draw the thread.

The Pardon as Character Reference

When this series began, the central argument was structural: that presidential clemency had been transformed from an instrument of mercy into an instrument of loyalty, and that the transformation carried costs that would eventually become visible.

Those costs are now visible, in three registers at once.

A man who texted about joining the Russian military and organized in a group chat called “Civil War” has been rewarded with a political appointment inside America’s special operations oversight apparatus. Nearly one hundred people charged with January 6 offenses have been accused of new crimes, including nineteen since the pardon. Two pardoned rioters have been convicted of crimes against children — one sentenced to life, one to four years for possessing over one hundred thousand abuse images.

Trump told us they went there with love. He told us they are great people. He is now telling us they deserve compensation.

Consider what that last word means in full. Andrew Paul Johnson told a child victim he would receive ten million dollars from the Trump administration and would share it as an inducement to stay silent. He was not inventing a fantasy — he was parroting a real White House policy discussion that was live and circulating at the time. The compensation rhetoric, seeded by the president and amplified by his allies, traveled from Mar-a-Lago to a Florida courtroom and into a child’s ear as a grooming tool. That is where the logic of the pardon-as-absolution leads when it meets a man with no conscience and a very specific use for it.

The courts have been telling us something else.
