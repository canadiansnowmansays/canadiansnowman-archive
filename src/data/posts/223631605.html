Every Monday, the people still living in Komyshuvakha walked to the village school to collect food and medicine. The school had been built after the full-scale invasion, with a reinforced concrete basement that doubled as a bomb shelter. Children studied there for only a few months before officials evacuated them. The adults who stayed were mostly old or disabled, and they stayed for the reasons people always give: animals to look after, and a dread of becoming a burden in some strange town far from their own land.

On Monday, November 2, the first glide bomb landed while the aid was being handed out. People ran for the basement, as they had been taught. Then the heavier bombs came. Russia dropped at least nine on the village, each carrying around 3,000 pounds of explosives, Ukrainian officials said, as Siobhán O’Grady and Liubov Sholudko reported for The New York Times. The concrete could not hold. Two rescue workers and four police officers who had rushed in after the first bomb were killed by the next ones, the tactic known as a double tap. Through the following day, Russian drones kept circling over the rubble, forcing rescuers to dig in short bursts. “It’s a cynical thing to do,” said Mykhailo Semikin, the acting head of the region’s military administration.

By Thursday, November 5, the dead numbered 43, Volodymyr Zelensky said, and dozens of people might still be under the rubble, as Interfax-Ukraine reported. It was the deadliest attack on civilians in the Zaporizhzhia region in nearly five years of war, and it may prove one of the deadliest of the whole conflict.

It came one day after a ceasefire ended. Hours later, Steve Witkoff announced peace talks.

The six days

The ceasefire was the one Part VIII watched begin. For six days, from 3 a.m. on Tuesday, October 27, to late on Sunday, November 1, the guns around Oleshky were supposed to be silent, and for once they mostly were.

Ukraine’s human-rights ombudsman, Dmytro Lubinets, crossed into occupied territory with his Russian counterpart, Yana Lantratova, and a convoy of 25 buses with room for 750 people, as Carlotta Gall and Oleksandr Chubko reported for the Times. He was the first Ukrainian official to set foot in Russia or Russian-occupied Ukraine since February 2022. “We are in Oleshky!” he posted on Friday, October 30, and then recorded a video explaining the route to the people waiting in the rain, and telling them they could bring their pets.

The route was long. Evacuees bound for free Ukraine travelled for 19 hours to the Russian resort of Anapa, then flew to Belarus, then crossed home. On Saturday, October 31, they were reunited with families they had not seen for years. The oldest was 94. The youngest was just over a year old. One man had survived 16 drone attacks and four missile strikes on his house. “When the world pays attention, people are rescued,” Zelensky said.

How many were rescued, nobody will quite say. Melania Trump, who announced the truce, said “nearly 800 people” were evacuated, as Ukrainska Pravda reported. Neither government has said how many went to Ukraine and how many chose Russia. Ukraine’s prime minister told Parliament that 527 were coming to Ukraine, then retracted the figure. A Russian military blogger posted that 17 buses were heading to Ukraine and four to Russia, then deleted the post. The numbers are sensitive because Russia’s ombudsman had said that few people wanted to leave, and Ukrainians suspect that people who have lived for four years under occupation do not always say what they mean in front of soldiers.

Not everyone made it. Natalia Sierova, 72, had refused for months to leave the dogs she looked after. On the morning of the evacuation she came to the assembly point, felt unwell and went home. She tried again after her granddaughter pleaded with her, but one of her dogs ran off. By the time she found it, the buses had gone. “Maybe it was intentional,” her daughter-in-law told the Times. “Her parents, sister, husband, and daughter are buried there. Some people prefer to die on their own land, rather than die on the road and become a burden to others.”

She was not the only one left behind. On November 5, Oleshky’s exiled mayor, Tetiana Hasanenko, told the Kyiv Post that some residents had sat in basements without any means of communication and never learned an evacuation was happening. People who could not walk were not brought to the assembly point. Some feared Russian “filtration,” the screening and interrogation that occupied Ukrainians know well, and some women waiting by the highway gave up and walked home, believing the convoy had already passed. The aid that was delivered, she said, has been reaching residents in amounts too small to last a week. Up to 1,000 people may still be in the town. Those who got on the buses, Hasanenko said, “left with the idea that it could be a ‘one-way trip.’”

And while civilians boarded buses, soldiers were busy. On Monday, November 2, Ukraine’s General Staff said Russian forces had used the pause to rotate units, fortify positions, add anti-drone netting along the roads and bring up ammunition, as Reuters reported. “The enemy, by contrast, used the humanitarian pause to improve its positions,” it said. The Kremlin’s spokesman, Dmitri Peskov, did not deny it. “It is safe to assume that the Ukrainian armed forces also used this period to regroup,” he said.

Ukraine took the deal anyway, and paid the political price in praise. “We wouldn’t have done it without Melania Trump and her team,” Lubinets told reporters. “I am saying this absolutely seriously.” Zelensky awarded the first lady the Order of Princess Olha, First Class, the highest honour Ukraine can bestow on a woman. On Thursday, November 5, she was to host both delegations at the White House, Siobhán O’Grady reported for the Times. Lantratova would be in the room, though Ukraine has charged her in absentia for her part in deporting children from the Kherson region to Russia. Both governments, O’Grady wrote, had concluded that keeping Mrs. Trump happy was a way of managing her husband. “We have no choice,” said Mykola Bielieskov, an analyst with the Ukrainian military charity Come Back Alive. “We need to dance this dance.”

That is the arithmetic of a country fighting for its survival: a few hundred grandmothers, toddlers and survivors of 20 strikes on one house were worth six quiet days for the enemy and a medal for the first lady.

Meanwhile, everywhere else

The truce covered Oleshky and humanitarian convoys in the Kherson region. Nothing else.

At about 11 a.m. on Thursday, October 29, while the convoy was preparing to cross, a Russian jet-powered drone slammed into a platform at Dnipro station, where Train No. 866 to Zaporizhzhia was waiting. At least 14 people were killed, Cassandra Vinograd reported for the Times. It was one of four jet-powered drones Russia launched at the area, the kind Part VII described Ukraine’s pilots chasing across the sky; the other three were shot down. The same day, two 15-year-old girls were killed in Chernihiv and three people in Kremenchuk.

“The Russians are carrying them out right as the humanitarian operation in Oleshky is unfolding,” Zelensky wrote. “Russia’s signals are clear — they want the war to continue.”

That evening

Hours after the bombs fell on Komyshuvakha, Witkoff announced that the United States would mediate working-level talks between Ukraine and Russia in Abu Dhabi on November 10 and 11, the first three-way round since February. They would cover “post-war security arrangements, de-escalation, humanitarian efforts, and next steps toward peace,” he said, “building on the progress made in Kyiv, Oslo, and St. Petersburg,” as the Kyiv Post reported. American officials are to meet the British, French, Germans, the European Union and NATO in Brussels first. Both Moscow and Kyiv have confirmed they will send negotiators.

Ukraine will go, because Ukraine always goes. But it knows what Russia will bring. Russia’s deputy foreign minister, Alexander Grushko, has already said that any post-war arrangement must require Ukraine to give up its NATO ambitions and bar foreign troops from its soil: no alliance, and no one to stand beside it.

The ground under Washington moved the next day. On Tuesday, November 3, American voters handed both the House and the Senate to the Democrats. Moscow’s response was a study in calm. “President Trump continues to be in office, and we sincerely hope that he will also continue his peacemaking efforts,” Peskov said, as Jim Tankersley, Damien Cave and Annie Correal reported for the Times. In Kyiv and across Europe, the paper found, there was no consensus on what the vote would change. Nico Lange, a Berlin security analyst and long-time backer of Ukraine, warned Europeans against assuming that “the big brother U.S.A. will do everything for us.” A new Congress can hold hearings and attach conditions. It cannot sign a treaty, and it does not sit until January. The talks in Abu Dhabi will happen in the lame-duck weeks Part VII warned about.

Russia also knows what it has already been given. Part VIII reported that the Treasury Department was weighing whether to lift sanctions on the Russian Direct Investment Fund, the state fund the Biden administration called Putin’s “slush fund.” On Wednesday, October 28, the Kremlin confirmed it. “Such a conversation is taking place,” said Putin’s foreign-policy adviser, Yuri Ushakov. “It has been going on for some time, though I do not know what the outcome will be,” as Anton Troianovski reported for the Times. Two members of Congress, Marcy Kaptur and Rosa DeLauro, said more sanctions relief “only emboldens Putin and gives him the resources to prolong this conflict.”

The diesel deal at the centre of all this is not going to plan. Of the tankers that sailed for America this month, two have turned away: one now lists Brazil as its destination, another is idling off Algeria “awaiting orders,” as Meduza reported, citing the Financial Times. The Agioi Fanendes is still headed for the United States, but its owner says the port where it will unload “is not confirmed yet.” American diesel prices rose after the deal was announced. Zelensky called it “an investment in the war.”

What Ukraine is waiting for has not moved either. Twenty-three Patriot interceptors that European allies have already paid for are still held up for lack of signatures at the Pentagon, Zelensky said. “There are a few documents that he’s already signed and maybe he thinks he’s signed them all,” he said of Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth, “but it is not so,” as Hromadske reported. This year Ukraine has received about 12 Patriot missiles a month from the United States. It needs about 150.

The war below

Much of what Ukraine has left, it has taken underground. Maria Varenikova of the Times rode an electric buggy six miles through one of the old gas pipes near Kupiansk that Part VII described, in 40 minutes, in a gas mask, through dust that seemed to change the colour of the soldiers’ skin. A 46-year-old soldier, Rostyslav, told her that on leave, “sometimes you wake up at home and, before getting up, you first try to measure the height of the ceiling.” But, he added, “it turns out you can live in the pipe.”

Above ground, the drones have killed something older than any weapon: the “golden hour,” the principle of modern military medicine that a wounded soldier should reach surgery within 60 minutes. Now the wounded shelter for days or weeks in tree lines before anyone can move them, Gina Kolata and Marc Santora reported for the Times. By the time they reach a hospital, 30 to 40 per cent carry severe infections, often resistant to every antibiotic doctors have. Some lose limbs to infection rather than to the blast.

One clinical pharmacologist, Hailie Uren, decided to treat the problem as hospitals treat a biological attack. Wounded soldiers are now stripped, scrubbed and showered in a tent before they enter one hospital in Dnipro; about 150 have been through it so far. Private Oleksandr, shot in the leg six days before he arrived, got his first shower in months. “The casualties consistently are telling us they feel like they are a human again,” Uren said. “It’s like we are washing off the war from them.”

At the edges

The war does not stay inside Ukraine’s borders. On Tuesday, November 3, Poland’s prime minister, Donald Tusk, launched what he called a “special operation” on the frontier with Russia’s Kaliningrad region, with counterterrorism police and intelligence officers, after allied services warned of possible “armed incursions” in the first half of November, Andrew Higgins reported for the Times. “It’s not a coincidence that this tension, provoked by the Russians, is occurring today,” Tusk said, pointing to the American midterms. Most of the Western weapons that reach Ukraine pass through Poland.

Moscow is working the inside of the border as well. When Tusk sent riot police to seal off Poland’s Constitutional Tribunal, at the climax of a decade-long fight over the court, Dmitri Medvedev declared that “a constitutional coup is underway in Poland,” Higgins had reported a day earlier for the Times. Russia’s foreign ministry spokeswoman, Maria Zakharova, compared it to Kyiv in 2014, the uprising Putin later used as his excuse for invasion. Russia has lost two friendly governments in Europe this year, Viktor Orbán’s in Hungary in April and Aleksandar Vučić’s in Serbia on October 25. A Poland at war with itself would be worth more than both.

It does not stay in Europe either. Mykhailo Shekhtman, a truck driver from Vinnytsia, came to the United States in April 2024 under Uniting for Ukraine, the programme America created for people fleeing the war. In February, Oklahoma troopers arrested him at a weigh station. He has been in immigration detention ever since, Jazmine Ulloa reported for the Times. He has no criminal record. He missed the appointment to renew his status because he was locked up. “I was invited here to be protected from the war,” he said.

And in Moscow, the last Russian voices willing to call the war a war are being shut off. Novaya Gazeta, whose editor Dmitri Muratov won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2021 and which once printed a front page reading “Russia. Bombs. Ukraine.,” will close on November 9 after losing its lease of 29 years and the bank service that let readers support it, Valerie Hopkins reported for the Times. The journalists put it in three words: “They killed us.”

What six days buy

Put the week together. A pope recovering from surgery asked for a road out of one town. A first lady announced it. For six days the road was open, and several hundred people walked down it, through Russia and Belarus, to get home. The army that had starved them used the quiet to dig in. The day the road closed, the same army buried a school full of people collecting their weekly groceries. That evening, the American envoy who had saluted Putin as a humanitarian announced the next round of talks.

None of that makes the rescue less real. Ukraine will take every life it can get back, from whoever helps. But mercy measured in days, granted by the side doing the killing, is not peace. It is a pause that one side spends rescuing grandmothers and the other spends bringing up ammunition.

The people of Komyshuvakha and Natalia Sierova never met. They stayed for the same reasons: the animals, the graves, the fear of being a burden. In Oleshky, someone opened a road, and Sierova chose not to take it. In Komyshuvakha, no one opened anything. The road, the shelter and the school were all there, and the bombs came anyway.

On November 10, the negotiators sit down in Abu Dhabi. Ukraine will ask for what it has asked for since February 2022: to be left alive, and left Ukrainian. Russia will ask for the rest.
