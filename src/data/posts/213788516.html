If Asheville was a summit that documented its own dysfunction in real time — a boycotted photograph, a disinvited founding member, a press corps admitted or excluded by whichever government applied the most pressure — then the Shanghai Cooperation Organization summit convened the same week in Bishkek, the capital of Kyrgyzstan, was its careful photographic negative. Where the G20 produced fracture, the SCO produced the appearance of unity. The two summits happened on opposite sides of the planet, on overlapping days, covering substantially the same anxieties — a wartime global economy, a fraying rules-based order, the question of who gets to set the agenda when the old rules stop applying — and arrived at opposite images of what power currently looks like.

The Chinese leader Xi Jinping did not travel to Central Asia by accident, and he did not travel there quietly. He arrived with the leaders of Russia and Iran already gathering, and with a message calibrated for a world he judges to be increasingly unsettled by the United States: China has friends, China has influence, and China is prepared to help write whatever comes next. He described Beijing as a source of stability and the champion of what he called an “orderly multipolar world” — a phrase doing a great deal of quiet work, since it manages to sound like an argument against chaos while functioning as an argument against continued American primacy.

What Bishkek actually offered its guests

The substance of what Xi extended to his fellow attendees is worth separating from the choreography, because the two are not the same thing, and the gap between them is the whole story.

To Vladimir Putin, whose war against Ukraine is now in its fifth year, Xi offered warm rhetoric — “the foundation of China-Russia relations will become ever stronger” — delivered just days after the CIA director, John Ratcliffe, had reportedly made a secret trip to Moscow urging the Kremlin toward an end to the fighting. Whatever passed in that Moscow meeting remains opaque, but its timing against Xi’s own overture to Putin suggests Washington and Beijing were, in the same week, both trying to shape the endgame of a war neither is fighting. It is a strange feature of this moment that the two governments most invested in ending America’s rivalry with the other are each, independently and without coordination, working the same Russian channel toward the same general outcome, for reasons that have nothing to do with each other and everything to do with what each hopes Moscow’s gratitude might be worth later.

To Masoud Pezeshkian, Iran’s president, Xi offered rather less: the two men reportedly met on the sidelines without a formally scheduled bilateral, a notable asymmetry given that Iran is, at this moment, absorbing American airstrikes. It was Putin, not Xi, who gave Pezeshkian the warmer public embrace, praising the Iranian people’s “courage and resilience” and describing Russian material support in terms specific enough to sound like a supply line rather than a sentiment: “We have discussed this with you and are supplying you with the necessary goods.”

That asymmetry is the tell. Xi’s caution around Iran specifically, even while standing shoulder to shoulder with Iran’s other patron, is not squeamishness. It is math. Xi is scheduled to travel to Washington later this month to try to extend a fragile economic truce with the Trump administration, and an overly visible embrace of the country the United States is currently bombing would complicate that trip considerably. So Beijing gets to have Tehran in the room, gets the photograph of solidarity among war-adjacent leaders, without accepting the diplomatic bill that a genuine bilateral with Pezeshkian would incur. Putin pays that bill instead, and calls it courage.

The argument for why this works

Ryan Hass, a former National Security Council official now at the Brookings Institution, described China’s operating theory succinctly: bend the international order in Beijing’s preferred direction not through confrontation but through accumulation — amass the “global majority,” and let the accumulated weight do the arguing. Xi’s presence in Bishkek, on this reading, is not really about Kyrgyzstan, or even primarily about Russia and Iran. It is about the running total, the visible tally of leaders willing to stand in a room with China at a moment Washington is making that increasingly costly for some of its own allies to do.

Vladimir Putin made the pitch for that accumulated weight explicit, describing the SCO as one of “the independent and influential centers of the emerging multipolar world,” and noting — accurately — that its members account for nearly half the planet’s population and more than a third of global GDP. Numbers like that are precisely the currency Xi’s approach trades in: not military commitment, not formal alliance, just scale, repeated often enough to start sounding like inevitability.

And here is the structural insight that makes the Bishkek model different in kind from anything on offer in Asheville. Zhou Bo, a retired PLA senior colonel now at Tsinghua University, put the SCO’s actual appeal plainly: it is not based on alliance relationships, and that is precisely why it isn’t provocative, and precisely why “people have no problem accepting it or engaging with it.” The Shanghai Cooperation Organization asks nothing of its members that a NATO-style alliance would ask. Beijing offers diplomatic backing to Moscow and Tehran, expanded trade, useful technology — but has been unwilling to commit any of its own forces to either country’s defense. That is not a weakness Xi is trying to paper over. It is the entire design. A country can stand next to China at a summit, absorb the reputational benefit of the association, and incur none of the reciprocal obligation that Article 5 or its equivalents would impose. Zhou’s closing line captured the logic of an entire diplomatic era: “Naturally everyone is hedging their bets.”

The cracks Beijing cannot quite paper over

None of this should be mistaken for a monolith, and the summit itself supplied the evidence. Narendra Modi, India’s prime minister, used his own sideline conversation with Putin not to reinforce the display of solidarity but to puncture it, urging the Russian leader to end the war in Ukraine “for the sake of humanity.” Putin’s reply — that Russia was “moving in this direction” — was the kind of noncommittal deflection leaders give when they have no intention of being pinned to a timeline, but Modi’s willingness to say it publicly, at Xi’s own summit, undercuts the idea that the “global majority” China claims to be assembling shares much beyond a shared discomfort with American primacy. India and China have their own long-standing tensions inside BRICS over exactly how confrontational to be with Washington, and Modi’s line in Bishkek was a reminder that India intends to keep hedging its own bets independently of whatever narrative Beijing is building around it.

Xi’s own position is less commanding than the choreography suggests, too. Even as he was courting Moscow and tolerating Tehran, his government was managing a flood along the Nepal border that has left thousands dead or missing and raised real questions about Chinese disaster preparedness, alongside a multiyear property crisis that has left Beijing more dependent on exports — the same exports now drawing new restrictions from Brussels, which is moving to slow the flow of Chinese electric vehicles, solar panels, and industrial chemicals into the European market. And Washington has not been idle on the specific relationship Xi most needs to manage before his Washington trip: the administration has banned imports of Chinese robots, expanded sanctions on Chinese firms importing Iranian oil, and is reportedly preparing to warn other countries away from a Chinese AI initiative on pain of exclusion from an American-led AI coalition. As Yun Sun of the Stimson Center put it, the balance Xi is trying to strike in Bishkek is a narrow one: gather enough support and endorsement to matter, “without poking Trump directly in the eye” before the two men sit down together.

The itinerary is the argument

Bishkek was never meant to stand alone, and understanding it as one stop on a longer circuit is what makes Xi’s strategy legible. He arrived in Kyrgyzstan having already hosted a string of Western leaders this year — Germany, Britain, Canada among them — a detail that cuts against any reading of Xi as simply choosing a side. From Bishkek he is expected to travel to Cairo, then on to a BRICS summit in New Delhi, before finally arriving in Washington. Each stop builds the same case in a different register: to the SCO, Xi is the champion of a multipolar alternative; to Cairo and New Delhi, he is the indispensable partner emerging economies cannot afford to ignore; to Washington, he arrives as a leader who has spent the preceding month demonstrating, visibly, that he has other places to be. Wu Xinbo, a Fudan University scholar who advises China’s Foreign Ministry, described the underlying logic without much subtlety: with the American economic engine “losing steam — or even generating negative side effects,” he said, China “must step up and play a greater role in driving and leading global growth.” That is not a diplomat’s hedge. It is a direct pitch for succession, made in public, timed to a week when the alternative model was busy providing the supporting evidence in Asheville.

Two summits, two theories of power

Put the two gatherings side by side and the contrast is not actually about who has more power in some abstract sense. It is about which country currently needs the other kind.

Asheville was run by a government confident enough in its raw leverage — economic, military, diplomatic — that it no longer bothered disguising how that leverage gets used: Russia seated without explanation, South Africa excluded by presidential fiat, American executives granted access denied to everyone else’s, a press credential dispute settled by whoever had a G7 finance minister willing to make a phone call. That is what leverage looks like when a government stops managing its optics because it has concluded the optics no longer cost it anything.

Bishkek was run by a government that cannot yet claim that kind of raw leverage and has built, instead, an entire diplomatic architecture around not needing it — an arrangement that asks nothing binding of anyone, offers reputational benefit without reciprocal risk, and grows more attractive to hedge-your-bets governments precisely in proportion to how unmanaged the alternative on offer from Washington looks. Xi does not need Modi to agree with him about Ukraine, or Pezeshkian to get a full bilateral, or the SCO to function as a real alliance. He needs the photograph, the GDP statistic, and the accumulating sense that when Washington alienates an ally, Beijing has a room ready for them that asks for nothing in return but attendance.

Two summits, one week, two theories of how the next phase of global order gets built — one by the exercise of leverage so confident it no longer needs to look coordinated, the other by the patient accumulation of company that never has to prove it agrees on anything. Which theory wins is not a question either summit answered. It is the question both of them were, without quite saying so, competing to answer first.

Postscript

A reader pushed back hard on Putin's "one-third of global GDP" line after this ran, and the pushback deserves an honest answer rather than a polite dodge. Strip China and India out of the Shanghai Cooperation Organization's membership and what remains — Russia, Iran, Pakistan, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, Belarus, Mongolia — is not a rival economic bloc by any reasonable measure. It is closer to a mid-sized country's worth of combined output, and the reader's instinct to laugh at the "global majority" framing on those terms is fair. But it's worth separating the two numbers Putin actually offered, because he didn't only claim GDP. He also claimed population — nearly half the planet's — and that figure survives the same subtraction test rather better, since it doesn't depend on China's economy at all, only on China and India's combined headcount, which is a fact of geography rather than a claim about growth or output. That distinction matters because the argument this essay made about Bishkek was never that the SCO rivals the G20 in economic weight. It was that Xi doesn't need it to. The model runs on accumulated diplomatic company and a population statistic, not GDP — which means the reader's math is correct, and also, in a way, is the point: an "ankle-biter club" that never had to prove its economic heft to begin with was exactly what made it useful to Beijing.
