Every opera has a moment after the curtain comes down, when the house lights rise and the audience finds out what it actually saw. For the Washington Opera, that moment came on the evening of September 25, 2026, a few hours after Xi Jinping’s aircraft left for Beijing, on the PBS programme Washington Week with The Atlantic.

Four reporters sat round the table with the moderator, Jeffrey Goldberg. Between them they supplied the two scenes that the three days of pageantry had kept offstage.

The Last Document

Vivian Salama of The Atlantic had been the pool reporter that day. She noted something the running coverage had passed over. At the National Archives, Trump had led Xi through America’s founding documents, and the last one he showed him was the Bill of Rights.

The First Amendment was, as she put it, “right there in writing,” the one that protects the freedom of the press. There stood the American president, beside an authoritarian who tightly controls the press in his own country, “trying to sort of show off these documents with pride, while also kind of undermining them back at the White House.”

On the same day, the television cameras came back. The networks had resumed pool coverage on the final morning of the visit, after their two-day boycott over the banning of CNN, MS NOW and Politico. In a clip the programme aired that evening, the president greeted CNN’s Kaitlan Collins in his own fashion.

“I’m surprised that CNN is here covering me,” he said. “You shouldn’t be here. You should not be here covering me. You said you weren’t going to cover me.”

A reader of Parts VII and VIII will know the sequence by now. A judge ordered the reporters restored. The administration let them in by noon. The cameras stayed away until a promise was made. And when they returned, the man who had banned them told them they had no business being there, a few hours after showing a foreign dictator the parchment that says otherwise.

Salama said the White House press corps was left with “an eerie feeling that it’s not done yet.”

Paragraph Five

The second scene was Taiwan.

In Part VIII, I wrote about a single verb: Washington’s long-standing formula that it does not support Taiwan independence, and Beijing’s years of effort to make it say oppose. The Xinhua account of the Oval Office meeting had Xi urging Trump to “adhere to the correct stance of opposing ‘Taiwan independence.’” The White House said nothing either way.

On Washington Week, Salama described how the Chinese had closed the summit. Their final statement was “very flowery,” all cooperation and partnership. Then, “tucked in, like, paragraph number five,” came a stern passage on China’s territorial claim to Taiwan, which she summarised as: the United States needs to stay out of it.

That is how Beijing writes its terms. The warmth goes up top for the cameras, and the substance goes into paragraph five for the diplomats, who read to the end.

Peter Baker of The New York Times described the American side of the same question. Trump’s advisers, he said, keep telling him there has been no change in US policy towards Taiwan. So he asks them why the president does not simply say so.

“And they say, the president’s going to do what he’s gonna do.”

Here, whole, is the Taiwan policy of the United States as of September 25, 2026. It is not “not support,” and it is not “oppose.” It is whatever he’s gonna do. Beijing has its position in paragraph five. Taipei has a $14 billion arms package still frozen, and an answer from the White House that is not an answer.

A Problem, Not a PR Problem

The panel spent a few minutes on artificial intelligence, the subject the summit had been billed around. Goldberg set Xi’s line that AI must be “always under human control” beside Trump’s remarks to the United Nations that week, where he explained that “the use of the word artificial makes intelligence fake. It makes it sound fake.” The leader of a Communist Party dictatorship, Goldberg observed, sounded “more with it” on the subject than the President of the United States.

Susan Glasser of The New Yorker answered with the line that sums up Part IX’s “SUPER!” episode better than anything I managed there: “Sometimes you have a PR problem and sometimes you just have a problem.”

The summit’s one announced AI outcome was agreement on a name. The problem was left where it was.

The Clock

Part IX closed on Keith Bradsher’s argument that what Xi took home from Washington was time: time to manage China’s housing crash, time to prepare for next autumn’s party congress, time to wait. Evan Medeiros had supplied the corollary. If the Republicans lose badly in November, he said, “the negotiation price changes because Trump will be on his back foot.”

On Washington Week, the back foot was already visible. Goldberg read from Glasser’s latest column: “40 days before the midterm elections, Washington is increasingly consumed not by Trump’s endless antics so much as by the rapidly clarifying political reality of the predicament in which the president, deeply unpopular and trapped in an unwon war of his own choosing in the Middle East, has got himself and his party. A reckoning, one far harsher than was expected even a few months ago, now seems imminent.”

Glasser said the Cook Political Report had moved 15 more House races towards the Democrats that day. The Senate, unthinkable as a Democratic prize at the start of the year, is now in play, with Iowa mentioned as a possible pickup. The president is due in Alaska next week to campaign for a Republican, which, as she noted drily, is not a great sign. Salama added that the Iran war has split his party, with former loyalists now condemning his decisions.

None of this was news to Beijing. It may be why the truce was extended by two months and not twelve, as Bradsher suggested, and why Xi brought Politburo members rather than chief executives. The Chinese side came to Washington, accepted the ceremony, left the pandas, and flew home with time on its side and the calendar to prove it.

In Beijing, they think in centuries. In Washington, the next forty days now look like a long time.

The curtain is down. The orchestra, as the press corps suspects, is still tuning.
