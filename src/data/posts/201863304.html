When I first imagined writing on Substack, I pictured something lighter than what it became — satire, primarily, the Canadian’s natural defence against a world that refuses to behave. That instinct didn’t disappear; it migrated. It found its home on Sundays. Once a week, I step back from the investigative series and the war diaries and the accountability tallies, and I try to make someone smile. Call it a day of rest. Call it maintenance. Call it what it is: a reset before the onslaught of the new week descends on us all.

I have come to believe that this reset is not optional. It is, in some fundamental sense, what keeps the machinery running.

Which is why I find Donald Trump not merely unpleasant, not merely dangerous, but genuinely difficult to comprehend. Because by every available measure — and I mean this as diagnosis, not insult — the man is entirely without humour. Not unfunny in the way that some people simply lack timing or can’t tell a joke. Unfunny at a deeper level. Constitutionally, architecturally, perhaps irreversibly unfunny.

A British writer named Nate White, asked some years ago why his countrymen found Trump so difficult to stomach, put it plainly: Trump has never once said anything rye, witty, or even faintly amusing. Not once, not ever. White meant it literally. So do I.

But why? That is the more interesting question.

Philosophers have been arguing about the nature of humour for roughly as long as they have been arguing about anything else, which is to say forever, and with comparable results. Three theories have survived the centuries with their reputations more or less intact.

The first is the superiority theory, traceable to Hobbes: we laugh when we perceive ourselves as superior to the object of ridicule. This is Trump’s entire operating theory of comedy — the cruel nickname, the physical mockery, the sneering dismissal. He believes, one suspects genuinely, that this is what funny looks like. It is not. What he produces is not laughter but a kind of uncomfortable recognition, the way one might watch a child pull the wings off a fly. The audience has to be in on a joke for it to work. Cruelty directed downward does not invite shared laughter; it invites the question of whose side you are on.

The second is the incongruity theory, associated with Kant and Schopenhauer: humour arises from the collision of two incompatible frames of reference. The setup leads you one way; the punchline snaps you somewhere else entirely. This is the mechanism behind almost every joke worth telling. It requires the capacity to hold two ideas simultaneously — to build one expectation and then detonate it. Trump demonstrably cannot do this. His mind, as White observed, operates as a simple algorithm: petty grievance in, petty grievance out. There is no second frame because there is no depth in which a second frame could hide. No depth, no surprise. No surprise, no laughter. This is not a rhetorical flourish. It is a structural observation about how his thinking appears to work.

The third theory is Freud’s relief theory: laughter releases psychic tension around forbidden or anxiety-producing thoughts. Great political satire has always worked this way — it lets us say the unsayable, laugh at the terrifying, survive by making the monstrous briefly ridiculous. This is what court jesters were for. It is what Leacock was for. It is what, on a good Sunday, I am modestly attempting. Trump’s humour, such as it is, moves in the opposite direction: it creates anxiety rather than releasing it, because the target is always someone with less power, and there is always the implication that the laughter is not shared but enforced.

There is a moment that crystallizes all of this. In 2011, at the White House Correspondents’ Dinner, Barack Obama spent several minutes methodically, brilliantly, and with evident pleasure dismantling Donald Trump — who was seated in the room, stone-faced, as the laughter swelled around him. Obama’s timing was impeccable. The writing was sharp. The audience, including many of Trump’s allies, was helpless with laughter.

April 30, 2011. The night President Obama took on Donald Trump

Trump did not laugh. Not once. He sat with the expression of a man watching his own execution, which in a sense he was — because what Obama was doing, effortlessly and publicly, was something Trump cannot do and cannot survive: he was demonstrating that the powerful can be made ridiculous, and that the ability to laugh at oneself is a form of strength, not weakness.

Some historians believe that evening radicalized Trump’s ambitions in ways that shaped everything that followed. A man who cannot take a joke, given sufficient power, eventually stops tolerating them. This is not a small thing.

The humourlessness, I would argue, is not incidental to Trump’s pathology. It is load-bearing. The same cognitive architecture that makes genuine wit impossible — the inability to perceive one’s own absurdity, the absence of self-awareness, the grandiosity that cannot coexist with self-deprecation — is what makes accountability impossible too. You cannot laugh at yourself if you cannot see yourself clearly. And if you cannot see yourself clearly, the feedback loops that keep most of us tethered to reality simply do not engage.

Humour is, among other things, a reality-testing mechanism. When something is genuinely funny, it is because it has captured something true — an incongruity that was always there but unnoticed, a pretension that needed puncturing. To be capable of humour is to be capable of truth-recognition. To be entirely without it is to live in a sealed room where everything reflects only yourself back at you, undistorted and unchallenged.

That is the world Trump inhabits. It is, as White said, not merely unpleasant. It is almost inhuman.

Tomorrow, the sport turns 80. He will mark the occasion, characteristically, with a UFC fight on the White House lawn — because nothing says “I have a rich inner life” quite like organized violence as a birthday party. Eighty years on this earth, and not one joke. Not one moment of genuine self-deprecation. Not one laugh that wasn’t at someone else’s expense.

Glenn Kirschner, the former federal prosecutor who has made a parallel career dissecting Trump’s legal exposure, has found his own perfect method of deflation: he calls him “sport.” Not Mr. President. Not even Trump. Just — sport. The thing you call a child who has gotten above himself. There is no insult to punch back at, no grievance to nurse. Just a mildly patronizing term of address that implies, without elaboration, that none of this is quite as serious as the birthday boy believes.

Marilyn Monroe JFK Happy Birthday Madison Square Garden 1962

There will be no Marilyn Monroe moment tomorrow. No breathless, sequined serenade. No Happy Birthday, Mr. President drifting across the White House lawn while a nation watches, charmed. Just a UFC fight, a man who cannot take a joke turning 80, and the rest of us, quietly, offering the only greeting that fits.

I think about this on Sundays when I sit down to write something that might, if I am lucky, produce a smile somewhere. The act feels small. It is not. Laughter is how we process the unbearable, reset the overloaded circuits, remember that even the most grandiose figures are, at bottom, absurd. It is how we survive weeks like the ones we keep having.

A man with no reset button, with no capacity for the relief that laughter provides, with no ability to step outside himself and find any of it even faintly amusing — such a man accumulates. The grievances stack. The scores mount. Nothing discharges. Everything is always urgent and personal and deadly serious, because there is no other register available.

That is not just a miserable way to live. In a man with power, it is a blueprint for everything we have been watching.

So yes: I will keep writing on Sundays. Someone has to hold the reset button down.

Happy Birthday, sport. From Glenn, from me, and I suspect from millions of others.
