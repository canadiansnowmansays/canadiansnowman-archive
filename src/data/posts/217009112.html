A word first used in Davos

In January 2026, Mark Carney stood before the World Economic Forum in Davos and told the room, to a standing ovation, that the US-led rules-based international order was “over.” He urged the world’s “middle powers” — nations too small individually to set terms with Washington or Beijing, but numerous and wealthy enough collectively to matter — to band together rather than wait for great powers to decide their fate for them. The timing made the speech more combustible than it might otherwise have been: Trump had, only days earlier, publicly praised a new Canada-China trade arrangement Carney had just announced, calling it “good for him.” Once the Davos remarks reached Washington, the goodwill evaporated within a week. Trump called Canada’s economy dependent on the United States for its survival, pulled Canada out of his so-called “Board of Peace” initiative, and threatened 100% tariffs on all Canadian goods — the same week he referred to Carney, for the first time, by the nickname he had previously reserved for Justin Trudeau: “Governor.” That collision, and the trade war that followed from it, is its own story, told in full elsewhere. What matters for this series is simpler: eight months later, with the trade relationship still unresolved, Carney used the word “rupture” again — this time on American soil, a few blocks from the man who had reacted so badly to it the first time.

No podium this time

The most telling fact about Carney’s UN General Assembly appearance this September is what he didn’t do there. Canada’s floor speech to the 81st session went to Foreign Affairs Minister Anita Anand; Carney himself gave no address to the Assembly at all, spending his three days in New York on bilateral meetings, a roundtable on “strengthening multilateral collaboration,” and a leader-level event on the two-state solution he co-hosted with France and Britain. Davos had been a set-piece: a scheduled address, a packed hall, a standing ovation that made headlines on its own terms. New York was a press scrum after a caucus-adjacent news conference, the word delivered to reporters rather than to an assembly of peers. It travelled from the biggest stage available to a head of government down to one of the smallest — and arrived, if anything, sharper for the demotion.

The word itself, evolved

What Carney said in New York was not a repeat of January so much as a broadening of it. “This is, in many respects, a time that comes once or twice every century,” he told reporters, describing a “rupture” to the world order — but where the Davos speech had located the rupture specifically in American conduct, the UN remarks named two authors rather than one: a “rupture to the world order by the United States and China.” Eight months of watching both great powers manoeuvre against the multilateral system he’d defended in Switzerland appears to have changed the diagnosis, if not the underlying alarm. He offered “values-based realism” as his position between two failure modes he named explicitly: “wishful multilateralism, which defends only the status quo,” and “a cold realpolitik that dismantles the status quo altogether.” It is, in substance, the same argument Davos made — that the old order cannot simply be preserved by insisting on it, nor abandoned by declaring it dead — restated with eight more months of evidence behind it.

From diagnosis to institution

The more significant development since January is not rhetorical but structural. At Davos, Carney’s “middle powers must band together” line was, by his own admission at the time, short on specifics — a call to arms without an organisation attached to it. By September, it had one. Carney used the New York trip to advance “Partners for Multilateralism,” an initiative launched with the European Union and six other nations (delayed a day by air traffic control disruptions on his way in, a small, almost bathetic footnote to an otherwise weighty announcement). He was careful to define it by what it is not: “This is not a bloc or a bureaucracy,” he said, in French, but “a new forum that looks at universal institutions, practical coalitions and capacities that exist outside of governments in order to build trust and bring people together.” The operating logic is ad hoc rather than standing — “groups formed around problems rather than geography or size” — with Canada’s own work alongside France and Britain on the two-state solution, and separate cooperation on ocean preservation, offered as evidence the model already functions rather than as aspiration. The stated end state is not a rival to the UN but a feeder into it: coalitions that form around a specific problem, solve what they can, and then bring the result back to bodies like the Security Council or the Assembly “to be strengthened, to be broadened and to be scaled.”

Whether that last step is realistic is worth treating with some scepticism. A coalition of the willing can solve a problem outside the UN’s machinery. Getting the UN’s own machinery to then adopt, broaden, and scale that solution runs straight back into the same veto-bound Security Council this series’ companion project on the UN has spent all year documenting. Carney did not address that tension directly in New York, and it is fair to note that no one has yet asked him to.

It is worth noting, too, what “Partners for Multilateralism” is explicitly not trying to be: a bloc aligned against the United States, or a replacement for NATO, the G7, or any of the institutions Canada already belongs to. Carney’s own language — “calibrated alliances, integrating deeply with those countries who share our values, remaining open-eyed with those who do not” — is carefully built to avoid the appearance of choosing sides between Washington and everyone else, even as the initiative’s practical effect is to give Canada options that do not run through Washington at all. That is a difficult balance to hold rhetorically, and Carney has, so far, mostly managed it by simply not naming names.

The Trump question, still unanswered

The personal stakes underneath the diplomatic language were higher this time than in January. New York marked the first Carney-Trump face-to-face opportunity since trade talks between the two countries collapsed last month, after Carney recalled his own negotiating team from Washington over what he described as unacceptable eleventh-hour demands from the American side. Asked directly, at a caucus retreat before he left Ottawa, whether he planned to meet Trump in New York, Carney offered nothing: “We’ll meet with lots of leaders in New York.” A senior official briefing reporters was no more forthcoming: “Nothing is confirmed right now.” Whether the two men crossed paths at all during UNGA week is not established in the public record as of this writing.

It is worth being precise about just how far Carney’s language on that trade collapse actually went, because it is sharper than anything else in this piece. He did not simply say talks had failed. He said Canada was “at war” with the United States — “You’re at war when you’re attacked, and we got attacked.” That is a considerably harder line than “rupture” or “sovereign country,” and it puts Carney’s approach to Trump in useful relief against a fellow leader making his own first visit to the White House orbit the same week: Britain’s new prime minister, Andy Burnham, meeting Trump in person for the first time on Tuesday, went out of his way to avoid confrontation — skipping past trade and the disputed Chagos Islands entirely, backing Trump’s posture on Iran and the Strait of Hormuz, agreeing with him on immigration — and was rewarded with Trump calling him a “natural business person” who “gets it.” One NYT account of the Burnham meeting noted explicitly that Britain’s aides expect their prime minister to take a more cautious approach with Trump than either Carney or Spain’s Pedro Sánchez has taken in recent months. Three leaders, three visible strategies for handling the same unpredictable American president: accommodate quietly and be praised for it, as Burnham did; or say the word “war” out loud and mean it, as Carney has now done twice in two different registers within the space of a month.

One unscripted exchange from the same period is worth preserving regardless. Asked what it might mean for Canada that Mexico’s Claudia Sheinbaum was reportedly close to her own bilateral deal with Trump, Carney’s answer ran to five words: “It means we’re a sovereign country.” It is a smaller line than “rupture,” and it will not make headlines the way Davos did. But it carries the same argument in miniature — that Canada’s choices are its own to make, deal or no deal, ally or no ally — compressed into something closer to Question Period than to a World Economic Forum keynote. It is also, as it happens, the line this series takes its name from.

“He’s speaking the language of war”

The most direct thing Carney said all week had nothing to do with rupture, realism, or reform. Asked about Trump’s threat, delivered from the UN podium that same Tuesday, to “annihilate” Iran, Carney answered in French, flatly: “There is a war underway, and he’s speaking the language of war.” No hedge, no diplomatic softening, no attempt to contextualise it as a negotiating tactic the way several American commentators did within the hour. It is the shortest sentence Carney is on record saying about Trump all year, and arguably the most legible one — a head of government from an allied, dependent, economically entangled country declining, in eight words, to pretend that what he’d just watched was anything other than what it plainly was.

What this sets up

Davos gave Carney his most quoted line and his angriest reaction from Washington. New York gave something quieter and, in its way, more useful: the same diagnosis, tested against eight more months of evidence, broadened to include a second great power, and this time attached to an actual initiative rather than only a warning. Whether “Partners for Multilateralism” becomes the beginning of the alternative architecture Carney gestured at in Switzerland, or simply another well-intentioned coalition that dissolves the first time its members disagree about something that actually matters, remains to be seen.

But rhetoric is the easy half of this story, and Carney’s critics — in Washington and at home — have not been shy about pointing out that a Canadian prime minister declaring the old order “over” is one thing, and a Canadian economy actually able to stand on its own terms is another. That is a testable claim, not a rhetorical one. Part II sets the words aside and goes to the numbers: GDP, interest rates, unemployment, inflation, trade exposure, and debt, measured directly against the United States over the same eight months Carney has spent making the diplomatic case. Some of what the data shows will support him. Some of it will not. Both halves are worth having on the record before deciding whether “sovereign country” is a description or an aspiration.
