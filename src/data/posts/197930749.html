Thucydides, 431 BC. Not too long ago, apparently

The costumes were packed. The reviews were filed. Air Force One was somewhere over the Pacific, refuelling in Alaska, and the president was talking to reporters. That is when it happened.

Asked about Taiwan, Trump revealed that Xi had posed the question directly: would the United States defend Taiwan if China attacked?

Trump refused to answer. “I said, ‘I don’t talk about those things,’” he told the press pool.

Then he went further. He suggested he might reconsider the $14 billion arms package that Taiwan’s legislature had just approved $25 billion to pay for. And when a reporter reminded him that Ronald Reagan had assured Taiwan, more than forty years ago, that no American president would consult Beijing on the size or nature of arms sales to the island, Trump dismissed it.

“That was a long time ago.”

Six words. Forty-four years of American security architecture in the Indo-Pacific, reduced to a shrug at cruising altitude. The Reagan assurances — the Six Assurances of 1982, a pillar of U.S.-Taiwan and U.S.-China policy — are not ancient history. They are the reason Taiwan has F-16s. They are the reason a democracy of twenty-three million people has not yet been absorbed by a party-state of 1.4 billion. And the president of the United States waved them away between Beijing and Anchorage as though they were a restaurant recommendation from a previous administration.

R. Nicholas Burns, Biden’s ambassador to Beijing, offered the verdict that none of Trump’s own officials would: the president’s approach “weakens Trump and the U.S.” He added: “Xi did not hesitate to warn Trump over Taiwan. Trump should not hesitate to be frank about our concerns, too.”

Peter Baker of the New York Times, speaking on Washington Week with the Atlantic, was more direct: Trump "was the supplicant in a way, coming to his territory, to his turf. And he didn't give anything away." Susan Glasser of the New Yorker went further: "If any other president of our recent lifetime, Democrat or Republican, had gone to China and said the things that Donald Trump has been saying, we would be having major front page headlines saying Donald Trump reverses American foreign policy in Asia.

Trump hesitated. Then he capitulated. Not in the Great Hall, not under the cameras, not under the pressure of the pageantry — but freely, on his own plane, without Chinese officials present, in his own words. That is worse. The accommodation in Beijing could be explained as diplomatic courtesy. The dismissal of Reagan’s assurances on Air Force One cannot.

The Troianovski and Sanger analysis that landed Friday afternoon framed the shift precisely: “He has thrown aside the adversarial approach of his first years in office, the Biden administration and the beginning of his own second term.” Da Wei of Tsinghua University was blunter: “The U.S. side looked a little passive. The Chinese side prepared very well.”

Sun Chenghao, also at Tsinghua, delivered the line that should keep American strategists awake: “Everyone has a learning curve. Mr. Trump knows how to deal with China.” The implication was unmistakable. He learned to accommodate.

And Evan Medeiros of Georgetown, a former senior Asia director on the National Security Council, named what Xi had built with his new phrase — “constructive strategic stability” — for exactly what it is: “These phrases are geopolitical quicksand. Once you step in you cannot get out, and the more you try, the deeper you get pulled in by China.”

Rubio, on NBC Friday, confirmed the quicksand had already reached the ankles. The Trump administration, he said, endorsed the concept: “strategic stability in our relationship, a constructive relationship.” Xi proposed the frame. Rubio accepted it. The cage door closed.

The White House, for its part, shared glossy videos of the president’s grand welcome and his appearance at a state banquet where the menu included lobster in tomato soup and roast duck. And as Air Force One lifted off for the return trip, the president posted his principal reflection on the visit to Truth Social: “China has a Ballroom, and so should the U.S.A.! It’s under construction, ahead of schedule, and will be the finest facility of its kind anywhere in the U.S.A.”

Xi got strategic stability. Trump got interior design inspiration.

Glasser, on the same programme, offered the summary that none of the official readouts would contain: "The difference here is that it is not clear at the moment what side the United States of America is on.”

One more thing.

One detail from the trip deserves its own sentence. Eric Trump — who leads the Trump family business, which has pursued Chinese business deals for years — accompanied his father to Beijing. The lines between government diplomacy and private enterprise were not blurred. They were erased. The son who runs the commercial operation sat in the rooms where the geopolitical concessions were made. Xi, who controls access to the world's second-largest consumer market, would have noticed.

While the cameras rolled and the state media celebrated and the foreign ministry issued readouts that described two different meetings, a quieter conversation was happening on the other side of the Great Firewall.

Li Yuan of The New York Times reported that Chinese citizens — posting anonymously on Threads and X, platforms banned inside China — were watching the same summit the rest of the world was watching. They saw the choreographed children and compared them to North Korean rallies for Kim Jong-un. They heard Xi’s “great changes unseen in a century” and recognized the phrases from their compulsory university exams on Xi Jinping Thought. They joked that Trump could now appreciate what it was like to sit through those classes. They noted that Xi appeared to be exactly the same height as Trump — and every other world leader he has ever stood beside.

One user, a 47-year-old small-business owner in California who asked not to be named because he still visits family in China, wrote: “A society that is truly confident doesn’t need children chanting slogans to prove its enthusiasm. A country that is genuinely open doesn’t need to repackage diplomatic events as collective performances.”

He asked the question that the Great Hall was built to suppress: “Is China moving backward?”

It is, as Li Yuan wrote, equal parts hilarious and heartbreaking — an act of trying to find humour in absurdity and cruelty. The jokes are more than jokes. They are the reviews that will never appear in Xinhua.

Nancy Youssef of The Atlantic, the Pentagon correspondent, identified the timeline that connects this summit to the war diary. Speaking on Washington Week with the Atlantic, she reported that according to U.S. intelligence, Iran believes it can hold out through the midterms. “In some ways they have checkmated the United States in terms of the timeline.” The Beijing Opera is connected to the Tehran one. It always was.

And Franklin Foer, writing in The Atlantic on the morning after, delivered the review that contains all the others: “Xi used the visit to humor the lame-duck president, waiting for his time to pass.” The Beijing Opera was never performed for Trump. It was performed for the world watching Trump. The audience understood. The leading man did not.

Back home, the week's optics were curated with equal care. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy starred in a YouTube series promoting "The Great American Road Trip" while gas sat at $4.52. FBI Director Kash Patel took a VIP snorkelling excursion at Pearl Harbor.

The president who told reporters this week that he does not think about Americans' financial situation thought about ballrooms instead. The Beijing Opera was never performed for the people paying $4.52 a gallon.

The Beijing Opera is over. The building remains. But so do the people inside it, watching from behind the wall, laughing at what they cannot say aloud.
