There is a particular look of his I keep returning to this week — that grin he aims at a camera, the one that says I know something you don’t, and it’s going to cost you your composure. He turned one hundred yesterday. I have been laughing at this man since before I had grey hair, which, I regret to report, was some time ago. It seems only decent to say something.

I should confess my credentials up front, in the interest of the evidence-based reasoning I am forever demanding of American politicians and only occasionally supplying myself. I am not a Brooks scholar. I am something worse: a fan. I have seen all of the films, and I have seen most of them so many times that I no longer watch them so much as accompany them, the way one hums along to a hymn whose words were installed in childhood and never removed. I can tell you when the Waco Kid is about to say “morons.” I know which water glass Zero Mostel throws. This is not expertise. This is devotion, and devotion is harder to argue with.

So this is not analysis. This is a tip of the hat from one Canadian who fell early for an American genius and never quite recovered. Pull up a chair.

The animal who read Gogol

Here is the detail that made me love him as a grown man rather than merely as a boy who liked the funny parts.

When Brooks first saw his own name in the credits of Your Show of Shows — Sid Caesar’s writers’ room, which also happened to contain Carl Reiner, Neil Simon, and Larry Gelbart, possibly the greatest concentration of comic talent ever assembled in one underheated room — he did not feel triumphant. He felt like a fraud. A kid from Brooklyn who had bluffed his way in among literate men. And so he marched to the library and checked out armfuls of the heaviest books he could carry. Gogol, Dostoevsky, Conrad, Tolstoy. He read them, by his own account, to find out “what these bastards do.”

The writer who seized him, and held him for the rest of his life, was Nikolai Gogol. Dead Souls in particular — that unfinished, deranged, glorious parade of Russian grotesques. I find this enormously clarifying, and not only because it gives me, a man who has spent a year and a half writing in the footsteps of Stephen Leacock, license to feel that comedians are permitted their literary heroes. Gogol is the patron saint of the exact thing Brooks would spend his life perfecting: the absurdity piled on absurdity until, somewhere past the point of sense, it arrives at a truth that earnestness could never reach. Gogol’s con man travels the countryside buying the names of dead serfs, and somehow it becomes a portrait of an entire rotting society. That is the Brooks method, fully formed, written a century before Brooks was born.

I take a certain Canadian comfort in this, and I will own the bias now rather than spring it on you later. I write these essays from Toronto, a long way north of Brooklyn, in the tradition of another Stephen — Leacock — who built a whole body of work on the proposition that the man standing slightly outside the room, watching the powerful make their absurd decisions, is very often the one who sees the thing most clearly. We outsiders are forever being told that the view from outside is unserious. Leacock heard it. Brooks, the Brooklyn animal with the library card, heard it. And both of them understood that the clown reading Tolstoy is not a contradiction. He is the whole point.

France, 1945

You cannot understand the man without the war, and the war is the part we fans tend to hurry past, because it does not come with a punchline attached.

A boy from Brooklyn, barely out of his teens, shipped to Europe as the Second World War ground toward its bloody finish. A combat engineer. Close enough to the fighting that, in one of his tellings, the road exploded around his command car, and the thought that surfaced in young Melvin Kaminsky’s head was not valour but bewilderment — why do they hate us, we don’t even know them. He has been telling that story for eighty years. It does not resolve, because there is no resolution to be had.

Consider the arithmetic of it. Here is a young Jewish man who arrived in Europe in time to stand at the very edge of the most efficient atrocity in human history — an industrial program designed expressly to erase people exactly like him from the face of the earth. And his life’s work, his answer to it, was to take the architect of that horror and put him in a sequined musical number. Springtime for Hitler. I want my American friends, who are presently relearning some hard lessons about how democracies come apart, to sit with the nerve of that. This is not a man who failed to grasp the gravity of what happened. This is a man who grasped it so completely that he understood the one weapon a tyrant cannot survive. Not solemnity — a dictator feeds on solemnity. Laughter. You cannot goose-step while the audience is giggling at you.

When a critic once sniffed that he, the critic, had served in the war and found The Producers offensive, Brooks replied: “So was I. I didn’t see you there.” I have read a great many ripostes in my time. I am not sure I have read a better one.

The films, briefly, with love

I promised this would not be a lecture, so I will be disciplined, which is not my native condition.

The Producers (1967) contains everything he would ever do, already complete. Watch what he does with that opening-night audience inside the film — they recoil in horror at the swastikas, and then, helplessly, against their own will, they begin to laugh. Brooks is staging, inside his own movie, the precise trick he intends to perform on you. Horror turned to laughter in real time. He won the Oscar for the screenplay and gave, I will insist on this, the finest acceptance speech ever delivered: “I’ll just say what’s in my heart. Ba-bump, ba-bump, ba-bump.” A century of that heartbeat, and he summarized it in four syllables before most of us had finished sitting down.

Blazing Saddles (1974) is the masterpiece, and a more radical piece of work than its fart jokes get credit for. Brooks looked at the Western — America’s flattering bedtime story about itself, the clean frontier and the noble settlers and the conveniently empty land — and called it, flatly, a lie. Then, rather than make a solemn correction, he blew the whole genre apart from the inside until the fistfight crashed through the soundstage wall and spilled onto the neighbouring sets, the lie exposed as exactly what it was: a movie. Richard Pryor co-wrote it. The joke always points at the bigots, never at the man they hate. People say it could not be made today. Dave Chappelle has the right answer: it is not that it could not be made today, it is that only one man on earth could ever have made it, and he already did.

Young Frankenstein (1974), the same year — and here is the proof he was never merely a wrecking ball. Shot in luminous black and white, on the actual laboratory equipment from the 1931 Universal film, with a tenderness toward its source that borders on reverence. This is the secret of the man. The parody is not contempt. It is the most intimate possible form of love. You cannot send up those old horror pictures with such precision unless they were wired into you on Friday nights at a Brooklyn movie house, a salami sandwich packed from home. The monster, in Brooks’s hands, gets civilized, gets married, gets the girl. The man who hated death took the Frankenstein story and made it a comedy about a dead thing brought joyfully back to life. Read that twice and tell me it is an accident.

Brooks in High Anxiety (1977), his send-up of Hitchcock — made, characteristically, by a man who loved Hitchcock far too much to leave him alone.. (20th Century Fox publicity still, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.)

And then the long shelf I will not do justice to here — Silent Movie, High Anxiety, Spaceballs, History of the World, Part I with its immortal civics lesson, “It’s good to be the king.” I will not pretend the whole shelf is gold. There are later pictures — Life Stinks, Dracula: Dead and Loving It — where the great man swung and the ball did not leave the infield, and it would be a poor sort of admirer who failed to notice. But here is the thing about a man who takes a hundred swings: you remember the ones that cleared the fence, and the fences he cleared are some of the tallest in the game. I have watched them all more times than a serious person would admit. I am not a serious person about this. I have made my peace with it.

A hundred candles, one crown, and not a trace of abdication. Note the foundation stones: even the misses hold up the throne. It is, as the man himself instructed us, good to be the king.

Anne, and Carl, and Gene

The man who would do anything for a laugh would not, it turns out, joke about the people he loved.

He met Anne Bancroft when she was the more famous of the two, walked up afterward, and said, “I’m Mel Brooks. Hiya, A.” Forty-one years. Spaghetti she cooked on their wedding night. She called him “incandescent,” said he glowed. When she died he did not remarry, and when asked why, the perpetual clown gave the least clownish answer of his life: once you have been married to Anne Bancroft, no one else seems appealing.

Carl Reiner was his best friend for the better part of a lifetime and the straight man who, with a microphone and no warning, would ambush him into inventing the 2,000-Year-Old Man on the spot — because, Reiner said, “a brilliant mind in panic is a wonderful thing to see.” And Gene Wilder, whom he directed to the outer edge of hysteria and beyond, kept in his office a photograph inscribed To my son Gene, with love, Daddy Mel. The loud man, it turns out, was running on an enormous quiet engine of love. The noise was never the point. The noise was the protest.

Comedy as a hatred of death

Which brings me to the line that unlocks the whole man, and it is the one I cannot stop turning over.

Brooks has said, again and again, that comedy is at bottom not a fear of death but a hatred of it. Sit with that distinction, because it is the entire person. Fear is passive. Fear makes you cautious, deferential, careful not to offend, careful never to take the big swing. Hatred of death is active. It is a raised fist. It sends a man charging up the hill bare-chested, shouting, in Wilder’s marvellous description of him, “Look at me!” and “Don’t let me die!” Every gag is a punch thrown at oblivion. “Laughter,” he wrote, “is a protest scream against death, against the long goodbye.”

He gave the loveliest version of it to Playboy, of all places — the image of infinitesimal bits of coral dying one upon another until, slowly, something rises out of the sea that you can stand on, live on, an entire island built from small things expiring in the right order. Writing, he said, is one thought dying upon the one before it. A life, I would add, is the same. He has been laying down coral for a hundred years, and the rest of us get to stand on the island.

The joke he refused to let die

I have saved my favourite proof for last, because it is the most Brooksian thing imaginable, and because it speaks directly to the marvel of his still being here, still working, at an age when most men have long since handed in their tools.

At the end of History of the World, Part I, back in 1981, Brooks ran a mock trailer for a sequel that did not exist and was never meant to. “Hitler on Ice.” “Jews in Space.” The entire joke depended on Part II never arriving. It was a throwaway — a punchline with no body attached.

And then, in 2023, at the age of ninety-six, he made it real. History of the World, Part II arrived as an eight-episode event, with a younger generation — Nick Kroll, Wanda Sykes, Ike Barinholtz — doing the heavy lifting, and Brooks himself back in the narrator’s chair, the very role Orson Welles had filled in the original film. Think about that gesture. Most men at ninety-six are not settling accounts with a joke they told when Pierre Trudeau was still in office. Brooks went back for it. He would not permit even an abandoned bit to die unredeemed.

It was of a piece with the rest of that astonishing late stretch. The memoir — All About Me! — he wrote during the pandemic lockdown and published at ninety-five, recording all fifteen hours of the audiobook himself, so that whoever bought it (I did) got the stories in that unmistakable Brooklyn rasp, as sharp at ninety-five as it had ever been. The man is not coasting on a legacy. He is still adding to it. Still laying coral. Still throwing punches.

That is the hatred of death made literal. Not the fear that tidies its affairs and goes quiet, but the active, stubborn refusal of a man who looks at a forty-year-old joke and decides, at ninety-six, that it is not too late to land it.

One hundred years

I write, most weeks, about the American experiment going wrong — the institutions strained, the norms abandoned, the long sad catalogue of a great country forgetting itself. It is a relief, for once, to write about the experiment going right. Because that is what Mel Brooks is. The fatherless son of immigrants, raised poor in Depression Brooklyn by a mother returning milk bottles for movie money, a runty kid who decided that the laugh was the great equalizer and was correct — and who turned all of it, the poverty and the war and the fear and the anger, into a hundred years of joy handed out to strangers. You could have been born at any moment in the long human story. You happen to be alive at one that still contains Mel Brooks. The odds against it are absurd. I choose to be grateful.

The view from Toronto, fifty winters deep. A Canadian, a husky, and a century of evidence that the funniest man who ever lived was working just south of the border the whole time.

Now ourselves, he always said. Not future ourselves. Now. So, right now, on the hundredth year of that ba-bump, ba-bump, ba-bump: thank you, Mr. Kaminsky, for the loudest and most defiant noise anyone ever made against the dark.

And thank you, Stephen Leacock, for teaching a different Stephen, a long way north of Brooklyn, that the man laughing at the back of the room is very often the one who has seen the whole thing most clearly.

Long live the king.

Happy birthday, Mr. Kaminsky — one hundred years, and counting. Keep it up and the 2,000-Year-Old Man stops being a bit and starts being a documentary.

— Stephen Gordon, Toronto
