Super Bowl XXVII, Rose Bowl, Pasadena, 1993. Michael Jackson's halftime performance of "We Are the World" — 98,000 people, a giant globe, 3,500 children from around the world, and a four-minute reminder that the choice to be neighbours has always been available to us. Twenty-one scorecards later, the question is the same

This series began with a seventy-five-second video and a question: Is your nation making humanity better or worse?

Twenty-one scorecards later, I have an answer. It is not the one I expected when I started.

What the Numbers Say

Stare at these two tables long enough and patterns emerge that no individual essay could reveal on its own.

The first is the most uncomfortable: the nations that contribute most to humanity are, without exception, nations that chose not to pursue dominance. Not one country in the top ten is a major military power. Not one is a permanent member of the UN Security Council. Not one possesses nuclear weapons. The top ten are nations that opted, at some point in their history, to invest in people and planet rather than in the machinery of global projection.

The second pattern is the inverse: every major military and economic power lands on the underperformer list. The United States, Russia, China, India, Saudi Arabia — the nations that dominate headlines, shape geopolitics, and set the terms of global discourse — are, by the evidence assembled across these twenty-one essays, the nations contributing least to humanity relative to what they could contribute. Power and contribution are not merely uncorrelated. They are inversely related.

The third pattern is the one the framework was designed to reveal: the Neighbour Test is the great separator. On human development, the gap between the two lists is meaningful but not always enormous. Australia scores 42 — close to Denmark’s 45. On environmental stewardship, there are overlaps. But on the Neighbour Test, the two lists diverge completely. The average Neighbour Test score for the top ten is 36.9 out of 50. For the underperformers, it is 13.0. The gap is a chasm.

This confirms the thesis that motivated elevating the Neighbour Test to its own category: the disposition of a nation toward the rest of humanity is not one factor among many. It is the factor that predicts everything else. A nation that chooses solidarity scores well across the board. A nation that chooses self-interest scores poorly across the board. The Neighbour Test does not merely measure character. It reveals destiny.

What Surprised Me

Four things I did not expect when I began this project.

First, the Neighbour Test’s predictive power. I hypothesized that disposition toward global citizenship would correlate with contribution. I did not expect the correlation to be this stark. The top ten’s average Neighbour Test score is nearly three times the underperformers’. No other category produces this ratio. Disposition is not merely important. It is determinative.

Second, Costa Rica. I knew the story before I started. I had read the data. I had prepared the scorecard. And still, writing that essay — holding Costa Rica’s numbers against the superpowers, watching a nation of five million people with no army and a modest economy outperform nations with four hundred times its resources — was the most important thing I did in this series. The Costa Rica Principle is not an abstraction. It is the entire argument, compressed into a single country.

Third, Bhutan. The Special Edition essay on the world’s only carbon-negative country produced the most instructive scorecard in the series — not because it was the highest or lowest, but because it refused to fit. A nation scoring 47 out of 50 on environmental stewardship — the highest single-category score across all twenty-one scorecards — while carrying the unresolved wound of the Lhotshampa expulsion. Bhutan is proof that environmental virtue is not moral completeness, and that a nation can be the best ecological citizen on Earth while failing its own people in ways that are profound, deliberate, and ongoing. If Bhutan had resolved the Lhotshampa question, it would rank among the top performers. That it has not is the most important unfinished story in this series.

Fourth, the innovation question was harder than I anticipated. The United States essay forced me to wrestle with the strongest counterargument to this framework: that incidental innovation spillovers — GPS, vaccines, internet protocols — represent a form of contribution that the Contribution Index underweights. I stand by my distinction between intentional contribution and incidental spillover. But I acknowledge that reasonable people can weigh these differently, and I suspect this will be the most debated methodological choice in the series.

What I Got Wrong — Or At Least, What I Would Reconsider

No framework this ambitious gets everything right.

The Neighbour Test, while I believe its elevation was correct, contains more editorial judgment than the other three categories. I have been as transparent as possible about my reasoning, and where the judgment is genuinely ambiguous, I have said so in the individual essays. But I would not pretend that scoring “consistency of values versus interests” is as empirically grounded as scoring emissions per capita.

The capacity ranking, while more transparent than my original attempt, remains imperfect. Institutional quality is harder to quantify than GDP, and some readers will reasonably argue that my capacity estimates are too generous to certain nations and not generous enough to others. I welcome that debate.

And the Iran conflict, which reshaped the US and Israel essays during the writing of this series, is still unfolding. Scores that reflect a moment of escalation may look different six months from now — or they may look even worse. The Contribution Index captures 2026. It does not predict 2027.

The Costa Rica Principle, Revisited

I return, as I must, to the insight that anchors this series.

We have been told — by economists, by political scientists, by the nations themselves — that contribution to humanity is a luxury. That you must first become wealthy, then powerful, then secure, and only then can you afford to think about the rest of the world. That the path to national virtue runs through national dominance.

Twenty-one scorecards say otherwise.

The nation that scored highest on environmental stewardship in this entire series — top ten and underperformers combined — is not Norway, not Denmark, not any Nordic nation with a trillion-dollar sovereign wealth fund. It is Costa Rica, tied with Bhutan. Countries that decided, decades ago, that they did not need armies and GDP growth to justify their existence, and spent their resources on forests and schools and hospitals instead.

The nations at the bottom of the Neighbour Test are not impoverished nations lacking the resources to engage with the world. They are the wealthiest, most powerful, most capable nations in human history. They have more than enough. They choose not to share it.

Contribution is not a consequence of capacity. It is a choice that precedes capacity, that shapes capacity, that ultimately defines what capacity is for.

What Nations Owe Each Other

Way Yuhl’s original question was whether we could rank nations by contribution rather than power. After twenty-one scorecards, I want to end with a slightly different question — one his video implied but did not ask directly.

Do nations owe each other anything?

The “America First” doctrine — and its equivalents in Russia, China, Saudi Arabia, and elsewhere — says no. Nations are sovereign entities pursuing self-interest in an anarchic system. Cooperation is transactional. Obligations are voluntary and revocable. The strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must.

The Contribution Index is built on the opposite premise: that sovereignty comes with responsibility. That the capacity to help creates the obligation to help. That a nation sitting on $28 trillion in wealth while launching discretionary wars and closing its borders has not merely failed a benchmark — it has failed a moral test that it had every resource to pass.

I am a Canadian. I live in a country that barely made the top ten of its own index — a nation that talks progressive values while extracting tar sands, that welcomes refugees while failing its Indigenous peoples, that lectures others on multilateralism while running one of the highest per-capita carbon footprints in the developed world.

I do not write from a position of moral authority. I write from a position of moral aspiration — the belief that nations, like people, can choose to be better than their worst instincts, and that measuring the gap between what we are and what we could be is the first step toward closing it.

Michael Jackson - We Are The World (HQ)

In 1985, forty-six musicians gathered in a studio and recorded a song built on a single proposition: that there are people dying, and it is time to lend a hand. In 1993, Michael Jackson performed that song at the Super Bowl while an entire stadium — 98,000 people — held up cards that formed a single image: children from every corner of the world, holding hands. No borders. No flags. No GDP rankings. Just hands, held. If you have not seen that performance, I ask you to watch it now. It will take four minutes. It may do more than twenty-one essays to remind you what is possible when we choose to be neighbours.

The Contribution Index measures that choice with data. Jackson measured it with music. The question is the same.

Costa Rica chose. It chose with a sledgehammer and a vision and almost no money at all.

The rest of us have far more. The question the Contribution Index leaves with its readers is simple, and it is the same question Way Yuhl asked in seventy-five seconds back in March:

What is your nation choosing?
