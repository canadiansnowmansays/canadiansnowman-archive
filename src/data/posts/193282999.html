It is generally understood, in the cultural funding circles of southwestern Ontario, that Elsinore and Mariposa do not compete. This understanding is cordial, professional, and maintained with the particular vigilance that surrounds understandings everyone knows to be incorrect.

Mariposa Folk Festival runs every July on the shores of Lake Couchiching in Orillia, ninety minutes north of Elsinore on roads that pass through the kind of Ontario landscape that makes people who grew up in it feel, returning, that they have been away too long. It has been running since 1961. It draws fifteen thousand people over three days to eleven stages plus presentations of story, dance, and craft. Its 2026 lineup includes Father John Misty, Sarah Harmer, Sharon Van Etten, Billy Bragg, Steve Earle, Taj Mahal, and the Great Lake Swimmers, which is a lineup that would not embarrass a considerably larger festival in a considerably larger city, and which Orillia receives with the quiet satisfaction of a town that has been doing this for sixty-five years and does not need to be told it has done well.

The Elsinore Festival runs from April to November on the banks of the Avon River, forty-five minutes south on the 401. It has been running for seventy years. It draws visitors from across Canada, the United States, the United Kingdom, and, increasingly, South Korea. It stages Shakespeare, Beckett, Chekhov, new Canadian work, and, this season, Something Rotten, which is a musical comedy about the impossibility of outshining Shakespeare, staged at the Shakespeare Festival, which Alistair has programmed and has not yet fully examined.

The two festivals apply to the Canada Council for the Arts in the same annual funding round.

Gerald Fitch discovered this while cross-referencing the arts funding calendar in February, the way Gerald discovers most things: systematically, at his own pace, while doing something else. He noted it. He filed it under Canada Council — Arts Festivals — Concurrent Applications — 2026. He mentioned it to no one for four days.

On the fifth day he mentioned it to Alistair, in the Tim Hortons, at the window table on the right, because Alistair had asked him directly whether there was anything in the funding calendar he should be aware of, and Gerald had decided that four days was sufficient processing time and that the answer to a direct question should be direct.

Alistair looked at his coffee for a moment.

He said: “Of course they do.”

He said this in the voice of a man who has known something for a long time without having been given the occasion to know it out loud.

Sounds Like Home. This Rough Magic. Both of them, on balance, are right. The Canada Council has presumably reached a similar conclusion.

The matter came before the Tourism and Culture Committee in March, when Wayne presented his quarterly competitive analysis of regional cultural tourism — a document he produces every quarter with the thoroughness of a man who believes that what is not measured is not managed, and which the committee receives with the mixed feelings of people who believe this in principle and find the specific numbers, in practice, challenging.

Wayne’s numbers indicated that Mariposa Folk Festival’s attendance of fifteen thousand over three days compared to the Elsinore Festival’s thirty-eight thousand over the full season represented, on a per-day basis, a closer comparison than the headline figures suggested. He presented this without editorial comment. The room did the arithmetic. The arithmetic was not entirely in Elsinore’s favour on the per-day metric, which Wayne acknowledged, while noting that the Festival’s season length and the Folk Festival’s concentrated attendance represented different tourism models rather than directly comparable outcomes.

He had a slide for this. The slide had a footnote. The footnote cited Gerald’s concurrent applications file, which Gerald had shared with Wayne at Wayne’s request, because Gerald shares documents when asked and Wayne is thorough enough to ask.

Alistair had been looking at the ceiling since slide three. He lowered his eyes at the footnote. He said he was not aware that the Canada Council funded both festivals from the same pool, and asked Gerald whether this was the case.

Gerald said it was more complicated than that and offered to circulate a summary.

The summary was ready the following morning. It was four pages. It covered the Canada Council’s arts festival funding streams, the distinction between operating grants and project grants, the separate assessment criteria for performing arts organisations and music festivals, and Gerald’s conclusion that while both festivals drew from the same broad federal arts funding envelope, they applied under different program streams and were assessed by different juries.

He noted, in the final paragraph, that he had been filing both organisations’ public grant disclosures for eleven years. He had a folder. He had always had a folder. Nobody had asked about it.

The folder was now available if required.

Carly had attended the committee meeting and had filed her notes and had written her piece and had submitted it to Norman at nine fifteen that evening. Her headline was: “Elsinore and Mariposa: A Rivalry Neither Will Officially Acknowledge.”

Norman read it. He read it again. He removed his glasses and held them for a moment, which Carly had learned meant something was being considered rather than rejected.

He changed the headline to: “Two Festivals. One Province. Sufficient Grant Money For Both, Probably.”

He sent it back with three edits. The edits were good. Carly incorporated them. She thought his headline was more honest than hers. She did not say this. She filed the piece. She started a new document and titled it “Notes on the Mariposa Problem” and wrote three paragraphs she would return to later.

The piece ran on Thursday. It was the second most-read Beacon piece of the month, after the swan ownership story. Two of the shares came from Orillia. This was noted by Wayne, who tracked these things, and by Norman, who tracked different things, and by Gerald, who tracked everything, and by nobody else.

What Gerald had not circulated, because it had not been requested and because Gerald does not circulate things that have not been requested, was the following observation, which he had made in 2004 and filed and not revisited since, and which he revisited now, in his kitchen, on the evening after the committee meeting, with Hansard on the opposite chair:

Mariposa is named after Leacock’s fictional town. Leacock’s fictional town was named after the Spanish word for butterfly, which Leacock chose because he liked the sound of it and because the town in question — Orillia — sat on a lake and had a quality of lightness that the name captured. The Mariposa Folk Festival has been running in the actual Orillia since 1961, which means the real town that inspired the fictional town has been hosting a festival named after the fictional town for sixty-five years.

The fiction had become more real than the reality it came from.

Elsinore, Gerald noted, was also fictional. It was named after Hamlet’s castle, in which something was rotten, which it turned out had been a reasonable name for a municipality on several subsequent occasions. Its Festival had been running for seventy years under its own name, without the mediating layer of a literary tradition, except that the literary tradition was the reason the Festival existed, which meant that Elsinore, like Mariposa, owed its identity to a writer who had found something true in a specific Ontario landscape and given it a name that stuck.

Gerald sat with this for a while.

Hansard sat on the opposite chair and did not offer a view, being constitutionally opposed to engagement on abstract questions, which was, Gerald had always felt, a sound position.

He filed the observation under Mariposa — Elsinore — Comparative Notes — Leacock. He made himself a cup of tea. He went to bed.

Alistair read the Mariposa Folk Festival’s 2026 theme statement on a Saturday morning in late February, sitting in his office with the Avon visible through the window and the morning light on the water in the way it is in late February, which is tentative and cold and promising simultaneously.

The theme was Sounds Like Home.

He read it twice.

He sat for a while.

He had spent nineteen years trying to articulate, in program notes and grant applications and parking lot statements, what the Festival was for. He had written, with Gerald’s assistance, that audiences would leave changed in ways that could not be predicted or quantified and that this was the point. He had written, in his letter to the Beacon about civic institutions, that the things which made a community recognizable to itself were not overhead but were the community. He had written, in his personal notebook that nobody had read, about the morning light on the Avon and what it meant to have spent nineteen years looking at it.

Sounds Like Home was three words. It was, he acknowledged to himself on that Saturday morning with the tentative February light on the water, more or less exactly right.

He considered this for a long time. Then he wrote it in his notebook, with a question mark, and then crossed out the question mark, and then sat with the notebook closed on his desk.

He told Gerald, the following Tuesday, that he had no objection to the Mariposa Folk Festival’s Canada Council application and wished them every success.

Gerald said he hadn’t suggested he did.

Alistair said he was aware of that.

They moved on to the Section 7 review.

Jim walked the dogs past the Festival Theatre on a Wednesday morning in March and read the marquee: THIS ROUGH MAGIC — 2026 SEASON. He had read it every morning since April. He thought about the Mariposa Folk Festival, which he had looked up the previous week — not because anyone had asked but because the committee meeting had generated a certain amount of conversation in Elsinore and Jim had found that when Elsinore generated conversation he often found it useful to look at the primary sources.

He had looked at the Mariposa Folk Festival website. He had looked at the lineup. He had looked at the ticket prices and the camping options and the audience reviews from previous years. He had formed a view.

His view was that both festivals were good and served different purposes and that the people who attended one were not, in the main, the same people who attended the other, and that the Canada Council had presumably worked this out over sixty-five years of funding both, and that the per-day attendance comparison Wayne had presented, while accurate, was not the comparison that mattered.

He mentioned this to Maggie at Sunday dinner.

Maggie said she’d had a client that week — the family from Festival Park Heights with the tortoise named Hamlet — who had asked whether she’d ever been to the Folk Festival. She said she had, twice, and that it was excellent. The client had said they were thinking of going this summer. Maggie had said she thought they’d enjoy it. The tortoise had been in for its quarterly check. The tortoise was fine.

Jim passed the bread. He said he thought he might go this year as well. He said this the way he says most things: as a statement of fact about a decision that has already been made, without particular drama.

Maggie said she’d think about it.

The boys ate their dinner.

The Perth County farmer was asked at the feed store, in the week after Carly’s piece ran, whether he preferred the Festival or the Folk Festival.

He thought about this for slightly longer than he usually thinks about things at the feed store, which is to say he thought about it for a moment rather than immediately.

He said: “Went to both.”

He paid for his feed. He drove home.

This was considered, in Elsinore, a cosmopolitan response, and was repeated at three separate dinner tables that week as evidence of something, though people disagreed about what.

A Note for the Record

Stephen Leacock named his fictional town Mariposa in 1912. He drew it from Orillia, which he knew well and loved in the complicated way that satirists love the places they satirize, which is to say completely and with clear eyes and with the specific tenderness of someone who finds the gap between aspiration and reality not contemptible but human and therefore comic and therefore worth preserving.

The Mariposa Folk Festival has been running in Orillia since 1961, named for the fictional town drawn from the real one. This year its theme is Sounds Like Home. Its lineup includes artists from Canada, the United States, the United Kingdom, and points further. It draws fifteen thousand people to Lake Couchiching in July. It has been doing this for sixty-five years and intends to continue.

The Elsinore Festival has been running on the Avon since 1956, named for Hamlet’s castle, in a city that chose that name in 1854 and left no record of its reasoning. This year its theme is This Rough Magic. It stages Shakespeare and Beckett and Chekhov and new Canadian work and a musical comedy about the impossibility of outshining Shakespeare. It draws thirty-eight thousand people over seven months. It has been doing this for seventy years and intends, under its next Artistic Director, to continue.

Both apply to the Canada Council for the Arts. Both receive funding. Gerald has a folder.

Between them, they cover a reasonable portion of what Ontario means by culture, which is to say they cover both the Shakespeare and the folk song, both the Beckett and the Billy Bragg, both the thing that makes you sit in the dark and confront what you are and the thing that makes you stand in a field on a July evening and feel, however briefly, that you are home.

Sounds Like Home, the Folk Festival says.

This Rough Magic, the Festival says.

Both of them, on balance, are right.

Neither festival is named after a person. The Mariposa Folk Festival is named after a fictional town drawn from a real one. The Elsinore Festival is named after a fictional castle that turned out to be prophetic. The signs say Mariposa and Elsinore. They say Shakespeare. They do not say anything else, because it did not occur to anyone that they should.

Neither festival has been shut down for a makeover. Neither has had its board replaced by someone who had not previously attended. Neither has lost its artistic director to a political appointment. Neither has watched its artists decline to perform because performing had become a statement about something other than the performance. Both festivals open in the summer. Both festivals close in the autumn. The artists show up. The audiences show up. The Canada Council sends its letter in June.

This is either an achievement or simply what happens when nobody decides that a folk festival or a Shakespeare theatre is a useful vehicle for anything other than folk music and Shakespeare. In southwestern Ontario, this distinction has not previously required explanation.

The Canada Council, working from separate program streams assessed by separate juries, has presumably reached a similar conclusion.

Gerald has the disclosure documents.

He has always had the disclosure documents.

He was not asked.
