TO: Stakeholders, Taxpayers, Historic Preservationists, and Anyone Still Under the Impression the Constitution Has a Say in This FROM: Office of Executive Fait Accompli, Bunker & Ballroom Division RE: Fiscal progress, legal headwinds, and a brief refresher on how the game is played

It is my pleasure, as Acting Vice President of Concrete Velocity, to present our Q3 results. I’m pleased to report that despite what our General Counsel keeps calling “an active federal injunction,” the Executive Fait Accompli Division has once again exceeded targets. Linear feet poured before judicial interruption: up. Days between an adverse ruling and a filed appeal: down to hours, a new division record. Commission votes secured before anyone outside the building had time to Google the commissioners’ names: same-day, as always.

For those joining us new to the program — welcome. You may be wondering how a 90,000-square-foot ballroom, previously a Congressionally-unauthorized fever dream, became a bunker complex with structural steel above ground before a single court reached a final judgment on its legality. The answer, as it so often is in this administration, is Calvinball. For the uninitiated: Calvinball is a game in which the rules are invented by whoever currently holds the ball, revised the instant they stop being convenient, and never written down where an opposing team — or a judge — might consult them.

Here, then, is the Executive Fait Accompli Division’s official rulebook, current as of this printing. Please note it will change by the time you finish reading it.

Rule One: A demolition is not a decision. In October, the East Wing came down. Nobody voted on this. Nobody in Congress was consulted, because — and this is important — asking would have implied the answer might be no. By the time the National Trust for Historic Preservation filed suit on December 12th, the East Wing was rubble and the question before the court was no longer “should this happen” but “how do we undo a hole in the ground.” Judges, it turns out, are far more comfortable halting a groundbreaking than ordering a building un-demolished. This is not an oversight. This is the strategy.

Rule Two: If a panel might say no, replace the panel. The Commission of Fine Arts exists, on paper, to weigh in on precisely this kind of project. Its previous members had a bad habit of independent judgment, so they were removed and replaced with a slate more amenable to expedited voting. The newly composed panel approved the ballroom in February with a speed previously associated with fast food drive-throughs. A second review panel followed two months later. Two boxes, checked, by the people put there to check them.

Rule Three: When a judge finds doubt, offer him architecture instead of law. By March, Judge Richard J. Leon — appointed by George W. Bush, a jurist not previously known for revolutionary sympathies — had heard enough. On March 31st, he concluded that no statute came close to giving the president the authority he was claiming, and ordered the halt. This is Calvinball’s most elegant countermove: at the precise moment the legal argument collapsed, the ballroom was redescribed as a superstructure sitting atop an underground military bunker. National security, once invoked, tends to make judges nervous about scope. The case was sent back down to clarify which parts of the hole in the ground were “ballroom” and which were “bunker,” a distinction with no known architectural precedent but considerable litigative value. Judge Leon clarified: the bunker was outside his order. Construction resumed within days.

Rule Four: Appeals are not a pause button, they are a runway. Every time this project has been ruled against, it has kept building during the appeal. The appellate stay is treated less as a legal safeguard than as a scheduling gift — additional weeks in which pile drivers and concrete pumps continue their work while attorneys argue, at a stately and dignified pace, about whether any of it was ever allowed. In June, Judge Patricia A. Millett — an Obama appointee on the D.C. Circuit panel — put the quiet part on the record, asking whether the government’s approach amounted to moving fast and breaking things on purpose, precisely so it would be too late to fix by the time anyone could stop it. It is worth noting: the two judges expressing the deepest skepticism of this project are a Bush appointee and an Obama appointee. This is not, in the end, a partisan objection. It is closer to two people who read the law for a living being told, repeatedly, that reading the law is optional if you pour concrete fast enough.

Rule Five: The finish line moves. As of this report, the panel has not ruled. If it rules against the project, there is still the Supreme Court, which offers, at minimum, several more months of pouring time. The Executive Fait Accompli Division does not consider this a contingency. It considers this the plan.

A WORD ON PRECEDENT, FOR THE HISTORICALLY MINDED

Skeptics of the Division’s methods sometimes point out that other presidents have renovated the White House, as though this settles anything. Truman gutted the interior down to the exterior walls in the late 1940s — a genuine architectural crisis, floors sagging under the weight of a bathtub, structurally condemned in places. He asked Congress for money. Congress debated it, argued about it, and eventually approved it, and the president lived across the street at Blair House for most of two years while the work was done properly, under public authority, in full view of the institutions meant to oversee it.

This is held up, in some circles, as the responsible model. We at the Division regard it, respectfully, as a failure of imagination. Two years! An entire Congress consulted! A president willing to live in a townhouse rather than simply demolish first and litigate later! It is charming, in the way a rotary telephone is charming. It is not, however, efficient, and efficiency is the animating principle of this administration’s approach to institutions generally: if a body exists that might slow you down — a historic preservation review, a fine arts commission, a legislative branch — the fastest path is not to persuade it but to outrun it, replace it, or reclassify the thing it was reviewing until it no longer has jurisdiction. Truman’s ceiling was actually falling in and he still asked permission. Ours was not falling in at all, and we did not ask.

There is also the matter of the money, which the Division prefers to describe as “privately financed” and which skeptics prefer to describe as “financed by parties who may reasonably expect something in return, administered by an executive branch under no obligation to disclose who they are or what they expect.” We consider this a matter of perspective. A ballroom built with anonymous donor funds is, after all, still a ballroom.

AN INVESTOR Q&A, LIGHTLY EDITED FOR CLARITY

The following is drawn from our quarterly call with concerned parties. Some questions have been paraphrased. None have been answered.

Q: Doesn’t a federal judge’s finding that the project is “unlawful from the beginning” concern you? A: We found his reasoning quite thorough, actually. We simply did not find it binding on our construction schedule.

Q: The judge himself called your legal arguments a “ragtag group of theories.” Isn’t that a problem? A: A ragtag group of theories is still a group of theories, and a group of theories is still enough to justify an appeal, and an appeal is still enough to keep the concrete trucks running. We consider this a portfolio-diversification strategy for legal risk.

Q: What was the underground bunker before it was a bunker? A: A hole. Before that, a foundation. Before that, the East Wing. Before that, a question nobody asked.

Q: Is it true the Commission of Fine Arts vote took less time than most City Council debates over a stop sign? A: We prefer “expedited.” The stop sign comparison is not one we endorse, though we note the stop sign would likely have received more scrutiny.

Q: If the Supreme Court eventually rules against the project, what recourse would there be? A: At that point, gentlemen, you would be asking us to un-ring a very large, very gold, very finished bell. We are told this is not how bells, or ballrooms, work.

A NOTE ON KPIs

For our stakeholders who prefer metrics to narrative, we are pleased to share the following performance indicators for the fiscal year:

  • Panels replaced prior to a vote: 1

  • Days from demolition to lawsuit: 49

  • Judicial rulings finding the project unlawful: 1 (and counting)

  • Days that ruling was permitted to take effect: 0

  • New legal categories invented mid-litigation: 1 (”aboveground superstructure atop a bunker”)

  • Appeals filed same day as adverse ruling: 100%

  • Percentage of construction timeline conducted under some form of active legal cloud: effectively all of it

We remain on pace, on budget, and — pending a ruling we intend to treat as advisory regardless of outcome — on schedule.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Q: What happens if the appeals court rules the ballroom unlawful? A: We appeal to the Supreme Court. Construction continues in the interim, as it always has.

Q: What happens if the Supreme Court rules it unlawful? A: By then, ask yourself honestly: what exactly would “unlawful” undo? A judge can order words on paper. He cannot un-pour a foundation.

Q: Isn’t this exactly what checks and balances are supposed to prevent? A: That is a wonderful, old-fashioned question, and we admire your faith in institutions. Next question.

Q: Was Congress ever consulted? A: Congress is welcome to hold a hearing about it. We understand there is a lovely new ballroom nearby where such a hearing could, hypothetically, be held.

Q: Who is the “we” in this document? A: We are simply following the rules of the game as they currently stand. We remind you the rules may change before you finish this sentence.

CLOSING REMARKS

There is an old, slightly quaint idea — one Judge Leon himself seemed to be groping toward in March — that the White House belongs to the country, not to whichever administration currently occupies it, and that altering it irreversibly ought to require something more than a fast contractor and a friendly commission. It is a nice idea. It assumes the building will still be standing, in its original unauthorized form, by the time anyone finishes agreeing on whether it was ever allowed to go up.

The excavators, as of this writing, do not appear to be waiting for that agreement. Neither, it seems, does anyone else in the building.
