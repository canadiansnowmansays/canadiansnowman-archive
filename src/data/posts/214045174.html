Fourth of five essays Stephen Gordon Sept 10, 2026

Part II of this series described four mechanisms built for an election that has not happened yet. This essay describes something stranger: federal law enforcement spending 2026 reopening an election that was decided, certified, and — in Georgia’s case specifically — counted three separate times, once by hand, more than four years ago. If Part II was about the machinery of the next contest, this is about the ballots of the last one, and it closes with a question this series cannot yet answer, because the country cannot yet answer it either: what happens at the polls themselves in November.

Fulton County, Georgia is where the reopening becomes concrete rather than administrative, and it is worth pausing on the fact that this is the same Fulton County at the center of Part I of this series — the county whose vote count Trump pressed Brad Raffensperger to alter on the January 2, 2021 call, and the county whose 2020 election is the subject of the racketeering indictment against Trump that remains, as Part I noted, procedurally stalled. On January 28, 2026, FBI agents executed a search warrant on Fulton County’s Elections Hub and Operations Center and removed roughly six to seven hundred boxes of material connected to the 2020 election — ballots belonging to more than half a million voters, according to a subsequent congressional accounting of federal voting-record seizures. The warrant, unsealed months later at a judge’s order, traced back to a criminal referral from an advisor who had worked on Trump’s own 2020 campaign to overturn the result, and alleged two possible violations: that the county had failed to preserve digital images of its 2020 ballots for the twenty-two months federal law requires, and that a portion of the physical ballots seized “look suspicious,” in the Justice Department’s own characterization as recounted by the presiding judge. Fulton County sued for the ballots’ return. In May 2026, U.S. District Judge J.P. Boulee — a Trump appointee — ruled that the Justice Department could keep them, writing in a sixty-eight-page opinion that the seizure had been “certainly not perfect” even as he declined to order the material returned; Boulee noted that Georgia’s 2020 presidential vote had already been counted three times, including once by hand, and that each count affirmed Joe Biden’s win. DOJ lawyers, notably, did not identify any individual target of the investigation, and did not dispute the county’s argument that the statute of limitations on both alleged violations appeared to have already expired. The Justice Department did not stop at the ballots. In April, it obtained a grand jury subpoena demanding the names and personal contact information of every Fulton County employee and volunteer who worked the 2020 election. A second federal judge, William Ray, quashed that subpoena in July, calling its breadth “staggering” and finding that the department had not shown a need for the information sufficient to outweigh the burden and risk to the people it named — language a plain reading does not distinguish easily from a warning that the subpoena looked less like an investigation and more like a list.

Georgia was not the only state where federal law enforcement went looking for 2020’s paperwork four to six years after the fact. The FBI used a subpoena in March 2026 to obtain records related to the Cyber Ninjas-led partisan audit of Maricopa County, Arizona’s 2020 vote — the same privately commissioned review that, when it concluded in 2021, had actually widened Biden’s margin over the county’s official count rather than narrowed it, a detail this series flagged as worth adding to Part I’s catalogue and one that makes a renewed federal interest in the same records harder to explain as anything other than dissatisfaction with an answer that did not change no matter who counted. In April 2026, the department separately demanded Wayne County, Michigan turn over 2024 ballots, ballot receipts, and envelopes, citing the same 1960 Civil Rights Act authority used against Fulton County; Wayne County responded that it does not have custody of the records the department was asking for. None of these three counties — Fulton, Maricopa, Wayne — is a state DOJ has accused of insufficient voter-roll maintenance. Each is a Democratic-leaning county in a state Trump lost or nearly lost, whose 2020 or 2024 count his own political operation once contested and failed to overturn. One further detail from the Fulton County search is worth noting for what it says about how far this reopening reaches into the rest of the government rather than staying contained to DOJ: Trump himself has said that Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard participated in the Fulton County search “at Attorney General Bondi’s insistence” — meaning the nation’s top intelligence official, whose statutory mandate concerns foreign threats rather than domestic election administration, was, by the president’s own account, drawn into a raid on a county elections office over a vote already certified in 2021.

The reopening reaches Chris Krebs personally, and it is worth returning to him here rather than leaving his firing as a closed chapter of Part I. In April 2025 — four and a half years after Trump fired him for calling the 2020 election secure — the White House revoked Krebs’s security clearance and issued a memo directing the Attorney General to investigate him as, in the administration’s own words, “a significant bad-faith actor who weaponized and abused his government authority.” No new evidence of wrongdoing accompanied the directive; the conduct under investigation was the same November 2020 statement that got him fired in the first place. In parallel, rather than restoring the agency Krebs once ran to the credibility it had before his removal, the administration has spent 2025 and 2026 dismantling its election-security function specifically. CISA ended its federal support for the Elections Infrastructure Information Sharing and Analysis Center, the hub through which state and local election offices share cyber-threat intelligence. Its fiscal year 2027 budget request eliminates the agency’s Election Security Program outright — fourteen positions and roughly $40 million — on top of workforce cuts that have already taken the agency from about 3,400 employees to roughly 2,500. One former CISA official said flatly in July 2026 that there was, at that point, no federal election-security plan in place for the midterms at all. Trump has continued, in the same period, to cite foreign interference as justification for the SAVE America Act and the voter-data campaign described later in this essay and in Part II. The agency built specifically to monitor and rebut that kind of interference, and whose director was fired for concluding there wasn’t any in 2020, has in the meantime been reduced by roughly a third, its one Senate-confirmed permanent directorship still unfilled as of this writing. A government genuinely worried about foreign interference in its elections does not, as a rule, close the office built to detect it while pursuing the man who once ran that office for having said, correctly, that there wasn’t any.

The dismantling reaches beyond CISA to the two federal bodies built specifically to police election and campaign conduct on a bipartisan basis, and both cases share the same conflict of interest: the person under review removing the people conducting the review. In February 2025, Trump attempted to remove Ellen Weintraub as chair of the Federal Election Commission — while the FEC, an independent agency Congress structured so no more than three of its six commissioners can share a party, was actively reviewing complaints against Trump himself. A House Democrat’s contemporaneous statement called the timing self-explanatory; the administration did not dispute that the review was ongoing. In July 2026, Trump went further and fired every remaining commissioner of the Election Assistance Commission — the bipartisan body that certifies voting-machine standards and has distributed more than a billion dollars in state election-security grants since 2018 — leaving the agency with zero sitting commissioners months before the midterms, a fact reported by Alan Feuer, Nick Corasaniti, and Alexandra Berzon in The New York Times, and an open legal question, per election-law scholars, over whether a body Congress built around bipartisan balance can even lawfully be emptied this way. Both removals rely on a Supreme Court ruling issued weeks earlier permitting Trump to fire an FTC commissioner; whether that precedent extends to agencies Congress specifically insulated for bipartisan election oversight remains, as of this writing, untested in court. What is not in question is the practical effect: the two federal bodies built to certify how elections and campaigns are run now have, respectively, a fully replaced membership answering to Trump and no membership at all.

The fourth item is not yet a fact so much as an open threat. In May 2026, asked directly whether he would send National Guard troops or ICE officers to polling locations during the midterms, Trump answered that he would “do anything necessary to make sure we have honest elections.” That was not, on the available reporting, an isolated remark. Per reporting from ms.now, Trump has separately said he does not believe the country “should even have an election” and has expressed regret at not having ordered the National Guard to seize voting machines after his 2020 loss — a wish, stated aloud, that federal force had been used against the very machinery Part I’s catalogue already shows produced no fraud that changed the outcome. He has also left open, according to the same reporting, the possibility of declaring a national emergency specifically to gain control over the November 2026 election. Steve Bannon has publicly floated ICE deployment to polling sites as a natural extension of the agency’s airport operations. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin has repeatedly declined to rule out sending agents in the event of an unspecified “threat,” and acting Attorney General Todd Blanche, asked under oath by Senator Amy Klobuchar whether he would commit to keeping armed federal agents away from polling places, answered only that he would “follow the law … no matter what it includes” — a formulation that commits to nothing beyond whatever the administration decides the law permits. Federal law is not actually ambiguous on any of this: statutes dating to the end of the Civil War bar armed troops from polling places outright, separate federal law makes voter intimidation illegal regardless of who commits it, and the National Emergencies Act does not grant the president authority over how states administer elections merely by his invoking it. ICE’s own director, Todd Lyons, told Congress in February that there is “no reason” for the agency to be at polling sites. Multiple Democratic-led states have moved to write explicit bans into their own election codes anyway, calling the federal assurances insufficient given how many times administration officials have declined to repeat them under direct questioning. Separately, and on considerably firmer legal footing, the Justice Department has announced plans to deploy roughly 1,000 federal poll monitors in November — the largest such deployment in the department’s history, though still within a monitoring authority the Civil Rights Division has held for decades under the Voting Rights Act. The distinction between that lawful monitoring authority and an unlawful armed deployment, or an invoked emergency, is real; the concern raised by election officials in both parties is that the administration’s own officials — and the president himself — have spent months declining to rule out the more extreme versions.

The largest piece of this reopening, and the one most likely to be forgotten once the specific images of a raided county fade, is not a raid at all. It is a request. As Nick Corasaniti and Alan Feuer reported for The New York Times, in December 2025 the Justice Department sued Nevada and twenty-nine other states, demanding they hand over complete statewide voter registration lists — including driver's license numbers and partial Social Security numbers. This is not, election-law scholars who have examined the DOJ's own legal filings note, a request the two statutes the department cites actually support: the National Voter Registration Act requires states to make records available for public inspection, not to hand over sensitive personal data, and every state is already in compliance with that public-inspection requirement; the Help America Vote Act requires states to maintain a computerized voter list, but contains no provision authorizing the federal government to demand a copy of it. Judges have already tossed twenty-three of these thirty lawsuits outright, including Nevada's; a judge in Rhode Island described the effort in a single phrase — a "fishing expedition." At least sixteen states have turned over unredacted rolls anyway rather than fight the demand in court. No court, as of this writing, has ordered a single state to produce its unredacted rolls on the merits. Nevada shows what the demand looks like once it moves past the lawsuit stage: the state's secretary of state confirmed in September that his office had received seven separate "voter-related" subpoenas from FBI agents over three months, issued through a U.S. attorney who has, by his own public record, repeatedly cast doubt on federal election integrity. A single raid, however striking, is one county. A request that has already reached fifty states, lost in court twenty-three times out of thirty, and kept issuing subpoenas anyway, is the shape of the whole effort — the raid is simply the part of it with boxes and tape.

One further detail belongs here rather than treated as a footnote to it: Democratic lawmakers have noted that a Justice Department policy memo dating to 2017, which had made it standing federal practice not to seek election materials until after an election’s results were fully settled, was quietly removed from the department’s public website sometime before this year’s midterm preparations began. No public statement has explained the removal, and DOJ has not disputed that it occurred. A policy against reaching for the last election’s records before the next one is decided is not, on its own, a law — nothing in this essay claims that removing an internal norm is the same violation as the seizure or subpoena that preceded it. But its disappearance, in the same year federal agents seized hundreds of boxes of a five-year-old election’s ballots and demanded rolls from every state in the country, is not, on the evidence assembled here, plausibly a coincidence.

Taken together, these developments do not fit the same shape as Part II’s redistricting maps or the SAVE America Act. Those were prospective — built for an election still to come, defensible in isolation as ordinary if aggressive politics. What Fulton County, Maricopa County, Wayne County, Chris Krebs’s continued pursuit, the emptied-out FEC and EAC, and the fifty-state data demand share is that they reach backward and inward at once: backward into a contest already certified and litigated to exhaustion in over sixty courtrooms, and inward into the very federal bodies built to certify, monitor, or check the next one honestly. A government confident that the 2020 election was fairly decided does not need hundreds of boxes of a county’s ballots four years after a hand recount confirmed the count, does not need the names of the volunteers who processed them, does not need every state’s driver’s license and Social Security data, does not need to keep investigating the official who correctly said the count was clean, and does not need to empty the commissions built to referee the next contest before it starts. It needs those things only if the goal is not to confirm what already happened, but to control the answer to a question that has already been asked and answered, repeatedly, under oath, by officials of both parties. Whether that is in fact the goal is a judgment this series has committed to reaching only once both records sit side by side — but the record itself, catalogued here without embellishment, is now complete enough that the concluding essay can finally do what the four essays before it were built to make possible.
