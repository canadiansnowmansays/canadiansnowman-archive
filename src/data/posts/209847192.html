Postmark: A workshop, somewhere ordinary. A half-built robot on a bench. A spool of titanium sheet. A father and daughter arguing, cheerfully, about whether the weapon spins clockwise or counter.

This series opened, in its very first postcard, with a fifteen-year-old in Burlington who watched a snapping turtle and built a robot in his grandparents’ pool. This is the territory that dispatch belongs to — From the Garage, the address for individual inventors, student scientists, makers, and tinkerers, the democratic-invention tradition that assembles the future from off-the-shelf parts on a kitchen table rather than in a corporate lab. That first postcard was gentle: a machine built to observe without disturbing. This one is not gentle at all. It is about a corner of the same tradition where the goal is not careful observation but total, gleeful, engineered destruction — and it turns out to be teaching the same lessons anyway.

The trophy called the Giant Nut

BattleBots, the American robot-combat television institution, awards its championship not a cup or a belt but a literal oversized steel nut — the fastener kind, the kind that goes on a bolt, scaled up into a trophy heavy enough to need both hands. It is a perfect emblem for the sport, because nothing about competitive robot combat pretends to be anything grander than what it is: two machines built by ordinary people, in ordinary garages, entering an arena to try to break each other apart, judged on damage, aggression, and control, until one of them cannot continue.

The sport’s lineage runs back further than the television cameras. As one hobbyist survey traces it, the earliest known robot-combat competitions date to the late 1980s in the United States, with an event called Critter Crunch, and the first formally staged “Robot Olympics” held in Glasgow in 1990 — a Scottish engineering hobbyist’s answer to a question that apparently occurred to tinkerers on both sides of the Atlantic at roughly the same moment: what happens if we just let two machines fight? Robot Wars brought the answer to British television in 1997; BattleBots followed on American cable in 2000. Both shows introduced a mainstream audience to a subculture that had, until then, existed entirely in garages, community-college workshops, and hobbyist forums — engineers and enthusiasts building machines to destroy each other for no reason beyond the pure engineering challenge of it, and the considerable fun.

From wedge to spinner

The design history of combat robots is, in miniature, the design history of weapons generally, compressed into a few decades of amateur engineering rather than a few centuries of state-sponsored arms races — and it carries the same recognizable pattern of move and countermove. As one 2026 design retrospective recounts, the earliest dominant machines relied on kinetic energy and durability alone: heavy, fast, built to survive the first hit and grind an opponent down by sheer mass and persistence. Early champions like the low-slung wedge-lifter BioHazard proved that surviving the opening exchange usually won the match. Then came the paradigm shift. A machine called Son of Whyachi demonstrated that a vertical spinning weapon — a bar or disc spun up to enormous rotational speed before impact — could deliver far more destructive energy than mass alone, and the meta of the entire sport changed overnight. Modern arena champions like Tombstone and Bite Force are, in the language of the sport’s own builders, kinetic-energy weapons on wheels, capable of unleashing forces comparable to a speeding car crash on impact.

The materials have kept pace with the ambition. As the same source notes, the most successful machines today rely on modular construction and advanced materials — AR500 armor steel, Grade 5 titanium — the sort of specifications that would not look out of place on a genuine piece of military hardware, purchased instead from hobbyist suppliers and machined in a garage. And the sport has its own quirks of physics-meets-rulebook: some builders have found that walking robots — machines that move on articulated legs rather than wheels — qualify for a fifty percent weight bonus under most league rules, since legged locomotion is harder to engineer and the rulemakers wanted to reward the attempt. A hobbyist who masters bipedal or quadrupedal combat robotics is, whether they intend it or not, working on exactly the balance and gait problems this series traced through Boston Dynamics’ BigDog in Part III — the same biomechanical challenge, funded by a much smaller budget and a considerably more violent incentive structure.

The prize pool that outgrew the hobby

What was, for its first two decades, an unambiguously amateur pursuit has developed a genuine competitive economy. As one 2026 industry guide reports, the Norwalk Havoc Robot League — NHRL, based in Connecticut — now offers a prize pool exceeding two million dollars, making it the largest robot-combat competition in the United States and a legitimate professional circuit rather than a hobbyist curiosity. Alongside NHRL and the televised BattleBots, leagues under names like the Robot Fighting League and a scattered constellation of regional organizers run events continuously across the country — as one 2026 event calendar for a single season shows, tournaments with names like Carnage at the Cube, April Annihilation, and the May Day Melee, drawing anywhere from thirty to well over a hundred registered robots per event, spread across weight classes from the fingertip-sized antweight division up through the full-scale heavyweight machines that headline television.

That weight-class structure is worth pausing on, because it is the sport’s most quietly important design feature. A builder does not begin at the two-hundred-and-fifty-pound heavyweight class where the television cameras point. As BattleBots’ own guidance to newcomers puts it, with a bluntness this series appreciates, you must start small — enter a local competition in a lighter weight class, learn the combined disciplines of engineering, electronics, computer-aided design, physics and fabrication, make mistakes, go back to the drawing board, and only then move up through the weight classes toward the heavyweight arena. There is no shortcut and, notably, no guaranteed entry — BattleBots explicitly reserves the right to reject any application, for any reason, without explanation. The sport polices its own on-ramp: you earn the right to build something dangerous by first proving, in public, at low stakes, that you can build something safe.

The kit that teaches a kid to weld

The democratization this series keeps tracking — the same instinct that produced the five-thousand-dollar open-source humanoid in Part XI — has its own combat-robotics chapter, and it is aimed squarely at children. As one 2026 buyer’s guide for the hobby documents, companies including Fingertech Robotics, Turnabot, and Banebots now manufacture combat-robot kits specifically designed for students — not toys, in the guide’s own framing, but genuine engineering platforms, complete with motors, controllers, and modular chassis components sized for a beginner’s first build. The guide’s claim is a strong one and worth stating plainly because it captures exactly what this series has found true of the Garage territory generally: children who start with these kits often progress to learning computer-aided design, then 3D printing their own custom parts, and from there into formal engineering study — the hobby, in the guide’s words, does not just teach a child to fight, it teaches them to innovate. There is a specific claim circulating in the hobbyist community, impossible to verify with precision but consistent with everything this series has observed about the maker tradition, that the first generation of BattleBots builders in the late 1990s and early 2000s now occupy senior engineering roles at aerospace and automotive companies — a pipeline running directly from a garage workbench and a kill switch to a career building the machines this series has spent eighteen postcards examining.

The hobby’s own autonomy question

There is a development inside this small, cheerful, garage-scale sport that connects it, unexpectedly, to the largest and darkest territory this series has visited — and it deserves a place in this postcard precisely because it arrives at such a modest scale.

Every combat robot competing today, from the antweight division up through the television heavyweights, is remotely piloted by a human operator standing behind the arena shielding, exactly the way Ghost Robotics’ armed quadrupeds and Ukraine’s front-line ground robots are piloted, as this series documented in earlier postcards. But a small and growing faction within the hobby has begun experimenting with autonomous elements — machines that use onboard sensors to track an opponent’s position and time a weapon strike without a human finger on the trigger at the moment of impact, entering the same conceptual territory this series found on the Ukrainian battlefield in Part XVI, at a scale where the only thing at stake is a hobbyist’s pride and a few hundred dollars of parts rather than a human life. The organizing bodies have, so far, kept a wary distance from full autonomy in competition — the appeal of the sport rests substantially on watching a human operator’s skill and reflexes translate into a machine’s violence, and a fully autonomous match would be a different and arguably less compelling spectacle, closer to watching two chess programs play each other than watching two people fight with proxies. But the technical building blocks — computer vision, real-time targeting, decision-making under time pressure — are the same building blocks this series has traced from the marathon-winning humanoid to the loitering munition, and a hobbyist tinkering with them in a garage on a Saturday afternoon is running, at negligible stakes, the same experiment the world’s militaries are running at the highest possible stakes. The garage, once again, turns out to be a small and safe rehearsal space for questions the rest of the world is answering for real.

The safety the sport takes seriously

None of this would be defensible, or survivable as entertainment, without a safety architecture built as deliberately as the weapons themselves — and the sport’s own rules are candid that the danger is real rather than theatrical. As multiple hobbyist guides confirm, competitors must wear impact-resistant goggles, leather gloves, and steel-toed boots; every arena is enclosed in heavy-duty polycarbonate shielding rated to contain flying debris; a technical inspection before every match confirms that weapons are secured, batteries are properly restrained, and emergency stop buttons function correctly; and a fire marshal equipped for lithium-battery fires stands by at every event, because the lithium polymer batteries that power a combat robot’s weapon systems are, in the plain language of one safety-focused builder, bottled lightning that can turn into a fiery, toxic mess if mishandled. Building, testing, and fighting a combat robot is, as BattleBots’ own guidance states without euphemism, dangerous — and the entire apparatus of goggles, shielding, inspection, and standby fire suppression exists because the builders and organizers take that danger as a design constraint rather than a marketing footnote.

What a build actually costs

The economics of the hobby are worth stating plainly, because they explain who actually populates the garages this postcard has been describing. A competitive antweight robot — the lightest class, capped at roughly a third of a pound — can be built for a few hundred dollars using off-the-shelf motors, a hobbyist radio controller, and a 3D-printed chassis, putting the sport’s entry tier within reach of the same maker who might otherwise spend that money on a decent bicycle. A heavyweight machine bound for the BattleBots television arena is an entirely different proposition: armor-grade steel and titanium, industrial-strength brushless motors, custom-machined weapon components, and the lithium battery packs capable of delivering the discharge rates a spinning weapon demands, easily running into tens of thousands of dollars once a team accounts for the inevitable rebuilding after every match a robot loses badly. That range — a few hundred dollars for a first antweight, tens of thousands for a televised heavyweight — is precisely the on-ramp structure the sport’s own newcomer guidance insists on: nobody is expected to arrive at the top tier on a first attempt, and the weight-class ladder exists specifically so a builder’s financial commitment can grow in step with their skill.

The community around that ladder is, by every account this postcard has gathered, unusually generous with its own hard-won knowledge. Builders who have spent years perfecting a wedge geometry or a weapon-motor pairing tend to publish their findings rather than guard them — the closed Facebook groups and hobbyist forums this postcard’s sourcing draws from function less like trade secrets and more like an open engineering journal, maintained by people who understand that a stronger field of competitors makes for a better sport. This is the same open, camaraderie-driven instinct this series found in the Hugging Face humanoid project of Part XI, applied to a domain built explicitly around adversarial competition — which is its own small proof that a hobby can be fiercely competitive and generously collaborative in the very same breath.

What the garage is actually for

From the balcony, competitive robot combat is the one territory in this entire series where the wonder can be embraced with almost no shadow at all — a rarer thing than this series’ subject matter usually allows. Nobody is displaced from a job by a combat robot. No civil liberty is threatened by a spinning weapon confined behind polycarbonate shielding. No teleoperated stranger sees into anyone’s living room. The stakes are, by design, contained entirely to the machines themselves, and the humans who built them, cheering from a safe distance behind the same shielding that protects everyone else.

What the sport actually produces, underneath the spectacle, is exactly what this series’ opening postcard found in a Burlington backyard pool: the democratic-invention tradition, doing its patient work. A parent and a child arguing cheerfully over a workbench about clockwise or counter-clockwise weapon rotation are doing engineering — real engineering, with real physics, real material science, real electronics, and real consequences if they get the balance wrong. They are doing it for the sake of doing it, which this series has learned, across every other territory it has visited, is often the purest and most durable motivation an engineer can have. The corporate labs building humanoid robots at industrial scale are chasing valuations and government contracts. The kid with the Fingertech kit and the Giant Nut poster on the workshop wall is chasing something considerably older and, in its way, considerably more honest: the simple, stubborn desire to build the thing that wins.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one arrived with sparks still flying off it.
