A pendulum has one virtue that a ratchet lacks. It comes back.

That is the whole premise of an election. The blade swings out, the voters push it back, and the people who were winning learn to govern with the people who were losing. Poe’s prisoner had no such mechanism. His pendulum descended a little with every pass, and nothing he did made it rise. It only went up into the ceiling again when his captors decided they had something worse in mind.

This series has been asking which of the things undone since January 2017 could be put back, and how much damage would be done in the meantime. The answer has always depended on the next Congress. On Sunday, 1 November 2026, two days before Americans choose that Congress, we learned that the White House has been working on the same question since April. It has reached a different conclusion about what the answer should be.

What the binder holds

Nandita Bose and Tim Reid reported for Reuters that senior officials have spent months preparing for a Democratic takeover of one or both chambers. Their sources were a White House official and two other people familiar with the plans. Stephen Miller, the deputy chief of staff, and Russell Vought, the budget director, have worked with the White House Counsel’s Office since April. Their task is to identify what the president can do on immigration, tariffs, housing costs and federal spending without asking Congress at all.

The items named so far are modest on paper. There would be new tariffs on imported critical minerals and wind turbines, and tighter visa rules for international students and temporary foreign workers. A package of measures would aim at housing and construction costs. Federal agencies have already submitted their own proposals, each with a legal review of what it could do alone.

The counsel’s office, led by Will Scharf, was described by the official as studying three things. The first is what the president can lawfully do by himself. The second is where he could push the limits of his authority. The third is how to defend those decisions in court. When one source was asked how comfortable the president would be governing by executive authority under a Democratic Congress, the answer was “very comfortable.”

None of this is a secret in the usual sense. It was described to a wire service, on background, by a White House official, two days before polling day. A plan that leaks this conveniently is also a message, and the message is addressed to the people who will take their seats in January. Whatever you pass, we have already worked out how to do without you.

The White House’s public response was to accuse the press of pushing a false narrative and to recite a campaign slogan. It did not deny any particular of the reporting.

The first lock: the purse

The Constitution gives Congress one power that no executive order can claim, and it is the power to decide how public money is spent. That is the power a new Democratic House would rely on most. It is also the one Vought has spent two terms trying to wear down.

According to Reuters, his office is now going through money Congress has already appropriated to work out which of the president’s priorities can be paid for with it, whatever the next Congress wants. Readers of the Reversal Ledger will recognise the method. On 29 August 2025, the administration sent Congress a “pocket rescission” of roughly $4.9 billion in foreign aid and peacekeeping funds. It was timed so late in the fiscal year that the money would lapse before lawmakers could act. As Government Executive reported at the time, no administration had carried one out in about fifty years. The Government Accountability Office, Congress’s own auditor, regards the tactic as unlawful under the Impoundment Control Act of 1974. It has separately found the administration in violation of that act for withholding money from the departments of Energy, Health and Human Services, Transportation and others. The ledger carries the pocket rescission as an item already done. Nothing in the Reuters account suggests the method has been retired.

The second half of the purse plan is quieter and probably more durable. On 7 August 2025, the president signed Executive Order 14332, which directed that political appointees make the final call on federal grants. On 29 May 2026, Vought’s office proposed a rule to carry it out. Every discretionary award would pass a “pre-issuance review” by appointees, and grants could be ended when the administration’s priorities changed. Democrats on the House Appropriations Committee questioned him about it at a hearing that summer, as the American Institute of Physics’ FYI reported. Reuters now says the rewriting of grant rules is part of the post-election plan.

This is the ratchet in its purest form. A Congress can appropriate a billion dollars for cancer research. If the person who signs the cheque is a political appointee screening for the president’s priorities, the appropriation has become a suggestion. A future administration could restore peer review with the stroke of a pen. Grants that were never made, laboratories that were never staffed and researchers who went abroad do not come back with it.

The second lock: the pen

Tariffs are where the series has seen the clearest instance of the pendulum swinging back. On 20 February 2026, in Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump, the Supreme Court held by six votes to three that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not allow a president to impose tariffs. The power to tax trade belongs to Congress. It was the most important restoration the ledger has recorded, and it came from the courts rather than the ballot box.

It was also incomplete. The ruling closed one statute, and the president turned to another within hours. The binder’s new tariffs on critical minerals and wind turbines will presumably rest on the trade laws the Court left standing. Those laws were written decades ago and gave presidents real discretion over national security and trade emergencies. The binder is, in effect, a map of the ground the Court did not take back. A Democratic Congress could repeal or narrow those statutes, but any such bill would meet the veto.

The visa measures are the same shape. Immigration law gives the executive branch wide latitude over who is admitted and on what terms. Miller has spent a decade learning exactly how wide it is. A rule on student visas can be written, published and enforced without a vote. Undoing it would need either another rule, which takes months, or a law, which takes a veto-proof majority.

The third lock: the shield

There is one thing a Democratic majority can do that no executive order can stop, and Reuters says so plainly. It can investigate. Committees can hold hearings, issue subpoenas and demand documents. Reuters has previously reported that senior House Democrats have discussed using all three to examine whether the president used his office to benefit himself, his allies or his donors.

The binder’s answer is a shield rather than a sword. The White House plans to ask the Justice Department for legal opinions on what the president can do without Congress and how to defend it. It has also sought a department opinion that could let the president claim executive privilege over his communications with private advisers. Those are people who hold no government office and, until now, would have had no obvious claim to the protection the office gives. White House lawyers have meanwhile been briefing political appointees and agency heads on how to handle congressional inquiries, document requests and subpoenas.

The first Trump administration offers a guide to how this goes. In 2019, during the first impeachment inquiry, Vought refused to appear before House investigators examining the hold on military aid to Ukraine. His office withheld its records on the instruction of the White House. The House chose not to spend months in court enforcing its subpoenas, and the records stayed where they were. A privilege claim does not have to win. It only has to last until the next election.

What the president said himself

The president offered his own version of the plan on the same day, in an interview with Miranda Devine of the New York Post. He said he would probably become “Mr. Veto” and would not let the Democrats ruin what he had built. He also predicted that after a month or two of vetoes, they would come to him and make a deal. He added, reasonably enough, that it would not be the first time a president had faced a Congress controlled by the other party.

Here the defence deserves a fair hearing. He is right that divided government is normal. Barack Obama, facing a Republican House in 2014, said he had a pen and a phone and meant to use them. Joe Biden used executive action on student debt after Congress declined to act. Presidents of both parties have stretched their statutory authority when the legislature stopped cooperating. Some of what is in the binder, such as housing measures and visa rules, sits comfortably within powers every recent president has used. A White House that did not plan for losing the House would be negligent, not virtuous.

The difference lies in three details that Reuters reports and the president’s interview leaves out. The plan’s own authors describe it as an exercise in finding where the limits are and going past them. The officer who controls the money has already been found by Congress’s auditor to have spent it unlawfully, more than once. And the preparation for oversight is a preparation to avoid it. Taken together, these suggest a White House that is not planning to share power with a Democratic Congress. It is planning to outlast one.

Two plans for the same morning

On 23 October 2026, The New York Times reported the Democrats’ own plan for a new majority, and the Interlude in this series held it up against the ledger. Four bills would come straight to the floor in the first hundred hours: restoring the health insurance subsidies, reversing the Medicaid cuts, reclaiming trade policy and forcing a vote on the war with Iran. The same day, the Cook Political Report moved the House to “Lean Democratic” for the first time this cycle.

Now there are two plans for Wednesday, 4 November, and they are written for different machines. The Democrats’ plan is a pendulum plan. It assumes that a vote changes the direction of government, and that bills passed by a majority will either become law or force a veto the public can see. The White House plan is a ratchet plan. It assumes the vote changes very little, and that the direction of government is set by appointees, agency rules and money already in hand.

Both are probably partly right. The Democrats’ four bills will mostly meet the veto, as Part III of this series argued they would. The ledger’s hardest items, the ones that need Congress, will stay hard. But the binder will meet something too. Courts have already taken back the emergency tariffs, and they can take back more. Appropriations bills for the 2027 fiscal year will have to pass a Democratic House, which can write limits on the use of funds into the bills themselves. A Democratic Senate can refuse to confirm judges and officials. Subpoenas can be resisted, but every refusal becomes a story, and every story is evidence.

The bands, not the blade

Poe’s prisoner never stopped the pendulum. He did not have the strength. What saved him was humbler. He rubbed the last of his food on the straps that held him, and the rats of the dungeon chewed through them while the blade went on swinging.

That is roughly what a divided government offers the restorers. It cannot stop the machine Miller and Vought have built, and it cannot reverse it by vote. It can wear through the straps: the appropriations rider here, the court ruling there, the subpoena that a judge eventually enforces, the confirmation that never happens. It is slow, unglamorous and incomplete, and it is the only work available.

The Reuters story makes one other thing clear. The people who built the ratchet do not regard the election as the end of the argument. Neither should anyone else. The pendulum will swing on Tuesday, 3 November. Whether it comes back depends on what is done afterwards, and on how many of the bands are still holding by 2028.
