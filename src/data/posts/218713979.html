I had hoped, on 8 August 2026, that the fourteenth instalment of this series might be the last. I said as much at the time, and then the news cycle reopened it within three weeks. I mention this not to complain but to set expectations: a series about the concentration of power in one man’s portfolio does not get to end on the author’s schedule. It ends when the portfolio stops growing. This week it grew a new layer, and it is the most consequential one yet.

On Wednesday, 30 September 2026, at Marine Corps Base Quantico, Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth stood before roughly 600 junior officers and enlisted leaders and announced a new initiative he called Project Meridian. Its job is to study how warfare will change over the next two decades and to identify where American forces are unready for it. Then he named its co-directors — Elon Musk, Anduril founder Palmer Luckey and former House Speaker Newt Gingrich — and asked the room to give them a standing ovation. The room obliged.

As Kate Kelly reported for the New York Times on 3 October 2026, in a piece titled “With Elon Musk’s Return to Washington, Possible Conflicts of Interest Resurface,” Mr. Musk then spent three days with his fellow panellists at Quantico, at the Pentagon, and at the McLean, Virginia offices of the Mitre Corporation, the nonprofit research body the Pentagon says will oversee the panel’s analysis. The panel’s recommendations are due on 28 January 2027.

The previous nineteen parts of this series have traced how Musk assembled the stack: rockets, satellites, cars, robots, an artificial intelligence company, a social platform, and finally, as Part XIII argued, the folding of much of it into a single corporate vehicle. Every layer of that stack sells something. Project Meridian is different. It does not sell. It decides what will be bought.

The Panel, and the Absences

It is worth looking at who is in the room, because the composition tells you more than the mission statement does.

Beyond the three co-directors, the Times identified the remaining members as Dino Mavrookas of the drone-boat maker Saronic; Joe Lonsdale, the venture capitalist who co-founded Palantir; Safra Catz, executive vice chair of Oracle; and General James Rainey, a retired Army officer who now advises two defence technology firms. Chris Walker, writing for Truthout on 1 October 2026 in a piece titled “Hegseth Gives Musk, Luckey, and Gingrich Key Roles to Shape Future Warfare”, also places Emil Michael, the Pentagon’s technology chief and a former Uber executive, at the centre of the effort.

Notice who is not there. The legacy prime contractors — Lockheed Martin, Raytheon and the rest of the old guard — are, by the Times’s account, largely absent. One could read that as reform: the incumbents who brought the Pentagon decades of late, over-budget programmes are finally being kept out of the planning room. I have some sympathy for that reading. Nobody who has followed the F-35’s history, including the Canadian government that spent much of 2025 reconsidering its own order, should be sentimental about the primes.

But removing one set of interested parties is not the same as removing interest. Every remaining civilian member of the panel runs, founded, funds or advises a company that sells — or wants to sell — precisely the kind of autonomous, software-heavy, networked capability Hegseth has said the Pentagon should have been pursuing a decade ago. The old incumbents have not been replaced by neutral experts. They have been replaced by the next incumbents.

Whoever Writes the Specification

Anyone who has spent time inside a large organization’s purchasing process learns an unglamorous truth early: the contest is usually decided before the bids arrive. It is decided when the requirements are written. Specify a capability in the terms one supplier already uses, set a timeline only one supplier can meet, define the problem around the architecture one supplier has already built, and the procurement that follows can be scrupulously fair and still have only one plausible winner.

This is not a hypothetical worry dreamed up by critics. It is the reason the defence world has rules about organizational conflicts of interest in the first place, and it is precisely the concern raised by the watchdogs the Times consulted. Virginia Burger of the Project on Government Oversight told the paper that panellists need never openly lobby for their own companies to benefit; they could simply shape the findings to match what their firms already have in development, and walk away knowing where the money will go before competitors do. Max Stier of the Partnership for Public Service put the point more bluntly, saying Musk’s financial interests are directly engaged by the role.

Now lay Project Meridian’s remit over the Musk portfolio. Hegseth, according to Truthout, framed the panel’s battlefield as running from beneath the Earth to beyond the moon. Space launch is SpaceX. Low-orbit satellite communications is Starlink and its military counterpart. Artificial intelligence is xAI, which — as this series noted when it happened — was merged into SpaceX earlier this year, along with X, the social platform that now sits inside the same corporate structure. There is very little in a two-decade forecast of warfare that does not touch a SpaceX division somewhere.

The Times counted at least $6.9 billion in Pentagon and NASA contracts awarded to SpaceX since early 2025. Anduril, for its part, won an Army contract worth up to $20 billion in March 2026, and an Air Force production contract for a new line of fighter jets in June. These are not peripheral relationships. The two most prominent co-directors of a panel advising the Pentagon on its future needs lead two of the companies best placed to supply them.

The Safeguard

The Pentagon’s answer is Mitre. A spokesman told the Times that the corporation will evaluate every input rigorously and apply conflict-of-interest and information safeguards. Anduril, for its part, said Luckey would comply with the highest ethical standards where actual conflicts arise. Musk did not respond.

There is a certain historical neatness in Mitre’s role. The corporation was founded in 1958 to work on air defence — the problem of the age being Soviet bombers coming over the pole, which is to say, over Canada. Organizations like Mitre exist precisely because the American government learned, in the early Cold War, that it needed technical advisers who did not also manufacture the hardware they were advising on. The whole point of the model was separation: the people who define the system should not be the people who sell its components.

Project Meridian inverts that arrangement and then asks Mitre to supervise the inversion. Mitre can review the analysis. It can scrub the recommendations for obvious self-dealing. What it cannot do is un-know who framed the questions, who chose which threats to discuss for three days in McLean, and whose engineering assumptions became the room’s default vocabulary. A conflict-of-interest safeguard applied at the end of a process is an audit. It is not a firewall.

The Gingrich Defence

The most revealing comment of the week came from Newt Gingrich, who spoke to the Times by phone on Friday, 2 October 2026, from Dulles International Airport. Gingrich dismissed the conflict concerns outright. His argument, paraphrased, was that the richest man in the world has no reason to bend a Pentagon study toward his own advantage — and he mocked the idea of Musk thinking to himself, “I could make another $5 million.” The suspicion itself, Gingrich said, showed how sick the system had become.

It is an argument with a certain surface charm, the sort a Leacock character might deliver from the verandah of the Mariposa House: a man so rich, surely, has no need of more. It does not survive contact with arithmetic.

Nobody is worried about $5 million. The concern is about defence architectures that will run for decades, contracts denominated in billions, and — this is the part Gingrich’s framing conveniently omits — the valuation of the company that holds most of Musk’s wealth.

Part XIV of this series, “The Discount Rate on Belief,” made the case that the market’s pricing of SpaceX has very little to do with its balance sheet. Since its record-setting initial public offering, the stock has at times fallen by half, while Musk’s projections — orbital data centres in 2027, a trillion dollars in revenue by 2030 — have only grown more ambitious. A valuation the Times now puts at nearly $1.8 trillion rests overwhelmingly on faith in things that do not yet exist.

There is one category of SpaceX revenue that does not require faith. Government contracts are signed, funded and paid. They are the floor beneath the belief. A company whose market value depends on investors trusting promises about Mars, robots and space-based computing has an enormous interest — far larger than its owner’s personal bank balance — in a durable, growing stream of sovereign revenue that makes those promises look financeable. The richest man in the world is not richest in cash. He is richest in a stock price, and the Pentagon is the most reliable customer that stock price has.

So Gingrich has it backwards. The scale of Musk’s fortune does not make a conflict less plausible. It makes the stakes of the conflict larger.

The Round Trip

It is also worth remembering how short the road back to Washington has been.

In 2025, Musk ran the Department of Government Efficiency as a special government employee. The Times’s account of that tenure is unsparing: he fell well short of his stated savings goals, left some agencies in disarray, and made claims — including about fraud in Social Security — that became an embarrassment. The Partnership for Public Service estimates DOGE’s disruption cost each taxpayer more than $1,000 in waste. Stier told the Times it was remarkable to see Musk return with no acknowledgement of the harm done the first time.

Then came the break. In June 2025, after leaving government, Musk declared on X that Trump would have lost the 2024 election without him and called the president ungrateful. Trump answered that he was very disappointed in Elon and mused aloud about cancelling Musk’s government contracts to save money. Musk threatened to found his own political party.

That episode deserves more attention than it has received, because it showed both sides of the arrangement plainly. Trump’s first instinct, when crossed, was not to argue policy. It was to reach for the contracts. The contracts were leverage — and everyone understood it.

The reconciliation followed, as reconciliations with this president tend to. Musk has since committed, and spent, at least $100 million supporting Republican candidates in battleground states ahead of the 3 November 2026 midterms. And now he has a co-director’s chair in the room where the Pentagon decides what it will need. Readers of my other series will recognise the pattern: Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate. The only question this time is which party did the capitulating.

What the Panel Says It Is Doing

In fairness, Gingrich told the Times that autonomy was only a small slice of the discussion — five or six percent, in his estimate — and that the conversations ranged across a wide array of military challenges. He said he was astonished by the intellectual firepower in the room. Pentagon images released on Thursday, 1 October 2026, showed the panellists gathered with Hegseth and his aides around a long conference table, which is what serious people doing serious work generally look like.

I do not doubt that the room was clever. Cleverness is not the question. Nor is sincerity; the watchdogs themselves acknowledged that panellists may give advice that is entirely honest and still produce a conflict, or the appearance of one. The question is structural. When the people who sell the future are invited to define it, the honest version of their advice and the self-interested version tend to look identical — because they genuinely believe their own products are the answer. That is not corruption. It is something harder to catch.

There is a further complication worth a sentence here. Politico reported on 2 October 2026, in a piece titled “Musk-led war commission triggers alarms in Pentagon”, that the appointments have raised concern inside the building itself. When unease comes from within the Pentagon rather than from outside watchdogs, it is rarely about optics. It is usually about who will be writing the paper that everyone else is later expected to implement.

The Top of the Stack

For nineteen parts, this series has described a stack built from the bottom up. Musk built the factories, the rockets, the satellites, the platforms and the AI models, and then sold their services to governments, militaries and the public. The power came from ownership of capability.

Project Meridian adds something new: a seat at the top. Not a customer relationship but a planning role. Not a bid but a hand in the specification. The stack now reaches from the hardware all the way up to the document that tells the world’s largest military buyer what hardware it should want.

From here in Canada — the country that gave Musk his first citizenship and that is, once again, rethinking how much of its own security it should entrust to American suppliers — this looks less like the return of a prodigal adviser than the completion of an architecture. The man who owns the means of launch, of orbit, of communication and of machine intelligence has now been asked, with a standing ovation, to help decide which of those things the Pentagon cannot live without.

The recommendations are due on 28 January 2027. I would not wager much on their surprising anyone.
