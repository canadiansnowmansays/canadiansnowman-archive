The former Trump International Hotel & Tower Vancouver.

There is a question that most people never think to ask when they look up at a building with TRUMP written across it in gold letters forty feet high.

Does he own it?

The answer, more often than not, is no.

This is the fact at the centre of the Trump business empire that his entire public persona was constructed to obscure — the fact that the name and the asset are, in most cases, entirely separate things. Trump did not build an empire of towers. He built an empire of letterhead. He did not accumulate properties. He accumulated placements. The gold letters were not the sign of ownership. They were the product.

Understanding this distinction is not merely a matter of financial literacy. It is the key that unlocks the psychology of everything else in this series. Because once you understand that Trump’s relationship to his own name is fundamentally commercial — that the name is a licensable asset, a revenue-generating commodity, a thing that can be rented to the highest bidder and repossessed when the contract expires — you begin to see the Kennedy Center, the Institute of Peace, the $250 bill, and the proposed arch over the Potomac in a different light entirely. Those are not the same project as the licensed towers. They are the opposite project. They are what happens when the commercial logic runs out and something older and more desperate takes over.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves. To understand what the name became, you have to understand what it was.

The licensing model — the business of renting a brand name to developers who build the actual buildings, take the actual financial risk, and pay a fee for the privilege of putting someone else’s name on the result — is not unique to Trump. Hotel chains operate on variations of this model. Franchise businesses are built on it. What was unusual about the Trump version, in its heyday in the 1980s and 1990s, was how nakedly personal it was. The asset being licensed was not a system, a process, or a proven operational methodology. It was a man’s name and the associations that name carried.

Those associations, in the Manhattan of the 1980s, were genuinely valuable. Trump had successfully positioned himself as the avatar of a certain kind of brash, unapologetic, gilded success at precisely the moment American culture was most receptive to that avatar. The publication of The Art of the Deal in 1987 — a book that was less a business memoir than a sustained act of personal mythologizing, ghostwritten by Tony Schwartz, who has since expressed considerable regret about his contribution — cemented the brand. Trump was not merely a developer. He was the idea of a developer. He was what success looked like when it stopped being embarrassed about itself.

Developers paid for that idea. They paid licensing fees to attach his name to their buildings. They paid management fees to have his company operate the hotels within those buildings. They signed agreements that gave Trump’s organization ongoing revenue from properties he had never financed, never built, and in most cases never visited more than a handful of times. The buildings were theirs. The name was his. The arrangement suited everyone, at least for a while.

The structure had an elegant simplicity from Trump’s perspective. He took none of the construction risk. He took none of the financing risk. He took none of the market risk. He provided the name, collected the fees, and if the building failed — as buildings sometimes do, as we shall examine in considerable detail in the next two essays — he could walk away. The name was a separate entity from the disaster. Or so the theory went.

Trump University is where the theory met its most instructive limit.

Launched in 2005 and operating until 2010, Trump University was the licensing model applied to education — or rather, to the idea of education, since the institution was not accredited, did not grant degrees, and was eventually found by the courts to have systematically misled the students who paid tens of thousands of dollars to attend its seminars and mentorship programmes. The name was the entire product. Students were not paying for a curriculum, a faculty, or an educational credential. They were paying for proximity to the Trump brand and the implicit promise that some portion of Trump’s success might transfer to them through osmosis and a three-day workshop in a hotel ballroom.

Trump himself appeared in promotional videos assuring prospective students that he had handpicked the instructors. He had not. The instructors were hired through a standard recruitment process. The curriculum was developed by a outside company. Trump’s actual involvement was the licensing of his name, his image, and his mythology — the same transaction he had been conducting with real estate developers for twenty years, now applied to human aspiration rather than square footage.

In 2016, with Trump running for president and his name suddenly carrying political as well as commercial weight, the lawsuits that had been accumulating against Trump University came to a head. New York Attorney General Eric Schneiderman had filed suit alleging the institution was a fraudulent scheme. Former students described high-pressure sales tactics, false promises, and a product that delivered nothing its marketing had claimed. The case was settled in November 2016, weeks after Trump’s election, for $25 million — a sum Trump paid without admitting wrongdoing, and without, characteristically, acknowledging that the institution bearing his name had caused measurable harm to the people who had trusted it.

The Trump University settlement is worth dwelling on because it illustrates something precise about the licensing model’s psychological core. When the name is the product — when what people are buying is not a building or a hotel room or a seminar curriculum but the name itself and everything it promises — the fraud, if there is fraud, is not incidental to the transaction. It is the transaction. The gap between what the name promises and what the name delivers is not a bug in the model. In the degenerate form of the model, it is the model.

Trump University promised that the name of a successful man could be transferred to you for a fee. It could not. The name belonged to one man and one man only, and its value derived entirely from that exclusivity. You could pay to sit in a room with people who worked for someone who worked for someone who had once been in the same building as Donald Trump, but you could not buy what the name represented. That was never for sale. That was the one thing he was keeping.

The name, of course, was keeping other things too.

By the mid-2000s, the Trump brand had begun accumulating associations that its licensing partners had not bargained for. The Apprentice, which premiered in 2004 and ran for fourteen seasons, had done extraordinary work in refreshing the brand for a new generation — Trump as the ultimate arbiter of competence, the man whose judgment was so refined that a single pointed finger and two words could end a career. The show was, in retrospect, the most sophisticated piece of brand management in the history of American television, and it was largely the work of Mark Burnett rather than Trump, though Trump received and claimed the credit with his customary generosity toward himself.

But The Apprentice also did something the licensing model had never had to contend with before. It made Trump’s personality, rather than merely his name, the core of the brand. The towers could maintain a certain neutrality — they were buildings, they housed people, their relationship to their namesake was architectural rather than personal. The Apprentice made Trump himself the product in a way that the licensing fees never quite had. You were not buying proximity to a name. You were buying the man.

When the man became a political figure — when the 2015 escalator descent and the speech about Mexican rapists transformed the brand from a commercial property into a political symbol — the licensing partners who had signed on for the former found themselves, without having agreed to anything, in possession of the latter. The buildings that had been assets became liabilities. The name that had commanded premiums in luxury real estate markets began commanding protests outside the lobby.

This is the pivot that will occupy us more fully in Part VIII of this series, when we examine the global collapse of the Trump brand after 2016. For now it is sufficient to note the structural irony: the licensing model that had insulated Trump from the financial risks of building things offered no insulation at all from the reputational risks of being Trump. The name that he had rented so profitably to others remained, in the end, irreducibly his. When it became toxic, there was no contractual provision that could separate him from it.

He was stuck with himself. As, in their various ways, were his licensing partners.

The range of products to which the Trump name was attached across its commercial peak is worth cataloguing, not for completeness but because the catalogue itself is psychologically instructive. Each category of product tells you something slightly different about what the name was being sold to represent.

Trump Steaks, launched in 2007 and sold exclusively through the Sharper Image — a retail chain better known for massage chairs and ionic air purifiers than premium beef — lasted approximately two years before being discontinued. The implicit promise was Trumpian abundance, the gold-plated dinner table, access to the kind of red meat that successful men ate. The reality was a mid-range product in an inappropriate retail channel that found no sustainable market.

Trump Vodka, launched in 2006 with the slogan “Success Distilled,” promised that drinking it would associate you with a certain category of achievement. It was discontinued in the United States in 2011, though it continued to be sold in Israel, where it found an unexpected market as a kosher spirit, which is perhaps the most improbable footnote in the history of brand extension.

Trump: The Game, a board game released in 1989 and reissued in 2004, was Monopoly with the serial numbers filed off and Trump’s face on the box. The premise was that players would compete to accumulate wealth in the manner of its namesake. The game was discontinued after modest sales. The irony of a man whose actual business career featured multiple bankruptcies licensing a wealth-accumulation board game was apparently not a significant marketing concern.

Trump cologne — “Success by Trump” — remains perhaps the most philosophically concentrated product in the catalogue. A cologne called Success, bearing the name of a man who had filed for corporate bankruptcy six times, sold in department stores to people who hoped that the scent of the bottle might communicate something about the wearer that the wearer’s actual circumstances did not. It is, in miniature, the entire Trump licensing proposition: the purchase of an association that the purchaser cannot otherwise claim.

And then, in April 2024, the God Bless the USA Bible. Retailing at $59.99, featuring the King James text alongside the United States Constitution, the Bill of Rights, the Declaration of Independence, the Pledge of Allegiance, and a handwritten chorus of Lee Greenwood’s “God Bless the USA” — a song whose copyright status in this context raised questions that were never fully resolved — the Trump Bible was the licensing model applied to scripture. God’s word, packaged with the founding documents of the republic, endorsed by a man facing multiple felony indictments, retailing at $59.99.

The name was on the Bible. Not as an author, not as a contributor, not as a theologian. As a brand. As the thing that made this particular Bible different from the other Bibles on the shelf, the thing that made it worth $59.99 rather than the $12.99 you could pay for a perfectly functional King James at any bookshop in America.

What that says about the name, and about the people who bought it, is something this series will leave the reader to contemplate at their own pace.

There is a moment in the licensing story that clarifies, with unusual precision, the difference between the commercial naming project and the political one — between the name as a product and the name as a monument.

It occurred in Toronto in 2017, when the new owners of the failed Trump International Hotel and Tower — a building whose saga will occupy the entirety of Part III of this series — reached a financial settlement with the Trump Organization to remove the name from the building. The Trump Organization accepted the settlement. Trump said nothing publicly. No tweets, no Truth Social posts, no threats, no wounded dignity, no social media storms about the radical left and the failing institution. The name came off the building. The cheque cleared. The building became the St. Regis.

Compare this to the Kennedy Center. Compare this to the Institute of Peace. Compare this to the fury that attended Judge Cooper’s ruling, the threats to hand the building back to Congress, the social media posts about radical Democrats and dying institutions. The contrast is not incidental. It is the distinction between two entirely different relationships to the name.

When the name is a commercial asset — a licensed property, a contracted arrangement, a revenue-generating commodity — its removal can be negotiated. There is a price. The price can be paid. The name comes off. Business is business.

When the name is a monument — when it has been inscribed on a public institution, a piece of national infrastructure, a memorial to someone else, a building that belongs not to a developer but to the republic — its removal is an existential threat. Because monuments are not contracts. You cannot negotiate with a monument. You cannot buy out a monument’s management agreement. A monument is permanent, or it is nothing. And for a man whose entire psychological project is the permanent inscription of himself on the world, the impermanence of a monument is not a legal question.

It is the original wound, reopened.

This is what the licensing model, for all its commercial elegance, could never actually provide. You can license a name. You can place it on buildings and steaks and vodka and Bibles and colognes and board games and university seminars. You can collect the fees and walk away from the failures and let the contractors and the developers and the students bear the losses while the name floats free and clean above the wreckage.

But you cannot license permanence. You cannot rent immortality. You cannot put your name on a $59.99 Bible and have it mean what the Kennedy Center means, what a federal building means, what a denomination of currency means, what a triumphal arch visible from the Lincoln Memorial means.

The commercial naming project — the towers, the products, the licensed mythology — was always, at some level, a rehearsal for the real project. The real project is not revenue. The real project is the one Judge Cooper interrupted on Kennedy’s birthday with ninety-four pages of federal jurisprudence and a two-week deadline to remove eighteen letters from a marble wall.

The real project is permanence.

The real project is: I was here, and you cannot make me leave.

The licensing fees were never going to deliver that. Which is why, when the commercial logic ran out, something older took over.

And the name went on the Kennedy Center.

And the Institute of Peace.

And the currency.

And the arch.

And eventually, if the legislation passes and the prohibition that has held since George Washington’s deliberate restraint is finally broken, the face of the man from Queens will look out from a gold $250 bill at a country that has been, for better or worse and in every denomination, his audience all along.

Donald Trump has spent a lifetime writing his name on things. Towers. Golf courses. Hotels. A university. A Bible. A perfume called Success. A peace institute he gutted before the paint on the letters dried. A performing arts centre dedicated to a murdered president he has spent his entire career trying to impress.

The name is always the same: gold where possible, large where mandatory, permanent where permitted, and imposed where neither permission nor permanence was ever his to grant.

This series is about what that compulsion reveals — psychologically, politically, historically, and occasionally comically. It is not a flattering portrait.

Then again, neither is a loading dock on Bay Street.
