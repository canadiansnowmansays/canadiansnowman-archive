Postmarked from an earnings call, October 21, 2026 — the return address is a former car line in Fremont, now building a robot nobody outside Tesla has been allowed to see in full, let alone buy.

Here is the complete customer inventory of Tesla’s humanoid robot business as of its most recent earnings call: zero units delivered. Not zero sold to the public — zero delivered to anyone outside Tesla at all. On October 21, 2026, as TechCrunch reported, the company announced it had begun what Musk called “early production” of Optimus on a high-volume line in Fremont, on the very factory floor where the Model S and Model X spent a combined quarter-century rolling off the line before production ended in May. Tesla called the robots coming off that line its first “production-quality” units. It did not say how many there are. Deliveries to companies outside Tesla are targeted for around the end of 2027, and Musk told investors the line is at least a year from its target of 20,000 robots a week. The full third-generation robot, whose public unveiling has slipped repeatedly this year, still hasn’t been shown.

And on the call before that, on July 22, Musk repeated the line he has used all year: Optimus, he said, will be “the biggest product ever.” Not the biggest Tesla product — the biggest product, full stop. In the same answer, according to the transcript published by The Motley Fool, he called it “the hardest product to scale manufacturing that we’ve ever made at Tesla, because everything on the robot is new.”

Coming from most executives, that’s an earnings-call flourish, the kind of line written to move a stock price for an afternoon. Coming from someone running two of the more improbably successful industrial companies of the last twenty years, it’s worth taking seriously enough to ask what he’s actually comparing it to. Because he isn’t comparing it to cars.

The number almost nobody quotes

The global automotive industry — every car, truck, and van sold anywhere on Earth — is worth something in the neighbourhood of $3 trillion a year. Tesla captured a sliver of that market and was, at its peak, valued at more than the next several carmakers combined.

Global spending on human labour — wages, salaries, contract work, the total sum of what the world pays people to do things — runs north of $50 trillion a year. A large share of that is physical: lifting, assembling, cleaning, harvesting, building, caring for people who can’t care for themselves.

Musk isn’t measuring Optimus against the car business. He’s measuring it against work itself. Even a robot that only ever touches a tenth of the world’s labour bill is chasing a market more than one and a half times the size of the entire global auto industry. That arithmetic, more than any demo video, is the honest explanation for why a famously attention-constrained executive has apparently decided this is where his attention belongs.

What “physical AI” actually means, and why it’s a harder problem than it sounds

Every AI product most people have used — the chatbots, the image generators, the coding assistants — lives entirely behind glass. It reasons, it writes, it generates, and none of it ever has to survive contact with gravity, friction, or a human being’s actual physical space.

Physical AI, sometimes called embodied AI, is the attempt to close that gap: a system that perceives its surroundings through sensors, interprets what it’s seeing, plans a sequence of actions, and executes that plan through actuators — hands, legs, joints — while adjusting in real time when reality doesn’t match the plan. That last step is the entire difficulty. Industrial robots have worked on assembly lines for more than sixty years, executing the same motion ten thousand times with machine precision, and they are useless the moment you move the part six inches to the left. Physical AI is meant to handle the version of the world nobody scripted for it: a cup it’s never seen, a door with an unfamiliar latch, a floor with something spilled across it.

It’s a categorically different kind of problem than anything language models have solved, for a reason worth stating plainly: language has no physics. A bad sentence gets regenerated at no cost. A seventy-kilogram machine executing a bad movement near a person is a liability event, not a retry.

Three bottlenecks, not one

Strip away the marketing and there are three specific, unglamorous constraints standing between where this technology sits today and the labour-market disruption Musk is pricing in.

The first is hands. A human hand has roughly twenty-seven degrees of freedom. Most humanoid robot hands on the market today manage somewhere between six and twelve, and the ones that push higher tend to be the least durable. Musk himself has named the hand the main problem left to solve — “it’s mostly dexterity,” he told analysts on October 21 — which is consistent with what Tesla has actually shown: a tendon-driven, twenty-two-degree-of-freedom design laid out in patent filings that Drive Tesla Canada reported on in April, well ahead of any full-body unveiling, because the hands were the harder engineering problem to solve in the first place.

The second is insurance, and it’s the constraint almost nobody outside the industry talks about. There is no actuarial model, no loss history, no claims data for a seventy-kilogram autonomous machine operating in close proximity to people. Underwriters price risk off precedent, and there isn’t any yet. Even the market forecasters say so: the research firm Interact Analysis, in its 2026 humanoid report, listed insurance mechanisms among the things still missing before deployment makes economic sense, as Supply & Demand Chain Executive reported. Until that changes, deployment stays confined to controlled industrial sites regardless of how capable the hardware becomes — a regulatory-economic bottleneck binding harder, right now, than the engineering.

The third is data. Large language models were trained on a public internet built by decades of human writing, essentially for free. There is no equivalent internet of physical manipulation. Every company in this race is building that dataset the hard way — robot by robot, hour by hour, inside its own facilities — which is precisely why Tesla’s first production-quality units are going to its internal “Optimus Academy” to generate training data, as Tesla North reported, rather than to any customer at all.

The honest counterweight

None of this is a case for imminent transformation, and it’s worth resisting the temptation to treat Musk’s framing as a forecast rather than a bet. Even one of the most bullish forecasts on the table is sobering at this scale. As 24/7 Wall St. reported in September, Goldman Sachs nearly quintupled its forecast and now expects about 6.5 million humanoid robots to ship worldwide by 2035 — a genuinely large number in isolation, and a rounding error against a global workforce of something like 3.5 billion people. That’s under a fifth of one per cent, a decade out, on an optimistic projection.

And robotics forecasting has a long, consistent history of being wrong in the same direction. Self-driving cars were reportedly “two years away” for the better part of a decade; as of 2026 they remain geofenced to specific cities rather than roaming freely. Optimus has its own version of that record: as Electrek has tallied, Musk said in January 2025 that Tesla would build roughly ten thousand of the robots that year, and a year later conceded that none were yet doing useful work. The honest position is one that can hold two things at once: Musk is very possibly right about the eventual destination, and very possibly wrong, by years if not a full decade, about the pace of getting there.

Which work is actually exposed

The instinctive assumption is that physical jobs sit in the crosshairs and desk jobs are safe. That gets the mechanism backwards. Robots, at their current stage, don’t struggle with difficulty — a machine can lift fifty kilograms all day without complaint. They struggle with unpredictability. A robot that can lift is not the same as a robot that can reliably handle a room it has never seen before.

That reframes which jobs are actually exposed. Most exposed are repetitive tasks inside controlled settings: warehouse picking and sorting, palletising, machine tending, depot logistics, assembly-line work, high-value crop harvesting — precisely the categories where Agility Robotics and Figure AI have already been racking up deployment hours, and precisely why those two companies, rather than Tesla, currently lead the “real-world deployment” category in the broader industry scorecard. Agility’s Digit has moved more than 100,000 totes at a GXO Logistics warehouse in Flowery Branch, Georgia, and on October 26, 2026, the two companies announced it would expand to GXO sites in North Carolina and Tennessee, as The Robot Report reported. Least exposed is anything unstructured or judgment-heavy: construction finish work, plumbing inside existing buildings, residential last-mile delivery, equipment diagnosis, livestock handling, and any role where the core task is being a human for another human.

There’s a specific case worth naming on its own: healthcare and elder care. Japan’s health ministry projects the country will need roughly 570,000 additional care workers by 2040, a figure Japan’s Digital Agency publishes on its government dashboard, and there is no realistic scenario in which enough humans fill that specific gap. Robots arriving into that vacancy aren’t displacing anyone — they’re filling positions nobody is applying for in the first place. It’s a reasonable bet that this is the sector most likely to welcome humanoid labour rather than resist it, for the simple reason that the alternative isn’t a human doing the job instead. The alternative is the job going undone.

The second-order effect nobody’s pricing in

If the labour bet lands at all — even partially, even a decade later than Musk’s own timeline — the more interesting consequence isn’t job displacement in the categories above. It’s what happens to the geography of physical work. Early humanoid deployments will run under human supervision, with one operator overseeing the confusing ten per cent of edge cases for a handful of robots at once. That arrangement decouples where the physical work happens from where the person directing it lives, in roughly the same way remote software work decoupled the office from the desk. Physical labour, under this model, starts to look a lot like remote work: still physical, still happening somewhere specific, but directed from somewhere else entirely. And the scarce skill in that world stops being “can perform the task” and becomes “can correctly judge which tasks are worth automating and which aren’t” — which is exactly the skill that mattered in every prior automation wave, and there’s no obvious reason this one is the exception.

What’s actually worth watching

For anyone tracking this rather than just watching the demo reels — and it’s worth saying plainly that demo footage in this space is routinely staged and frequently teleoperated, telling you almost nothing about genuine readiness — three signals matter more than any keynote moment.

Cost per operating hour is the first. When humanoid labour gets publicly quoted at something like three to eight dollars an hour, the economics have actually arrived; until then, it’s a research programme wearing a business-plan costume. Fleet size is the second — capability follows data, and data follows deployment, which means the company running the largest working fleet in 2028 is a reasonable bet to have the most capable robots by 2031, regardless of whose hardware looks more impressive on a stage today. And insurance products are the third, arguably the most underrated: the first major carrier to publish a standard liability policy for humanoid deployment will move this industry faster than any single hardware launch, because it removes the one constraint that no amount of engineering progress can solve on its own.

A note on what didn’t make it into this postcard

In gathering material for this piece, a video circulated widely under titles like “Tesla Optimus Gen 3: Cleaning Master Bathroom Mode Activated,” showing a humanoid robot performing detailed household chores with a Tesla logo on its chassis. It’s worth being direct about this: nothing in Tesla’s own October 21 disclosures — which describe early production only just beginning, no published unit count, and a third-generation robot the company has yet to show in public — supports the existence of that footage as an authentic company demonstration. A real Gen 3 cleaning video would be the unveiling Tesla has repeatedly postponed. No Tesla-official channel or credible outlet corroborates it. The video’s own circulation pattern, wrapped in clickbait titles across TikTok and YouTube, is far more consistent with an unofficial or synthetic clip than with an unreleased product reveal. It’s left out of this postcard for that reason. The gap between the story being told about Optimus and the machine actually being built is, at the moment, the most reliable fact in the entire story — and a video promising to have already closed that gap is the first thing worth doubting, not the first thing worth sharing.

Where this leaves the postcard

Zero units delivered. A market comparison that reaches past the entire global auto industry into the $50 trillion economy of human labour itself. Three specific, boring, unresolved engineering and regulatory problems standing between here and there. And a viral video claiming the whole thing has already arrived, which almost certainly hasn’t.

That’s not a contradiction. It’s the actual shape of a genuine industrial bet in its earliest, least-resolved days — enormous in ambition, real in its underlying arithmetic, and still, for now, mostly a claim rather than a machine.
