There is a particular satisfaction, familiar to anyone who has ever dealt with a contractor pouring concrete without a permit, in watching the inspector come back a second time. Part XLV left the administration’s two doors — the DHS hunt for noncitizen voters, and the Postal Service’s ballot-screening portal — freshly reopened after the courts had supposedly closed them. This week the inspector came back. Twice. And a third door, one this series hadn’t yet had occasion to open, got nailed shut as well.

None of this means the crew has stopped working. It means the crew has stopped pretending the permit situation is settled, which is a different thing entirely, and worth being precise about.

Door One Gets a Lawsuit of Its Own

The most direct answer yet to the DHS “Unlawful Voter Initiative” arrived on September 2nd, when the Coalition for Humane Immigrant Rights, or CHIRLA, filed suit in federal court in Washington on behalf of itself and a California voter, seeking to block DHS’s database-matching campaign entirely. The complaint’s central fact is a number that deserves to be sat with for a moment: DHS’s own claim, offered without public substantiation, that more than 190,000 people on California’s voter rolls may be noncitizens.

Compare that figure to the ones Part XLV already established. Weeks of the department’s manual voter-portal searching had produced, at that point, a little over a thousand referrals for further review, out of a national voter file numbering in the hundreds of millions — against a backdrop in which the Heritage Foundation’s own accounting, the most rigorous conservative tally available, documents roughly one hundred confirmed instances of noncitizen voting nationwide across twenty-five years. Now a single state is being told, by the same department, that nearly two hundred thousand of its voters warrant suspicion, with no methodology offered for how that number was reached. A DHS spokesperson’s response to the lawsuit, quoted by The Washington Times, was characteristically unadorned: the department “accessed publicly available data from states’ voter rolls and cross referenced them with known illegal aliens in our systems. It’s not rocket science.”

It may not be rocket science, but it is exactly the kind of exercise Paul Gleason’s training tape, quoted in Part XLV, warned agents about — the risk of flagging naturalized citizens whose federal records predate their citizenship. CHIRLA’s complaint puts a name to that risk rather than leaving it as an abstraction: naturalized citizens, the filing argues, now face “fear and extra scrutiny of their right to vote enshrined in their very citizenship” — scrutiny generated not by anything they did, but by the accident of which federal database still remembers them as they used to be rather than as they now are.

Door Two, and the Judge Who Won’t Let It Close

Part XLV closed with the Postal Service continuing to build its ballot-screening portal despite the August 27th injunction against the underlying rule — the agency’s position being that construction and use are legally distinct, a distinction that has always sat more comfortably in a legal brief than in a mailroom. This week that distinction finally got a name and a face: Judge Indira Talwani, of the federal district court in Massachusetts, who has now ruled on this rule three separate times in under two weeks. As Anna Griffin and colleagues reported for The New York Times, Judge Talwani temporarily blocked the requirements nationally on August 28th while she weighed a longer injunction. On Thursday, September 3rd, the administration went over her head, filing an emergency appeal asking the Supreme Court to let it proceed anyway — hours after Talwani herself had warned, in open court, that the rule could cause enough chaos to produce “major disenfranchisement.” On Friday evening, she intervened a third time, ruling that the administration’s plans are likely illegal and should not be implemented ahead of the midterms.

Three rulings from the same judge inside two weeks is not the record of an agency finding its footing. It is the record of an agency that keeps discovering new ways to be told no by the same person whose job it is to say so — and, per a Postal Service whistleblower cited in the same reporting, one that kept building the underlying computer system in violation of the court’s own orders while it was losing. And yet the calendar keeps running underneath every one of Talwani’s rulings regardless. Twenty states were still on track to begin mailing ballots by September 19th, with North Carolina’s counties having started the previous Friday — Sam Hayes, the state’s elections director, told reporters plainly that his office intends to “proceed as planned,” injunction or no injunction, because the mail has to go out whether or not Washington has settled anything.

The ground-level version of this is more instructive than the docket. In Leon County, Florida, election supervisor Mark Earley’s office had already produced trackable-barcode envelopes to the Postal Service’s own specification — and had them rejected on inspection this week anyway, with Earley warning that Florida alone could see “tens of thousands, if not hundreds of thousands of disenfranchised voters” if the rule ever does take effect on schedule. In Alaska, where the Postal Service delivers not just ballots but entire polling places — the boxes, the stands, the flags — to villages that can go three weeks without mail service if the weather turns, the stakes of any disruption are not abstract at all. Los Angeles County’s registrar, Dean Logan, put the mandate election officials have set for themselves in one sentence: “If this is a problem, let’s make it an administrative problem, not a voter problem.” That is the entire job description of a county clerk in the fall of 2026 — absorb the uncertainty a federal rule keeps generating, and don’t let it reach the person filling out the ballot. Whether that is even possible is a separate question. Hector Sánchez Barba, who runs the Arizona voter-engagement group Mi Familia Vota, gave the blunter read: “This White House is making it so hard for people to participate in the democratic process.” Whether the confusion is the point or merely the byproduct, an injunction against a rule is not the same thing as an injunction against the anxiety the rule already generated in every mailroom, county clerk’s office, and postal workers’ union hall that spent the preceding weeks trying to figure out whether barcodes were coming or not.

The Third Door: A Database Built for Immigration Status, Repurposed for Voters

Late on Friday, September 4th, 2026, a three-judge panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit declined, by a 2-1 vote, to let the Department of Homeland Security restore access to a tool that would let states compare voter rolls against federal citizenship and Social Security data. As Zach Montague reported for The New York Times, Chief Judge Sri Srinivasan and Judge Robert L. Wilkins — both Obama appointees — formed the majority; Judge Gregory G. Katsas, a Trump appointee, dissented and would have lifted the lower court’s order immediately.

The tool in question is not the same apparatus as the DHS “Unlawful Voter Initiative” covered in Part XLV, though the two rhyme closely enough to be mistaken for each other, which is itself worth noting. That earlier operation had agents manually keying voter names into state self-service lookup portals. This one is a database, built out of infrastructure historically used to verify immigrants’ legal status, repurposed to let states run bulk citizenship checks against Social Security records. A lower court had already ruled in June that this repurposing violated federal privacy statutes governing the disclosure of sensitive federal data — the same body of law, not coincidentally, that governs why the Social Security Administration does not ordinarily hand its records to whoever asks nicely enough.

The majority’s language on Friday is worth sitting with, because it does something usefully blunt: it puts the burden of proof back where it belongs. “The public interest does not favor interim restoration of a system the government has not made a strong showing it may lawfully operate,” the opinion read. The panel noted, with what reads on the page as mild judicial irritation, that the administration had waited until after the case was decided to raise new arguments for why the Social Security data could be used at all — arguments the lower court never had a chance to evaluate, because they didn’t exist yet when the lower court was doing its evaluating.

This is not a permanent ruling. It is a ten-day placeholder, with the full merits decision promised by roughly September 15th. But it sits alongside a detail from Part XLV that now reads less like a footnote and more like the whole argument: Republican-led states including Florida, Ohio, Iowa, and Indiana already won a separate ruling in July restoring their own access to this same database, in a parallel Florida lawsuit. Which means the practical effect of Friday’s order is narrower than it looks — the tool stays offline for the country generally, while a specific bloc of states already has a court order letting them use a version of it. Two courts, two conclusions, the same underlying database. That is not a contradiction so much as the ordinary weather of litigation running on separate tracks at separate speeds, but it is exactly the kind of weather that produces the confusion this series keeps returning to as the actual product being manufactured here.

The Number That Changes What All This Is For

None of the above happens in a vacuum, and this week supplied the vacuum’s opposite: hard data on where the electorate actually stands. Gallup’s latest party-identification figures, reported by Ruth Igielnik and Caroline Soler for The New York Times (Sept. 5, 2026), show Democrats and Democratic-leaning independents holding a ten-point advantage over Republicans — the party’s widest edge since 2008, and comparable to the advantage Democrats held heading into 2020. Jeffrey Jones, a senior editor at Gallup, described this as thermostatic — the same mechanism that produced Democratic waves in 2006, 2008, and 2018, all of them following stretches of an unpopular Republican president and a public that had decided, in Jones’s words, that “things are going poorly.”

The demographic detail matters more here than the topline number. Pew’s companion data shows the shift concentrated in groups that had moved toward Mr. Trump as recently as 2024: young voters, who favored Republicans by a single point that year and now favor Democrats by sixteen; and Hispanic voters, whose Democratic edge has grown by nine points over the same period. These are not incidental demographic categories. They are, disproportionately, the same populations most exposed to the machinery this series has spent two installments describing — younger and more recently naturalized voters are exactly the population most likely to have an outdated federal record, most likely to be a first-generation registrant, and most likely to be caught by a citizenship-database mismatch that has nothing to do with their actual eligibility to vote.

This is not a claim that the timing is coordinated, and this series has been careful throughout not to allege intent it cannot document. It is a claim that the effect lines up regardless of intent: an electorate moving toward the opposition, concentrated in the very demographic groups the friction machinery is most likely to catch in its net, whether or not anyone designed it that way.

Three Doors, One Calendar

Read together, this week’s rulings tell a consistent story about where the administration’s legal position actually stands: it is losing, repeatedly, on the merits, in front of judges appointed by presidents of both parties, with the lone dissent in Friday’s ruling coming from the sole Trump appointee on the panel. That is not a marginal legal position. It is close to as decisive a losing streak as American litigation produces.

And it may not matter, in the only sense that matters here. Two Obama appointees and a Trump appointee splitting 2-1 is still, functionally, a ten-day placeholder rather than a final word — the merits ruling due by roughly September 15th is the next date worth watching, not this one. An injunction against USPS is still an agency that keeps building the system the injunction is meant to stop it from using. A lawsuit against DHS’s 190,000-voter claim is still a claim that will sit in the public record, unretracted, for however many news cycles the litigation takes to resolve. None of the courts’ Friday work makes any of that go away by Tuesday.

Kathy Boockvar’s line from Part XLV bears repeating here, because this week did nothing to retire it: the damage to “faith in a free and fair system” is not the kind of thing an injunction restores once it has been spent. Add to that a poll showing the largest Democratic advantage in eighteen years, concentrated among the young and the newly naturalized, and you get the shape of the actual contest underway — not a fight over whether the administration can win the election on the merits, which the numbers increasingly suggest it cannot, but a fight over whether enough ambient friction, sustained through November, can blunt a wave the courts have made clear it has no lawful way to stop.
