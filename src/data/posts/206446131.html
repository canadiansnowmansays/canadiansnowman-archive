By CanadianSnowmanSays | July 12, 2026

There is, somewhere in the Department of Global Affairs, a protocol officer whose entire professional existence is organized around one question: what does a head of government give another head of government, and does it match, in weight and meaning, what’s likely to come back the other way. I would like to believe this person had a very bad week.

The story I heard first was tidy enough to leave alone: President Erdoğan gave Prime Minister Carney an engraved handgun at the NATO summit in Ankara, and Carney, a touch outgunned, gave him a bottle of maple syrup.

Here is what actually happened. Erdoğan gave a gun to everyone.

Every leader who left Ankara this week took home the same parting gift: a Gümüşay .357 Magnum, engraved with their own name, boxed with a placard calling it Turkey’s first domestically produced revolver, and — for sincerity’s sake — six live rounds. Spain’s delegation confirmed everyone got the identical model. This was not a Carney bit. This was the whole alliance.

What followed was the most purely diplomatic spectacle of the summit: thirty-some governments improvising thirty-some answers to the question of what you do with a gift you cannot legally take home. Belgium’s prime minister didn’t even know what he was carrying until he’d already landed in Brussels — he found out mid-taxi, essentially, and handed the thing to airport police before it had cleared the terminal. The Dutch were more procedural about it: straight to the embassy in Ankara, quietly, for disabling, no fuss, no comment. Poland’s sat in Warsaw customs for days while an aide explained, with the patience of a man fielding his fourth version of the same question, that it would be stored somewhere appropriate and “certainly no one will be shooting it.” Starmer just left his in Turkey — it had arrived with a cleaning kit and five hundred bullets, which is either a generous gesture or a faintly pointed one, depending how you’re reading Anglo-Turkish relations this week. Hungary’s prime minister, alone among his colleagues, treated the whole thing as good content and simply posted a photo of the engraved barrel to social media, name and all. Von der Leyen and the Greek prime minister both announced, within hours of each other, that theirs would go to military museums — which means two separate European institutions are now, this week, drafting acquisition paperwork for Turkish sidearms they did not ask for and cannot really decline. One NATO official, asked to characterize how the various security details had responded to the news, offered a single word: “insane.” It is, I think, the most restrained possible way to describe thirty details discovering, more or less simultaneously, that their principal is now armed.

And then there’s the one leader for whom we have no answer at all. Whether President Trump received a revolver, and what became of it if he did, remains — as of this writing — a genuine mystery. The White House didn’t respond to reporters asking. I don’t know what to do with that except hope, sincerely, that if a Gümüşay .357 Magnum is sitting in a drawer somewhere in Washington, its owner doesn’t shoot himself in the foot with it — or, given the same forty-eight hours he spent reviving the push to annex Greenland, the other foot.

Because here’s what actually mattered that week, and it had nothing to do with revolvers. While thirty-some delegations sorted out gun customs, the summit’s one consequential gift exchange happened quietly between two men. Trump arrived with what people around him were calling a gift bag for Erdoğan — F-35s, F-110 engines, hundreds of millions in defence contracts, and the lifting of a six-year sanctions regime imposed after Turkey bought a Russian air-defence system. Asked if he was bringing something to make Erdoğan happy, Trump didn’t hedge: “I’m going to probably do something that’s going to make him very happy.” By week’s end, he had: “we’re going to be taking the sanctions off.”

Carney’s own gift, for the record, stayed exactly what it was. He put it best himself afterward, noting that his contribution “kind of undermatched” what came back. Fair — but the syrup was never actually competing with the gun, and neither of them was competing with the fighter jets. Erdoğan handed out thirty novelty revolvers destined for museums and embassy safes. In a quieter room, he handed the President of the United States a friendship, and got a jet program six years in the making back. Nobody needed a display case for that one.

I’m told Dior, hearing the whole saga on the lakeshore this week, offered no reaction whatsoever — her professional opinion, I’ve come to learn, of most things involving men, firearms, and fighter jets in the same news cycle. She has seen sturgeon in the harbour with more evident strategy.

That protocol officer is presumably drafting new guidance already. It writes itself: when in doubt, bring the syrup. Nobody has ever had to decommission a bottle of it, or explain it to airport police, or wonder for days where exactly it ended up.
