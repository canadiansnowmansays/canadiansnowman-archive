Every monarchy examined so far in this series has had some theory, however thin, of what happens when a king fails badly enough — a withdrawn Mandate, a prophet’s rebuke, a barons’ charter forced at the point of rebellion. England in the 1640s produced something none of those precedents quite anticipated: not a deposition, not an assassination in the dark, but a formal public trial of a reigning king, conducted by his own Parliament, ending in a public execution on a scaffold outside his own palace. It remains one of the starkest moments in this series precisely because it answers, with total clarity, a question every other monarchy in this series had left comfortably vague — can a king actually be held legally accountable to his own subjects — by simply doing it.

The theological ground Charles I stood on when this crisis arrived had been laid, deliberately and explicitly, by his father. James I, before uniting the English and Scottish crowns in 1603, had written a treatise, The True Law of Free Monarchies, arguing in the most unambiguous terms available that kings derived their authority directly from God, answered to no earthly institution, and that even a tyrannical king could not lawfully be resisted by his subjects, since to resist God’s anointed was to resist God himself. This was not a subtle or moderate position even by the standards of an era comfortable with strong monarchy; James was arguing something closer to the Egyptian model examined in Part I than to Zhou China’s conditional Mandate — kingship as an unconditional grant that no failure of the king’s own could revoke. Charles inherited this theology from his father as thoroughly as he inherited the throne itself, and he governed, for the whole of his reign, as though it were simply an accurate description of political reality rather than one contested theory among several available to him.

The practical consequences of that belief showed up almost immediately in Charles’s relationship with Parliament, and the pattern is worth tracing because it maps closely onto grievances this series has already seen catalogue themselves against other overreaching kings. Parliament in this period held the power to grant or withhold taxation, a check that, as Part VI showed with Magna Carta, English kings had been formally bound by since 1215 even when they resented it. Charles, needing money for wars with Spain and France that Parliament increasingly refused to fund, turned to a series of extra-parliamentary revenue mechanisms designed to raise money without asking Parliament’s consent at all — forced loans extracted from wealthy subjects under threat of imprisonment, and most famously, an expansion of “ship money,” a traditionally coastal, wartime levy for naval defense, which Charles extended in peacetime to inland counties that had never paid it before, arguing the king’s prerogative to defend the realm gave him this authority regardless of Parliament’s wishes. When John Hampden, a country gentleman, refused to pay and challenged the levy in court in 1637, the judges ruled, by a narrow margin, in the king’s favor — a legal victory for Charles that did enormous political damage, since it confirmed to much of the political nation that the king believed himself entitled to tax without consent whenever he judged it necessary, precisely the arrangement Magna Carta’s Clause 12 had been written four centuries earlier to prevent.

Religious policy compounded the crisis in a way that gave Charles’s opponents a second, equally combustible grievance running alongside the constitutional one. Charles, along with his close advisor Archbishop William Laud, pushed the Church of England toward more elaborate ceremony, greater clerical authority, and doctrinal positions many of his Protestant subjects regarded as dangerously close to Catholicism — a suspicion Charles’s own French Catholic queen, Henrietta Maria, did nothing to dispel. This reached its breaking point not in England but in Scotland, where Charles’s attempt to impose a new prayer book modeled on English practice in 1637 triggered open rebellion, riots in Edinburgh, and eventually a full Scottish invasion of northern England in what became known as the Bishops’ Wars. Charles, having governed for eleven years without calling Parliament at all — a period his opponents would later christen the “Personal Rule” and his critics the “Eleven Years’ Tyranny” — was forced back to Parliament in 1640 specifically because he needed money to fight the Scots, and the Parliament he finally summoned, having been shut out of government for over a decade, arrived with a decade’s worth of accumulated grievances and no inclination to be cooperative.

What followed was a rapid and mutually escalating breakdown of trust that neither side seemed able to arrest. The Long Parliament, so called because it would sit, with interruptions, for years, forced through a series of measures stripping away the extra-parliamentary mechanisms Charles had relied on and asserting Parliament’s own permanence against royal dissolution. In January of 1642, in an act that shattered whatever remained of the relationship, Charles personally marched into the House of Commons with armed soldiers, attempting to arrest five members of Parliament on charges of treason — the accused members, forewarned, had already fled by boat down the Thames, and Charles, standing in a chamber he had violated the sanctity of and finding his targets gone, is reported to have remarked that “the birds have flown,” a phrase that instantly became a symbol of exactly the kind of arbitrary, contemptuous royal power his opponents had been warning against for a decade. Within months, both sides were raising armies, and by the end of the summer of 1642, England had descended into civil war.

The war itself, running through most of the 1640s, is a genuinely complicated military and political story this series doesn’t have room to trace in full detail, but its trajectory matters for what came after: Parliament’s forces, reorganized into the professionally trained and rigorously disciplined New Model Army under commanders including Oliver Cromwell, proved decisively superior to the more traditionally organized royalist forces, winning a crushing victory at the Battle of Naseby in June of 1645 that effectively broke Charles’s ability to win the war outright.

Charles, defeated but still unwilling to accept any negotiated settlement that meaningfully limited his authority, spent the following years attempting to play his various opponents — the English Parliament, the Scots, the New Model Army itself, which increasingly had its own political demands separate from Parliament’s — against one another, in a pattern of bad-faith negotiation this series has already seen from John at Runnymede four centuries earlier. It was this refusal to accept any durable limit on his power, more than the original war itself, that ultimately convinced a critical mass of his opponents, particularly within the army’s leadership, that no settlement with Charles personally was possible, and that the king himself, not merely his policies, was the obstacle.

It is worth pausing on how far political thought inside the New Model Army itself had already moved during these years of negotiation and stalemate, because it shows the crisis was never purely a dispute about which specific limits to place on Charles personally. In the autumn of 1647, at a series of meetings in Putney later known as the Putney Debates, army officers and elected representatives of the ordinary soldiers — a genuinely radical innovation in itself, common soldiers debating constitutional first principles with their own commanders as something close to equals — argued openly over questions that went well beyond Charles’s fate. A faction known as the Levellers, led by figures like John Lilburne, pressed for a written constitution, near-universal male suffrage, and a fundamental reordering of political authority away from both crown and a narrowly propertied Parliament alike, arguing that “the poorest he that is in England hath a life to live as the greatest he.” These debates did not resolve in the Levellers’ favor — Cromwell and the more conservative officers ultimately prevailed, and the movement was suppressed by 1649 — but their occurrence at all shows that by the time Charles faced his judges, the argument in England had already expanded from “what limits bind this particular king” to “what, if anything, entitles any single person to rule over others at all,” a question this series will hear echoed again in France a century and a half later.

The trial itself, convened in January of 1649, was a genuinely novel legal and political undertaking, and its organizers were acutely aware of how unprecedented it was. No existing English court had jurisdiction to try a reigning monarch — the entire theory of the English legal system held that justice was administered in the king’s name, which made “the king versus the king” a structural impossibility under existing law. Parliament’s remaining members, having purged the body of members unwilling to proceed against Charles in what became known as Pride’s Purge, passed a special ordinance creating an entirely new High Court of Justice specifically empowered to try the king, and appointed commissioners, John Bradshaw prominent among them, to preside.

Charles, brought before this court, refused throughout the proceedings to enter a plea, arguing precisely the position his father had articulated decades earlier — that no earthly court held jurisdiction over an anointed king, that his authority derived from God alone, and that to submit to this tribunal’s authority would itself be an admission that kings could be tried by their own subjects, a principle he regarded as a contradiction in terms. “A king cannot be tried by any superior jurisdiction on earth,” he told the court, refusing to recognize its legitimacy even as it proceeded to convict him regardless of his refusal to participate in his own defense.

The formal charge read against him captured this same expanded reasoning: that Charles Stuart, as a man entrusted with limited power to govern according to law, had nonetheless taken it upon himself to erect unlimited and tyrannical authority over his subjects, and had waged war against Parliament and the people he was bound to protect in pursuit of that unlimited power — the language of trust and breach running through the charge as deliberately as the language of divine right had run through Charles’s own defense. He was found guilty of high treason on this theory, that a king who made war on his own Parliament and people had, in effect, betrayed the trust his own office was meant to embody, and was sentenced to death by a narrow margin among the fifty-nine commissioners willing to sign the death warrant at all. On the thirtieth of January, 1649, Charles was executed on a scaffold erected outside the Banqueting House at Whitehall, a building whose ceiling, in one of history’s more pointed ironies, had been painted by Peter Paul Rubens some years earlier with an allegorical scene celebrating the divine right of Charles’s own father James I. Charles reportedly met his death with considerable composure and dignity, wearing two shirts so that he would not shiver in the January cold and risk the crowd mistaking his shivering for fear, and his final words on the scaffold reaffirmed the same position he had held throughout the trial — that he died a martyr for his people’s true liberty, properly understood, even as he refused to the last to accept that liberty could include the power to try and execute him.

What makes this moment such a genuine hinge point for this series, rather than simply the most dramatic English constitutional crisis of the era, is what it establishes as a matter of demonstrated precedent rather than abstract theory: a king’s claim to unconditional, God-derived authority, immune to any earthly check, could be tested directly against organized political and military opposition, and could lose. Every monarchy examined earlier in this series that built in some conditional element — Zhou China’s revocable Mandate, Israel’s prophetic check on Saul and David — had done so as an internal feature of its own legitimating theology, a limit the system itself acknowledged. Charles’s execution was different in kind: it was his own subjects, organized through Parliament and the army, simply overriding his theology by force and legal invention alike, demonstrating that unconditional monarchy was, in the end, only as unconditional as the willingness of enough armed and organized people to accept it.

It is worth noting, in closing, how quickly and incompletely this outcome actually settled the underlying question, because England’s experiment with abolishing monarchy altogether proved considerably less durable than the execution itself. The Commonwealth that followed, and later the Protectorate under Oliver Cromwell as Lord Protector, never fully resolved the practical problem of how to govern England without a king, and Cromwell himself, by the end of his rule, exercised an authority in substance not far removed from monarchy, right down to being offered the crown outright by Parliament in 1657 — an offer he ultimately declined, reportedly out of concern for how it would be received by an army that had fought specifically to abolish the office, but the fact that it was seriously offered at all shows how quickly the practical need for a single stabilizing figure at the top of English government reasserted itself even after a king’s execution. Within two years of Cromwell’s death in 1658, his son and successor as Protector, Richard, proved unable to hold the fractious military and political coalition together, and in 1660, a restored Parliament invited Charles’s own son back from exile to take the throne as Charles II, in a restoration so thorough that the body of Oliver Cromwell himself was exhumed, symbolically hanged, and beheaded posthumously as a gesture of restored royal legitimacy.

England, in other words, executed a king for claiming unconditional authority, discovered that governing without any king at all was harder than expected, and within eleven years had a king back on the same throne. What had genuinely changed, and what would matter enormously for the different and far less bloody constitutional crisis this series will examine directly in Part XVII, was not that monarchy itself had been abolished, but that the specific claim Charles I had died defending — that a king’s authority was entirely unconditional and answerable to no earthly institution — had been demonstrated, in the starkest possible terms, to be false. Charles II and his successors would never again be able to claim, with a straight face, that Parliament’s power over them was merely theoretical. The scaffold outside the Banqueting House had settled that argument permanently, even if the crown itself came back.

Part IX travels far from England’s cold January morning to a very different kind of throne — one built not on a contested theory of divine right but on a deliberate, and for its time remarkably radical, experiment in religious tolerance, undertaken by a Mughal emperor who understood, better than almost any ruler examined so far in this series, that a throne governing a genuinely diverse population might need an entirely different kind of story to hold it together.
