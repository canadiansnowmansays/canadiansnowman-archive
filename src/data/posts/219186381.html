“Who controls the past controls the future; who controls the present controls the past.”

Most of what this series has catalogued has been loud. A wrecking ball swung at the Kennedy Center. Plaques prised off walls in national parks. A general’s portrait taken down within two hours of an inauguration, the holes patched and the wall repainted the next morning. Four hundred books carried out of a naval academy library. Warning signs ordered posted outside the front door of the National Museum of American History, as though the building were a slippery staircase rather than a repository of the nation’s past.

All of that was theatre, and theatre is meant to be watched. Its weakness, as Part 12 will argue, is that people do watch it, and a clear majority of them do not like what they see. Americans trust their museums more than their newspapers. They do not want the president editing the wall labels. You cannot restore Jefferson’s desk to 2008.

So it is worth paying attention when the campaign goes quiet. Silence, in an administration this fond of the megaphone, is usually a sign that someone has found a more efficient tool.

This week we learned what the tool is. It is a footnote.

The sentence

On October 6, 2026, Kelsey Ables reported in The Atlantic, drawing on public records no one had previously written about, that in late September 2026 the White House Office of Management and Budget had attached a footnote to two apportionments of federal funds for the Smithsonian Institution. The footnote covers money carried over from the previous year in two of the Smithsonian’s main salaries-and-expenses accounts — close to $190 million in all. It reads, in full:

To the extent permitted by law, amounts apportioned shall be available for obligation in a manner consistent with Executive Order 14253 (March 27, 2025), “Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History.”

Regular readers will recognise the executive order. It was the subject of Part 2, “Restored to 2008,” where it served as the series’ founding specimen of name inversion: an order that removes truth from public display titled as the restoration of truth, an order that imposes one man’s grievances on the national memory titled as a return to sanity. Its operative clause forbids spending on exhibits or programmes that “degrade shared American values” or “divide Americans based on race” — phrases broad enough that nobody can say in advance what they exclude, which is precisely the point.

The footnote does not say how the Smithsonian is meant to apply that clause. It doesn’t need to. It simply ties the institution’s operating money to compliance with a standard that only the White House is entitled to interpret.

And that is the whole design. Part 2 described a campaign that demanded the Smithsonian hand over its exhibit text for review. This footnote does not demand anything. It merely sits on the money, like a cat on a newspaper, and waits for the Smithsonian to work out for itself what it had better not say.

A word about apportionment

Apportionment is the dullest corner of the federal budget, which is exactly why it is worth understanding.

Congress appropriates money. It decides, by law, how much each agency gets and for what. OMB then apportions that money — releases it to agencies in instalments over the fiscal year. The process exists for a sensible housekeeping reason: to stop agencies spending a year’s budget by March and then coming back cap in hand. It was designed as a brake against overspending, not a steering wheel.

But an apportionment is legally binding. Footnotes attached to it are enforceable under the Antideficiency Act, and violating that Act can carry criminal penalties for the officials who sign off on the spending. So a footnote that says “spend this money consistent with Executive Order 14253” is not advice. It is a sentence — in both senses — hanging over every Smithsonian administrator who authorises a cheque for an exhibit someone at OMB might later decide was divisive.

Notice, too, the opening phrase: To the extent permitted by law. It reads like a concession, a polite bow to legality. It is the opposite. It shifts the entire burden of interpretation onto the person whose job and liberty are at risk. Is this exhibit on Reconstruction permitted? Is that oral-history programme on Japanese American internment “consistent” with the order? The footnote won’t say. The administrator must guess, and the safe guess is always to do less.

This is the series’ running pattern of the gentle word laid over the hard act, now operating at the level of a single subordinate clause. In 1984, nobody at the Ministry of Truth ever announced that history was being destroyed. They simply processed paperwork. The words “to the extent permitted by law” are paperwork of the purest kind.

A pattern, not an accident

The September 2026 footnote is not the first. It is the revival of a tactic that ran for most of a year and then seemed to have been put away.

According to Ables’s account, OMB began attaching footnotes to Smithsonian funds in September 2025, conditioning money on compliance with two documents: the March 2025 executive order and the August 2025 White House letter to Smithsonian Secretary Lonnie Bunch demanding exhibit materials for content review — the letter whose 120-day vetting deadline anchored Part 2.

Then, in April 2026, the footnote changed character. The new version told the Smithsonian to operate according to the president’s budget request rather than the appropriations bill Congress had actually passed — roughly $89 million less than lawmakers had legislated. A president’s budget request is a wish list. It has no force of law. Congress reads it, ignores most of it, and passes something else. OMB was attempting to resurrect the discarded wish list at the last stage of the spending process and treat it as though it had won.

That version also carried travel restrictions on Smithsonian staff, with two exceptions: the semiquincentennial festivities and the Arabian leopards at the National Zoo, a project underwritten by a gift from Saudi Arabia. One could hardly design a more precise portrait of priorities. The nation’s curators may stay home; the birthday party and the Saudi cats may travel.

After several revisions, that footnote was removed on July 2, 2026. Two days later, on July 4, the White House issued its 162-page report attacking the National Museum of American History — the document Part II treated as the climax of the loud campaign. The footnote tactic went dormant. The loud campaign took over. And then, in late September 2026, with the theatre having played to poor reviews, the footnote returned.

The sequence matters. When the quiet weapon was put down, the noisy one was picked up; when the noisy one failed to persuade, the quiet one came back. These are not two campaigns. They are one campaign with two volume settings.

What the witnesses said

The people who should have noticed largely did not, and that is part of the story.

Ables reports that Representative Chellie Pingree, the Maine Democrat who is ranking member of the House appropriations subcommittee overseeing the Smithsonian, spoke with Bunch over the summer and came away believing the institution had chosen to hold its fire until the footnotes actually interfered with its work. They were never challenged. Pingree’s concern is the next round. Senator Jeff Merkley of Oregon, ranking Democrat on the relevant Senate subcommittee, was blunter: holding funding to a budget request, he wrote, cannot override a law Congress passed, and withholding the money that way is itself illegal.

The most revealing testimony came from Cerin Lindgrensavage, a lawyer at the nonpartisan watchdog Protect Democracy, which has been tracking apportionments across the government. Her organisation had built a tool specifically to catch abuses of this kind. It did not flag the Smithsonian footnotes, because they were so bespoke that nobody had thought to look for them. “It never occurred to us that somebody would put something so egregious in text,” she told Ables.

Readers of Part 9 will recognise the shape of that sentence. The thesis there was that erasure depends on the absence of a comparison — on nobody keeping the copy, nobody noticing that the page used to say something else. The footnote is the purest form of that strategy yet: an act of memory control written down in a public record, in plain English, and still nearly invisible, because it was placed in the one document that only budget lawyers read.

The Washington Sun reported on some of the footnotes earlier in 2026. Otherwise, until this week, the institution’s financial strangulation took place in a room the public never enters — rather like the room at the Naval Academy where the removed books were declared “not immediately available.”

The founders saw it coming

What makes the footnote possible is an old vulnerability, and two of the Smithsonian’s earliest guardians warned about it in writing.

The Smithsonian began as a bequest from James Smithson, an English scientist who had never set foot in the United States. In 1842, John Quincy Adams — the former president, by then serving in the House of Representatives — opposed a bill that would have given the new institution federal money. He wanted it, in his words, “exclusively confined to itself and not entangled or commingled with any national establishment requiring appropriations of public money.” Adams understood that whoever pays the piper eventually wants to approve the programme.

Joseph Henry, the Smithsonian’s first secretary, accepted federal funding to run a national museum but was uneasy about it. In an 1877 letter he warned that the museum was “liable to be brought under direct political influence.”

They lost the argument, and for good reasons; a national museum on a private bequest alone would have been a much smaller thing. Today the Smithsonian receives about $1 billion a year in federal appropriations and is nearly two-thirds federally funded. And the division of labour between its two kinds of money tells you exactly where the pressure lands. Private donations pay for the glamorous things — blockbuster exhibitions, new wings, the openings with ribbons. Federal money pays for the unglamorous things: the lights, the guards, the climate control that keeps a two-hundred-year-old flag from crumbling, the conservators who know how to handle it.

Footnote the federal money and you are not threatening the next big exhibition. You are threatening the building’s capacity to keep remembering at all. This is the memory hole as a maintenance schedule.

The vacancy as a weapon

There is one more element, and it may be the most important. The footnote arrives at a moment when the Smithsonian is unusually short of people able to resist it.

Lonnie Bunch, the secretary who has carried the institution through every round of this campaign, retires at the end of 2026. Citizen seats on the Board of Regents have been falling vacant as terms expire and going unfilled, reportedly because the White House wants a hand in choosing replacements, as The New York Times reported in the summer of 2026. Museum directorships are open across the institution; in the week before Ables’s October 6 report, the director of the National Museum of Natural History announced he is stepping down.

Ables quotes one staffer, speaking anonymously, who said employees now fear the president may try to install a secretary of his own — just as he announced the “firing” of National Portrait Gallery director Kim Sajet on Truth Social in 2025. The Smithsonian insisted then that he had no such power. Sajet resigned anyway.

Part 5 of this series argued that removing a person removes a witness, and that every removal intimidates the people still on the wall. Part 11 adds the arithmetic: if enough witnesses retire, resign or are never replaced, you do not need to remove anyone. The institutional memory simply walks out the door on its own schedule, and the footnote ensures that whoever replaces it understands the terms of employment before the first day.

A power vacuum plus a conditional budget is not two problems. It is one machine.

The broader pattern

The Smithsonian is not alone. OMB has used footnotes against other federal bodies, including the National Science Foundation. In September 2026 a federal judge ruled against the administration in a case over Education Department apportionments, finding that using restrictive footnotes to force compliance with executive orders was unlawful in that instance; further claims in that case are still pending. Lindgrensavage is among the lawyers for the plaintiffs.

The president’s fiscal 2027 budget request proposes $961.3 million for the Smithsonian, about an 11 per cent cut and its lowest request in years, while emphasising the maintenance and protection of the museums on the National Mall — the same strip of ground where most of Trump’s commemorative ambitions for Washington are concentrated. Separately, the White House has called for cancelling some $810 million in federal spending, a move that prompted Senator Susan Collins, the Maine Republican who chairs Senate Appropriations, to accuse OMB on X of undermining Congress’s constitutional power of the purse.

When a Republican appropriations chair says that in public, the question of whether this is a partisan complaint has been answered.

Why this is the most Orwellian part yet

Every earlier part of this series could be photographed. A sign was there, then gone. A portrait hung, then a patched wall. An exhibit label read one way, then another. The erasure left a visible scar, and visible scars are what Part 9’s archivists and as Part 12 will show, a public that still trusts its museums — could organise against.

The footnote leaves no scar. If it works, nothing will visibly happen. An exhibit proposal will be quietly shelved in 2027 because no one wanted to be the official who signed for it. A travelling show on the civil-rights movement will be “deferred for budgetary reasons.” A curator will rewrite a label before anyone asks, to be safe. Nobody will ever be able to point to the moment the history changed, because it will never have been changed. It will merely never have been told.

Orwell described the end state precisely: The past was erased, the erasure was forgotten, the lie became truth. The footnote skips the first step. There is nothing to erase if the thing was never made.

That is why the Ministry of Memory has reached its most efficient instrument. The wrecking ball attracts crowds. The white-out pen gets taken to court. The footnote reads, “To the extent permitted by law,” and every administrator who reads it becomes, without being asked, a junior official of the Ministry.

What to watch

The Smithsonian has so far chosen not to fight the footnotes, and its silence is understandable; an institution that depends on Congress for its lights does not lightly sue the executive branch. But the Education Department ruling suggests the courts are willing to treat this tactic as what it is. Democratic lawmakers have already proposed legislation to protect the Smithsonian’s independence. And Collins’s intervention suggests that the power of the purse is one of the few remaining issues on which some Republicans will still defend their own branch.

The real test comes in two places. First, the next secretary: whoever replaces Bunch will tell us whether the footnote was a lever or merely a threat. Second, the next exhibit nobody announces. The danger is not what the Smithsonian takes down. It is what it never puts up.

John Quincy Adams wanted the Smithsonian kept clear of public money so that no government could hold its purse strings. Joseph Henry feared the purse strings would become political ones. Both men left their warnings in writing, in the record, where anyone could find them. That is the point of a record. It is also, as of this week, the point of a footnote — written in plain English, in a public document, and counting on nobody reading it.

Now somebody has.
