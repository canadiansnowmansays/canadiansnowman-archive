LISTING #1600-DC · JUST REDUCED (VISUALLY) · WON’T LAST

THE ARC DE TRUMP National Mall, Washington, D.C. Lot size: your entire sightline Status: Pre-construction, moving fast

UNOBSTRUCTED VIEWS (formerly obstructed by 100-year-old sightline). For over a century, the view running from the Lincoln Memorial to Arlington National Cemetery sat there doing nothing but connecting Americans to their Civil War dead. That’s a lot of empty air for one address. This listing changes that. Rising to skyscraper height directly in the vista’s path, the Arc de Trump finally puts that space to work.

AFFECTED COMPS: 35 other Washington landmarks, per the National Park Service’s own review. We prefer to call this “high walkability to 35 points of interest.” A less imaginative buyer might call it “collateral damage.” We call it “location, location, location.”

REVIEW PROCESS: Streamlined. On July 17, 2026, the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation quietly circulated a rewrite of the rules that have governed this kind of thing since 1966 — the ones requiring federal agencies to actually look at what a project might wreck before they build it. Council members got a week to vote. They voted July 24: 16 in favor, 5 opposed, 2 abstaining. The vice chairman had promised, just one month earlier, a 60-day comment window for council members alone before any of this moved forward. That promise did not survive contact with the calendar.

PUBLIC COMMENT: Tens of thousands of comments were filed on this very project and on the East Wing ballroom. Nearly all opposed. Both were approved anyway, which we mention not as an editorial position but as a data point about how much public comment matters once you’ve already decided to build the thing. Going forward, agencies will have, in the proposed language, “exclusive discretion to determine the usefulness of public participation” — a phrase we admire for doing in eleven words what used to take an entire lawsuit.

SITE VISITS: No longer required. Under the proposed rule, federal personnel don’t need to actually go look at a threatened site before deciding it’s fine to build on. We think of this as a time-saving feature, similar to buying a car without a test drive, except the car is a Civil War cemetery view and you cannot return it.

MITIGATION: Available, but only if “technically and economically feasible” — a standard so flexible it has never once, in recorded history, failed to favor the party writing the checks.

LISTING #EEOB-2 · FRESH PAINT · SELLER DISCLOSURE WAIVED

THE EISENHOWER EXECUTIVE OFFICE BUILDING 17th Street NW, Washington, D.C. Style: Second Empire (currently being renovated out of it) Recent updates: A full coat of stark white paint, applied directly to the building’s original gray granite

CURB APPEAL: Dramatically improved, according to the one buyer whose opinion mattered. Previously, the Eisenhower Building was widely regarded as the finest surviving example of Second Empire architecture in the United States — mansard roofs, columns, terraces, all in a rich, textured granite that had done its job since 1888. Now it’s white. All of it. We are told this is so it will “match” the White House next door, a building it was never designed to resemble and does not need to.

KNOWN ISSUES: Granite is not porous. It does not hold paint. Ask any conservator, or ask Paris, where you will not find a single painted Second Empire building, for good reason. This is less a known issue than a known certainty, currently being tested at public expense on a 137-year-old federal landmark.

COMPARABLE PROPERTIES: The Paris Opera. Not currently for sale. Not currently painted white. We rest our case.

PRIOR RENOVATIONS BY THIS DEVELOPER: The Bonwit Teller building, New York, 1980. Art Deco bas-reliefs on the facade were promised to the Metropolitan Museum of Art. The museum sent art handlers. The developer had the reliefs jackhammered off and destroyed instead, later citing the cost of the delay. Buyers considering an offer from this seller may find this relevant to their due diligence.

LISTING #RP-3 · WATERFRONT · REDUCED FOR QUICK SALE

THE REFLECTING POOL West of the Lincoln Memorial

FEATURES: Currently “a wreck,” in the words of one former New York Times architecture critic, following renovations funded in part by money diverted from the National Park Service’s operating budget. The bronze statues nearby have been painted gold. We are aware this is the second listing in this packet involving unauthorized paint applied to historic bronze or stone, and we would like to state for the record that we did not choose this pattern. It chose us.

WATER VIEWS: Present. Reflecting.

AGENT’S REMARKS

Some background for prospective buyers unfamiliar with how we got here. Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act has been quietly doing its job for sixty years: before a federal project can touch a place on or eligible for the National Register — currently about 1.4 million sites nationwide — the agency has to look at the damage, consult the public, and try to “avoid, minimize or mitigate” it. It doesn’t forbid anything. It has never once stopped a project outright. It saved the T.W.A. terminal at JFK and helped turn a chunk of Manhattan’s old main post office into Moynihan Train Hall, in both cases by getting people talking to each other before the wrecking ball showed up.

The Advisory Council on Historic Preservation — the body now proposing to gut it — is not, historically, hostile territory for this kind of law. It’s the agency Section 106 created to make it work. It is currently staffed, per its own critics, with appointees who arrived after the fact and found the mandate inconvenient. The council’s own draft argues the review process causes “confusion and uncertainty” and cites a study noting consultation can, in extreme cases, take nearly seven years. This is true, and also true of most permitting processes anywhere in the world that involve more than one interested party, which is the entire point of having one.

The proposed rule doesn’t repeal Section 106. It just quietly removes the reasons anyone would bother following it: public comment becomes optional rather than mandatory; the definition of what counts as a “historic property” is narrowed to exclude natural and sacred landscapes, including lands significant to Native American tribes; the requirement to consult early, while a project can still be redesigned, is gone; and federal agencies now get, per the proposed §800.1, to characterize the entire review as “procedural” — creating, in the ACHP’s own words, “no substantive historic preservation outcomes or obligations.” Which is one way of describing a fire alarm you’ve disconnected but left on the wall for appearances.

This is being finalized in the same year the country turns 250. We are told the arch and the ballroom are tributes to the nation’s legacy. We would simply note that a legacy is usually a thing you inherit intact, not a view you demolish in order to stand in front of something taller.

PRICE: Currently being paid by the public, whether the public agrees to the sale or not.

SHOWINGS: None required. The buyer has already toured the property, in his own words, “aloft,” holding a model of it over his head like a man who has never once needed to be shown anything twice.

CONTACT: Not the National Park Service, which flagged the damage and was overruled. Not the National Trust for Historic Preservation, which called this “an unprecedented and deliberate attack on our civic and cultural inheritance” and was also overruled. Try the Supreme Court. A federal appeals court already ruled the ballroom needed congressional approval it never got. That case is headed there next, and history — for the moment — is still deciding whether it’s for sale.
