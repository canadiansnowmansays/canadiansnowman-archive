Postmark: 500 kilometres up. A dead rocket adapter, tumbling, unpowered, since 2013. A robot with four arms closes in, matching the tumble, degree by degree, before it closes its grip.

This series has visited the Canadian arm that never comes home in Part X and the asteroid-mining robot still earning its keep on Earth in Part XII. This postcard visits a third and quieter corner of the same frontier — not an arm reaching toward something new, but a robot reaching back to clean up something old, because the alternative is a sky that slowly stops being usable at all.

The number that keeps a physicist awake

The scale of the problem is worth stating before any robot enters the story. As one 2026 industry account of the field puts it, tracking systems currently identify roughly forty thousand debris objects larger than a smartphone in Earth orbit — and that, a senior European Space Agency safety official said plainly at a recent debris conference in Bonn, is only the visible fraction of a far larger field. The agency’s own estimate for everything above the size of a cherry, roughly a centimetre, runs to 1.1 million objects; above a single millimetre, the estimate climbs to 130 million. None of this is visible from the ground. All of it moves at orbital velocity, which means even a fleck of paint or a stray bolt carries the kinetic energy of a small explosive on impact.

The theoretical danger behind that number has a name and a physicist. In the late 1970s, NASA scientist Donald Kessler described a scenario now known as the Kessler Syndrome: a cascade in which orbital collisions generate debris fields dense enough that those fields cause further collisions, which generate more debris, in a self-sustaining chain reaction that could eventually render entire orbital shells too dangerous for any satellite to safely occupy. Some scientists believe the earliest stages of exactly this cascade may already be underway. Earth observation satellites, according to European Space Agency analysis, already lose meaningful operational time to collision-avoidance manoeuvres they would not have needed to perform a generation ago — a slow, measurable tax on the entire satellite economy, levied by junk nobody currently owns the responsibility to remove.

The four-armed tow truck

The mission most likely to answer this problem first is ClearSpace-1, built by a Swiss start-up spun out of the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology in Lausanne, under contract to the European Space Agency. As ESA’s own mission announcement describes it, the spacecraft will rendezvous with the upper stage of a Vespa payload adapter — a piece of hardware left in orbit since a 2013 Vega rocket launch, tumbling and unpowered ever since — and capture it using a quartet of articulated robotic arms that close around the target the way a claw-machine game closes around a prize, before firing the combined vehicle’s thrusters to drag both itself and the debris down into the atmosphere to burn up together. The target was chosen deliberately for its simplicity: a relatively small, sturdily built, well-understood shape, meant to prove the capture technique works before the same company attempts a larger, more complex, and more valuable piece of debris.

ClearSpace’s own founder framed the stakes with a line this series finds hard to improve on: at orbital velocities, he has said, even a single loose screw can strike with explosive force, a threat no mission designer can shield against — which leaves active removal as the only remaining option once a dangerous object is already up there tumbling. ESA’s director general made the harder engineering point explicit: every orbital capture attempted until now has involved a cooperative target, a spacecraft designed and built to be caught. Space debris, by definition, offers no such cooperation. The object is adrift, spinning unpredictably, and the robot must match that unpredictable motion in real time before it can close its grip at all.

The rival approach, and the lighter cousin of an old friend

ClearSpace is not working alone, and the field’s other leading approach belongs to a company whose name this series has met before in a different context. Astroscale, with operations spanning Japan and the United Kingdom, has pursued its own sequence of increasingly ambitious missions — an earlier demonstration in 2021 successfully captured a piece of simulated debris using a magnetic docking system, though that technique only works on satellites specifically fitted with a compatible magnetic plate from the moment they launch, which does nothing for the vast majority of debris already in orbit without any such fitting.

The company’s newer Cosmic mission takes a different and rather elegant approach to the non-cooperative target problem this series has already found in ClearSpace’s design: as one exclusive report on the mission’s plans describes, Astroscale intends to use a lightweight version of the International Space Station’s own robotic arm — a direct engineering descendant of the Canadarm lineage this series traced in Part II and Part X, scaled down and repurposed from station assembly to orbital garbage collection — to grapple debris that was never built with any capture mechanism in mind at all. The same manipulator technology that assembled the ISS piece by piece, and that this series has now watched attempt to learn to stand on the Moon, has found its way into a third and rather more mundane role: cleaning up the mess an earlier generation of space activity left behind.

The question nobody wants to answer

Every account of this emerging industry converges, sooner or later, on the same unresolved problem, and it is worth stating plainly because it is not an engineering question at all. As one detailed industry analysis frames it, orbital debris is a textbook tragedy of the commons: everyone who operates a satellite suffers from a degraded, increasingly hazardous orbital environment, but no single actor currently has sufficient individual incentive to bear the full cost of cleaning it up, because the debris belongs, in any meaningful legal sense, to no one. Questions of liability — who is responsible if a removal attempt itself goes wrong and creates new debris — and of cost allocation — who actually pays for a service that benefits every satellite operator equally, whether or not they contributed to the mess — remain genuinely unsettled, even as the robots themselves move from laboratory demonstration to funded, scheduled missions.

Regulation, rather than altruism, has proven the strongest lever available so far. The United States Federal Communications Commission adopted a rule in 2022 requiring low-Earth-orbit satellites to deorbit within five years of the end of their mission, replacing a far laxer twenty-five-year guideline — a rule that applies to any satellite seeking access to the American market, which in practice means most of the world’s operators. Market analysts estimate the active debris removal industry could be worth three to five billion dollars annually by 2030, a figure driven overwhelmingly by exactly this kind of regulatory mandate rather than by the tragedy-of-the-commons logic resolving itself voluntarily. This is the same pattern this series has found in territory after territory — the personhood debate of a rotation ago, the construction-robot building codes, the AI Act’s risk tiers — technology outrunning the rules, and the rules arriving, unevenly and belatedly, mostly when a regulator finally forces the issue.

The swarm that might come next

A single four-armed robot chasing a single piece of debris at a time is, by every account of the field, only the opening move. NASA’s own Johnson Space Center has developed and patented a concept it calls the Active Debris Removal Vehicle — a smaller, purpose-built spacecraft designed specifically for the more numerous and more mundane category of large orbital debris, roughly one to four tonnes, that clutters the busiest orbital shells. The design’s genuinely clever feature is scale rather than sophistication: because each vehicle is small and single-use, up to eight of them can be launched together on a single payload, each one dispatched to a different derelict object within the same orbital inclination, turning one launch into eight separate cleanup missions rather than one.

That logic points toward the industry’s likely long-term shape, and it is a shape this series has now traced through several other territories. Rather than one enormous, expensive, reusable servicer chasing debris one careful capture at a time, the more scalable future may belong to fleets of small, purpose-built, largely disposable vehicles fanning out across a congested orbital shell — the same swarm logic Part XII found in asteroid-mining robotics, and Part XX found in disaster-response drones, applied here to a domain where losing one vehicle in a failed capture attempt costs vastly less than losing the single sophisticated servicer a whole mission depended on. Advances in autonomous navigation and miniaturized propulsion are, according to industry analysts tracking the sector, making this swarm-based approach increasingly practical rather than merely theoretical — and if it succeeds, the debris-removal industry may end up looking less like a specialized tow-truck service and more like an orbital sanitation department, quietly working the same unglamorous shift this series keeps finding underneath its more celebrated postcards.

There is a further twist on this industry’s own logic worth noting, because it echoes a theme this series has found running through its coverage of the space economy specifically. Rather than deliberately deorbiting captured debris for destructive reentry, several of the same companies pursuing capture technology are already thinking about harvesting materials from defunct satellites and spent rocket bodies instead — metals, composites, even residual propellant — as raw feedstock for building new structures directly in orbit, where launching equivalent material from Earth’s surface remains, by a wide margin, the single most expensive part of any space project. If that vision matures, debris stops being a pure liability to be burned away and becomes, instead, a resource already conveniently pre-positioned in orbit — the same reframing this series found in Part XII’s asteroid-mining ambitions, except applied to junk that is already there rather than a hundred million kilometres away and still unmapped.

What the tow truck is actually towing

From the balcony, this postcard belongs to a smaller and less celebrated category than most of what this series reports on: not a machine that does something new, but a machine built entirely to undo something old, cleaning up after the first six decades of a species learning to reach orbit before it had fully worked out what happens to everything it leaves behind. There is no glamour in a four-armed robot chasing down a discarded rocket adapter from 2013. There is, instead, something quietly essential about it — the recognition that every frontier this series has visited, from the factory floor to the ocean floor to low Earth orbit itself, eventually generates its own debris, and that a civilization serious about a frontier has to build the machines that clean up after it, not only the machines that explore it.

A Canadian reads this postcard with a particular appreciation for the lineage involved, because the very robotic arm now being adapted for orbital garbage collection is a direct descendant of the arm this series has followed since a Toronto clean room in 1981 — an arm built to assemble things, now being asked to tidy up after everyone else’s assembly. It is, in its way, the most Canadian possible fate for a robotic arm: quietly, competently, without much fanfare, doing the unglamorous job that keeps the whole enterprise usable for whoever comes next.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one arrived towing something nobody else wanted to touch.
