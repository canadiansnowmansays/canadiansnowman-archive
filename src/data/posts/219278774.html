Military people use the word “magazine” in two senses. One is the box of rounds that clicks into a rifle. The other, older one is the storehouse where a navy or an army keeps its ammunition. Both mean the same thing in the end: what you have left to fire.

This instalment is about America’s magazines after seven months of war with Iran, and about the gap between a Secretary who describes the war as won and the warehouses that describe it differently.

The Performance

“Allow me to lay it out simply for the anti-American losers in our media,” Pete Hegseth told the troops at Quantico on September 30, 2026. “These Americans, right here, and their peers, these have destroyed Iran’s navy, destroyed Iran’s air force, destroyed their air-defense systems, their leadership and their military industrial capacity, their entire defense base.”

It is a familiar claim. At a Pentagon briefing on March 19, 2026, less than three weeks into the war, he said that “never in recorded history has a nation’s military been so quickly and effectively neutralized.” On March 31 he announced a “decisive military victory.”

The New York Times’s Greg Jaffe, in his September 30, 2026 analysis “Hegseth Talks Tough as Republican Doubts About His Leadership Grow,” pointed out what the claim left out: Iran’s large stockpile of missiles, which it continues to fire at its neighbours and at tankers in the Strait of Hormuz. Helene Cooper and John Ismay, reporting for the Times the same day, added that Iran has kept its ballistic-missile and drone capability despite the administration’s claims, and that as Iran’s tactics evolved, the U.S. military burned through its munitions.

Every Iranian missile that is still flying has to be shot down by something. The question this essay asks is what.

The Paperwork

The early burn. The consumption started fast. As George Headley reported for Small Wars Journal on March 27, 2026, in “Magazine depth: Rapid depletion of missile stockpiles in Iran raises concerns about US readiness,” the first sixteen days of the war, which the Pentagon named Operation Epic Fury, used more than 6,000 defensive and offensive munitions. By the estimates he cited, that included about 400 Patriot PAC-3 interceptors, roughly 200 THAAD interceptors (about 40 percent of the inventory), 535 Tomahawk cruise missiles and about half of the Army’s stock of ATACMS and PrSM long-range missiles. Those are outside estimates, not Pentagon figures, because the Pentagon does not publish them. But they set the scale.

The summer count. By midsummer the picture was worse. As Natalie Oliverio reported for Military Times on July 30, 2026, in “Iran war depleted US Patriot missile stockpiles, creating readiness challenges, experts say,” the Center for Strategic and International Studies estimated that the United States had used about 65 percent of its pre-war Patriot interceptor inventory, leaving fewer than 1,000. Its estimate for THAAD put the remaining stock at somewhere between 234 and 278 interceptors.

As Jeff Schogol reported for Task & Purpose the same day, in “It could take the US military 3 years to restock its missile defenses,” that meant a fall in the Patriot inventory from about 2,330 to between 759 and 827, and in the THAAD inventory from 452 to the same 234-to-278 range. In other words, somewhere between 38 and 48 percent of all THAAD interceptors were gone. CNN reported on August 4, 2026, under the headline “Nearly 80% of interceptors for a key missile defense system depleted,” that sources put one system’s losses higher still.

The production problem. Munitions can be bought. What cannot be bought quickly is the factory capacity to make them.

The CSIS analyst Mark Cancian explained the lag simply: “What we’re getting now are missiles that were funded in 2023.” Interceptors are not artillery shells. They contain advanced seekers, rocket motors and electronics, with long supply chains and limited suppliers. Virginia Burger of the Project on Government Oversight told Task & Purpose that three years to rebuild pre-war levels was a conservative estimate, and that she expected it to take much longer.

The contracts are large. The Army has given Lockheed Martin a $58.6 billion contract through fiscal 2032 to expand production of the PAC-3 MSE. Lockheed has also signed a seven-year, $35 billion deal to quadruple THAAD interceptor production. General Motors Defense delivered its first PAC-3 missile housing components on September 17, 2026, just 22 days after signing a manufacturing agreement. That is genuine progress, and the defence industry deserves credit for it. But a housing is not a missile. It still needs a seeker, a motor, assembly and testing.

The official estimate. The Congressional Budget Office has now said the same thing. As Jack Buckby reported for National Security Journal on September 29, 2026, in “The Congressional Budget Office Warns That America’s Stock of Missile Interceptors Will Stay Depleted for Years Due to Iran War,” the CBO warned in September that U.S. interceptor stocks would remain depleted for years. Jaffe’s Times analysis put replenishment at two to three years, according to defence experts.

The warnings were already on file. None of this should have surprised the Pentagon. Two earlier conflicts had already rehearsed it. When Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022, the United States discovered that its factories were producing only about 14,000 155mm artillery shells a month, a small fraction of what Ukraine was firing. It took years of emergency contracts to raise output substantially, and the original production targets slipped. Then, in 2023 and 2024, the Navy spent many hundreds of millions of dollars’ worth of Standard Missiles and other munitions shooting down Houthi drones and missiles over the Red Sea, a campaign that prompted senior officers to warn openly about the rate at which expensive interceptors were being used against cheap threats.

The lesson of both episodes was the same: modern wars are won or lost partly in the factories, and American munitions stocks are sized for short, sharp conflicts, not long ones. Defence analysts have a name for the problem: magazine depth. A military with deep magazines can absorb a long war and still deter the next one. A military with shallow ones has to choose.

The Iran war has turned out to be exactly the long, grinding kind of conflict that magazine-depth studies warned about. Its Secretary declared victory within three weeks. The magazines have been fighting it ever since.

The arithmetic of cost. There is a cruel asymmetry in this kind of war. Iran’s drones and missiles are relatively cheap; the interceptors that stop them are not. A Patriot PAC-3 MSE costs millions of dollars a round, and a THAAD interceptor considerably more. Headley’s estimate put the THAAD interceptors used in the first sixteen days alone at about $3 billion. The supplemental request examined in Part II of this series includes $21 billion for munitions, but as that instalment showed, Congress has not been told in any detail what the $67 billion would buy.

The Bill

Deterrence elsewhere. The interceptors fired over the Gulf were not bought for the Gulf alone. They are the same weapons the United States needs to defend Guam, Japan, Korea and Taiwan against a far larger Chinese missile force, and to keep supplying Ukraine against Russia’s. Jaffe’s Times analysis made exactly this point: the stocks drained in Iran are the ones needed to deter a larger war with China or Russia. CSIS’s Cancian and Chris Park framed the real risk not as running out in this war, but as facing another high-intensity crisis before the stocks are rebuilt.

That window, two to three years at best, is exactly the period that much American planning has identified as one of maximum danger in the Taiwan Strait. A rival watching American magazines empty over the Gulf can do the arithmetic.

The allies. Gulf partners have fired their own interceptors too. Defence analysts have reported that Saudi Arabia’s Patriot stocks have been badly depleted, and all of them are now competing with Ukraine, Poland, Japan and the United States for the same production lines. Every interceptor delivered to one customer is one that cannot go to another. The war’s cost to allied air defence will be counted for years after it ends.

Ukraine is the most exposed. Its cities depend on Patriot batteries against Russian ballistic missiles, and every interceptor that goes to the Gulf is one that does not go to Kyiv. European governments that have been buying Patriots for their own defence and for Ukraine’s now find themselves in a queue behind the United States itself. Canada, which has been working to rebuild its own ground-based air defence after decades without it, is in the same queue.

The troops. The most direct bill falls on the American soldiers and sailors in the Middle East, about 50,000 of them by the Times’s count. They depend on interceptors for their lives. Every month of war with a shrinking magazine means harder choices about which bases, which ships and which partners to protect first. Those are decisions made by officers, out of public view, with numbers that are getting smaller.

The taxpayer. Restocking will cost tens of billions of dollars on top of the war itself. The contracts already signed run past $90 billion for Patriot and THAAD alone, before the Tomahawks, the long-range Army missiles and the Navy’s Standard Missiles are counted. That money will be spent whether or not the war ends tomorrow. The bill for the past seven months arrives over the next seven years.

What candour would look like. It is worth imagining, briefly, the speech that was not given at Quantico. A Secretary of Defense who took the magazine problem seriously would have told the troops, and through them the country, something like this: the war has cost us a large share of our missile defences; rebuilding them will take years; until then we will have to make hard choices about where we can defend; and I need Congress to fund multiyear production now, not after the next crisis. That speech would have been less fun to deliver. It would also have been the most useful thing a Secretary could say to the appropriators whose votes he needs, and to the allies whose air defences are drawing on the same production lines.

Instead the speech described an enemy whose “entire defense base” had been destroyed, and spent its final minutes on a new base, a new command and a new study led by celebrities. Not one of the initiatives announced that day was about rebuilding the stocks that the war has used. The factories, at least, appear to have read the reports.

The claim. Finally, there is the cost to the Secretary’s own credibility, and therefore to the department’s. If Iran’s military had truly been destroyed, the interceptors would have stopped flying in March. They are still flying in September, because Iran’s missiles are still flying. A Secretary who keeps declaring a victory his own magazines contradict asks Congress, the allies and the troops to choose between his words and their own eyes. Most will choose their eyes. Some, like the 45 senators who wrote to him in September, will also ask for the receipts.
