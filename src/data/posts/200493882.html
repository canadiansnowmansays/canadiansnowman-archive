Mass AI-powered surveillance in China — facial recognition and predictive profiling that turns technology from a tool of liberation into one of control.

In 2006, the political risk analyst Ian Bremmer published a book called The J Curve. The argument was elegant. Countries that are stable because they are open — the United States, Japan, the Nordic democracies — are more stable than countries that are stable because they are closed — China, North Korea, Saudi Arabia. The openness itself is the source of strength. Technology reinforced this: the internet enabled bottom-up communication, allowed citizens to learn what their governments were doing, and made it harder for authoritarian systems to maintain the information control their stability depended on. The Arab Spring. The colour revolutions. The smartphone as liberation technology.

Speaking with Ezra Klein in June 2026 for the New York Times, Bremmer said the J Curve no longer applies. “It is completely wrong today,” he said. “The world has changed.”

What changed is technology. Not technology in general — the direction of technology. The internet was bottom-up. AI is top-down. The internet distributed the capacity for communication. AI concentrates the capacity for interpretation. The internet made it harder for governments to control what their citizens knew. AI makes it easier — if the government controls the models.

“Technology back in 2006 was actually advantageous to more open societies,” Bremmer said. “Then you move from that system to one where technology is top-down. It’s a surveillance-based system. It’s a data-based system. It’s algorithmic nudging of people by other people and even by bots. It’s attention-based, it’s addictive, and it benefits closed societies, and it undermines open societies.”

The inverted J-Curve — technology that once empowered open societies (bottom-up internet) now strengthens closed ones (top-down AI surveillance and control).

This is the Asimov Gap stated as a geopolitical argument. The gap between what AI can do and what democratic institutions are empowered to govern is not merely a domestic failure of American policy. It is a structural advantage that the technology is conferring, right now, on the governments that are willing to govern AI without democratic constraints. The governance deficit in democracies is a geopolitical gift to autocracies. Every month that the United States fails to build adequate AI governance is a month that China’s model — AI as an instrument of state power, disciplined and directed — looks more coherent by comparison.

This essay is about what the geopolitical AI race actually is, who is winning it, and why the answer has almost nothing to do with which country has the best models.

Two Models

The technology press covers the AI race as a competition between capabilities. Who has the most powerful model. Who has the most compute. Who has trained on the most data. By this measure, the United States leads — though the margin is narrower than it was three years ago, and the DeepSeek release in early 2025 surprised even the people inside the leading American labs.

Bremmer argues, and this series has been arguing since Part 1, that the capabilities race is the wrong race to watch. The race that matters is the governance race. Who will establish the rules that govern how AI is used — in warfare, in surveillance, in democratic elections, in the economy — and whose model of governance will other countries adopt as AI diffuses globally.

The United States is losing the governance race. Not because China is winning it. Because the United States is not running it.

Consider the Chinese model. China is not deploying AI to empower its citizens. It is deploying AI to surveil them, to direct them, to predict who might become a critic of the state before the criticism occurs. The Geedge Networks documents, reported in June 2026, show a Chinese company developing AI tools to examine location data and internet use to generate profiles that predict who could “do or say something critical of the government.” Brett Goldstein of Vanderbilt’s Wicked Problems Lab described it directly: “This is what happens when mass surveillance meets AI. Without checks and balances, what China is doing to its own citizens is a preview of what becomes possible anywhere these tools go unchecked.”

China’s surveillance model going global — exporting the Great Firewall logic and AI tools to authoritarian governments worldwide.

But China is not trying to export its governance model in the crude way that authoritarian states once exported ideology. It is exporting infrastructure. Geedge Networks sells a commercial version of the Great Firewall to governments in Ethiopia, Kazakhstan, Myanmar, and Pakistan. The surveillance tools China developed for its own citizens are products that authoritarian governments elsewhere can purchase. The governance model travels with the infrastructure. You buy the tools; the logic of the tools shapes how you govern.

This is the geopolitical AI race in its most consequential form. Not who has GPT-5. Who is building the surveillance infrastructure that the next generation of authoritarian governments will run on.

The Electrostate and the Petrostate

Bremmer offered a frame for understanding the long-term competition that goes beyond surveillance technology.

The United States has become, through fracking and shale oil, the world’s largest producer of petroleum. American energy independence — once a distant aspiration — has been achieved. The Trump administration has doubled down on this position: drilling, export, the revival of coal, the withdrawal from climate commitments that might constrain hydrocarbon production.

China, meanwhile, is becoming what Bremmer calls the world’s largest “electrostate.” China’s exports of green energy technology — solar panels, battery storage, electric vehicles, wind turbines — now exceed American exports of oil and gas by value. China is not merely producing this technology for domestic use. It is exporting the infrastructure that other countries will use to build out their 21st-century energy systems. “The thing they’re exporting,” Bremmer said, “which we are way behind on — is what you use, the physical machinery that turns sunshine into energy. China has the infrastructure of how any country becomes an electrostate.”

China as the emerging “electrostate” — dominating solar, batteries, and EVs, building the energy infrastructure that will power the world’s AI compute.

The connection to AI is direct. AI requires compute. Compute requires energy. The country that controls the infrastructure through which other countries generate energy for AI compute has leverage over those countries’ AI development. China’s dominance in renewable energy infrastructure is not merely an economic advantage. It is a strategic position in the AI governance race.

The United States, by contrast, is optimising for the energy of the 20th century while China builds the energy infrastructure of the 21st. Texas produces more renewable energy than any other American state — and more petroleum. The appropriate response, Bremmer argues, is both. “We should be able to do both of those things.” The Trump administration has chosen petroleum. The choice has consequences not for energy policy alone but for the geopolitical capacity to govern AI at scale.

The Chip Decision

The most concrete AI governance decision the United States has made in the geopolitical context is the chip export control — the decision to restrict China’s access to the most powerful AI processors designed by Nvidia.

The Biden administration’s export controls were designed to maintain an American advantage in AI capabilities by preventing China from acquiring the compute necessary to train frontier models. The logic was straightforward: if China cannot access H100 and H200 chips, its models will lag. The lag will preserve American leadership.

The Trump administration has weakened those controls. During Trump’s summit with Xi Jinping in Beijing, American officials indicated that China would have access to a more advanced version of Nvidia’s chips. Nvidia’s lobbying — which Bremmer described as being “of sole interest to Nvidia” and “not aligned with U.S. policy toward China” — appears to have been effective.

Bremmer’s assessment of this decision is worth recording carefully: once the United States declared a cold war on semiconductors by implementing export controls, relaxing those controls does not make China unsee what America has already done. The investment in domestic chip manufacturing that China accelerated in response to Biden’s controls will not stop because some of the controls have been eased. China is still building toward chip independence, and it will get there. “All you’re doing with the H200s is letting them catch up when they’re spending — they are moving as fast as humanly possible, in the constraints of their economy, to catch up with the Americans on semiconductors.”

The chip decision matters for AI governance because the argument for maintaining controls was not primarily about capabilities. It was about the uses of those capabilities. As documented in this series, the Geedge Networks surveillance infrastructure was measurably hampered by Biden-era chip controls. The predictive political profiling technology — the system designed to identify who might become a critic of the Chinese government before any criticism occurs — was constrained by limits on compute. Easing the chip controls does not merely narrow the US capability lead. It removes a constraint on the development of the most dangerous applications of Chinese AI.

The chip divide — US export controls on advanced AI semiconductors as a key (and contested) tool in the governance race with China.

Jimmy Goodrich of the University of California put it with precision: “Chinese security services are dealing with an overload of data. They have too much information at once. The real value of artificial intelligence is that they can triage the data and find the threats. But their ability to scale that depends on their access to compute. This is what makes export controls so important.”

The governance question is not whether China will eventually develop the compute it needs for frontier surveillance AI. It almost certainly will. The governance question is whether the United States has chosen to accelerate that timeline, and whether it has done so with clear eyes about the consequences.

The Gracchi Trap

The Gracchi brothers addressing the Roman people — reformers whose norm-breaking tactics foreshadowed the Republic’s descent into dysfunction and civil strife.

Bremmer offered a historical frame for what America’s AI governance failure looks like from the outside — not the Thucydides trap, which frames the US-China relationship as a contest between a declining hegemon and a rising challenger, but the Gracchi trap.

The Gracchi brothers were Roman tribunes who, in the second century BC, attempted a political revolution on behalf of citizens who felt the Roman system no longer represented them. They broke the norms of Roman political life in doing so. They were killed for it. But the norms they broke stayed broken. When later leaders — Marius, Sulla, Caesar — broke those same norms, the precedent had been set. The internal political dysfunction that the Gracchi initiated was what ultimately destroyed the Roman Republic, not any external enemy.

“The U.S. is unilaterally withdrawing from its alliances,” Bremmer said. “It’s saying: We don’t want to be dependable.” America is no longer the architect of free trade, no longer reliably present for its treaty partners, no longer investing in the multilateral institutions that it built and that gave American values global reach. The consequence is not that China fills the vacuum — China has its own problems and is not positioned to build the global architecture the United States is abandoning. The consequence is that the vacuum itself becomes the governing condition. A world without reliable rules.

For AI governance, the Gracchi trap is this: the United States is the only country currently capable of establishing global AI governance norms that would have real force. It has the leading companies, the leading research institutions, the deepest financial markets, and the most advanced legal infrastructure. If the United States does not lead on AI governance, the governance that emerges will be a patchwork — European AI regulations that apply in Europe, Chinese infrastructure logic that shapes the developing world, and no coherent global framework for the questions that matter most: autonomous weapons, political manipulation, mass surveillance, the concentration of economic power.

The Gracchi trap is not that China wins. It is that no one does. The governance gap becomes permanent not because an authoritarian alternative prevails but because the democratic alternative fails to show up.

The Asimov Frame

Asimov set his Robot stories in a future where the Earth and the Spacer civilizations had developed fundamentally different relationships with technology. The Spacers had robots. The Earth had people. The tension between the civilizations was not merely technological. It was civilizational — a contest between two models of what human society could be.

The geopolitical AI race is that contest, running in real time. The United States has the most capable models. It does not have adequate governance of those models. China has extensive governance of AI — in the sense that the state directs AI development toward state purposes and constrains AI deployment that might threaten state power. It does not have the most capable models.

The question Asimov’s stories asked was not which civilization had better technology. It was which civilization had thought more carefully about what technology was for. The Spacers had robots, and the robots were slowly making the Spacers obsolete — not through malice but through comfort, through the accumulation of delegations that left the Spacers incapable of the things they had delegated. The Earth had people who retained their capacities because necessity required it.

The geopolitical version of this question is: which civilization is thinking more carefully about what AI is for, and what constraints are necessary to ensure it serves human flourishing rather than undermining it?

The answer, at this moment, is neither. China is using AI to extend state power at the expense of its citizens. The United States is allowing AI to concentrate private power at the expense of its citizens. Both are governance failures. The difference is that the United States has the democratic institutions, the legal infrastructure, and the civil society to correct its failure — if it chooses to use them.

The laws are beginning to come, as documented in Part 43. They are domestic laws, addressing domestic concerns. The international dimension — the governance race, the infrastructure exports, the chip decisions, the question of who writes the global rules — remains almost entirely ungoverned. No international treaty. No multilateral framework. No equivalent of the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty for autonomous weapons, no equivalent of the Geneva Conventions for AI-enabled surveillance, no equivalent of the internet governance institutions for the political information problem.

The J-curve has inverted. The technology that once gave open societies a structural advantage now gives closed societies one. The democracies that built the technology are not governing it. The autocracies that are governing it are not leading it. And the institutions that could reverse the inversion — the alliances, the multilateral frameworks, the international norms that the United States spent eighty years building — are being dismantled at the precise moment they are most needed.

The gap is not just domestic. It is global. And it is widening.
