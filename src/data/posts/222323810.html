For the second time this month, Tom Bartlett of The Atlantic has handed this series its material. On October 15 it was Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s claim that RSV came out of a laboratory. On October 27 it is a peptide called BPC-157, the man who has spent forty years trying to prove it works, and the federal health department that has decided it would rather not wait for the proof.

Bartlett’s piece is long, careful and funny, and it lands on two words. After weeks with Predrag Sikirić, the 72-year-old Zagreb pharmacologist whose name sits atop nearly every paper on the compound, Bartlett concludes that the professor’s message boils down to trust me. Trust me about where it comes from. Trust me that it works in people. Trust me that it is safe.

That is a reasonable thing for an inventor to say. It is not a reasonable basis for a drug policy. And yet, as of this week, it is the basis of one.

The Compound

BPC-157 is a chain of fifteen amino acids that Sikirić first described in 1993 as a fragment of a protein in human gastric juice. In the decades since, it has been tested on mice, rabbits, chickens, honeybees and, above all, rats, which have had their tendons cut, their stomachs ulcerated and their spinal cords compressed so that the treated ones could heal faster than the untreated ones. In the published literature they nearly always do. Bartlett’s summary is that the peptide is a rodent panacea.

The trouble starts when you ask the ordinary questions. Polish researchers writing in Pharmaceuticals found that more than 80 per cent of the 190-odd papers on the compound list Sikirić or his close collaborator as an author, and that no published animal data actually show the tumour-inhibiting effect Sikirić claims, while his own work shows the kind of blood-vessel signalling that oncologists worry can feed tumours.

Then there is the question of where the thing comes from. Bartlett asked William Pearson, a computational biologist who helped build the first widely used tool for searching protein and DNA databases, to find BPC-157’s sequence in nature. Pearson combed through hundreds of millions of sequences and found no match in humans or in any other animal, rats included. Sikirić’s answer is that he isolated it from newborns in the 1990s, never published the work, and does not intend to. He compared disclosing the details to a country giving away its nuclear secrets.

The human evidence is thinner still. A Phase 1 safety trial ran in Tijuana in 2015, sponsored by a company Sikirić co-owns. Its results have never been published. Bartlett was allowed a brief look at a summary on the professor’s laptop and nothing more. Earlier trials under the Croatian drugmaker Pliva stopped short of Phase 3 after GlaxoSmithKline bought Pliva’s research arm in 2006. Sikirić says GSK buried the drug because it worked too well. The Pliva researchers Bartlett spoke to suggest something duller: it did something, but nothing that beat what was already on the shelf.

None of that has slowed the market. BPC-157 is sold online as a freeze-dried powder labelled “for research use only”, mostly manufactured in China, and injected into the abdomens of people who heard about it from Joe Rogan or Andrew Huberman. Kennedy has called himself a big fan of peptides and has said he used them to recover from injuries.

What the Agency’s Own Scientists Said

In September 2023 the FDA placed BPC-157 and a group of other injectable peptides on its list of bulk substances that compounding pharmacies may not use because they raise significant safety concerns. The worries were specific: contamination, immune reactions, and the possibility of promoting tumour growth.

This June, ahead of a review requested by the peptide industry, FDA staff produced a 68-page assessment of BPC-157. As AJMC summarised the briefing materials, the agency’s scientists found the available human studies, five trials in all, too short and too small to establish safety or effectiveness for any of the uses proposed. They could not tell from the literature how the dose changed the outcome. They flagged the risk of harmful immune responses. They recommended against allowing it, and against all six of its companions.

At the meeting itself, as The Hill reported, one FDA staffer made a point that ought to have ended the conversation: the agency had found many different substances being sold as “BPC-157” that turned out to contain different active molecules. A product with no agreed identity cannot have an agreed safety profile.

The Panel

The decision was not the staff’s to make. It belonged to the Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee, and the committee that met on July 23 and 24 was not the one that had rejected peptide after peptide in previous years.

Weeks before the meeting, the department named new members. The Associated Press found that the reshaped panel included more than half a dozen people who ran clinics, online businesses or pharmacies specialising in peptides, among them a Florida operator of a men’s-health clinic chain that sells peptide injections. ABC News reported that six of the eight recent appointees ran clinics offering peptides. An expert quoted by Healio called it a structural conflict-of-interest problem rather than a personal one, which is the polite way of saying the problem was the design, not the individuals.

Two days before the vote, after the criticism landed, the FDA added a further group of temporary voting members, many of them limited to particular peptides, as Bloomberg Law reported. It did not change the result. The committee voted 8 to 6, with one abstention, to recommend that BPC-157 be added to the list of ingredients pharmacies may compound. Over the two days it backed six of the seven peptides before it. NPR called the outcome a striking rejection of the agency’s own stance.

The yes votes were framed as harm reduction. One clinician who voted yes told reporters he was frankly disappointed by how incomplete the data were, and voted yes anyway. A dissenting member observed that the randomised data that did exist suggested BPC-157 might be no better than placebo.

The market did not wait for the transcript. Shares of Hims & Hers, the telehealth company best known for discreetly mailing men generic Viagra, rose more than 11 per cent on the day of the BPC-157 vote, according to Reuters, which also reported that the company’s chief medical officer had told the panel Hims stood to benefit commercially. In its August earnings call, as Glossy reported, chief executive Andrew Dudum said the company had already begun stability testing on peptide ingredients at its California facility so it could move extremely quickly once the FDA acted. Analysts have put the market for these compounds at two to three billion dollars.

This series noted the stacking of that panel back in July. What has changed since is not the panel. It is the route.

The Side Door

An advisory committee’s vote is advice. To actually add a substance to the compounding list, the FDA has to propose a rule, take public comment and finalise it, a process the AP noted could take years. As of mid-October the agency had done none of that for any of the six peptides.

So the department found a shorter road. On October 14, the FDA sent a document titled “Interim Access Policy for Compounding Using Certain Peptides” to the White House Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs. OIRA’s public docket lists it as a notice, not a proposed or final rule, and classes it as not economically significant. Its text has not been released. According to two people familiar with the plan who spoke to the AP, it would let pharmacies temporarily compound some of the most popular peptides, BPC-157 among them, and assure them they would not be targeted for doing so.

The regulatory term for that is enforcement discretion. The plain term is that the sheriff has agreed to look the other way. No drug is approved. No rule changes. Nothing is put to public comment. The agency simply promises not to enforce the law it already has.

Kennedy was asked about the plan on Fox News on October 20. His answer, as reported by Reuters, was that “Americans are going to be buying these anyway,” and that the goal was a regulated marketplace in which people get what the label claims. Becker’s Hospital Review, summarising Bloomberg’s reporting two days later, said the plan now includes more patient protections than expected: pharmacies would have to certify the purity and identity of what they make and report serious side effects. Separately, the Partnership for Safe Medicines filed a citizen petition on October 16 asking the FDA to block compounding of all seven peptides and set aside the July vote.

The Fair Argument

There is a genuine case on the other side, and Bartlett makes it himself. If people are going to inject BPC-157 regardless, better that it come from a licensed American pharmacy than from a storage unit supplied by an overseas chemical plant. Bartlett sent the sample Sikirić gave him to a lab. It came back pure and full strength, unlike much of what is sold online. Purity certification and adverse-event reporting are real improvements on the status quo, and Kennedy deserves credit if they survive White House review.

But look at what the harm-reduction argument actually establishes. It is an argument for knowing what is in the vial. It is not an argument for knowing what the vial does. Bartlett’s lab could confirm that his sample was one hundred per cent BPC-157. It could not tell him whether BPC-157 heals a tendon, protects a stomach, feeds a tumour or numbs a mind. The online group of users who believe the compound left them unable to feel pleasure may be wrong. So may the podcasters who say it fixed their elbows. Both are anecdotes, and anecdotes are all there is, because the trials that would settle it have either never been run or never been published.

A government that genuinely wanted Americans to stop buying grey-market peptides had a straightforward option: fund, or require, the clinical trials. Instead it chose to supply the product first and leave the question of whether it works for later, which in practice means never, since nobody runs an expensive trial on a compound they can already sell.

Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate

The TACO lens this series uses fits uncomfortably well. Before taking office, as the AP recalled, Kennedy declared that the FDA’s war on peptides and other wellness products would soon be over. That was the threat. The reshaped committee was the accommodation. The FDA overruling its own reviewers is the capitulation. And the obfuscation is the framing: a policy that loosens oversight presented as one that tightens it, because the alternative, in Kennedy’s telling, is “gray market” peptides made in China.

That last point deserves more than a slogan, because it is where this story touches the national-security brief of this series. The supply chain for these compounds runs largely through Chinese chemical manufacturers, and a former founding director of the FDA’s Office of Biotechnology, writing in the Washington Examiner, pointed to investigations tracing online peptide sales to firms linked to the trade in fentanyl precursors. An interim policy that turns American pharmacies into eager buyers of bulk peptide ingredients does not cut that chain. It gives it a licensed customer.

The Uncle’s Law

The first essay in this series, back in April, contrasted Kennedy with his uncle and his father. It is worth returning to, because the law Kennedy’s department is now stepping around has the family name all over it.

American drug regulation was built one disaster at a time. Harvey Wiley’s “Poison Squad” of volunteers ate measured doses of food preservatives at a government table to show the public what was in its dinner, and the campaign produced the Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906. In 1937 a Tennessee company dissolved a new antibiotic in an untested solvent and sold it as Elixir Sulfanilamide. More than a hundred people died, many of them children, and the next year Congress required that drugs be shown safe before they were sold. In 1962, after thalidomide deformed thousands of babies abroad and was kept out of the United States by a single stubborn FDA reviewer, Congress added the requirement that a drug be shown to work. The president who signed the Kefauver-Harris Amendment on October 10, 1962 was John F. Kennedy.

Every one of those laws was written because someone, somewhere, had said trust me.

Whatever That Is

It is hard not to feel some sympathy for Sikirić. He is not getting rich. The grey-market sellers are. He has spent his working life on one idea, he takes the compound himself, and he plainly believes it. In Bartlett’s portrait he is less a con man than a man in love, which is what one of his former Pliva colleagues called him.

But the FDA does not exist to measure sincerity. The patent-medicine sellers whose advertisements decorate Sikirić’s office wall were, many of them, sincere too. The point of the system his visitor’s government is now bypassing is that belief, however heartfelt, is not evidence, and that the people asked to swallow a medicine are owed more than the inventor’s word.

Bartlett asked Sikirić what would happen to the secret of BPC-157’s origin if he were hit by a bus. The professor allowed that it could be a huge problem. The same is true of the policy now waiting at the White House. It rests on one man’s unpublished certainty, a committee rebuilt to agree with it, and a health secretary who is a fan. Take any one of them away and there is nothing underneath.

On the same day Bartlett’s piece appeared, the agency next door lost one of the people the system depends on. The New York Times reported on October 27 that Dr. Jennifer Shuford, the former Texas health commissioner who ran that state’s measles response and whom Trump appointed CDC deputy director to steady the agency, is leaving after four months. She cited family. Nobody has tied her exit to peptides, and this essay does not. But a remark from Dr. Anne Zink, Alaska’s former chief medical officer, fits the whole department: “taking these jobs, we all have to know our red line and when it is important to walk away.” At Kennedy’s HHS, the people who ask for evidence keep finding theirs. The people who say trust me stay.

Bartlett’s test result is the right place to end. The powder was pure, full strength, one hundred per cent BPC-157. It was the real thing, he wrote, whatever that is.

Nobody at HHS can yet answer that question. They have decided to start selling it anyway.
