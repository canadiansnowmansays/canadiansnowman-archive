On July 8, 1994, Kim Il-sung died of a heart attack at his villa in Pyongyang. He was eighty-two years old and had ruled North Korea for forty-six years — longer than almost any non-royal head of state in the twentieth century. He had built a country from nothing, survived a war, outlasted Stalin, outmanoeuvred Mao, defied every American president from Truman to Clinton, and constructed a political religion so total that millions of people wept in the streets of Pyongyang with a grief that outside observers struggled to interpret and North Korean officials did nothing to moderate.

The grief was real, at least in part. Kim Il-sung had been the only leader most North Koreans had ever known. He had been the father of the nation in the most literal ideological sense — not a president or a general or a party secretary, but a patriarch, the Sun of the Nation, a figure whose portraits hung in every home and whose birthday was the most important date in the national calendar. To lose him was, for people whose entire reality had been constructed around his presence, genuinely disorienting.

What came next was worse.

The Odd Son

Kim Jong-il was not an obvious successor. He was short — roughly 5’3” — sensitive about his height to the point of wearing platform shoes and bouffant hair to add inches. He was reclusive, uncomfortable with crowds, painfully shy in public settings in ways that his father, a natural performer and propagandist, had never been. He wore oversized glasses and khaki jumpsuits that became, over time, a kind of accidental brand. He had a stammer. He drank heavily — his reported consumption of Hennessy cognac was legendary enough that he was at one point alleged to be one of the largest individual purchasers of the brand in the world.

He was also, by all accounts of those who encountered him, exceptionally intelligent, operationally ruthless, and possessed of a strategic sophistication that his public image entirely concealed. He had spent two decades in the Korean Workers’ Party apparatus, running propaganda, managing culture and the arts, and positioning himself as his father’s obvious heir through a combination of institutional control and carefully cultivated personality cult. By the time Kim Il-sung died, Kim Jong-il had already been the effective administrator of the state for years.

What he lacked was military credibility — a serious deficit in a state whose ideology and identity were built around anti-Japanese guerrilla resistance. Kim Jong-il had never fought anyone. He had been born in the Soviet Union in 1941 while his father was training with Red Army units, and his most formative years had been spent not in the field but in the corridors of the party apparatus. To compensate for this weakness, he constructed an alternative legitimacy through the songun — military-first — policy that elevated the Korean People’s Army to the supreme position in North Korean governance, making it not merely an instrument of state power but the very definition of it.

The logic was elegant in its circularity: Kim Jong-il could not claim the military’s legitimacy through personal history, so he made the military’s legitimacy dependent on him instead.

The Film Collector

Before he was a supreme leader, Kim Jong-il was a film obsessive. He had amassed a personal collection of over 20,000 films — Hollywood blockbusters, European art cinema, anything his North Korean diplomats stationed in Western capitals could obtain and ship to Pyongyang. He watched them in his private screening rooms, studied them with the attention of a serious student of the form, and wrote a book — On the Art of the Cinema, published in 1973 — that was at once a genuine aesthetic treatise and a political manifesto about the role of art in revolutionary society.

His particular favourites included the Rambo films, the James Bond series, and any film featuring Elizabeth Taylor. The combination is not without psychological interest: hypermasculine action heroes, globe-trotting spies with license to kill, and one of Hollywood’s great icons of impossible glamour. A man who lived in a hermetically sealed state, consuming fantasies of freedom and excess in private while publicly demanding austerity and sacrifice from his people.

The film obsession had one extraordinary practical consequence. In 1978, dissatisfied with the quality of North Korean cinema — which he considered dull, didactic, and internationally invisible — Kim Jong-il ordered his agents to solve the problem directly. He had his operatives lure South Korea’s most famous actress, Choi Eun-hee, to Hong Kong with the promise of a film project. Men in speedboats bundled her off a beach and six days later she arrived in Pyongyang, where she was installed in a luxury villa and told, with the blandness of a man discussing a scheduling matter, that she was there to help improve North Korean cinema.

Six months later, her ex-husband — director Shin Sang-ok, one of South Korea’s most celebrated filmmakers — was also kidnapped in Hong Kong, transported to North Korea, and eventually imprisoned for four years when he refused to cooperate. In 1983, Kim arranged what he presented as a casual dinner party and “introduced” the two former spouses to each other — neither having known the other was in the country. Shin and Choi made seventeen films over the next several years, including a monster movie, a kung-fu film, and a historical drama for which Choi won Best Actress at the Moscow International Film Festival. In 1986, while attending a film festival in Vienna, the pair slipped their handlers, ran to the American embassy, and defected. They eventually settled in the United States.

The story is baroque enough to seem invented. What it reveals about Kim Jong-il is not eccentricity but a governing philosophy: if the state requires something, the state takes it. There are no limits. There is no jurisdiction. The world outside North Korea’s borders is a resource to be exploited for the state’s purposes, and anyone in it is potentially available.

The Famine

Kim Jong-il inherited power in a country already in serious economic difficulty. The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 had been catastrophic for North Korea — Soviet subsidies and preferential trade terms had been a crucial prop under the Juche facade of self-reliance, and their removal exposed the structural dysfunction of the North Korean command economy with brutal clarity. China had moved to market-rate pricing in its trade with Pyongyang in 1993. North Korea’s economy, which had already been stagnating for a decade, was in free fall.

Then came the floods. In 1995 and 1996, catastrophic flooding destroyed crops across the agricultural regions that fed the country’s population. Then came drought. The state distribution system, already hollowed out by the collapse of Soviet support, simply broke down in large parts of the country. People were left to forage, barter, and — in the worst-affected areas — die.

The famine that followed, which North Koreans call the Arduous March — a name drawn from a piece of Kim Il-sung mythology about a desperate winter campaign against the Japanese — killed somewhere between 600,000 and 2 million people, depending on whose estimates you use. The range itself tells a story: the North Korean state never acknowledged the true scale, never allowed independent assessment, and actively suppressed information about the death toll. Outside researchers working from demographic data, refugee testimony, and satellite imagery have tried to reconstruct what happened. The honest answer is that no one knows with precision how many people starved to death in North Korea in the mid-to-late 1990s. The range of credible estimates spans a population the size of a medium-sized city to one the size of a small country.

What is not in dispute is that the famine was not natural. It was a policy failure compounded by a political decision. Food was available in some parts of the country while people starved in others. The regime’s distribution priorities favoured Pyongyang, the military, and the politically loyal. Those in disfavour, those in the hostile songbun categories, those in the northeastern provinces furthest from the capital — they received the least, and they died the most.

The northeast was the epicentre. Provinces like North Hamgyong and South Hamgyong, historically the most industrialized regions of the country, had been hit hardest by the collapse of Soviet trade — their factories had run on Soviet inputs, their workers had depended on state rations tied to factory production, and when both disappeared simultaneously, the regional economy simply ceased to function. Defectors from these provinces describe scenes of a society in total dissolution: children begging at train stations, bodies left on roadsides because families lacked the means for funerals, people boiling grass and tree bark to produce something with the consistency of food. Markets emerged spontaneously in some areas — people selling whatever they owned, whatever they could scavenge — because the state distribution system had abandoned them and they had no other option. These improvised markets, illegal but tolerated by local officials who were themselves hungry, were the origin of the informal economy that Kim Jong-un would later spend enormous energy trying to destroy. The famine created the very thing that would most threaten the regime’s ideological control — a population that had learned to survive without the state, and that had learned, in the process, not entirely to trust it.

Kim Jong-il, during the worst years of the famine, continued importing luxury goods. His personal Hennessy cognac budget was reportedly running at several hundred thousand dollars a year. His palaces were maintained. His private train, with its luxury carriages and mobile communications equipment, continued operating. The military budget was not cut. The nuclear weapons programme was not cut. The people who were cut were the people the regime considered least essential to its survival.

The Nuclear Acceleration

The famine years coincided with the most intensive phase of North Korea’s early nuclear development — and the period of the most serious international attempt to halt it. In 1994, the Clinton administration came close to authorising military strikes on North Korean nuclear facilities. The crisis was resolved by the Agreed Framework — a deal in which North Korea agreed to freeze its nuclear programme in exchange for fuel oil, assistance building light-water nuclear reactors, and movement toward normalised relations.

The Agreed Framework was, in retrospect, a pause rather than a resolution. North Korea took the concessions, slowed the visible programme, and continued developing covert uranium enrichment capabilities. When the Bush administration confronted Pyongyang with evidence of the covert programme in 2002, North Korea acknowledged it and withdrew from the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty in January 2003 — the only country ever to have done so. The Six Party Talks — involving North Korea, South Korea, the United States, China, Japan, and Russia — attempted over several years to construct a new diplomatic framework, producing a 2005 joint statement in which North Korea agreed in principle to abandon all nuclear weapons in exchange for security guarantees and normalized relations. By 2006 North Korea had conducted its first nuclear test. The joint statement had lasted less than a year before both sides found reasons to abandon it. By 2006, under Kim Jong-il, North Korea had conducted its first nuclear test — and the era of serious denuclearization diplomacy was effectively over, though neither side would admit it for another decade.

The calculation behind this trajectory was coldly rational. Kim Jong-il had watched Saddam Hussein’s Iraq disarmed of its weapons of mass destruction programmes and then invaded anyway. He had watched Muammar Gaddafi voluntarily surrender his nuclear ambitions in 2003 and then, eight years later, be dragged from a drainage pipe and killed by his own people with NATO air support overhead. The lesson was unambiguous and Kim Jong-il drew it with precision: states that surrender their deterrents are states that can be destroyed. States that have nuclear weapons cannot be.

This was not ideology. It was not paranoia. It was a reading of recent history that was, on the available evidence, entirely correct.

The Dynasty’s Second Proof

Kim Jong-il died on December 17, 2011, of a heart attack aboard his private train. He was sixty-nine years old and had ruled for seventeen years. He left behind a country that had nearly collapsed — and hadn’t. A famine that had killed hundreds of thousands — and had been survived, at least by the state. A nuclear weapons programme that had crossed the threshold — and could not be uncrossed. And a succession problem that he had solved only partially and at the last minute, by belatedly designating his youngest son, the Swiss-educated, basketball-obsessed Kim Jong-un, as his heir.

The second Kim’s legacy is not straightforwardly heroic even in the terms his own state uses. The famine happened on his watch. The economy shrank on his watch. The country became more isolated and more impoverished during his seventeen years than it had been at any point since the Korean War. The songun policy that placed the military above all other priorities produced a military that consumed resources the population desperately needed, and a state that spent money on missiles while its people ate bark.

And yet the state survived. It survived the end of Soviet support. It survived a famine that killed perhaps a tenth of its population. It survived the Agreed Framework’s collapse and the confrontation with the Bush administration that followed. It survived every prediction, made by every analyst in every decade, that it was about to fall.

The survival itself became proof of legitimacy — not because it meant the system worked, but because it meant the system endured. In the theology of the Kim dynasty, endurance is the ultimate argument. A state that cannot be destroyed proves, by its continued existence, that it deserves to exist. The logic is circular. It is also, for a family that has now held power for three generations, empirically difficult to refute.

The Dear Leader is gone. The foundation he built — shaky, brutal, and improbable — held long enough to be handed to his son. His son is standing on a balcony in Beijing.
