August 28, 2026

There is a story, possibly apocryphal, that clings to Henry II of England: exasperated with his own Archbishop of Canterbury, the king is supposed to have muttered something like “will no one rid me of this turbulent priest?” — after which four of his knights rode to Canterbury Cathedral and murdered Thomas Becket at the altar. Henry never issued the order. He never needed to. The wish, spoken aloud by the most powerful man in the kingdom, did the work an order would have done, and everyone around him understood that.

Robin Wigglesworth reached for that story on Ezra Klein’s podcast this week, and it is, I think, the single best image anyone has produced yet for what has been happening to American bond markets this August. Wigglesworth — the Financial Times editor who covers this territory for a living — was trying to explain why Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent keeps doing things to the bond market that Bessent himself, by his own professional history, ought to know will not work. The explanation Wigglesworth settled on was not incompetence. It was Becket. Donald Trump says he wants bond yields lower, wants borrowing cheaper, wants the economy to feel more affordable before the midterms — and does not, technically, order anyone to do anything about it. People around him simply understand what is wanted and go do it, whether or not it will actually help the king.

Trump’s Own Deniability, On Tape

The clearest illustration of the dynamic came from Trump’s own mouth. Asked directly whether he had ordered Bessent’s recent interventions in the Treasury market, Trump answered: “No, not at all. No, he’s a very capable man. He wanted to do it.” Then, asked whether another kind of intervention might follow, Trump offered this, apparently as a joke: “We have many types of intervention. That’s one. The ultimate intervention is our military, and if we have to use that, we will, yeah.” Both he and the interviewer laughed.

I want to sit with that exchange for a moment longer than the laughter allows, because it is the most alarming sentence to appear in this series’ four instalments, and it arrived wrapped in a chuckle specifically so that it would not have to be taken seriously. An American president, asked about the bond market — the mechanism that sets the price of every mortgage, auto loan, and credit card balance in the country — responded by gesturing, however jokingly, at the armed forces of the United States as a tool of last resort against bond traders. Wigglesworth’s own reaction is worth recording plainly: “that escalated fairly quickly. I’ve not heard of people trying to use the military against the bond market before.” Neither, I suspect, had anyone else, which is precisely why it was said as a joke rather than as policy. Jokes are how you say the unsayable thing and retain the ability to deny you meant it.

The Slingshot, Graded

I have quoted Bessent’s slingshot line in two previous instalments of this series — the OBBBA’s factory expensing as potential energy being pulled back before it becomes kinetic. Bessent used a version of the same image again this week, on the same podcast material Wigglesworth was discussing: pulling back the slingshot, a lot of potential energy that will turn into kinetic energy. The metaphor has now outlasted two separate policy justifications, which either means it is a genuinely durable way of thinking about fiscal policy, or means it is the only device Bessent has for describing a plan whose payoff keeps arriving in a future that keeps receding.

Wigglesworth’s verdict on the broader Bessent programme — his self-styled “3-3-3” plan, a 3 percent deficit, 3 percent growth, three million additional barrels of oil a day — was delivered in four words, after a pause and a laugh of his own: “Not well, I think.” This is a useful moment for the series’ running scorecard. Every ambitious numerical target this administration has set for itself — DOGE’s trillion dollars, Bessent’s 3 percent deficit path, the OBBBA’s promised growth dividend — has now been independently assessed by someone with direct professional exposure to the relevant data, and none of them has cleared its own bar.

A Water Pistol on a Wildfire

The specific mechanism drawing scrutiny this week is the Treasury’s buyback programme, which the Second Addendum covered as a headline event — the announcement that Treasury would double the amount of its own debt it is permitted to repurchase from investors. Wigglesworth’s description of what that programme actually does, mechanically, is worth preserving in full because it deflates the announcement precisely. A newly issued Treasury bond is highly liquid — tradable in enormous volume without moving the price. As it ages, it becomes “stale,” locked into vaults and pension funds, thinly traded, and available at a slight discount. The buyback programme spends a few billion dollars purchasing those older, cheaper bonds, funded by issuing new, more liquid ones. Bessent expanded that programme this week, framed as a liquidity intervention. Against a Treasury market that trades over a trillion dollars a day, Wigglesworth’s assessment is precise: “This is like putting out a wildfire with a water pistol.”

The result, predictably, was that yields fell briefly on the announcement and then climbed back above where they had started — a pattern documented by multiple outlets covering the days immediately preceding Jackson Hole, including Reuters and CNBC, both of which noted that Bessent responded to the failure not by abandoning the approach but by publicly signalling the Treasury stood ready to intervene “with much higher amounts,” left deliberately undefined. Peter Boockvar of One Point BFG Wealth Partners offered the blunt read that has now become close to consensus among the analysts covering this: “the market is in the driver’s seat,” and no Treasury department, however aggressive its buyback programme, can substitute for the market’s own price-setting power over long-term debt.

Warsh Finally Speaks

I noted in the last instalment that Kevin Warsh had inherited the Fed’s $6.8 trillion balance sheet and a task force charged with reviewing it by year’s end, without yet detailing a specific plan. On the morning of August 28, 2026, Warsh delivered his first Jackson Hole keynote as Fed chair, and the closest thing this series has gotten to real information about his governing philosophy arrived not as a policy commitment but as a description of what he intends to withhold.

Warsh confirmed, in remarks prepared for the Kansas City Fed’s annual symposium, that the American economy remains at “full employment,” while inflation figures “are more concerning” — the Fed’s preferred gauge having risen 3.7 percent over the year to July, comfortably above the 2 percent target, even as the economy shed 23,000 jobs the prior month. What he did not do, despite a CNBC survey in which 80 percent of respondents said he should explain his economic views in more detail, was commit to a specific rate path or clarify what economists call his “reaction function” — the rule by which the Fed would respond to changing data. The Washington Post’s characterization of the speech is the cleanest available: Warsh “doubled down on his controversial strategy of providing investors less explicit forward guidance.”

This is worth setting directly against Wigglesworth’s account, recorded on the Klein podcast before the speech itself, of what that strategy is actually for. Warsh, in Wigglesworth’s telling, is sympathetic to the idea that a degree of unpredictability might be structurally healthy — that markets behaving as though the Fed’s reaction is perfectly knowable in advance encourages exactly the kind of unchecked risk-taking that inflated the housing bubble before 2008. It is a genuinely defensible theory of central banking. It is also, invoked at this specific moment, a theory that happens to justify giving a jittery bond market nothing to hold onto, at the precise moment a nervous Treasury Secretary is asking the same market to trust that the fundamentals are sound.

Two Men, Two Theories, One Market

What Jackson Hole has clarified, more than anything Warsh actually said, is the shape of the disagreement now sitting inside the administration’s own economic leadership. Reporting from NBC in the hours before the speech laid it out plainly: Warsh has signalled he is willing to let bond yields rise in response to market forces, while Bessent appears unwilling to let longer-dated yields go up at all. These are not two tactical variations on a shared strategy. They are opposite theories of what the Treasury market is currently telling Washington, arriving from the two officials most responsible for managing it, in the same week, days apart.

Analysts covering the divide before Warsh spoke framed the choice starkly. BofA strategists warned that unless Warsh adopted a more hawkish communication style or offered clearer guidance on the inflation outlook, the 30-year yield — already trading above 5.17 percent, its highest level since 2007 — could climb rapidly toward 5.5 percent and beyond. Warsh’s actual remarks split the difference in the way least likely to satisfy anyone: acknowledging inflation as a genuine concern while declining to commit to the rate action that acknowledgment would normally imply. The market’s initial reaction, characteristically, will tell us more than the speech’s text does — which is itself the point Wigglesworth was making about the entire Bessent-Warsh dynamic. Nobody quite knows what either man will do next, including, it increasingly seems, the two men themselves.

The Structural Change Nobody’s Discussing

Buried in the middle of the Klein conversation, and easy to miss beside the Trump-military line, is what I’d argue is the most consequential fact in this entire instalment for the series’ long-term argument: the composition of who actually buys American debt has shifted underneath the political drama. Through the 2000s, the Treasury market was dominated by what Wigglesworth calls “price-agnostic” buyers — central banks and sovereign wealth funds holding Treasuries primarily for liquidity and safety rather than yield. That pool has stabilized, even shrunk. In its place, hedge funds have grown from roughly 2 percent to close to 8 percent of the Treasury market — meaning, as Wigglesworth put it, hedge funds now own more U.S. government debt than Japan, China, and Saudi Arabia combined.

This matters because hedge funds are leveraged in a way sovereign buyers are not. A fund can put down $100 million and control a billion dollars of Treasuries; when the cost of that leverage rises, the fund gets “shaken out” of the position fast, in a way that produces the opposite of the traditional crisis pattern. Normally, in an economic emergency, investors flee to Treasuries and yields fall. Wigglesworth notes this inverted in both March 2020 and April 2025’s Liberation Day episode — yields rose during the panic, because leveraged funds were being forced to sell into it. This is the mechanical version of the Blankfein “auction nobody attends” scenario from the Addendum, and it is arguably more dangerous than Blankfein’s framing suggested, because it does not require a crisis of confidence in American solvency. It only requires a crisis of confidence in the leverage of the specific funds currently holding a disproportionate and growing share of the debt.

The Survey That Explains Everything

I want to close on the detail from this week’s reporting that most directly extends the argument I made in the Third Addendum about the amargi and the reset nobody will hold a ceremony for. Wigglesworth cited a National Bureau of Economic Research survey in which bond investors, asked about the odds of a U.S. debt crisis within the next decade, put the probability at roughly 50 percent. Asked whether this belief had changed their portfolio strategy, the overwhelming majority said it had not.

Wigglesworth’s explanation for this apparent contradiction reached, unprompted, for the same borrowed Augustine line I used to close the Second Addendum: “Lord, make me chaste, but not yet.” He is right that this is not really an economic puzzle. It is the oldest form of human self-deception, applied at trillion-dollar scale — the capacity to hold a genuine, quantified belief that catastrophe is roughly a coin flip away, and to take no action whatsoever, because the catastrophe has not yet arrived on a Tuesday with your name on it. Jerome Powell, in his own valedictory remarks before leaving the Fed chairmanship, put the same idea more soberly to a group of students: “The level of the debt is not unsustainable, but the path is not sustainable.” Everyone measuring this problem professionally has now said some version of the same sentence. Nobody measuring it professionally has changed what they are doing about it.

Henry II never ordered anyone to kill Thomas Becket, and the ambiguity of what he actually wanted has occupied historians for nine centuries since. Whatever Donald Trump actually wants from Scott Bessent and Kevin Warsh — a bond market that behaves, an economy that feels affordable before November 2026, or simply the appearance of control over a mechanism that no single government official has ever fully controlled — the two men currently tasked with divining his wishes are, this week, pulling in opposite directions, on a $32 trillion Treasury market that trades a trillion dollars a day and answers, ultimately, to neither of them.
