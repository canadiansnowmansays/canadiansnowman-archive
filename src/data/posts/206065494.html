Somewhere underneath Wednesday’s noise in Ankara — the insults, the Greenland demands, the trade threats against a NATO ally — there was an actual piece of news, and it is worth digging it out before it gets buried entirely under everything louder that happened around it. President Trump told President Zelensky, at the start of their bilateral meeting, that the United States would license Ukraine to manufacture its own Patriot air defense systems. “Let’s say, ‘make them yourself,’” he said. “We haven’t informed the company of that yet, but that’ll work out all right.” The company in question is Raytheon, which currently holds sole production rights to the system Ukraine has spent the better part of a year begging allies for. If this actually happens — and “we haven’t informed the company yet” is doing a great deal of load-bearing work in that sentence — it is the single most consequential thing to come out of two days of theater, and it arrived as an aside, sandwiched between a joke about sending Zelensky to Moscow and a complaint about NATO’s “free ride.”

As the New York Times reported, the alliance’s own communiqué backed this up with numbers: a reaffirmed “ironclad commitment” to Article 5, and $80 billion in military aid for Ukraine over this year and next — though the fine print matters here, since $30 billion of that figure is an EU loan and the rest is a mix of previously committed funds, not fresh money. Mr. Rutte added two more figures worth sitting with alongside it: $50 billion in new weapons deals sealed at Tuesday’s defense industry forum, and a further $30 billion earmarked for fuel storage and pipeline infrastructure, including on NATO’s eastern flank, which he described as ensuring allied forces “have the energy supplies they need for war fighting readiness” — a sentence that assumes a war NATO itself isn’t yet fighting, aimed at a front that, for now, still belongs to Ukraine alone. Notably absent from the six-paragraph communiqué was any renewed pledge toward Ukrainian NATO membership, a commitment Mr. Trump opposes and which allies apparently decided wasn’t worth the fight. The document calls for “a modernized alliance” with more European responsibility and less American involvement — the same rebalancing this diary flagged as NATO’s real Day One agenda, now formalized in communiqué language that reads calmer than the summit that produced it. The same document also restated opposition to Iran ever acquiring a nuclear weapon and called on Tehran to respect freedom of navigation in the Strait of Hormuz — the diplomatic version of Tuesday’s split screen, folded quietly into six paragraphs about Ukraine.

The Day’s Designated Target

If Tuesday’s outburst was aimed broadly at Italy, Germany, and France, Wednesday’s had a single, specific target: Spain. “I don’t want anything to do with Spain,” Mr. Trump said, seated beside Secretary General Rutte. “Cut off all trade with Spain, please, including visits. They’re hopeless, bad people.” Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez, for his part, seemed almost bored by the threat. “Commercial relations are woven between companies, not between governments,” he told reporters, noting that Spain runs a trade deficit with the US and that trade policy is an EU competency Madrid doesn’t control alone. He added that he and Mr. Trump had spoken informally about golf and the World Cup “with absolute cordiality” — the two facts sitting side by side without apparent contradiction to anyone involved.

Spain’s health minister, Mónica García, was considerably less diplomatic, calling the episode confusion between “diplomacy” and “bullying.” The more interesting reaction came from Brussels, where a European Commission spokesman noted, simply, that the US was expected to honor a trade agreement finalized only weeks earlier — a reminder that Mr. Trump’s threat, whatever else it accomplishes, requires breaking a deal his own administration just signed. One Brussels economist offered the more cynical read worth repeating here: that the outburst functioned as a way of shifting attention away from what’s actually happening in the Middle East, a diagnosis that will sound familiar to readers of this diary’s own compass metaphor, applied here inside a single news cycle rather than across a full year.

The Host’s Other Guest List

Here is the part of the week’s coverage that deserves far more attention than it got, because it says something about this summit that neither the Patriot deal nor the Spain spat quite captures: Turkey spent the run-up to hosting NATO’s leaders arresting its own. In the weeks before the summit, Turkish authorities detained 225 people in a security sweep, sent 103 to pretrial custody, and blocked websites critical of NATO and the summit itself. Among those swept up were environmental activists, a political science professor, the editor of an LGBTQ magazine, and a 32-year-old comedian named Deniz Goktas, arrested at Istanbul’s airport and charged with insulting the president and inciting hatred by mocking religion — his offense, in part, a joke suggesting Mr. Erdogan’s own son would not intervene to keep him out of prison if he killed someone in a car accident, a reference to an actual 1998 case in which the elder Erdogan’s son was acquitted after a fatal collision. Separately, a gay cruise line was barred from docking at Turkish ports at all, its scheduled stops in Istanbul and elsewhere cancelled outright, on the stated grounds that its passengers didn’t align with “our society and our moral values.”

NATO’s own founding treaty commits its members to “the principles of democracy, individual liberty and the rule of law.” Nobody at this summit raised that preamble against its host, and NATO’s own spokesperson, asked about journalist accreditation being denied to reporters who’d covered previous summits without issue, said the alliance simply “relies on the host nation” for those assessments — an answer that hands Ankara a veto over its own press coverage and calls it procedure. Human Rights Watch’s Turkey director put the asymmetry plainly: Turkey is further outside NATO’s stated democratic norms than any other member, and that gap has become oddly invisible precisely because Ankara’s usefulness to the alliance — as a bridge to Russia, to Iran, to Hamas — has grown at exactly the moment Washington’s own commitment to the alliance looks shakiest. The country hosting a summit about collective defense spent its preparations defending itself against a stand-up comedian.

The War That Didn’t Pause for Any of This

Amid the theater, the actual war ground on. Russia launched 169 drones and seven missiles overnight Tuesday into Wednesday; thirty drones and all seven missiles evaded Ukrainian air defenses, killing three people in the Kharkiv and Kyiv regions. Mr. Zelensky, for his part, confirmed Ukraine had struck Russian oil and production infrastructure in Saratov, Tatarstan, and Bashkortostan overnight — what he called, on social media, “long-range sanctions,” a formulation that reframes Ukraine’s deep-strike campaign as a policy instrument rather than a battlefield tactic, and one worth watching for how often it gets repeated. When Mr. Trump, in front of reporters, asked Mr. Zelensky whether he’d take Vladimir Putin up on an invitation to Moscow, Mr. Zelensky’s answer landed with more edge than the moment’s smile suggested: “It’s difficult — there are a lot of Ukrainian drones there. It’d be dangerous.” He was joking. He was also not, entirely.

Denmark, meanwhile, used the summit to restate something Mr. Trump’s Greenland fixation keeps forcing it to restate. “We are ready to defend every inch of NATO, including our own territory,” Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen said, a sentence that would be unremarkable at any other NATO summit in the alliance’s 77-year history and is instead, this week, a live diplomatic position taken against a fellow member’s head of state.

The Quieter War of Nerves

While Ankara argued about tariffs and dance-floor anthems, a separate and considerably colder confrontation was building on NATO’s actual border with Russia, and it deserves more than a footnote. On Friday, President Putin told his own military commanders that Moscow “must also analyze the involvement of each” European nation “in actual combat operations” against Russia — language that reads, in the careful register the Kremlin uses for this sort of thing, as a threat aimed at any ally supplying or hosting support for Ukraine. Days later, Russia’s foreign intelligence service accused the British government of orchestrating a Ukrainian strike on a museum in Sevastopol, an accusation London did not bother to answer.

The frontier states are responding by shedding the postwar guardrails one at a time. Finland, which shares an 830-mile border with Russia, voted last month to lift a Cold War-era ban on hosting or transiting nuclear weapons — prompting Russia’s foreign ministry to warn that “blind Russophobia” had overtaken what Moscow called Finnish “pragmatic common sense,” and to promise unspecified “political and military steps” in response. Finland and the Baltic states withdrew from the Ottawa Treaty’s ban on antipersonnel land mines eight months ago, calculating that the weapons will matter more than the treaty’s principles if a Russian advance ever needs blunting. Lithuania is now considering lifting its own nuclear-transit ban. And in May, Russian intelligence separately accused Latvia of preparing to host Ukrainian drone launchers, a claim Riga denies while warning, in turn, that Moscow is readying “military provocations” of its own along the Baltic frontier. Latvia’s president put the psychological dimension of all this more plainly than any communiqué managed to: “Russia loves that we are scared,” Edgars Rinkevics said, arriving at the summit — which is either a warning about Russian strategy or an admission about NATO’s, depending on how charitably you read it.

None of this made Wednesday’s headlines, which belonged to Spain and a mispronounced ally. But a nuclear-transit ban lifted in Helsinki and a landmine treaty abandoned in Vilnius and Riga will still be in effect long after this week’s insults are forgotten, and they are a far better measure of how seriously NATO’s own frontier believes in the deterrent Ankara spent two days talking around.

What Ankara Actually Produced

Strip away the insults, and Wednesday produced three real things: a Patriot manufacturing license that could matter enormously if it survives contact with Raytheon and Congress; a communiqué that quietly confirmed Europe is now expected to carry Ukraine’s war on its own books; and a host government that spent its moment of maximum international scrutiny demonstrating exactly the kind of governance NATO’s own founding document says its members are supposed to stand against. Two of those three will make history. The third barely made the news.
