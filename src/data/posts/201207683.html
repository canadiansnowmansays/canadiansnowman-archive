How Russia’s invasion of Ukraine transformed North Korea’s strategic position, broke the international sanctions regime, delivered the fastest economic growth in eight years, and carried Kim Jong-un to the Beijing balcony.

On February 24, 2022, Russian tanks crossed into Ukraine, and Kim Jong-un watched with the careful attention of a man who had been waiting for exactly this kind of moment for his entire political career.

The invasion was, from Pyongyang’s perspective, an opportunity of a kind that arrives perhaps once in a generation. Russia — a permanent member of the UN Security Council, possessor of the world’s largest nuclear arsenal, a country that had spent thirty years periodically voting for sanctions against North Korea — suddenly needed things. Artillery shells. Ballistic missiles. Bodies to fill the front lines. The items on Russia’s shopping list were precisely the items that North Korea’s munitions industry, idling under sanctions, had in abundance or could produce rapidly. And the seller’s market that had existed for twenty-five years — in which the international community held the levers of economic pressure over North Korea — abruptly and comprehensively reversed.

Kim didn’t hesitate. North Korea began transferring munitions to Russia in 2022, initially through back channels and deniable intermediaries. By 2023 the transfers were substantial enough that they could no longer be denied — South Korean intelligence estimated approximately 6,700 shipping containers of munitions had moved from North Korean ports to Russia, carrying millions of artillery rounds. By 2024, the relationship had been formalized in a document that Vladimir Putin travelled to Pyongyang to sign in person: the Comprehensive Strategic Partnership Treaty, which included a mutual defense clause requiring each side to provide immediate military assistance if the other were attacked.

The Soviet-DPRK military alliance, which had lapsed with the Soviet Union’s collapse in 1991, was back. This time, Kim Jong-un was the senior partner.

The Transaction

The exchange between North Korea and Russia that developed over 2022 to 2026 was more comprehensive than a simple arms deal. It was a strategic partnership in which each side provided what the other most urgently needed, with a sophistication that reflected the interests of two states whose isolation from the Western-led international order had given them considerable practice in finding alternatives to it.

North Korea provided, on the munitions side, quantities of artillery shells and ballistic missiles that Russia’s own depleted production base could not supply fast enough for the pace of the war in Ukraine. The shells were Soviet-era calibres compatible with Russian artillery systems. The ballistic missiles — KN-23 short-range systems — performed poorly at first, with early reports suggesting high malfunction rates, but improved substantially by early 2025, achieving accuracy within 50 to 100 metres of intended targets. North Korea’s munitions factories — at least twenty operating at or near full capacity according to intelligence assessments — expanded production, and Kim Jong-un ordered further increases in 2026.

North Korea also provided troops. Approximately 11,000 to 16,000 soldiers — the estimates range across this period — were deployed to Russia’s Kursk region, which Ukrainian forces had partially occupied since their surprise incursion in August 2024. The North Korean troops gained something invaluable: combat experience on a modern battlefield, including exposure to drone warfare, electronic jamming, precision artillery, and the operational lessons of a high-intensity conflict fought against a NATO-armed and NATO-trained adversary. South Korean defence officials noted that North Korea’s military had been essentially untested in combat since 1953. The soldiers being rotated through Kursk returned home with knowledge that no training exercise could replicate.

In exchange, Russia provided: food, including grain shipments that alleviated North Korea’s persistent food insecurity. Fuel — refined petroleum products that kept North Korea’s economy functional and its military mobile. Hard currency and barter arrangements that gave the Kim regime its first substantial cash inflows from a major partner in decades. Diplomatic cover at the UN Security Council, where Russia vetoed the renewal of the expert panel that had been monitoring North Korea’s sanctions evasion since 2010 — effectively disbanding the principal international mechanism for documenting what North Korea was doing. And military technology: air defense missile systems, electronic warfare equipment, drone technology, and assistance with North Korea’s space programme and satellite launch capabilities. The US Forces Korea Commander testified to Congress in April 2025 that Russia was “expanding sharing of space, nuclear, and missile-applicable technology, expertise, and materials to the DPRK.”

The economic dimensions of this exchange were transformative. Estimates of North Korea’s total earnings from troop deployments and munitions exports to Russia between August 2023 and December 2025 range from USD 7.67 billion to USD 14.4 billion — the higher estimate potentially exceeding half of North Korea’s entire 2024 GDP. North Korea’s economy grew by 3.1 percent in 2023 and 3.7 percent in 2024 — the fastest expansion in eight years. The heavy chemical sector, which encompasses munitions production, surged 10.7 percent in 2024, the fastest growth on record. A country that had been economically contracting for most of the pandemic period had found, in Russia’s war, a revenue stream that sanctions could not touch because the country enforcing sanctions compliance had stopped enforcing it.

The Sanctions Obituary

The international sanctions regime against North Korea was one of the most comprehensive in history. Constructed over two decades through successive UN Security Council resolutions, it targeted virtually every source of foreign revenue that North Korea might exploit — coal exports, iron ore, seafood, textiles, financial transactions, the overseas labour programme that sent North Korean workers to earn foreign currency for the state. The regime was not perfect — smuggling, ship-to-ship transfers of oil at sea, cryptocurrency theft, and the general creativity of a state that had spent thirty years learning to evade international restrictions had always kept it from being fully effective — but it represented the international community’s primary non-military tool for pressuring Pyongyang.

Russia’s veto of the expert panel’s renewal in 2024 gutted what remained of that architecture. The panel had been the documentation mechanism — the body that assembled evidence of violations, named ships and companies and officials involved in sanctions evasion, and provided the factual basis for enforcement actions. Without it, the sanctions remained on paper, but the principal accountability mechanism that had given them teeth was gone. China, which had been North Korea’s primary economic lifeline for decades and had periodically cooperated with sanctions enforcement when relations with Pyongyang were strained, now had even less incentive to enforce restrictions that its strategic partner Russia was openly violating. The direction of travel became explicit in May 2026, when Xi and Putin issued a joint statement declaring their opposition to “the use of diplomatic isolation, economic sanctions, force and pressure” against North Korea — as the New York Times reported, a direct repudiation, by two permanent Security Council members, of the diplomatic architecture that had defined international policy toward Pyongyang for a quarter century.

The death of the sanctions regime as a meaningful constraint on North Korean behaviour is one of the most consequential strategic developments of the post-2022 period, and it has received far less attention in Western policy circles than it deserves. For twenty-five years, the diplomatic theory of the Korean problem rested on the premise that economic pressure, sustained and coordinated, could eventually modify Pyongyang’s behaviour — on nuclear weapons, on human rights, on inter-Korean relations. That theory was always contested. By 2025, the empirical case against it had become overwhelming. North Korea had nuclear weapons. Its economy was growing. Its international isolation was diminishing. And the sanctions that were supposed to prevent all of this were being openly circumvented by a permanent member of the Security Council that had decided, for reasons entirely unrelated to Korean policy, that North Korea’s cooperation was worth more than the sanctions regime’s integrity.

The Trump Variable

No account of North Korea’s strategic ascent can omit the role played by American policy — specifically, the particular approach to North Korea taken during Donald Trump’s two terms in office.

Trump’s relationship with Kim Jong-un began in hostility — the “fire and fury” rhetoric of 2017, the exchanges of personal insults about nuclear buttons — and then pivoted, with a speed that surprised almost everyone, to a series of summits that the North Koreans had wanted for decades. The Singapore summit in June 2018 was, from Kim’s perspective, the single most valuable diplomatic gift any American president had ever given him: a handshake with the sitting leader of the United States, broadcast globally, that transformed North Korea from an international pariah into a negotiating partner deemed worthy of personal presidential attention. Kim received the photograph. Trump received a temporary halt to missile tests.

The Hanoi summit in February 2019 collapsed when Trump walked away from a deal that Kim believed was essentially agreed — North Korea offering to dismantle its main nuclear facility in exchange for partial sanctions relief, the United States insisting on more comprehensive dismantlement as a precondition. The breakdown stung. Kim had invested political capital in the process and received nothing tangible in return. North Korea subsequently resumed missile testing and withdrew from further negotiations.

But the net effect of the Trump first term on North Korean strategic interests was positive, for a reason that had little to do with the summits themselves. Trump’s assault on the multilateral order — his contempt for NATO, his transactional approach to alliances, his dismissal of the UN as a useful institution, his withdrawal from international agreements — eroded the political and institutional architecture that gave the sanctions regime its coherence. Sanctions work when they are enforced by a coalition. Coalitions require leadership. Trump’s consistent message was that American leadership of international coalitions was a bad deal for America. That message, delivered repeatedly and with evident sincerity, undermined the willingness of allied states to sustain costly enforcement actions against a country that, after all, posed no direct threat to most of them.

In Trump’s second term, which began in January 2025, the approach was more explicitly transactional still. Trump expressed an interest in renewed personal diplomacy with Kim, framing North Korea as a potential partner rather than a problem to be solved through pressure. The rhetorical warmth toward Kim that had characterized the first term — Trump calling him a “very talented man” and praising his ability to “take over a country at a young age and run it” — returned, undimmed by the Hanoi failure. Trump’s broader foreign policy orientation, which treated traditional alliances as cost centres and adversarial relationships as potential business opportunities, was structurally incapable of generating the coordinated multilateral pressure that meaningful sanctions enforcement required.

There is an additional dimension to the Trump-Kim dynamic that analysts have frequently noted and that deserves naming directly: the two men share a governing aesthetic. Both have built personality cults around themselves. Both treat institutional constraints as obstacles to be circumvented rather than guardrails to be respected. Both communicate through maximalist rhetoric and sudden pivots. Both have demonstrated a consistent preference for bilateral personal relationships over multilateral frameworks. Kim Jong-un, who has studied his adversaries with forensic attention throughout his rule, appears to have understood Trump with some clarity — which is to say, he appears to have understood that the most effective approach to Trump was flattery, bilateral engagement, and the projection of strength rather than concession. The Singapore summit was Kim’s proof of concept. It worked.

Whether any substantive engagement resulted in the second Trump term was, as of mid-2026, unclear. What was clear was that the United States, under Trump, had no coherent strategy for reversing the deterioration of the sanctions regime or the deepening of the Russia-North Korea alliance.

The Balance Sheet

By mid-2026, the ledger of North Korea’s war dividend was strikingly positive for Pyongyang.

On the debit side: the won’s continuing depreciation, rice prices still elevated from pandemic levels, private trade still suppressed below pre-pandemic levels, the fundamental economic dysfunctions of a command economy that has never solved the problem of feeding its population without importing food or exploiting it. North Korea’s per capita income remained among the lowest in the world. The material conditions of ordinary North Koreans outside Pyongyang had improved marginally from the pandemic nadir but remained desperate by any objective measure.

On the credit side: GDP growth for two consecutive years. A mutual defense treaty with a nuclear-armed great power. Military technology transfers that had measurably advanced North Korea’s air defense and missile capabilities. Combat-experienced soldiers who had tested North Korean weapons systems under real battlefield conditions and brought back operational knowledge that would take years to fully absorb. The dissolution of the expert panel that had been documenting sanctions violations. Record food and fuel shipments from Russia. And — not least — a recalibration of North Korea’s relationship with China.

The Russia partnership had done something subtle but strategically important to Beijing’s leverage over Pyongyang. China had long been North Korea’s indispensable patron — the country that provided approximately ninety-eight percent of North Korea’s external trade, that blocked the most damaging UN resolutions, and that had used its economic dominance to exercise at least occasional pressure on Kim’s behaviour. With Russia now providing food, fuel, hard currency, and military technology, North Korea had an alternative patron for the first time since the Soviet Union’s collapse. Kim could now play Russia against China in something like the way his grandfather had played Russia against China during the Cold War. Xi Jinping’s June 2026 state visit to Pyongyang — his first in seven years — was, among other things, a signal that Beijing understood this dynamic and was prepared to invest in maintaining its primacy. The new road bridge across the Yalu River, the resumed train service, the additional flights: these were Chinese responses to the fact that North Korea had, for the first time in decades, somewhere else to go.

The diplomatic rehabilitation implicit in Kim Jong-un’s attendance at the 2025 Beijing military parade alongside Putin and Xi — the most visible symbol of North Korea’s emergence from the isolation that the international community had spent two decades trying to make permanent — was the visual summary of all of this.

Frank Aum, a former Korea expert at the Pentagon, captured the shift succinctly: “North Korea has the most leverage today that it has had in the last 30 years.”

This is the man who stands on the Beijing balcony. This is the position from which he addresses his people as having achieved a “miraculous transformation.” The transformation is real. Whether it is permanent — whether the war dividend can outlast the war that produced it, whether the Russia partnership can survive the stresses that all great-power partnerships eventually encounter, whether Kim’s reassertion of domestic control has actually closed the fracture that the jangmadang generation opened or merely driven it underground — those are the questions the final essays of this series will attempt to answer.
