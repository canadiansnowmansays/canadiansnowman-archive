Everything about the lawsuit in this piece is real. It was filed on October 22, 2026, in federal court in Anchorage, signed by Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward, and asked that certification be held for "an investigation" into what Daniel J. Sullivan's voters really meant. On November 1, Judge Sharon Gleason dismissed it, and the department appealed the same day. In fairness to the department's suspicions, the New York Times reported on October 28 that people around Mary Peltola's campaign had discussed recruiting a second Dan Sullivan, which her campaign denies. If that is true, it is a matter for voters and prosecutors. This form is about the remedy. The Office of Retroactive Justification, its forms, its officers and its hold music are invented.

SECTION A: WHY YOU ARE RECEIVING THIS FORM

You are receiving this form because the United States asked a federal court to pause the certification of Alaska's Senate election until it could be established whether votes cast for Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. were "meant to be cast for Senator Sullivan." The court declined on November 1. The United States has appealed, and the Office has been instructed to keep the forms printed in the meantime.

The Office was asked to conduct this investigation. The Office then read Article 5 of the Alaska Constitution and discovered that Alaskans vote by secret ballot. This was a setback.

After a brief period of reflection, the Office determined that the most efficient way to investigate what was in voters’ hearts, without examining their ballots, was to ask them to fill in a form. You are holding that form.

SECTION D: HOW YOUR ANSWERS WILL BE SCORED

The Office has adopted the two-part test set out in the United States’ complaint. Certification will be withheld if:

  1. Senator Sullivan does not win; and

  2. had every vote for Daniel J. Sullivan been cast for the senator instead, the senator would have won.

Please note what the test does not include. It does not ask whether any vote for Daniel J. Sullivan was actually cast in error. It assigns all of them to the senator, including yours, including the ones cast by people who disliked the senator and chose the other Dan as a protest, and presumably including the one cast by Daniel J. Sullivan himself.

The Office acknowledges that this produces a test designed to switch on in exactly the kind of race the polls predicted: a close one. On the state's unofficial count as of Wednesday, November 4, Mary Peltola leads by 637 votes, and Daniel J. Sullivan has about 3,600. The test has switched on. The Office regards this as efficient.

SECTION E: FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Q. Is there a form for voters who meant to vote for Mary Peltola and accidentally voted for a Sullivan? A. No. The Office looked into this possibility and found it did not advance the integrity of the election.

Q. Who signed the lawsuit? A. Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward, the department’s third-ranking official, who before government defended the president’s valet in the classified-documents case. The Office mentions this only for completeness.

Q. Who confirmed the Attorney General? A. The Senate, by 50 votes to 49, in August. One of the fifty was Senator Dan S. Sullivan. The Office mentions this only for completeness, and would like to stop being asked about completeness.

Q. Has this question not already been decided? A. Alaska’s Division of Elections removed Dan J. Sullivan from the ballot in June. A Superior Court judge ruled that it had no authority to judge his motives. The Alaska Supreme Court agreed three days later. Fourteen Republican state attorneys general filed a brief asking it not to. The voters then voted. So yes, but not in the Office’s favour, which in the Office’s view means it was not fully decided.

Q. What does an election-law professor think of the Office’s legal position? A. Notre Dame’s Derek Muller called the argument one of the worst he had ever seen, and suggested it was written to win the press release rather than the lawsuit. The Office has reviewed this criticism and found it an accurate description of the Office’s objectives.

Q. What happened when the court rejected the lawsuit? A. On November 1, U.S. District Judge Sharon Gleason dismissed it, writing that the United States "provides no workable plan as to how it proposes to segregate ballots cast for Daniel J. Sullivan," or how it would investigate them while keeping them secret. The Office notes that this is the problem described in Section A, and that the Office described it first. The United States appealed the same day. Its final briefs are due several weeks after Alaska plans to certify. The Office does not consider this a loss. The sentence "the federal government warned this result might be tainted" remains in the public record, and the appeal keeps it there.

Q. Was the other Dan a plant? A. On October 28, the New York Times reported that people around the Peltola campaign had discussed finding as many Dan Sullivans as possible. The campaign denies any involvement, Daniel J. Sullivan denies any contact, and a federal grand jury and a state investigation are looking into it. If it is true, it was what Professor Muller called "a dirty campaign trick." The Office has reviewed the trick and notes that it did not require a single Alaskan to reveal how they voted. The Office's remedy would.

Q. Has the President proposed a simpler procedure? A. Yes. On Thursday morning he proposed that the votes for "the Fake Dan Sullivan," plus those of "the other slug that the Dems put on the Ballot," simply be given to the real one, after which the senator "easily wins." Readers have disagreed about whether the slug was Mary Peltola or Gerald Heikes, the other Republican on the ballot. The Office has prepared forms for both readings. It notes with professional admiration that the President's procedure requires no court, no investigation, no ballots and no form, and has forwarded it to the Office's efficiency review, where it has been flagged as a threat to the Office.

Q. Who decides whether Alaska certifies? A. Under state law, the director of the Division of Elections, Carol Beecher, who is a Republican. Governor Mike Dunleavy has declined to say whether a Peltola win would be certified while investigations continue. And in January, under the Constitution, the Senate judges the elections of its own members. On Tuesday, voters gave that Senate to the Democrats. The Office regards this as a jurisdictional complication and has asked the Office of Retroactive Justification to look into it. The Office is the Office of Retroactive Justification.

Q. I am Dan J. Sullivan. Do I need to fill in this form? A. You are already the subject of a federal grand jury investigation that surfaced on primary day, and now of a state one ordered by Governor Mike Dunleavy. Please do not add to your file.

Q. I am Dan S. Sullivan. Do I need to fill in this form? A. No, Senator. This form exists so that you never need to.

A Note From the Canadian Side of the Border

In Canada, if you are worried that voters cannot tell two candidates apart, Elections Canada prints the ballot, the returning officer counts it, a judge recounts it if it is close, and the Attorney General finds something else to do that week. The Prime Minister does not post a corrected total before breakfast. We also have a great many people named MacDonald, and somehow we have managed.
