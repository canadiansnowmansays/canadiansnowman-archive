Long-time readers will know that I have a weakness for a framework. Give me a recurring pattern of bad behaviour and I will give it an acronym and chase it across a dozen essays, because the great advantage of a framework is that it lets you recognize the same con the second time it is run on you, which is usually about ten minutes after the first.

The framework I keep returning to — the one that anchored the entire Trump Accountability Project across its forty-six parts last winter — is TACO. Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate. I built it to describe a particular style of governing-by-bluster, but I have come to think it describes climate policy at least as well as it describes a trade war, and possibly better, because climate policy gives the obfuscation step more room to breathe. So let me run the world’s climate commitments through the four stages and see what comes out the other end. The exercise is unkind. It is also, I am afraid, accurate.

Threaten. This is the stage of the grand announcement, and we are extraordinarily good at it. The Paris Agreement, adopted by nearly two hundred nations in 2015, committed the world to holding warming “well below” two degrees Celsius and pursuing efforts to keep it under 1.5. Net-zero pledges were issued by governments and corporations with the solemnity of wedding vows — net-zero by 2050, by 2040, by some year safely beyond the political lifespan of anyone making the pledge. The threats, in this telling, are aimed at the carbon itself: we are coming for you, emissions, and here is the date by which you will be vanquished. It is a stirring stage. Everyone enjoys the threatening stage. There are summits, and communiqués, and photographs of leaders looking resolute against a backdrop of the word FUTURE.

Accommodate. Then the targets meet the economy, and the accommodations begin. The dates get softer, expressed as “ambitions” rather than commitments. The accounting gets creative — emissions are measured at the point of production but not the point of consumption, so a country can offshore its dirtiest industries and book the reduction as virtue. Loopholes appear for agriculture, for aviation, for shipping, for whichever sector has the most effective lobby. The targets remain officially intact while quietly acquiring an asterisk, and the asterisk acquires a footnote, and the footnote acquires a working group. Nobody repudiates the goal. The goal simply enters a long process of being accommodated to the way things actually are, which is the bureaucratic equivalent of being talked out of it by a very polite committee.

Capitulate. Then comes the surrender, and it is rarely announced as one. It arrives as a new drilling permit, a pipeline approval, a “transition” that requires, regrettably, a great deal more of the thing being transitioned away from. It arrives as the United States withdrawing from Paris for the second time — the formal exit took effect this past January — and declaring a “national energy emergency” whose remedy, conveniently, is more fossil fuel. It arrives, as I will detail in the next essay, as a Canadian prime minister killing the consumer carbon price as his first act in office. The capitulation is almost never framed as giving up. It is framed as realism, as affordability, as putting hard-pressed families first — and the families are real, and hard-pressed, and that is exactly what makes the framing work. You cannot capitulate effectively without a sympathetic human shield, and the cost of living provides an inexhaustible supply.

Obfuscate. And then the cleanup, which is where climate policy outperforms even the trade war as a venue for the TACO method, because the obfuscation tools here are so wonderfully abstract. Carbon offsets, which allow a company to keep emitting while paying someone else to theoretically not emit, in a forest that may or may not exist, that may or may not burn down next summer. “Transition fuels,” a phrase that has done heroic work converting natural gas from a fossil fuel into a sort of climate medicine. “Clean coal,” to which I will devote an entire essay, because it is the purest specimen of obfuscation the English language has produced since “enhanced interrogation.” Net-zero accounting that leans on technologies not yet invented to remove carbon not yet uncaptured at a scale never yet demonstrated. The whole vocabulary is engineered to let the threatening stage and the capitulating stage coexist in the same press release without the reader noticing they contradict each other.

That is the cycle. Threaten the carbon, accommodate the economy, capitulate to the fuel, obfuscate the gap — and then, the truly elegant part, return to the threatening stage with a fresh announcement, a new and more distant target, a summit in a new and more interesting city. The cycle does not need to be hidden because it is protected by its own length. Each stage is years apart. By the time the capitulation arrives, the threat is old news and the obfuscation is sophisticated enough that pointing at the contradiction makes you sound naïve, as though you alone failed to understand that the grand pledge was never meant literally.

Now, I can hear the objection forming, because I would form it myself: this is all very clever, Stephen, very pleased with itself, but where is the evidence that the cycle is real rather than just a satisfying shape you have imposed on events? It is a fair challenge, and it happens to have an answer, because somebody actually measures the gap I am describing. Each year a consortium of research institutes — the Stockholm Environment Institute, Climate Analytics, and others — publishes something called the Production Gap Report, which does nothing more complicated than lay the world’s climate pledges beside the world’s actual plans to dig up and sell fossil fuels, and measure the distance between them. The distance is the whole story of this essay, rendered as arithmetic.

Here is what the 2025 edition found, ten years after Paris. Governments collectively plan to produce roughly 120 per cent more fossil fuel in 2030 than would be consistent with holding warming to 1.5 degrees — more than double the safe amount. And the gap is not closing as the deadline approaches, which is what you would expect if the pledges were sincere. It is widening: in the equivalent 2023 report the figure was 110 per cent, and it has grown since. Seventeen of the twenty largest fossil-fuel-producing nations still plan to increase production of at least one fuel. Coal production for 2030 is planned at five times the level a 1.5-degree world allows. Look out to 2050, the magic year of all those net-zero wedding vows, and the planned production of fossil fuels is something like four and a half times what the 1.5-degree pathway permits. The pledges say one thing. The drilling permits, the mine leases, the pipeline approvals — the documents with money attached — say the opposite, and they say it louder every year.

There is your TACO cycle, not as a rhetorical flourish but as a measured, peer-reviewed, widening chasm between what is promised at the podium and what is signed in the regulatory filing. The threatening stage produced the 1.5-degree target. The capitulating stage produced plans to blow through it by 120 per cent. And the obfuscating stage produced the serene official confidence that these two facts can coexist, that a nation can pledge net-zero by 2050 and simultaneously plan to be pumping oil well past mid-century, and that anyone who points at the contradiction is a scold who does not understand how these things work.

I want to be careful here, because the TACO frame can curdle into the very cynicism I warned against in the introduction — the comfortable nihilism that says it is all theatre, nothing can be done, the game is rigged, pass the wine. That is not my argument. The argument is narrower and, I think, more useful: the gap between the threatening stage and the capitulating stage is measurable — there is, as we have just seen, an entire annual report devoted to measuring it — and measuring it is the single most important discipline available to anyone who wants to think honestly about this. Do not listen to the pledges. Watch the permits. Do not count the summits. Count the barrels. The distance between what was promised at the podium and what was approved in the regulatory filing is the only number that has ever predicted an emissions curve, and it is a number that almost no one in power wants you to keep.

And once you start keeping it, the natural next question is cui bono — who benefits from the gap staying open? Because a gap this stable, this well-defended, this carefully obfuscated, does not persist by accident. Somebody is being served by it. The obvious answer is the fossil fuel industry, whose entire business model is precisely the production that the Production Gap Report measures, and whose decades-long campaign to keep the public confused I will take up two essays from now. But the more uncomfortable answer — the one I keep circling back to in this series — is that a great many of the beneficiaries are us. The motorist who wants cheap petrol. The homeowner who does not want to pay to rip out the gas furnace. The voter who will, with great reliability, punish any politician who makes energy more expensive today in the name of a cooler planet tomorrow. The cycle runs not only because powerful people are cowardly, though some are, but because the public rewards every single step of it: the threat is popular, and the capitulation, when it lowers the price at the pump, is also popular. We applaud the wedding vows and we applaud the affair, and we are sincerely puzzled when the marriage fails.

In the Trump Accountability Project I argued that the TACO cycle was not incompetence but technique — that the apparent chaos was load-bearing, because chaos is what lets the capitulation hide inside the noise. I think climate policy is the same story told in slow motion. The noise is just spread across decades instead of news cycles, which makes it harder to see and therefore more effective. A trade war’s TACO cycle runs in weeks; you can hold the threat and the capitulation in your head at once. The climate cycle runs across electoral terms and entire careers, and human memory is not built to hold a 2015 pledge next to a 2026 drilling permit and feel the contradiction in the gut. So the gap survives, not because it is hidden, but because remembering is hard and forgetting is the path of least resistance.

Which is, I suppose, why I keep writing these down. Not because the framework is clever — it is barely clever; it is an acronym — but because somebody has to hold the 2015 pledge next to the 2026 permit, lay them side by side the way the Production Gap Report does, and refuse to let the polite committee talk them out of noticing. The next two essays do exactly that, at closer range: first the capitulation that happened at home, in my own country, performed by the one man most qualified to know what he was giving up; and then the man south of the border who has dispensed with the threatening stage altogether and gone straight to obfuscation, insisting that the surrender was the plan all along, and that the coal was always beautiful.

Next: the capitulation that happened at home, and the prime minister who, of all people, knew exactly what he was giving up.

120 — the percentage by which the world’s governments, as of the 2025 Production Gap Report, plan to overshoot in 2030 the level of fossil fuel production consistent with 1.5 degrees of warming. The permits are the ones with money behind them.
