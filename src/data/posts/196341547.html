In 1965, Senator James Eastland of Mississippi wanted to kill the Voting Rights Act. He did not hide his reasoning. He had once stated publicly that Black people were “an inferior race.” Governor George Wallace of Alabama, at his 1963 inauguration, declared: “Segregation now, segregation tomorrow, segregation forever.” These were the men whose power the Voting Rights Act was designed to break. They said what they meant. The historical record is unambiguous.

Last Thursday, Donald Trump posted on social media urging Tennessee’s governor to redraw his state’s congressional map and eliminate its last majority-Black district. Trump wrote that doing so “should give us one extra seat, and help Save our Country.”

Save our Country.

The Supreme Court, in striking down Louisiana’s majority-Black district on Wednesday, ruled that to prove racial discrimination in redistricting, a plaintiff must show that map-makers “intentionally” drew lines to disadvantage a racial group — and that the evidence of that intent must be disentangled from partisan motivation. Justice Kagan, dissenting, called this standard “well-nigh impossible” to meet. Congress, she noted, has long known that finding smoking-gun evidence of racist motives is precisely that: well-nigh impossible.

It is well-nigh impossible because the people who draw the maps learned, sixty years ago, to stop saying what they mean.

The Problem Has a Name

Stephen Menendian, an academic researcher at Berkeley, identified the core legal crisis in a 2023 law review article — three years before Wednesday’s ruling made it the governing standard. He called it “the problem of entanglement.”

The problem is this: in the American South, race and partisan affiliation are structurally entangled. Black voters are overwhelmingly Democratic. White voters in the Deep South are overwhelmingly Republican. This entanglement did not arise spontaneously. It was produced, over decades, by the Democratic Party’s embrace of civil rights legislation and the Republican Party’s deliberate exploitation of white Southern resentment of that legislation — what became known as the Southern strategy. The architects of that strategy understood exactly what they were doing. Lee Atwater explained it on tape, in 1981, in terms too explicit to reproduce here without content warnings.

The entanglement they created has now become the legal shield for its own consequences.

The Supreme Court ruled in 2019 that federal judges have no power to hear challenges to partisan gerrymanders. Race-based gerrymanders remain nominally prohibited — but now only if intent can be proven separately from partisan motivation. Menendian warned in 2023 of the precise risk this created: “that unconstitutional racial gerrymanders will escape judicial review under the cover of partisanship.”

On Wednesday, the Supreme Court handed the cover of partisanship to every Republican-controlled Southern legislature in America. Draw the map to favour Republicans, document it in partisan terms, and the racial consequence — the elimination of majority-Black districts — becomes legally unreviewable.

The problem of entanglement is now the solution to the problem of accountability.

McCleskey’s Ghost

The legal architecture of Wednesday’s ruling is not new. It was built case by case, over decades, in courtrooms far from the redistricting fight. Understanding it requires going back to 1987, and to a man named Warren McCleskey.

McCleskey was a Black man sentenced to death in Georgia for killing a white police officer. His lawyers presented the court with a study — rigorous, peer-reviewed, comprehensive — showing that defendants in Georgia who killed white people were four times more likely to receive a death sentence than those who killed Black people. The statistical evidence of racial disparity was overwhelming.

The Supreme Court ruled it was not enough.

To win his case, the Court said, McCleskey needed to show that he personally had been the target of intentional racial discrimination in his specific prosecution. The system’s documented pattern of racial outcomes was inadmissible as proof of the system’s racial intent.

Warren McCleskey was executed in 1991.

Stephen Bright, who teaches law at Georgetown and Yale and spent decades as director of the Southern Center for Human Rights, draws the direct line from McCleskey to Callais: “You have to prove that the prosecutor, in using peremptory strikes, intentionally discriminated. You can’t possibly know that unless the prosecutor tells you.”

Nobody tells you. That is the point. The lesson of McCleskey — absorbed by every prosecutor, every map-maker, every Republican state legislature in the South — is that you do not have to hide your intent. You simply have to document it in different language.

The same standard that allowed all-white juries to persist in counties with substantial Black populations — because no prosecutor ever said, on the record, that they struck Black jurors because they were Black — now governs the drawing of congressional districts. The outcomes can be racially explicit. The stated motivations must be partisan.

The ghost of Warren McCleskey is in every gerrymandered Southern district that will be drawn in the next eighteen months.

What Conservative Honesty Reveals

Quin Hillyer is a white conservative opinion writer from Alabama. He is not a Democrat. He is not a civil rights advocate. He is a man who believes, genuinely, that the Republican Party’s motives in redistricting are primarily partisan rather than racial.

He made this case to the New York Times this week with an example he intended as exculpatory:

“If there were an enclave, or a neighborhood, that was 100 percent Black, but that voted 80 percent Republican,” he said, “I think that the white Republicans would gladly take those people.”

He is almost certainly correct. Republican map-makers would welcome a 100-percent-Black, 80-percent-Republican enclave into their district. This is offered as evidence that race is not the motivation — only partisan advantage is.

But consider what the example actually demonstrates. It concedes that Republican map-makers are drawing districts based on how people vote, not how they look. It concedes that the relevant variable is Democratic affiliation, not Black skin. And then it asks us to accept that in a country where ninety percent of Black voters are Democrats — a figure produced by the very history of racial resentment and civil rights backlash this essay has been tracing — targeting Democratic voters is meaningfully distinguishable from targeting Black voters.

It is not distinguishable. The entanglement makes it indistinguishable. The Southern strategy made it indistinguishable. The people who built the entanglement knew they were building it. Their successors now benefit from the legal protection the entanglement provides, and point to the entanglement as proof that race has nothing to do with it.

The map-makers don’t need to say they are drawing maps to dilute Black political power. They say they are drawing maps to protect Republican incumbents. The effect is the same. The language is different. The language is the point.

This is not a new pattern. It Starts at the Top

I want to be precise about what Donald Trump has done in the redistricting war, because precision is the only honest response to language designed to evade it.

Trump told Texas Republicans to redraw their maps, triggering the redistricting war. He praised Louisiana's governor for "tremendous Vision, Strength, and Leadership" in suspending primaries and preparing to eliminate majority-Black districts. He personally called Tennessee's governor, claimed the governor had committed to acting, and posted publicly that eliminating the Memphis district "should give us one extra seat, and help Save our Country." By Friday, Alabama's Governor Kay Ivey — who had said two days earlier that her state's own court battles precluded new redistricting — reversed herself and announced a special legislative session to redraw the maps. She had applauded the ruling on Wednesday. By Friday she was acting on it.

He did not say: eliminate Black representation. He said: save our country. He did not say: the majority-Black district is the problem. He said: one extra seat.

In 1965, James Eastland said Black people were an inferior race. In 2026, Donald Trump says “Save our Country” and his administration calls the ruling that enables the elimination of majority-Black districts “a complete and total victory for American voters.”

The Supreme Court has now ruled that the 1965 language — Eastland’s language, Wallace’s language — is what would be required to prove discrimination. The 2026 language — partisan language, electoral language, seat-count language — provides legal cover. The racism has not disappeared. It has learned the vocabulary the Court created for it, and the Court has now confirmed that vocabulary as the correct one.

Gerald Griggs, a civil rights lawyer in Georgia and past president of the state NAACP, described what has happened to racism in the South with clinical precision: it has “gotten into the system and corroded the arteries of the system.” It no longer presents with the symptoms the 1965 diagnostic tools were designed to detect. The patient is still sick. The test no longer returns a positive result.

Proving it in a courtroom, Griggs said, is “another matter.”

The Colorblind Architecture

Wednesday’s ruling is part of a larger structure. In 2023, the Supreme Court ended affirmative action in college admissions. Chief Justice Roberts wrote: “eliminating racial discrimination means eliminating all of it.” The second Trump administration has used executive power to dismantle diversity, equity, and inclusion programs across the public and private sectors.

The architecture is coherent. It is built on the premise that race-conscious remedies are themselves a form of racial discrimination — that the Constitution is, or must become, colorblind. That colorblindness, applied to a society whose current racial inequities were produced by centuries of explicitly race-conscious exclusion, functions not as neutrality but as the permanent entrenchment of those inequities. You cannot remedy a structured disadvantage by pretending the structure does not exist.

Roberts knows this. Alito knows this. The conservative legal movement has known it for forty years. They have chosen the principle of colorblindness over the reality of entrenched racial inequality, and they have built a legal architecture that makes the choice effectively irreversible through ordinary democratic means.

The American Contradiction series examined this architecture from a different angle — how a country that wrote “all men are created equal” has spent two and a half centuries building legal structures that exempt itself from the promise. The Callais ruling is the latest chapter in that series, not an aberration from it.

The Voting Rights Act required legislation to fix. It took years, a president willing to stake his political legacy on it, and the moral authority of a movement that had absorbed violence without returning it. Restoring what Wednesday’s ruling eliminated will require the same — a Congress that does not currently exist, a president who is not currently in office, a political will that is not currently assembled.

What Remains

Racism in America in 2026 is not the racism of 1965. James Eastland is dead. George Wallace recanted before he died and won Black votes in Alabama. The explicit language of racial hierarchy has largely left formal political discourse, replaced by the vocabulary of partisan advantage and electoral strategy.

What remains is the entanglement. The consequence without the confession. The outcome without the admission. The majority-Black district eliminated not because Black people should not have representation but because those Black people vote Democratic and Democratic representation must be minimised to save our country.

The Supreme Court has ruled that without the confession, there is no case.

Part 37 of the Trump Accountability Project asked what fifty years of consequence-free racism means for America. Wednesday’s Supreme Court ruling is the answer arriving in legal form: it means the consequences are now structural, embedded in the architecture of representation itself, and no longer dependent on any individual actor’s willingness to say the quiet part out loud.

From across the border, watching a country that wrote “all men are created equal” in 1776 and needed until 1965 to begin meaning it — and has now, in 2026, built a legal structure that makes it meaningless again — I find myself returning to the question this series has been circling for twenty-three instalments.

Democracy fights back. It is fighting back. But it is fighting, now, against a legal architecture specifically designed to make the fight unwinnable through the courts. The only remaining arena is the one the Voting Rights Act was designed to open: the ballot box itself.

The people who are working to close that arena know exactly what they are doing.

They just don’t say it that way anymore.

Postscript — May 5, 2026, published after this essay

On the day this essay published, seven Republican state senators in Indiana faced primary challenges backed directly by Donald Trump — punishment for their votes last year against his demand to redraw Indiana’s congressional map. These were not Democrats or civil rights advocates. They were staunch conservatives who concluded, as State Senator Spencer Deery put it, that mid-decade redistricting was “clearly wrong from a constitutional and moral and ethical standpoint” and “really bad politics.” Trump called them Republicans in name only and invited their challengers to the White House. The Indiana purge is the redistricting war turned inward — the president applying the same retribution logic to members of his own party that he has applied to every institution that has told him no. The essay above argues that the language of partisan advantage has replaced the language of racial animus. Indiana is what happens when even the partisan advantage argument isn’t enough — when the only acceptable position is unconditional compliance. The results will be known by this evening.
