Six years ago today, the United States, Mexico and Canada stood in a Rose Garden-adjacent moment of self-congratulation and launched the USMCA. Today, the three countries dialed into a video call instead.

That is not a metaphor. It is the actual mechanism by which North America’s trillion-dollar trading relationship is being reviewed in the year 2026: a virtual meeting, on the sixth anniversary of the agreement’s entry into force, with Canada’s chief negotiator Janice Charette and Trade Minister Dominic LeBlanc logging on from Ottawa, their American and Mexican counterparts logging on from wherever they happened to be. No summit. No signing ceremony. No handshake photo. Just a joint review clause in Article 34.7, doing exactly what it was written to do: forcing a decision that nobody in Washington particularly wants to make.

Welcome to Part XIV of The Great Canadian Trade War. The deadline everyone saw coming has arrived, and — true to form — almost nothing has actually happened.

The Clause That Ran Out the Clock Let’s be precise about what today is and isn’t, because the coverage has been sloppier than the treaty text.

Under Article 34.7, the three governments had two options going into today: confirm in writing a clean 16-year extension, or don’t. If any party declines, the agreement doesn’t expire — it enters a cycle of annual reviews that runs until either a deal is struck or the agreement lapses on its own sixteen-year term in 2036. Canada’s chief negotiator has been telling anyone who’d listen for months that July 1 is a checkpoint, not a cliff, and on the narrow legal question, she’s right. The USMCA does not evaporate at midnight.

But “doesn’t evaporate” is a low bar, and the gap between that bar and a functioning trade relationship is where this series lives.

The practical reality, reported today, is stark in its asymmetry. The United States and Mexico have already completed two negotiating rounds and have a third scheduled for the week of July 20. Canada and the United States have not held a single formal negotiating session. Mexico has a calendar. Canada has a video call.

The Auto Math, Freshly Run This morning’s reporting on the automotive sector — which represents roughly 18 percent of all trade among the three countries — puts numbers to what has so far been mostly rhetoric.

The current rule requires 75 percent of a passenger vehicle’s value to originate in North America to qualify for USMCA’s tariff benefits. The Trump administration wants to push that to 82 percent, with fully 50 points of that value produced inside the United States specifically — a new requirement to separate American content from Canadian content that doesn’t currently exist in the rulebook at all.

Here is the number that should concern every auto executive on both sides of the border: roughly a dozen vehicles currently clear the existing 75 percent threshold. Not one clears 80. The Volkswagen ID.4 all-wheel-drive Pro tops the entire 2026 model-year list at 76 percent U.S./Canadian content, according to National Highway Traffic Safety Administration data. The administration is proposing to raise the bar to a height that essentially nothing on the road today can reach.

Mark Wakefield of AlixPartners put the operational problem plainly: automakers don’t even have a plan as to how to hit these numbers yet, and building one will be neither quick nor cheap. His firm estimates up to a 20 percent premium just to move a product from Mexico to Canada under a stricter regime, and up to 50 percent in added costs for parts sourced from China into the U.S. Boston Consulting Group’s Aakash Arora raised the more perverse possibility: set the bar too high, and automakers may simply stop trying to qualify at all, building cheaper, lower-declared-value vehicles and eating the tariff instead — the exact opposite of the “more U.S. content” outcome Washington says it wants.

Twenty thousand parts in an average vehicle, sourced from anywhere between 50 and 120 countries. That is the system being asked to reorganize itself around a rule that took effect on paper today and that nobody has yet figured out how to satisfy.

Flavio Volpe, president of Canada’s Automotive Parts Manufacturers’ Association

Flavio Volpe, who sits on the Canadian prime minister’s council on Canada-U.S. relations, remains the most notable optimist in this series, telling reporters this week he expects a deal hammered out by fall. It is worth noting that Volpe has skin in the outcome and every incentive to project calm. It is also worth noting that “by fall” is itself an admission that July 1 was never going to be the finish line.

The Six-Year Lease There’s a framing device making the rounds in Washington trade circles this week that deserves attention, because it comes from a man who helped write the original deal: Jared Kushner, one of USMCA’s original architects, has described the agreement’s six-year review clause as functioning like a sixteen-year lease with a market-rate adjustment built in every six years. The idea being that a stronger U.S. negotiating position today means the landlord — Washington — has every reason to ask for more rent now rather than lock in yesterday’s terms.

It is a useful, if unflattering, way to understand why the administration has shown no urgency to extend cleanly. Why sign a favorable long-term lease for your tenant when you believe you can charge more next year? Duke law professor Timothy Meyer offered Canada’s counter-logic: if you’re Mark Carney, you might reasonably calculate that you’ll get a better deal at the next review, in 2029 under the annual-cycle track, than you will trying to close now under maximum pressure. Meyer’s caveat matters too — Washington understands that calculation as well as Ottawa does, which is its own form of leverage.

Trump himself has spent the past month demonstrating precisely why nobody can plan around his intentions. Ahead of today’s review he told reporters he was not looking to renew it, full stop. Then, in France for the G7 gathering in Évian-les-Bains, he softened just enough to keep everyone guessing: he’d rather see it terminated, he said, but conceded he may sign it. A man who cannot decide, out loud, in the same news cycle, whether he wants the agreement to exist is not a man operating on a fixed timeline — which is itself the point. Uncertainty is not a byproduct of this administration’s trade strategy. It is the strategy.

Ford’s Two Words Not every Canadian response to the moment has been diplomatic hedging. Ontario Premier Doug Ford, asked yet again about the president’s on-again, off-again talk of a 51st state, offered four words that will not appear in any USTR transcript: Canada is not for sale.

It’s a useful bookend to the more careful language coming out of the Prime Minister’s Office, where Carney has spent the past month declining to engage with Trump’s social media output at all, describing him simply as an active poster whose commentary the government won’t chase headline by headline. Both responses are strategic. Ford’s plays to a domestic audience that polling suggests has only hardened in its resentment of the annexation talk. Carney’s plays to the room actually doing the negotiating, where public escalation has a track record of costing Canada leverage rather than gaining it — a lesson this series has documented since the 100 percent tariff threat that followed Carney’s Davos speech back in January.

The Next Fuse Is Already Lit If today’s non-event feels anticlimactic, that’s because the real pressure point isn’t July 1. It’s July 24.

That’s when the Section 301 tariff regime — the replacement architecture Washington built after the Supreme Court struck down the president’s IEEPA-based tariffs — is set to roll out in full. For now, USTR has maintained a carve-out for USMCA-compliant goods. But a carve-out that exists at the discretion of an administration that just spent a month unable to say whether it even wants USMCA to continue is not a carve-out anyone should treat as durable. Threatening to strip it would cost Washington nothing to say and would instantly raise the temperature on Canada and Mexico alike — the same playbook, this series would note, that turned a $6.8 billion U.S. trade surplus with Brazil into a pretext for tariffs anyway.

Public Citizen’s read on today’s proceedings is worth sitting with, if only because it cuts against the anxiety this series has spent thirteen parts documenting: in strictly legal terms, they argue, the annual-review trigger the deal now moves into is relatively meaningless, since the agreement doesn’t lapse for another decade regardless. That may be true on paper. It is considerably less true for a business trying to plan a five-year capital investment inside a rules-of-origin regime that resets its terms every twelve months indefinitely.

The Arithmetic, Updated Fourteen parts in, the ledger looks like this: the clause did what clauses do — it forced a decision, and the decision was to not decide. Mexico has a negotiating calendar through the end of July. Canada has a video call and a letter. The auto industry has a new target number that almost nothing on the road can currently hit. The next tariff cliff arrives in twenty-three days. And the man on the other side of the table spent the weeks leading up to today unable to settle, even in his own head, on whether he wants the agreement to survive at all.

Checkpoint, not cliff. Charette is technically correct. But six years ago, nobody signed a trade agreement so that its landlord could come back at the six-year mark to ask, out loud, in front of the tenants, how much more the market might bear.

The lease didn’t expire today. It just came up for renewal — and the landlord hasn’t decided if he wants the tenant to stay.
