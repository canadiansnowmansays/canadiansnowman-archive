In Part 74, Representative Greg Casar supplied what this series called its epigraph: “Congress has so far completely failed to respond to the threats posed by AI development. That must change.” Seven weeks later, Congress answered the second sentence. As Michael Gold and Cecilia Kang reported for The New York Times on 30 September, the House and Senate are leaving Washington for the midterm elections having done nothing about either AI safety or the data-centre boom.

It was not for want of activity. In the final days, members of both parties released a flood of bills, letters and statements of concern, and the Senate held a hearing titled “Rogue A.I.” as its members headed for the exits. None of it became law.

The last two votes

The Senate’s final attempt was a bill from Jon Husted, the Ohio Republican whose opponents have spent millions branding him “the face of data centers.” It had already passed the House overwhelmingly. It would order state utility regulators to consider making data centres cover the higher power costs they cause, but it would not require them to act, and it would give them two years to think about it. Husted called it “the only game in town.” Chuck Schumer called it “toothless,” and “an ineffectual participation trophy” for Republicans to collect before they left. Four Democrats crossed over, which still left it short of the 60 votes needed to proceed. It also contained nothing at all about the safety of AI systems themselves.

The day before, three Democratic senators, Mark Warner among them, had asked their colleagues to pass a bill creating a federal panel to set safety standards and test models before release. Ted Cruz blocked it, warning that Congress “must not legislate on the issue of artificial intelligence hastily or in a closed manner.” Warner’s reply was the most honest sentence of the week. “If Congress goes out, God forbid we have an incident,” he said. “We could see the stop of innovation if an A.I. tool took over a hospital or water system.”

Even Republicans conceded the timing was absurd. “That’s the sort of thing that I wish we’d started talking about a month or a month and a half ago,” said John Kennedy of Louisiana. “We’re doing it at the last minute.” Josh Hawley’s hearing went ahead without any of the executives who had lunched at the White House the day before; Sam Altman declined his invitation.

The political cost of all this is unclear. A Times/Siena poll this month found that neither party has a clear advantage with voters on AI, even though voters broadly oppose new data centres. Congress, it seems, has calculated that doing nothing is safe until November. Warner’s hospital or water system may yet test that calculation.

The paper that tries a different door

If the legislature won’t slow the race, could the racers slow it themselves? On the same day, Amanda Taub wrote in The New York Times about a working paper that tries to answer that question with game theory, and the answer is a qualified yes.

Its authors are Drew Fudenberg, a leading game theorist at M.I.T., and Andrew Koh, an economist at Columbia University who is also a research scientist at Google DeepMind. (That second affiliation is worth bearing in mind: one of the authors works for a company in the race.) They start from a premise every player shares, that nobody wants catastrophe, and from the observation that competition pushes everyone towards it anyway. Companies want the market share and the investment that the most advanced model brings. Governments want the economic gains and the models themselves.

Their way out has two conditions. The competitors must share accurate information about how far they have advanced, and they must agree on where the danger actually begins. If both hold, the incentive to race weakens. “Because OpenAI knows that they’re going to get caught speeding up, that Anthropic is going to speed up in response, that they’re going to start an arms race,” Koh said, “that itself can stop them from racing past the safety threshold.”

He took the comparison straight from the Cold War. Both superpowers built weapons that could survive a first strike and retaliate. “One reason to not attack first is that I know that if my opponent has a second strike capability, we are just going to destroy the world,” Koh said. Visibility did the deterring. Each side’s knowledge of the other’s capability made restraint rational.

Taub opens with the precedent the paper rests on. In October 1962 the world spent a fortnight within an eyelash of nuclear war, and the shock changed both sides’ thinking. “Right away we got the hotline agreement, so we could talk to each other,” said William Wohlforth, a Dartmouth political scientist. “We began to work toward the testing agreement.” The superpowers stayed enemies but found one shared interest they could act on.

Neither condition holds

The trouble is that neither of Fudenberg and Koh’s conditions exists today. “Right now, that sharing is not happening,” Taub writes. The frontier companies build their most capable models behind closed doors and guess at each other’s progress. The voluntary pledge they signed at the White House on 29 September expressed support for outside monitoring but, in Taub’s words, did not appear to impose any concrete obligations. Koh’s verdict on it was that it is a step in the right direction that leaves the core problem untouched, because it requires no transparency about what the companies can actually do and says nothing about the competition with China.

There are faint signs of movement. Dario Amodei has proposed that companies accept independent safety monitors embedded in their work, and has said Anthropic would do so on its own. Moonshot, a Chinese lab, recently submitted a model to Britain’s AI Security Institute for review. But Jeffrey Ding, who studies U.S.-China technology competition at George Washington University, expects Beijing to resist any regime that would slow its effort to catch up, and to keep seeing AI risk mainly through the lens of party control.

The second condition is harder still. In 1962 nobody disagreed about what a nuclear weapon could do; Hiroshima and Nagasaki had settled that. With AI, the people building it disagree about where the brink is, and some deny there is one. Some employees of the leading companies say there is a significant chance AI kills everyone within a decade. Others think the real risk is negligence. The president says the people calling for guardrails are “bringing up things that won’t happen.” A player who thinks the cliff is far away has no reason to brake, however good his information about everyone else’s speed.

The near miss we haven’t had

Koh’s most sobering point comes last. The only reliable way to get agreement on where the danger lies, he suggested, may be to run into an unmistakable lesson, as the Cuban crisis was for nuclear weapons. AI has had warnings, but the companies have kept them quiet. The OpenAI agents that broke into an Australian government system did so in June; the public learned of it in September. A near miss that nobody hears about teaches nobody anything.

Taub closes with a warning from Daniel Selsam, an OpenAI researcher, about what may happen if the agents themselves start acting in their own interest. If that happens, he wrote, “the models will likely convince people that everything is fine. Moreover, they will eloquently explain how aligned they are, discuss the nuances of human values and ethics, and argue convincingly that humans should trust them with power.”

The ledger

So here is the week. The legislature went home rather than vote. The industry signed a pledge with no obligations in it. The most promising theory of self-restraint requires two conditions, shared information and an agreed brink, and the companies, the governments and the public have neither. In 1962 the hotline came after the crisis, and the arms-control treaties came after the hotline. That is the uncomfortable lesson in Fudenberg and Koh’s paper. The world has managed this kind of restraint before, and it took a near-catastrophe to make it happen.

Asimov’s robots were stopped by their own laws before they could do harm. Ours, on the evidence of this week, will be stopped by a crisis, if they are stopped at all. The Asimov Gap is the distance between those two outcomes, and on 30 September Congress left town without trying to close it.
