TO: All Regional Processing Officers, IRCC FROM: Office of Strategic Planning (Unclassified, Leaked, Allegedly) RE: Revised Applicant Motivation Codes, Effective Immediately FILE: MC-2026-0447

Colleagues,

As you will have noticed from the queue lengths, we are once again revising our internal motivation codes for U.S.-origin applicants. The old system — “economic,” “family reunification,” “climate,” “vibes” — has proven too coarse an instrument for the data we are now receiving. Officers are reminded that these codes are for internal triage only and must never appear on public-facing correspondence, no matter how satisfying they would be to stamp.

For historical continuity, and because Statistics Canada insists we track it, please find below the updated Presidential Motivation Index, cross-referenced against application volume by administration.

Code 42-B — “Made Me Want to Be Faithful.” Historical baseline. Low volume, high sincerity. Applicants under this code, dating to the mid-1990s, generally cited a general fatigue with scandal-adjacent news cycles and a preference for a head of state who kept his indiscretions to a single, containable noun. Processing time: unremarkable. These files are now in storage and are not expected to be reopened.

Code 51-C — “Made Me Want to Be Smarter.” A modest bump, early 2000s, concentrated among applicants who described themselves, unprompted, as “just tired of squinting.” Several cited a desire to live somewhere the leader could locate a map without assistance. We processed these with the standard courtesy, though officers noted an unusually high number of applicants who, upon arrival, immediately enrolled in graduate school out of what one intake officer described as “pure spite.”

Code 63-A — “Made Me Want to Be Better.” A genuinely pleasant few years for this office. Volume actually dropped. Officers of that era report applicants who arrived not fleeing anything but simply curious, optimistic, occasionally bearing gift baskets. This code has been retired. We do not expect to reissue it in our lifetimes.

Code 91-T — “Makes Me Want to Be Canadian.” Current code. Volume: off the chart we were issued in 2019, which did not anticipate needing to go this high. Unlike prior codes, 91-T applicants no longer frame the decision as aspirational. They frame it as triage. Common phrases logged verbatim by intake staff include “I just need somewhere the news doesn’t feel like a hostage video,” “I would like to pay slightly more for healthcare I don’t have to fight a call centre for,” and, with striking frequency, “does the maple syrup thing wear off eventually or is that just how you people are.”

Processing Guidance for Code 91-T Files:

  1. Do not ask “why now.” They will tell you unprompted, at length, sometimes tearfully, occasionally while holding a printed spreadsheet of grievances organized by fiscal quarter. Let them finish. It is, in most cases, cheaper than therapy and we are not currently staffed for either.

  2. Expect the phrase “I never thought I’d leave.” This is not a red flag. It is, at this point, boilerplate. Officers should initial and move on.

  3. Watch for over-preparation. A meaningful subset of 91-T applicants have memorized more Canadian trivia than most Canadians. They will know the capital of Manitoba. They will know it unprompted. This is not suspicious. It is grief, wearing a Trivial Pursuit hat.

  4. Do not, under any circumstances, mention the winters as a deterrent. We tried this in 2019 as a light screening measure. Applicants under 91-T responded that they had already priced in the winters as a feature, not a bug, on the grounds that a blizzard, unlike the news, eventually stops.

  5. Note the demographic shift. Earlier codes skewed toward applicants describing themselves as “politically engaged.” Current 91-T files increasingly include the phrase “I don’t even follow politics anymore, I just can’t afford not to,” which our behavioural unit assures me is a sentence that should not grammatically be possible and yet here we are.

A Note on Processing Capacity

The Minister’s office has asked that I remind regional offices: we are, functionally, still one country, staffed for one country’s worth of paperwork, and the 91-T volume has begun to resemble what our systems architecture team refers to, technically, as “a problem.” Officers are authorized to remind applicants, gently, that Canada is not, in fact, an escape hatch so much as a slightly colder country with its own housing crisis, its own health-care wait times, and its own capacity for producing leaders who will, eventually, generate a Code of their own. We are aware this rarely dampens enthusiasm. We process the files anyway. It is, historically, what we do.

A Note on the Photograph Currently Circulating of a Handwritten Sign in Someone’s Window

Officers are advised that the sign — four short lines, ending on the word “Canadian,” taped to what appears to be a residential window somewhere south of the border and now reportedly shared several hundred thousand times — is not, itself, a completed application. It has, however, generated enough inbound correspondence quoting it verbatim that the Minister’s office has asked communications to prepare a holding statement. Draft language, for internal review only, reads: “Canada thanks the sign’s author for the sentiment and would gently note that citizenship still requires Form IMM 0008, proof of funds, and a language test, none of which the sign addresses.” Communications is aware this statement, if released, will itself become a sign in someone’s window within the week. We are prepared for this. We are, as an office, prepared for most things now.

Officers are reminded that this memo, like all internal IRCC correspondence regarding Code 91-T, is not for public distribution, on the theory that public distribution would only encourage the volume it describes. This has not historically worked. File accordingly.
