By Stephen Gordon July 8, 2026

Wednesday, Ankara: seated beside Zelensky, having just insulted him to his face, the president describes an attack by a country that surrendered in 1945.

Twenty-eight days ago, this series left off on Day 102 of a war that had been declared “pretty much complete” on Day 10, with thirty-eight documented announcements of imminent peace, each one overwritten by the next voice in the room. The question Part XVII asked was whether any of that information — the water tanks in Sirik, the Indian sailors on the Marivex, the thirty-nine cents a gallon — was closing the loop between action and consequence, or simply arriving as noise.

Twenty-eight days later, the loop has produced its own answer, and it did so today, in Turkey, in the worst possible way for anyone still hoping the pattern might break.

I. Where the Count Stood

On June 10th, Day 102, the ceasefire with Iran was already what the UN Secretary-General had called “a lesser-fire.” On July 8th, the president told reporters at the NATO summit in Ankara that the ceasefire was simply over. Tehran had struck shipping in the Strait of Hormuz earlier in the week; the United States would, in the president’s word, “probably” hit Iran hard again that night. He called Iran’s leaders “scum.” He said, of the same leaders with whom his own negotiators had spent months brokering a deal, “I don’t want to deal with them anymore.”

The National reported the exchange live from Ankara, where allies had arrived already on edge over what one report characterized as the president’s foul mood and his renewed demands that NATO members prove their loyalty rather than merely their spending.

This is not a new chapter. It is the same chapter, thirty-nine-plus times over now, if anyone is still counting. The Baseline Problem has always maintained that counting is the point.

II. The Fourth of July Interlude

Between Part XVII and today, the republic had its 250th birthday, and the president gave the country a preview of every category this series has catalogued, compressed into one weekend.

On July 4th, severe weather forced an evacuation of the National Mall roughly three hours before the president’s scheduled remarks. According to a senior White House official who spoke to the Washington Post on condition of anonymity, every entity involved in organizing the event — including the National Park Service — recommended the evening’s festivities be cancelled outright. The president overturned that recommendation and had the crowd invited back in, even if it meant delivering his address at two in the morning. He ultimately spoke behind bulletproof glass at 11:15 p.m., roughly ninety minutes behind schedule, to a crowd he claimed had rebounded to 150,000 from an unverified peak of 422,000.

Cameras caught him sitting motionless through long stretches of the fireworks display that followed, in a manner several outlets described as appearing to doze. At one point, he and the First Lady were filmed by Fox News’ own crew standing in a holding room, watching themselves on Fox’s live broadcast of the event they were currently inside.

It is worth sitting with that image for a moment, because it is not really about fireworks. A man stands in a room, watching a screen that is showing him the room he is standing in, and the moment is presented — by his own campaign apparatus, uncritically — as good television. The loop, this time, is almost literal.

The same weekend produced the disinhibition data point: over one hundred Truth Social posts between 11 a.m. and 11 p.m. on a single Sunday, including a meme attacking the Italian prime minister and a doctored image of Barack and Michelle Obama that drew accusations of racism from multiple commentators. The Daily Beast catalogued the full sequence.

III. A Document That Does Not Say What He Said It Says

At the same July 4th event, in a passage meant to condemn socialism, the president told the crowd that the Declaration of Independence states “we are all made in the image of one almighty god.” It does not. He also called the Constitution “the most righteous political document ever conceived” before pivoting, apparently without transition, to his own legal grievances — “although I wasn’t treated that well, but we won’t get into that” — in the middle of a sentence about the Bill of Rights.

A rhetoric professor at the University of North Georgia summarized the moment for the record: the president of the United States, on the country’s 250th anniversary, misquoted the founding document the anniversary was built around. This is Category IV, confabulation, in its purest form — not a lie told for advantage, but a confident substitution of one text for another, delivered as though the substitution were self-evidently true.

IV. The Somnolence Weeks

The president turned 80 on June 14th. The weeks bracketing the birthday produced exactly the kind of evidence this series has learned to expect around milestones — the body keeping score even when the schedule says otherwise.

IBTimes UK reported that the president appeared to nod off during a June 4th Oval Office briefing on coal policy, and that at a June 5th event at Custer Farms in Chippewa Falls, Wisconsin, he spent roughly six minutes of his remarks on the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool — holding up before-and-after photographs — while the farmers in attendance were more immediately concerned with falling commodity prices. A June 20th Truth Social post, posted late, litigated the correct spelling of the word “DUMB.” Rep. Jamie Raskin’s request for a full neuropsychological assessment, addressed to the White House physician, went unanswered in any substantive way; the standard deflection followed.

None of this is new in kind. It is new in density. The interval between documented episodes has been shortening since this series began, and the Baseline Problem has never claimed to know what that means clinically. It has only ever claimed to be counting.

V. Loyalty, Not Money

Before the gaffe, before the ceasefire announcement, there was the theater of the summit itself, and it is worth pausing on because it shows this series something new: not just how the president behaves, but how the people around him have learned to manage the behavior.

NATO is built on a single load-bearing idea, codified in Article 5 — an attack on one member is an attack on all. Mutual loyalty is not a bonus feature of the alliance; it is the entire mechanism. And yet the president arrived in Ankara still relitigating a grievance from earlier this year, when several allies declined to open their bases for U.S. strikes on Iran. “I just want their loyalty,” he told Secretary-General Mark Rutte at an Oval Office meeting on June 24th, adding, of the allies, “we don’t need their money — we don’t need anything.” At the summit itself, he called NATO a “paper tiger” and, unprompted, reopened his demand to acquire Greenland from Denmark — a request with no evident connection to the week’s agenda, raised anyway, because it was apparently on his mind.

Rutte’s response to all of this has itself become a data point. Last month, Europe’s self-appointed “Trump whisperer” travelled to Washington bearing a gold-lettered chart titled “The Trump Trillion” — $1.2 trillion in additional allied defense spending since 2017, presented as a personal achievement. The president brushed past it. In Ankara, Rutte tried again in person, telling him directly, “I would argue that without you in this chair, this would not have happened. Grab the win. It’s there” — and, on the overnight strikes against Iran, “I think what you did last night was absolutely necessary... I’m with you on this.”

This is worth reading against Part XVII’s theory of the last voice in the room. What that installment described as an involuntary mechanism — a president whose position reflects whoever spoke to him most recently — has, in the space of a few weeks, become an openly deployed strategy among the people who need something from him. Rutte is not flattering Trump because Trump is persuadable in the ordinary sense. He is flattering him because the alliance’s leadership has concluded that the only reliable lever left is being the last, loudest, most agreeable voice before a decision gets made. That is not diplomacy adapting to a colleague’s style. It is an institution adapting to a documented cognitive pattern, without ever using the word.

The same weekend produced the pettier version of the same disinhibition this series tracks elsewhere: a Truth Social meme of Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni, captioned “Restraining order needed,” following a feud that began when the president claimed she had “begged” him for a photo at the G7 — a claim Meloni called “totally fabricated.” A head of a G7 nation, mocked in a meme, over a disputed photograph, days before a summit meant to demonstrate alliance unity.

VI. The Islamic Republic of Japan Which brings us to today.

The president was seated beside Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky at the Bestepe Presidential Compound in Ankara, in a bilateral meeting held on the margins of the NATO summit, flanked by Secretary Hegseth, Secretary Bessent, and Secretary Rubio. Moments after directing an insult at Zelensky to his face — met, by most accounts, with a cold stare and nothing else — a reporter asked whether the United States would license Europe to manufacture Patriot interceptors for Ukraine.

The president’s answer began reasonably. He likes defensive weapons better than offensive ones, he said. Then he pivoted to an anecdote about the U.S.S. Abraham Lincoln, an aircraft carrier he called one of the most beautiful in the world, and what happened to it during a missile attack earlier this year.

“We had 111 missiles shot by the Islamic Republic of Japan,” he told the room. “They were shot at the aircraft carrier over a period of about one hour. 111 missiles going to a very expensive ship, and every one of those missiles was knocked down, pretty much most by patriots, but by other means also.”

Japan has not fired on an American vessel since 1945. The attack the president was describing — real numbers, a real ship, a real defensive success he was entitled to tout — was carried out by the actual Islamic Republic, the one bordering the Strait of Hormuz, the one whose ceasefire he had declared dead an hour earlier in the same city.

There is a second layer to this that deserves its own paragraph. Almost exactly two years before today, at the 2024 NATO summit in Washington, President Biden introduced Zelensky as “President Putin,” and the moment became a fixture of every argument about his fitness for a second term. The symmetry here is not subtle: the current president, sitting across from the same Ukrainian leader, at the same alliance’s summit, produced a mixup of comparable magnitude — swapping a wartime adversary for a World War II-era one, ally for enemy, 2026 for 1945 — and the coverage, so far, has treated it as a gaffe rather than a pattern. The Baseline Problem exists precisely to note that one man’s isolated slip is not automatically another man’s isolated slip, and that the only way to tell the difference is to keep counting.

VII. What Ankara Confirms

Three things happened in Ankara within roughly twenty-four hours, and they belong in the same paragraph because they are the same phenomenon wearing three different sets of clothes.

First: the president declared the Iran ceasefire over and promised renewed strikes — the thirty-ninth-plus data point in a war-and-peace cycle that Part XVII already established runs on the suggestibility of whoever last had his ear, not on the state of the battlefield.

Second: discussing that same conflict less than a day later, he substituted an ally of eighty years for the adversary of the last four months.

Third: the alliance’s own leadership has begun openly managing him through the mechanism this series identified — Rutte’s gold chart and in-person flattery are not persuasion aimed at his judgment, but timing aimed at his memory, an attempt to be the input still active when a decision gets made.

These are not three separate failures. They are one failure observed from three angles. A president whose position updates entirely on the basis of the last voice in the room is also a president for whom the names of adversaries and allies become interchangeable, and also a president whose allies have quietly stopped trying to win arguments and started trying to win timing. If nothing is retained across time, nothing is retained across categories either, and nothing is retained across relationships either. Perseveration, confabulation, and the flattery economy building up around him are not, on this evidence, three items on a checklist. They are the same collapsed distinction between what happened and what is being said right now, expressed in three different grammars — military, geographic, and diplomatic.

VIII. The Question, Again

Eighteen installments in, the question from Lawrence O’Donnell that opened this series has not changed, and neither has the method for answering it: not diagnosis, which none of us are qualified to offer, but pattern, which anyone can count.

A man stood in a room watching himself on television inside the room. A man told the country its founding document said something it does not say. A man spent six minutes on a reflecting pool while farmers waited to talk about their crops. A man declared a war over and then declared it resumed, in the same capital, in the same week he told an ally’s president that his own aircraft carrier had been attacked by an ally.

The Baseline Problem has always maintained that a man who has always been erratic provides cover for a man who is becoming more erratic — that the noise obscures the signal. Today in Ankara, for one unusually clean hour, the noise and the signal briefly said the same thing at the same time.
