A word of apology before we begin. This satire series keeps to the Sabbath — these reflections on presidential envy are a Sunday affair, meant to be read with coffee and the unhurried contempt that only a day of rest affords. Yet here I am, publishing on a Tuesday, and I can only plead that the news would not wait for the weekend. Some stories ripen on a schedule of their own. And as it happens, Tuesday turned out to be the right day after all — because it was on a Tuesday morning in Washington, as you will see, that a man in a high-visibility vest knelt at the edge of the great Reflecting Pool and began pouring bottles of hydrogen peroxide into it by hand, and I am not the sort of writer who can be expected to sit on a thing like that until Sunday.

When last we visited the subject of the President’s complicated relationship with his predecessor — in a piece I called The Green-Eyed President — I suggested, perhaps unkindly, that no amount of executive power had managed to fill the particular hole in the man shaped exactly like Barack Obama. I want to issue a correction. I was wrong to call it a metaphor. I have since learned that the National Mall has, with the quiet cooperation of pond biology, made it literal.

The Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool — that long, mirror-flat rectangle of water built to hold the sky and the obelisk and whatever solemn thoughts a visitor cares to bring it — has turned green. This is the part that I, as a Canadian who has watched a great deal of American self-regard from the cheap seats to the north, find almost too perfect to be true: he ordered it painted blue.

The directive, as BBC has reported, came down from the President himself ahead of the 250th anniversary of American independence — paint the pool blue, make it gleam, make it better than ever, in time for the great patriotic birthday. A pool was to be made beautiful by presidential fiat, the way a king might command the tide to flatter him. The water was to be the colour of a clear sky over a confident republic. And the water, having consulted nobody and nothing but its own chemistry, turned the colour of a salad left in a warm car.

Not a tasteful green. Not the dignified verdigris of old bronze. A bright green — the green of a slushie, the green of a highlighter, the green of an emotion that has finally found its visible wavelength. He asked for blue. He got green. There is, in all of literature, no more economical illustration of the gap between what a man demands of the world and what the world quietly decides to give him.

Now, I am obliged to lay out the numbers, because the numbers are themselves a small comic opera. The President, as the New York Times has reported, initially announced the renovation would cost something like $1.8 million. He has been candid about his original estimate — “I originally thought I’d do it for two or three million,” he told reporters, in the wistful tone of a man recalling a simpler, cheaper time. The figure then climbed to $13.1 million before settling, with the serene inevitability of all government numbers, at $14.2 million. For this sum the Republic purchased a pool that cannot hold its colour, a fact I will return to, because the reason it cannot hold its colour is the finest part of the whole story.

But first, the denials, which deserve their own monument.

When the algae appeared — and it appeared almost immediately, the way truth tends to — the administration’s first instinct was not to fix it but to deny that it was algae at all. As CNN reported, when the network sent over video of the green water, an Interior Department spokesperson initially said the Fish and Wildlife Service wasn’t convinced it was algae. Pause on that. There exists, somewhere in the federal government, an official who looked at a pool gone visibly green with what is unmistakably algae and offered, on the record, the position that the jury was still out. This is the bureaucratic equivalent of looking at rain and declining to speculate on the weather. Eventually they upgraded the diagnosis to “residual algae” — algae, but the good kind, the leftover kind, the kind that is merely finishing up. Part of the normal startup process, they said. Everything is fine. The pool is simply being green at us on a temporary basis.

Then came the triumph. The administration announced that its newly installed nanobubble technology had, and I quote as reported by The Hill, “successfully destroyed the algae.” Mission accomplished. The banner, one imagines, was already being printed. And the algae, having heard the news of its own destruction, did what all things do when a Trump administration declares them defeated: it came back, greener than before, in time for the morning shift.

Which brings us to Tuesday, and to the image I cannot get out of my head.

On Tuesday morning, as CBS reported — its reporter Bob Kovach filming the whole thing — national park staff were videotaped kneeling at the edge of the great Reflecting Pool, pouring jugs of hydrogen peroxide into the water by hand. Let me set the scene properly, because it is a tableau worthy of a Dutch master. The morning sun. The Lincoln Memorial standing in its marble dignity behind. And there, crouched at the lip of the $14.2-million pool in a high-visibility vest, a man emptying bottle after bottle of the same stuff you’d use to clean a scraped knee, in a doomed hand-to-hand combat with pond scum. Copper sulfate and hydrogen peroxide, the treatment is — a process expected to take four to six weeks. Four to six weeks of a man on his knees, bailing chemicals into a mirror, so that the mirror might briefly consent to reflect.

And here is the part that elevates the whole affair from farce to something close to tragedy, if tragedy can be measured in dollars and leaking pipes. As the New York Times explained back in May, none of this is going to work, and everyone responsible knew it would not work, because the renovation never addressed the actual problem. The pipes leak. The pool draws its water from the Tidal Basin, runs it through a treatment plant meant to filter and ozonate it, and cycles it back — except the pipes keep breaking under the pressure of the surrounding soil, so the water level cannot be maintained, so the pool cannot stay full enough to be still enough to reflect anything at all. As a former Park Service official put it with devastating plainness, it is almost impossible to maintain the water level required to make the pool reflective. They painted it blue. They did not fix the pipes. They spent $14.2 million on the part you can see and nothing on the part that actually holds the water. If you wished to design, in concrete and chemicals, a perfect monument to a certain style of governance, you could not improve on this.

Consider the cruelty of the symbolism, which Nature appears to have arranged with the timing of a professional. Here is a pool built to be a mirror. A mirror is a humble thing; it has no content of its own, it simply shows you back to yourself. For a century this one has shown the Republic its monuments, its marches, its inaugurations, its grief. He asked it for blue, and it would not give him blue. Instead it clouded over with the exact pigment that has meant envy in English since long before there was a Washington to be jealous in. The pool has, in effect, looked at the current occupant of the White House for long enough, and taken on his colour. Mirrors do that, eventually. They become what they spend their time reflecting.

I walked Dior this morning — she is a husky, and therefore regards all bodies of water as either drinking opportunities or personal insults — and I tried to imagine her reaction to the Reflecting Pool in its current state. She would, I think, approach it with the suspicion she reserves for anything that has changed since she last approved of it. She would sniff. She would decline to drink. She would note, with the proprietary irritation of a creature who believes all outdoor spaces are administered for her benefit, that a small family of ducks had already taken up residence — three of them, a mother and two half-grown young, paddling through the murk with the serene unconcern of tenants who have not read the lease. The ducks, you understand, are untroubled by the symbolism. They do not know the water is the colour of envy, nor that it was supposed to be blue; they know only that it is warm, and full of the soft green stuff that ducks regard as a feature rather than a failing. They have made their peace with the pond scum in a way the rest of us cannot, and they paddle past the kneeling man with his peroxide jugs without so much as a glance, three small citizens entirely indifferent to the $14.2 million spent on their behalf. Dior would watch them with frank betrayal — the water is ruined and yet they remain — and then she would look up at me with the expression dogs reserve for the inexplicable failures of human stewardship, and try to roll in it anyway, because she is a husky and standards are for other breeds.

But Dior’s instincts are sound, and they point to the deeper truth of the thing. The pool is not malfunctioning. The pool is working perfectly. It was commanded to show blue — to show calm, mastery, a republic gleaming for its birthday — and instead it showed the truth. You can order a mirror to flatter you. You cannot make it. It will show you what is actually there, in the water and in the man looking into it, and what was actually there turned out to be green.

There is a tidy little theory in psychology — I am not a psychologist, and as a retired Canadian businessman I am professionally unqualified to diagnose anyone, which has never stopped me — that we are most irritated in others by the qualities we cannot tolerate in ourselves. The man has spent a decade unable to say his predecessor’s name without a flinch, unable to inherit a functioning economy or a foreign-policy success without insisting it was secretly his all along, unable to look at anything built by another hand without resolving to paint over it in his own colour and call it better than ever. So he painted the pool. And the pool, asked to reflect him, did so with a fidelity no contractor could have managed: a vanity project, over budget by a factor of seven, declared a triumph, betrayed by the plumbing nobody bothered to fix, and now tended at dawn by a single exhausted worker pouring peroxide into the green by hand.

They will clean it, of course. They always do. The copper sulfate will do its slow work, the skimmers will skim, and for a week or two the pool will consent to show the sky again. The bloom is temporary. But the pipes still leak, and the water level still falls, and the condition the green so briefly illustrated is, I suspect, not temporary at all.

And the rest of us — those of us watching from the cooler, greyer north, where our waters reflect mostly cloud and the occasional disappointed husky — will simply have to remember what we saw, the week a President ordered a mirror to be blue, and the mirror, with the patience of all honest things, turned green instead, and showed him precisely what he had brought to the water’s edge.
