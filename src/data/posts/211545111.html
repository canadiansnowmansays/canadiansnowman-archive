I finished this series eleven essays ago with a title I chose carefully: “What the Republic Owes Him.” I stand by every word of the argument underneath it — that Anthony Fauci gave sixty years to a country that spent his last, weakest ones humiliating him in a committee room, and that the Fifth Amendment invocation on 29 July 2026 said more about the Republic’s manners than about his guilt. I have not changed my mind about that.

But a series that only ever complicates its subject in one direction isn’t biography. It’s advocacy wearing biography’s clothes. And on 17 August 2026, the molecular biologist Alina Chan, writing for the New York Times, published a piece that forced me to sit with a part of Fauci’s record I had mostly left in the footnotes: not what Donald Trump did to him during Covid, but what he himself helped build in the decade before it — a regulatory vacuum around the riskiest category of virology on Earth, one that Chan argues has still not been closed, and that he personally defended in print.

This essay is the coda I didn’t plan to write. It doesn’t retract anything in Parts I through VIII. It sits beside them, uncomfortably, the way honest addenda are supposed to.

What Chan actually reported

Strip away the framing and the piece is built on a small number of hard facts, and they are worth stating plainly before I get anywhere near Fauci.

In February 2025, the journal Cell published a study in which virologists in China screened bat samples gathered from five provinces and stored at the Wuhan Institute of Virology, looking for coronaviruses with pandemic potential. They found relatives of MERS. One could enter human cells through the same receptor SARS and Covid-19 use. The researchers grew it in lab-made human airway and intestinal tissue, where it multiplied, and proposed testing it next in humanised mice. All of this happened two biosafety tiers below what leading virologists say a novel, potentially airborne human pathogen warrants — and none of it broke any rule, in China or the United States, because almost no binding rule exists.

A month later, a worker at Fort Detrick found a deliberately cut hole in a biocontainment suit. The FBI investigation that followed turned up unrelated safety violations serious enough to shut the facility for nearly six months — failures that would likely never have surfaced without the sabotage that triggered the inquiry.

Chan’s larger point is structural. At least fifty-three BSL-4 labs now operate across twenty-two countries, up from a handful a generation ago. A 2024 review counted roughly three hundred documented lab-acquired infections between 2000 and 2021, a figure its own authors called the tip of the iceberg. And six years on, the U.S. and international bodies still disagree about how Covid-19 began — some intelligence services and academies favouring natural spillover, several others having moved toward a lab-associated origin since their first assessments. Chan is careful, and I want to be equally careral in relaying her: she is not claiming the origin question is settled. She is arguing that it doesn’t need to be, because the regulatory gap that made either explanation plausible is still open today, regardless of which one is true.

The Trump administration’s July 2026 gain-of-function policy — which replaced the Biden-era rule Trump had scrapped the day before it took effect in May 2025 — closes some of that gap on paper. It bars the most dangerous experiments outright rather than merely routing them for review, and it creates an independent body to weigh ambiguous cases. Chan credits that much. But she identifies three gaps the policy leaves standing: it binds only federally funded research, leaving privately funded labs “strongly encouraged” rather than required to comply; it leaves agencies free to overrule or ignore the independent body’s recommendations; and it exempts the initial hunt for novel viruses in the wild — the exact category of work that produced both the Cell study and, years earlier, the Wuhan bat coronavirus program — from independent review entirely.

The sentence with Fauci’s name on it

Buried in the middle of the piece, doing more work than its length suggests, is this: in a 2011 Washington Post essay headlined “A Flu Virus Risk Worth Taking,” Francis Collins and Anthony Fauci — then directors of the NIH and NIAID respectively — defended gain-of-function experiments that had made a deadly bird flu more transmissible in mammals.

I want to be precise about what that sentence does and doesn’t establish. It does not place Fauci at the Wuhan Institute of Virology. It does not touch the origin question at all. What it does is puncture a framing I leaned on across this series without ever quite naming it: that Fauci’s Covid-era troubles were something that happened to him, engineered by an administration that muzzled a man trying to tell the country the truth. That framing is accurate for 2020 and 2021. It is not the whole forty-year record.

A decade before Trump ever stood at a podium beside him, Fauci was one of the two most powerful people in American biomedical funding, and he used that platform to argue — publicly, by name, over his own byline — that engineering a more contagious version of a lethal pathogen was a risk worth taking. Chan’s broader argument is that funders who control hundreds of millions in grant money don’t just fund gain-of-function research; when they defend it in the op-ed pages of the Washington Post, they set the incentive structure that tells every ambitious virologist underneath them which kind of paper gets published, which kind of career advances, and which kind of caution reads as timidity. Fauci was not a bystander to that incentive structure. For the better part of two decades, he was its chief architect.

Part V of this series was called “The Man Who Kept Correcting the President.” It’s a fair title for the specific, narrow drama of 2020 press briefings. It is not a fair title for the man who, nine years earlier, was the correction nobody else in Washington was in a position to offer — and chose not to.

Why this isn’t a contradiction, and why that’s worse

I could resolve the tension I’ve just created by picking a side: either the sympathetic Fauci of Parts I through VIII was the real one and this is a footnote, or the 2011 op-ed reveals the “true” Fauci and the earlier essays were credulous. I don’t think either move is honest, and I don’t think Chan’s piece asks for it.

The uncomfortable reading is that both things are true at once, in the same man, without contradiction. The Fauci who reversed himself on community advisory boards after Larry Kramer’s 1988 letter — who let ACT UP change his mind about who belonged in the room — is the same Fauci who, twenty-three years later, saw no comparable reason to change his mind about a virologist’s right to make a lethal pathogen more contagious in the name of preparedness. He was capable of listening to outsiders when the outsiders were dying of AIDS in front of him. He did not extend the same capacity for doubt to his own professional caste when the danger was hypothetical, distant, and dressed in the language of readiness rather than mortality. That is not hypocrisy. It is something more common and more forgivable and, in its way, more damning: an extremely good man’s blind spot for the institution that made him.

Chan’s essay never mentions Fauci’s Senate testimony, his diary, or the Fifth Amendment. It doesn’t need to. The through-line she’s tracing predates all of it and will outlast it — Collins is gone from public life, Fauci is retired, and the Cell study she opens with was published under an administration neither of them served. The regulatory failure is bigger than any one director of any one institute. But American biomedical science does not run on anonymous, institutional culture. It runs on the specific, named judgment of specific, named people who sat where Fauci sat, and who used the authority of that seat to tell Americans, over their own bylines, that this was a risk worth taking.

The irony of who has been right about this

Part IX of this series, “A Vote That Settles Nothing,” covered the Senate committee’s 6 August 2026 contempt vote and noted, almost in passing, that the bill with the best chance of building real oversight — the Risky Research Review Act — has been stuck for over a year partly because its lead sponsor, Rand Paul, has spent a decade in open warfare with Fauci over exactly this subject. I wrote at the time that the bill deserved to be judged on its merits rather than on Paul’s grudge. Chan’s reporting makes that harder to say cleanly, because it turns out Paul’s grudge was, on the narrow question of gain-of-function oversight, more empirically grounded than I gave it credit for. The Risky Research Review Act cleared the Senate Homeland Security Committee eleven votes to two more than a year ago and still hasn’t reached the floor. A bill this uncontroversial in committee doesn’t stall for a year without an interest group content to let it stall — and the NIH, spending nearly eight billion dollars annually on infectious-disease research with no dedicated capacity to audit its own risk portfolio, has never been a disinterested party in how fast that bill moves.

None of this makes Paul’s Senate-floor theatrics or his conspiracy-adjacent asides about Fauci any more defensible; I stand by what Part IX said about the difference between a legitimate policy critique and the performance of one. But intellectual honesty requires noting that a man I’ve spent nine essays treating as Fauci’s least serious antagonist has, on this one narrow point of institutional accountability, been arguing something closer to what Chan is now documenting from the outside, with none of Paul’s political baggage attached to it. Being right for partisan reasons doesn’t make you wrong.

There’s a second irony worth sitting with, and it touches Part VII of this series, “What a Diary Actually Proves.” I argued there that the diary controversy told us less about Fauci’s honesty than about how badly a private notebook can be misread once it’s subpoenaed into a public fight. I think that argument holds. But Chan’s essay is a useful corrective to a habit I fell into across that essay and the one after it: treating the entire Fauci controversy as a question of what he said, privately or publicly, true or false, under oath or under duress. The 2011 Washington Post op-ed was none of those things. It wasn’t a diary entry pried loose by subpoena or a hearing-room answer extracted under hostile questioning. It was a considered, voluntary, co-bylined argument for a specific research policy, published in the country’s second-largest newspaper, at a moment when nobody was forcing Fauci to say anything at all. If the diary tells us little because it was never meant to be read this way, the op-ed tells us a great deal precisely because it was.

What the Republic still owes him — and what he owed it back

I am not walking back the epilogue. Fauci’s PEPFAR legacy alone — an estimated twenty-five million lives, by the program’s own accounting — sits on a different moral order than a research-policy dispute, and nothing in Chan’s reporting touches it. The Senate hearing that opened this series was still a disgraceful way to treat a seventy-nine-year-old man who had, whatever else is true of him, given the government fifty-four consecutive years of service across seven presidents. I have not found a reason in this new reporting to think otherwise.

But biography that only accumulates sympathy stops being biography. Fauci spent this series as a man wronged by an administration that wanted a villain and built one out of convenient footage. Chan’s piece is a reminder that before Trump needed a villain, American science had already built something more durable and less accountable than any one president’s grudge: a system in which the people best positioned to say no to catastrophic risk were, for decades, also the people most professionally rewarded for saying yes. Fauci did not invent that system. But in 2011, when he had every opportunity to be the outsider his own AIDS-era reversal proved he was capable of being, he chose instead to defend it, in print, under his own name.

The Republic still owes him its respect. It’s fair to ask whether, on this one count, he owed it a little more doubt than he gave.
