Bradley Fenn announced his candidacy for Mayor of Elsinore on a Wednesday morning in September, outside the Municipal Hall, with a sign, a microphone, and twelve slides.

He was forty-three years old, bald by choice rather than circumstance — he had shaved his head in his mid-thirties with the deliberate energy of a man closing a negotiation — and in possession of the best shoes in Elsinore. He had lived here for seven years, having relocated from Mississauga with his family following a career in municipal consulting, and had served two terms on the planning committee. He had submitted public comments on the federal arts funding framework. He had opinions about the parking situation on Ontario Street that he expressed with precision and regularity.

He was not, in the assessment of most people who had encountered him, an unreasonable man. He was organised. He prepared thoroughly. He returned emails. He had twelve slides for every occasion and had occasionally produced a thirteenth.

He had twelve slides today.

The Swan Warden set her alarm for five forty-five. She will be on the bank at first light with her notebook, writing down what she sees, as she has done every morning for twenty-two years.

The slides covered, in order: Elsinore’s current budget allocation by department; a comparative analysis of municipal operating costs against four similar-sized Ontario cities; three proposed efficiency measures involving the consolidation of administrative functions; a recommendation regarding the tendering process for municipal grounds maintenance; and, on slide nine, in a section titled “Governance Review: Unelected Influence and Democratic Accountability,” a note that the Swan Warden position — which did not appear in the current municipal organisational chart because it predated the organisational chart — lacked democratic accountability, and should therefore be subject to a formal mandate review and integration into the parks department structure.

He used the phrase “unelected influence” twice in slide nine. He had used it twice in his public comment on federal arts funding. He used it, Gerald had observed, the way that certain people use certain phrases: not as a description but as a conclusion arrived at before the analysis and confirmed by it.

The slide was on screen for approximately forty seconds before Bradley moved to slide ten.

The Swan Warden was not present at the announcement. She was conducting her September habitat survey. She heard about slide nine from Gerald the following morning in the municipal hall parking lot. She listened to his description. She nodded. She went inside.

Mayor Doug received the news of Bradley’s candidacy with the equanimity of a man who has been elected four times and understands that elections happen. He wore his good coat to the announcement. He had not known about the twelve slides in advance. He listened to all of them from the back of the small crowd. When Bradley finished, Mayor Doug shook his hand and said he welcomed the conversation and that a spirited civic debate was a sign of a healthy democracy.

He drove home. He called his wife. He said it was going to be a busy autumn.

He did not change his platform. His platform was that Elsinore was Elsinore, and that this had, on balance, proven sufficient, and that he saw no compelling reason to present it differently now than at any previous stage. This was either a statement of principle or a description of a man who knows that the platform that has worked four times is the one you run on again, and both things were true simultaneously, which is often how things are with Mayor Doug.

Alistair wrote a letter to the Elsinore Beacon.

He had been asked for eight hundred words. He submitted fourteen hundred. The Beacon ran all fourteen hundred because Norman read it and thought: this is what I would write if I wrote the way I mean to.

The letter argued, in Alistair’s complete and carefully constructed paragraphs, that the institutions of a civic life were not efficiency measures. That the things which made a community recognisable to itself — its theatre, its river, its particular arrangement of committees and positions that predated the organisational chart — were not overhead. They were the community. He wrote: “A mandate review of an institution is not a neutral administrative act. It is a statement about what the institution is for. If you do not believe the swans require a warden, you do not believe the swans require attention. If you do not believe the swans require attention, you have not spent enough time on the river to know what attention costs.”

He did not mention Bradley Fenn. He did not need to.

Wayne shared the letter on the Festival’s social accounts. He wrote: “Worth reading.” Two words. He did not need more. Norman ran Alistair’s letter and considered, briefly, writing his own. He decided against it. His forty percent was more powerful unpublished. He did, however, move Carly’s campaign coverage to page one for the remainder of the race.

Reg Plimpton was photographed at Bradley Fenn’s candidacy announcement, standing slightly behind the sign — close enough to signal support, far enough to maintain options. When the Beacon asked whether he was supporting Bradley, he said he believed in a robust democratic process and thought it was healthy for Elsinore to have this conversation.

In November, Reg attended Mayor Doug’s victory party at the Legion Hall. He was photographed shaking Mayor Doug’s hand with considerable warmth. He told a reporter he had always believed in Doug’s steady leadership and that Elsinore had made the right choice.

Bradley Fenn has not spoken to Reg Plimpton since election day. Reg has not noticed this, or has decided not to notice it, which in his case amounts to the same thing. His arm, at the victory party, was extended with its customary ease.

The Swan Warden attended Bradley Fenn’s all-candidates meeting at the Legion Hall on a Tuesday in October. She sat in the front row. She brought her dark green notebook.

Bradley presented his twelve slides. He was organised and clear. When the floor opened, the Swan Warden raised her hand.

She asked one question. She asked who, under the proposed parks department integration, would hold the institutional knowledge regarding the April bank usage patterns, the quarterly habitat surveys, the downstream implications of changed agricultural practice documented in the Farm Bureau meeting’s environmental submission, and the Avon River navigational records that the Literary River Tour had drawn upon in its second week of operation following the grounding incident at Waypoint 3.

She asked, specifically, whether the parks department had a designated person with the relevant training and twenty-two years of continuous observation.

Bradley said the parks department would absorb the function and that institutional knowledge would be preserved through a proper transition process.

The Swan Warden wrote something in her notebook. She did not ask a follow-up question. She had asked the question she came to ask. The answer was the answer she had expected. She had wanted it on record that she had asked it.

Gerald had, by this point, identified three public comment submissions to the municipal governance review that had arrived via the online portal in late September, all supporting the mandate review of the Swan Warden position. He had attempted to verify the submitting email addresses against the municipal voters’ list. He had not been able to match any of them to registered Elsinore residents.

He filed this observation in a separate document. He mentioned it to Jim one Tuesday morning in the parking lot, when Jim was walking the labrador past the municipal hall and happened to nod to Gerald on the steps.

Jim said: how many verified submissions were on the other side?

Gerald said: forty-seven.

Jim nodded. He walked on. He said nothing further about it to anyone, because he had noted the information, formed a view, and moved on to the next dog, which is what you do when the situation is clear and your role in it is complete.

Carly found out about the unverifiable submissions through a source she has not identified in print, because Norman told her sources were sacred and she has held to this. She mentioned it to Norman. He read Gerald’s document, which Gerald provided when asked, which Gerald always does when asked. Norman held the information for two weeks. He ran a single paragraph on page two, three days before the election, noting that three of the public comment submissions supporting the governance review could not be verified as originating from Elsinore residents. He did not editorialize. He stated the fact. Gerald’s observation, three paragraphs, page two. It was enough.

Reverend Faye voted at seven-fifteen in the morning, before the hall was busy, at the polling station set up in the gymnasium of Elsinore Public School on Wellington Street, two blocks from the oaks. She did not tell her congregation who she voted for. This is not quite accurate — she told God, in her morning prayer, and she noted it in her journal that evening, both of which are the same privacy level and considerably more secure than most communication methods available in Elsinore.

Constance Hay voted at nine o’clock, having walked from the house on Wellington Street at her own pace, which is not Gerald’s pace but has a similar quality of not being hurried by anything external. She has voted in every Elsinore municipal election since 1968. She considers this the minimum required. She wore her pale blue cardigan. She nodded to three people she knew in the queue, which was everyone in the queue. She did not discuss who she was voting for, because in Constance’s view a vote is a private act and discussing it publicly is a form of imprecision she has never been comfortable with.

Jim voted at noon, between morning walks and the afternoon session. He noted, on his way in, which candidate signs remained on which lawns and compared this to his October map. The pattern held. He voted. He walked out. He had two dogs to collect by two o’clock.

Mayor Doug won by four hundred and twelve votes.

He gave his remarks at the Legion Hall that night in his good coat. He thanked his family. He thanked his volunteers. He thanked Gerald, who had distributed campaign literature at his own pace across the entire municipality over six weeks and covered every street at least twice. He thanked the people of Elsinore. He paused.

He said: “I want to thank the people who showed up. Not the people who filed comments. The people who showed up.”

He did not look at anyone when he said this. He was looking at his notes. He moved on immediately to the butter tart coupon from the Perth County Pantry, which he described as a reliable pleasure, which it is.

The room applauded.

Carly wrote the line down. She had it in her notebook already. She wrote it again anyway, because some sentences deserve to be written twice.

The Swan Warden stood near the door. When the remarks concluded she looked at Mayor Doug for a long moment. She nodded once. She collected her coat. She left. She had an early morning. The September survey had identified two items requiring follow-up and she intended to follow up.

Gerald stacked the chairs afterward at his own pace, because no one had asked him to and he had noticed they needed doing. He turned off the hall lights. He locked the door. He drove home.

Outside, the Avon moved through Elsinore in the dark, past the Festival, past the dock where the paddle boats had gone back to Guelph three weeks earlier, past the bank where the swans would land again in April, out toward Perth County, where someone was already up and moving toward the barn because the work does not wait.

To the south, a president has said that states are agents of the federal government in elections, and that the federal government should therefore run them, particularly in the places that voted the wrong way. The FBI has visited election offices. Federal agents have requested voter rolls. The language used is the language of accountability, of correcting unelected influence on democratic processes.

It is Bradley Fenn’s language, written in capital letters, with federal agents.

In Elsinore, the Swan Warden’s mandate was not reviewed. The forty-seven verified submissions outweighed the three unverifiable ones, by a margin of four hundred and twelve votes. Local knowledge held. The count was accurate. The certification was not contested.

The Swan Warden set her alarm for five forty-five.

She will be on the bank at first light with her notebook, writing down what she sees, as she has done every morning for twenty-two years, unelected and indispensable and entirely unconcerned with the distinction.

That is, in the end, what institutions are: the accumulated mornings of people who showed up.
