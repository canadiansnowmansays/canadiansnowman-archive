On the afternoon of November 8, 2017, the Forbidden City was closed to tourists. Its courtyards, which on an ordinary day hold tens of thousands of visitors, held two presidents, two wives, a delegation of American officials and a great many cameras. Xi Jinping led Donald Trump through the imperial treasures himself, a break with precedent, and in the evening the two couples sat down inside the palace walls to watch Chinese opera and acrobats, as Reuters reported at the time.

So the first opera of this relationship was not a metaphor. It was a performance in costume, on a stage the host had chosen, for an audience of one.

Somewhere on the walk, Xi mentioned that China had 3,000 years of written language. Trump, as AFP recorded, had a reply ready: “I guess the older culture, they say, is Egypt with 8,000.”

Nine years later, at the National Archives in Washington, he was still keeping score. “Ours goes 250 years,” he remarked during Xi’s visit in September 2026. “Theirs goes 6,000 years.” Readers of The Washington Opera will remember the line. It framed the last full act of that series, and it is as good a place as any to open this one.

This is the third opera in what has become a cycle. The Beijing Opera covered Trump’s state visit to China in May 2026. The Washington Opera covered Xi’s return visit in September. The Shenzhen Opera follows the two men to the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in southern China on November 18 and 19, and the Miami Opera will follow them to the G20 in December. Every act so far has had the same structure: more pageantry than substance, and a host who controls the staging. What has changed this time is the condition of the visiting tenor.

The general’s tour

H. R. McMaster walked the Forbidden City with Trump that day. He was the president’s national security adviser, a retired Army general and a historian by training, and he lasted a little over a year in the job. In May 2020 he published the most useful account of the visit I know of, in The Atlantic, adapted from his book Battlegrounds.

His central observation was that Xi and his advisers used history to deliver their message, choosing some subjects and steering carefully around others. He cited John King Fairbank, the Harvard historian who more or less founded American China studies, who wrote in 1948 that historical perspective on China’s leaders is “not a luxury, but a necessity.”

The palace itself was the first lesson. It was built under the Ming dynasty, which ruled from 1368 to 1644 and which Chinese leaders remember as a golden age of wealth, territory and culture. At its heart stands the Hall of Supreme Harmony, where the emperor held court beneath golden pillars carved with dragons, ruling over what the Chinese called tianxia: everything beneath heaven. The message of the tour, McMaster wrote, matched the speech Xi had given to the Communist Party’s 19th National Congress three weeks earlier. The party was pursuing the “great rejuvenation of the Chinese nation,” and the two centuries in which Europe and then America dominated the world were an aberration now being corrected.

I have walked those courtyards myself, as an ordinary tourist with a ticket, and the scale is the point. The halls are designed to make a visitor feel small before he reaches the throne.

But McMaster noticed something the tour did not mention. The three great halls were built to defend against threats from inside the walls as much as outside them. The Yongle emperor, who built the Forbidden City, worried more about his own officials than about the Mongols. He ran an elaborate network of spies and executed suspected opponents along with their entire families. China’s core provinces, McMaster noted, had been ruled by a strong central authority only about half the time since the fall of the Han dynasty in 220 AD. Behind the confidence on display he sensed a profound insecurity, and he thought the party shared it.

The guide also showed the Americans where the boy emperor Puyi lost his throne in the revolution of 1911, in the middle of what Chinese leaders call the century of humiliation. That century began with Britain’s victory in the First Opium War in 1842 and ended with Japan’s defeat in 1945 and the Communist victory in 1949. Xi had told Trump about it over dinner at Mar-a-Lago seven months before.

The last meeting of the visit was with Li Keqiang, then China’s premier, in the Great Hall of the People. McMaster recalled Li delivering a monologue to the effect that China, having built its own industrial and technological base, no longer needed the United States. America’s role in the future world economy, as Li described it, would be to supply China with raw materials, farm products and energy.

Nine years later, the centrepiece of the trade truce between the two countries is a Chinese promise to buy American soybeans.

Narcissism and empathy

McMaster borrowed two terms to explain where American policy had gone wrong. The first came from Hans Morgenthau, who observed that Americans tend to see the world only in relation to the United States and to assume that events will follow American plans. McMaster called this strategic narcissism, and he blamed it for the long-held assumption that a richer China, welcomed into the world economy, would liberalise. The second came from the historian Zachary Shore: strategic empathy, the effort to see the world as others see it, through their own history, fears and ambitions.

McMaster was arguing about China policy, but the two terms work just as well as a way of watching summits. Trump’s summitry is strategic narcissism in its purest form. It rests on personal rapport, flattery in both directions, and scores out of ten. He rated his October 2025 meeting with Xi in Busan “a 12,” as NPR reported. Xi’s summitry looks more like strategic empathy put to work: careful study of what the visitor wants to feel, followed by a production designed to make him feel it.

The cycle so far

The first act opened in Beijing on May 13, 2026, when Trump arrived for the first presidential visit to China in nearly a decade. Vice-President Han Zheng met him at the airport, and his entourage included Jensen Huang of Nvidia, Tim Cook of Apple and Elon Musk, as the South China Morning Post reported. The most consequential moment came afterwards, on Air Force One, when Trump would not say whether the United States would defend Taiwan and dismissed Ronald Reagan’s Six Assurances to Taipei as “a long time ago.” The Beijing Opera’s TACO scorecard (Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate) scored trade and Taiwan as Accommodate, Iran as Obfuscate, and the overall framing of the relationship as Capitulate.

The second act opened in Washington on September 23. Trump gave Xi a tour of the construction site for his new ballroom, presented him with a bald eagle, and quarrelled with the American press pool over who would be allowed to cover it. Taiwan surfaced only in the fifth paragraph of China’s closing statement. The two sides extended their trade truce to January 10, 2027, and very little else of substance was agreed.

The pattern across both acts is that the pageantry has grown and the substance has not. Each host has tried to impress the other, and Trump has openly studied Beijing’s choreography with a view to copying it. China, meanwhile, has given up little that it was not already prepared to give.

A stage of Xi’s choosing

The third act is different in kind. Shenzhen will host the 33rd APEC Economic Leaders’ Meeting, China’s third time as host and its first in 12 years, according to a briefing by The Rio Times. Foreign Minister Wang Yi has named openness, innovation and cooperation as China’s priorities and has called for a substantive outcome document. Trump is not coming as a guest of honour on a state visit. He is coming as one of 21 leaders to a gathering that Xi chairs.

That distinction matters. Alyssa Chen argued in the South China Morning Post after the Washington visit that in Shenzhen, home to Huawei, BYD and Tencent, Xi will have far more control over the agenda than he could in a bilateral summit, and more room to display China's diplomatic reach. When Trump lands on the afternoon of November 18, after a stop at the ASEAN summit in Manila, he will be the first sitting American president to set foot in the city, the Post noted in a separate report.

There will also be a third tenor. On November 13 the White House confirmed that Trump, Xi and Vladimir Putin will hold the first three-way meeting between the leaders of the United States, China and Russia, on November 19 in Shenzhen, as AFP reported. Nuclear arms reduction and the war with Iran are on the agenda, and expectations of a deal are low. According to ABC News, it will be the final event of Trump's Asia trip, following a bilateral meeting and working lunch with Xi that afternoon. No separate meeting with Putin is scheduled, and Beijing has yet to confirm the arrangements publicly. The Polymarket traders who priced a photograph of all three men together at better than nine in ten look set to collect.

Shenzhen is 13 hours ahead of Toronto. The leaders' meeting opens on the morning of November 18 there, which will be the evening of November 17 here. Trump's lunch with Xi and the three-way meeting with Putin fall on the afternoon of November 19 in Shenzhen, the small hours of that Thursday in Toronto.

The visitor arrives diminished

On November 3, Trump’s party lost both houses of Congress. A South China Morning Post analysis drew the obvious parallel: a decade ago China hosted another politically hobbled American president, Barack Obama, at the G20 in Hangzhou. That visit is remembered chiefly for its arrival. No rolling staircase met Air Force One, Obama left the plane by a door in its belly, and a Chinese official shouted at American staff on the tarmac that this was China’s country and China’s airport. Obama played it down. Beijing did not need to.

Chinese analysts are divided on what a weaker Trump means for them. Wu Xinbo of Fudan University, who advises China’s Foreign Ministry, told Reuters that the president may be even less willing now to upset the stable relationship he has built with Beijing. Shen Weizhong, a former Chinese Foreign Ministry official, called the $14 billion Taiwan arms package that Trump has been withholding, and that a Democratic Congress may now try to release, “risk number one.”

John Feng, the China editor of Newsweek, made the opposite case. A wounded Trump is less predictable, and predictability is what Xi values most in the relationship. Craig Singleton of the Foundation for Defense of Democracies warned that if Beijing expected the election to humble Trump, it was probably mistaken; a president weakened at home may look for bolder moves abroad.

Applying McMaster’s empathy test, the view from Beijing is clearer than the view from Washington. Xi is widely expected to seek a fourth term at the party’s 21st Congress in about a year. He has purged dozens of senior party and military officials, and Feng reported that a recent Central Committee gathering had the lowest attendance in more than half a century. A leader with that calendar wants a quiet year with Washington, not a dramatic one.

The pre-summit signals fit that reading. On November 6, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Trade Representative Jamieson Greer held what both sides called a frank call with Vice-Premier He Lifeng. Washington pressed China to carry out its commitments in full. Beijing complained about new American sanctions over Cuba that hit two Chinese companies, according to an AFP report carried by the Kuwait Times. Observers quoted by the South China Morning Post expect few concrete agreements in Shenzhen, with the larger deals held back for Miami.

The overture

McMaster ended his tour convinced that fear and ambition drive the Chinese Communist Party in equal measure, and that the two cannot be separated. Its leaders, he wrote, believed they had a narrow window to strengthen their rule and reshape the international order, before the economy soured, before the population aged and before other countries realised what was happening. Six years later every one of those clocks is still running, and the property crash and the ageing population have both arrived on schedule.

In 2017 the host chose a palace and an opera to tell the visitor that China had always stood at the centre of the world. In 2026 he has chosen a city that was a border town of 30,000 people when Deng Xiaoping launched his reforms, to tell the visitor where the world is going. The question for this cycle, as it has been from the start, is which of the two men has been practising strategic empathy, and which has simply been enjoying the show.

The curtain goes up on November 18. First, the set.
