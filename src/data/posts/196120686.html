In 1920, a man travelling from Beijing to Canton — a journey of roughly 1,500 miles through the heart of China — would cross the territory of at least six different military commanders, each with his own currency, his own tax collectors, and his own ideas about who should pay for his army. The national government in Beijing controlled the customs revenue because the foreign powers insisted on it, collected by foreign officials and deposited in foreign banks. Beyond that, the national government controlled very little.

This is what the fall of the Qing dynasty produced: not a republic, but an ungoverned space where governance had been.

The warlords who filled that space are a source of genuine historical complexity. They were not simply bandits in uniform, though some were close to it. Some were genuine administrators who brought a degree of order and even development to their territories. Yan Xishan, who controlled Shanxi province for nearly three decades, built railways, promoted education, and maintained a level of stability that made Shanxi one of the more liveable parts of China during the warlord period. Feng Yuxiang, the “Christian General” who baptised his troops with a fire hose, was a genuine reformer by warlord standards. Zhang Zuolin, who controlled Manchuria, ran it as an efficient if brutal personal fiefdom and negotiated with the Japanese for years before they blew up his train in 1928 when they decided his successors would be more compliant.

Others were simply violent men with armies. Zhang Xun, who briefly restored the boy emperor Puyi to the throne in 1917 for a twelve-day imperial restoration before being driven out by other warlords, required his soldiers to wear pigtails as a symbol of loyalty to the old regime. The pigtail, which the Qing had imposed on Han Chinese men as a mark of submission to Manchu rule, had been one of the first things cut when the republic was declared. Zhang Xun’s men grew theirs back. They were routed in twelve days.

For Chinese peasants — who constituted roughly eighty-five percent of the population — the warlord period meant something more immediate than political instability. It meant conscription.

Warlord armies needed men. The conscription methods varied from formal enlistment with promises of pay that were rarely kept to simple kidnapping of young men from villages along the army’s line of march. Once conscripted, soldiers might be paid occasionally, fed irregularly, and were certain to be ordered into battle against other warlord armies over terrain that meant nothing to them in service of disputes they did not understand. Desertion was the rational response, and it was common. The warlord armies of this period were largely composed of men who would have rather been anywhere else, commanded by officers whose main qualification was loyalty to the warlord who had promoted them.

Into this environment, the Chinese Communist Party was trying to build a revolution.

The party that had been founded in a Shanghai apartment in 1921 with thirteen delegates and perhaps fifty-seven members nationally was, by the late 1920s, operating under nearly impossible conditions. The Shanghai Massacre of April 1927, in which Chiang Kai-shek’s Nationalist forces killed thousands of Communist Party members and trade union activists, had destroyed the party’s urban network. The Comintern’s advice — which had emphasised organising urban workers on the Soviet model — had led the party into the cities precisely when the cities became the most dangerous place to be.

Mao Zedong had been arguing since at least 1927 that the Chinese revolution needed to be built in the countryside, among the peasants, rather than in the cities among the industrial workers. China was not Russia. The industrial proletariat was too small, too concentrated in treaty port cities controlled by either warlords or the KMT, and too exposed. The peasantry was vast, had genuine grievances against landlords and tax collectors, and occupied terrain that a guerrilla force could use. Mao’s insight was correct. The party leadership, taking its direction from Moscow, took several years and several catastrophic defeats to admit it.

The Autumn Harvest Uprising of 1927, one of several Communist insurrections following the Shanghai Massacre, failed comprehensively. Mao retreated with the surviving forces to the Jinggang Mountains on the border of Hunan and Jiangxi provinces — terrain that was sufficiently remote to be ungoverned and sufficiently agricultural to be fed. He began building what he called a “base area”: a liberated zone with its own government, its own land reform programme, and its own military force.

The Jiangxi Soviet, as it became known, attracted other Communist military leaders who had survived the KMT purge. Zhu De, whose military competence complemented Mao’s political skills, became the commander of the Red Army. The two men developed, through years of fighting, the guerrilla tactics that Mao would later codify in his military writings: the enemy advances, we retreat; the enemy camps, we harass; the enemy tires, we attack; the enemy retreats, we pursue. Against the warlord armies of the period, these tactics worked. Against Chiang Kai-shek’s German-trained and German-advised Nationalist forces, they would be tested more severely.

Chiang launched five successive encirclement campaigns against the Jiangxi Soviet between 1930 and 1934. The first four failed. The fifth, which used a strategy of slow advance with fortified blockhouses — building a wall of concrete positions that the Red Army could not bypass or outmanoeuvre — succeeded. By the autumn of 1934, the Jiangxi Soviet was surrounded. The Red Army faced annihilation or breakout.

They chose breakout.

The Long March — the retreat of the Red Army from Jiangxi to Yan’an in Shaanxi province, covering roughly 5,600 miles over 370 days from October 1934 to October 1935 — is the foundational myth of the Chinese Communist Party in the precise sense: a true event that has been shaped into a myth. Both the truth and the myth matter for understanding contemporary China.

The truth is that approximately 86,000 soldiers, party members, and support personnel left Jiangxi. Roughly 8,000 arrived in Yan’an. The losses — to battle, to cold, to starvation, to disease, to the rivers crossed, the mountains climbed, the Nationalist aircraft overhead — were catastrophic. The survivors were not a victorious army. They were the remnant of an army that had failed to hold its base and was searching for somewhere to rebuild.

The myth is that the Long March was a strategic retreat that preserved the revolutionary core against overwhelming odds, demonstrated the indomitable will of the Chinese Communist Party, and proved that the movement could not be destroyed. The myth is also true. The survivors of the Long March became the founding generation of the People’s Republic — its military commanders, its senior officials, its ideological authorities. Their shared experience of survival under impossible conditions created a bond and a legitimacy that no subsequent generation of Chinese leadership has quite replicated.

At the Zunyi Conference in January 1935, midway through the march, Mao Zedong emerged as the dominant figure in the party for the first time. The conference assessed the military failures that had made the retreat necessary and assigned blame to the party’s Comintern-directed leadership. Mao’s analysis — that the correct military strategy had been abandoned in favour of Soviet advice that did not apply to Chinese conditions — was vindicated by events. From Zunyi forward, Mao led the party.

The Japanese solved the Communist problem for the Communists.

The full-scale Japanese invasion of China, which began with the Marco Polo Bridge Incident in July 1937, forced Chiang Kai-shek to abandon the fifth encirclement campaign that had driven the Red Army from Jiangxi. The Communists and Nationalists formed the Second United Front — a second alliance of mutual convenience, with even less trust than the first — to resist the Japanese invasion together.

The Marco Polo Bridge Incident itself was symptomatic of how Japanese military expansion in China was being managed — or mismanaged — in Tokyo. Japanese troops stationed near the bridge under treaty rights exchanged fire with Chinese soldiers on the night of July 7, 1937. The skirmish was local and potentially containable. Japanese field commanders on the ground escalated without full authorisation from Tokyo; Tokyo then authorised the escalation after the fact; and within weeks China and Japan were in full-scale war. The Japanese military had been expanding in Manchuria and northern China for years through a combination of deliberate aggression and calculated ambiguity about Tokyo’s intentions, and the Marco Polo Bridge became the point at which ambiguity ran out.

The alliance was real in the sense that neither side openly attacked the other during the main period of Japanese occupation. It was fictional in the sense that both sides were simultaneously fighting Japan and preparing for the resumption of the civil war that both sides knew was coming the moment Japan was defeated. Chiang Kai-shek received American aid intended for the anti-Japanese war effort and stockpiled significant quantities of it for postwar use against the Communists. Mao Zedong expanded the Red Army’s territory and peasant base across northern China while the Nationalist forces bore the brunt of direct combat against the Japanese.

The strategic calculation was brutal and, in Mao’s case, largely correct. The Nationalists spent their best troops fighting Japan. The Communists spent the war years building a political organisation embedded in the villages of northern China that no external force had been able to destroy.

When Japan surrendered in August 1945, the Nationalists had a larger army and American equipment. The Communists had a political organisation embedded in the villages of northern China that no external force had been able to destroy. The Civil War that followed, 1946 to 1949, will be addressed in Part V. But its outcome was determined in significant part by what both sides had done and not done during the eight years of Japanese occupation.

The Japanese occupation of China — which, in Manchuria, began in 1931 and, nationally, in 1937 — requires its own accounting that this series will provide in more detail. For now, one number serves to mark its scale: an estimated 14 to 20 million Chinese civilians and soldiers died during the Sino-Japanese War, the second-largest death toll of any theatre in the Second World War. The Nanjing Massacre of December 1937, in which Japanese forces killed an estimated 100,000 to 300,000 civilians and prisoners of war over six weeks in the former Nationalist capital, remains one of the most contested events in the historical memory of both countries — contested not in the sense that it did not happen, but in the sense that its scale and precise nature are disputed between Chinese and Japanese historians in ways that continue to shape relations between the two countries today.

Xi Jinping’s government conducts an annual national ceremony commemorating the Nanjing Massacre. It is not a memorial. It is a political statement, renewed every December 13, about the relationship between historical memory and national purpose.

The warlord period produced nothing that lasted except the lessons it taught the people who survived it.

Mao Zedong learned that power flows from the barrel of a gun and that political authority without military control is provisional. Chiang Kai-shek learned that military control without political legitimacy is equally provisional. Both men spent the next two decades applying those lessons, and one of them was right.

The Chinese Communist Party that emerged from the Jiangxi Soviet, the Long March, and the Yan’an base area was not the party that had been founded in Shanghai in 1921. It had been tested in conditions that destroyed most political organisations. The survivors had earned their authority in a way that no theoretical programme could replicate. When the party took power in 1949, it was led by people who had crossed the Luding Bridge under fire, climbed the Great Snowy Mountains in summer clothes, and arrived in Yan’an with nothing but the discipline to begin again.

The Yan’an years — roughly 1935 to 1945, when the Communist base area in Shaanxi served as the party’s headquarters — deserve their own note. It was in Yan’an that the party developed the administrative and ideological practices that would govern China after 1949: the mass line (learning from the people in order to lead them), rectification campaigns (ideological purges conducted through self-criticism and study), and the cult of Mao as the indispensable leader whose thought was the source of correct policy. These practices were forged under conditions of genuine hardship and genuine threat — the KMT blockade was real, the Japanese bombing was real — and they carried the psychological weight of having worked when survival was genuinely in doubt. They also contained, in embryo, the mechanisms that would produce the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution. The mass line, which sounded participatory, in practice meant that the masses validated the leader’s pre-existing conclusions. The rectification campaign, which sounded like honest self-examination, in practice meant that anyone whose examination produced the wrong conclusions was purged. What was forged in necessity in Yan’an became pathology in power.

That formation — the bone-deep conviction that the party had survived what should have killed it because it was historically necessary — is the foundation on which everything that followed was built. Including the catastrophes.
