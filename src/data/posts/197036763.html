Canadian Snowman’s Guide to the Most Important Meeting Nobody Expects to Matter

Beijing Opera: Where the performance matters far more than the plot. Sound familiar?

As I write this, Donald Trump is in Beijing — the first American president to visit China since Trump himself was last there in November 2017. He arrived Wednesday evening for a two-day summit with Xi Jinping. By the time you read it, the welcome ceremony at the Great Hall of the People will have concluded, the first bilateral meeting will be over, and Xi Jinping will be hosting a state banquet. The photographs will already exist. The question is whether anything behind them does.

Both publics will be told different stories about what happened. Both stories will be substantially true and comprehensively misleading. This is not a defect of the system. This is the system.

I wrote this essay on the eve of the summit — Wednesday, May 13, 2026 — and updated it Thursday morning as the first meetings got underway, from the vantage point of a Canadian who has spent the previous three essays tracing how this meeting was conceived, disrupted, delayed, and reassembled under circumstances that neither side anticipated. The story so far has been a story of declining expectations. Every analyst I have read, from Brookings to the Foreign Policy archives to the think tanks that line Massachusetts Avenue like competing restaurants on a mediocre street, has arrived at the same conclusion: nothing of substance will emerge.

The question, then, is not what the summit will produce. The question is what the summit is for.

Let me begin with what each side says it wants, because the gap between the stated agendas and the achievable outcomes is where the real story lives.

What Trump wants — or says he wants:

The American agenda, as assembled from press briefings, senatorial readouts, and the not-very-subtle hints dropped by everyone from Scott Bessent to Marco Rubio, includes: Chinese pressure on Iran to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. A major Boeing aircraft purchase. Continued Chinese purchases of American agricultural products. An extension of the trade truce negotiated at Busan. A bilateral “Board of Trade” to manage economic ties. A pause on Chinese rare earth export restrictions. Continued Chinese cooperation on fentanyl precursor chemicals. And — this last one spoken sotto voce, but audible to anyone paying attention — the optics of a president conducting statesmanship on the world stage while a war rages in the Middle East, which requires projecting the image of a leader in command of events rather than being dragged along by them.

The structure of the American delegation reveals the agenda’s true centre of gravity. The chief negotiator leading up to the trip has been Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, not Secretary of State Rubio. R. Nicholas Burns, who served as ambassador to China under Biden, noted that “for the first time since Kissinger, the secretary of state and national security adviser are not driving the relationship with China.” This is a trade trip. Everything else is staging. David Sanger of the New York Times, reporting from Beijing, put it plainly: this is not how Trump wanted to arrive. He delayed the trip by six weeks betting he would show up having forced Iran to capitulate. Instead he arrives with Iran’s nuclear stockpile still under the rubble, the Strait of Hormuz still closed, and Chancellor Merz of Germany having called him “humiliated” by a smaller power.

What Xi wants — or signals he wants:

The Chinese agenda, as communicated through Premier Li Qiang’s meeting with Daines, Wang Yi’s calls with Rubio, and the careful semaphore of state media commentary, includes: an extension of the trade truce with predictable terms. A reduction or easing of US semiconductor export controls. A shift in American declaratory language on Taiwan. Eased sanctions on Chinese technology companies. Recognition of China’s role as a responsible mediator in the Iran crisis. A commitment by Trump to attend APEC in Shenzhen in November, followed by Xi’s reciprocal state visit to Washington. And — also sotto voce but equally audible — the validation of Xi Jinping as an indispensable global leader, the man without whom the world’s largest bilateral relationship cannot function, convening the American president in Beijing on Chinese terms and Chinese soil.

What the analysts expect:

Craig Singleton at the Foundation for Defense of Democracies provided the most quotable summary: “beans, Boeings, and a board of trade, but certainly not a breakthrough.” He framed Xi’s real win as “continued stability without surrender” — buying time because Xi views the United States as a long-term strategic risk and wants to use this period to enhance China’s position without provoking a rupture.

Xi formed his verdict of Trump nearly a decade ago. In late 2016, weeks after Trump's election stunned the world, Xi met Obama for the last time at a summit in Lima, Peru. According to Ben Rhodes, Obama's deputy national security adviser who was in the room, Xi seemed baffled that American voters could choose someone so unconventional. Obama tried to explain Trump's rise as a symptom of economic frustration — the loss of manufacturing jobs to China, the theft of intellectual property. Xi was displeased with the explanation. He put down his pen, folded his arms, and said: "If an immature leader throws the world into chaos, then the world will know who to blame." That was 2016. Xi has had a decade to prepare for the man he is hosting this week.

This is not improvisation. It is doctrine. The intellectual architect of China’s patience strategy is Wang Huning, a member of the seven-person Politburo Standing Committee and one of Xi’s closest advisers. In the 1990s — the height of American unipolar dominance — Wang travelled through the United States and was so shaken by what he saw that he wrote a book called America Against America, in which he described a nation beset by social fragmentation, inequality, and political dysfunction. Homelessness, drug addiction, racial violence, low educational standards — Wang concluded that America contained the seeds of its own destruction. Three decades later, Wang is among the most powerful men in China, and the thesis he outlined as an obscure academic has become the operating assumption of the Chinese state. In private conversations and public writings, Ryan Hass of the Brookings Institution reported this week in The Atlantic, China’s leaders and their advisers describe America as “declining but dangerous” — a late-stage power prone to bursts of aggression in the hopes of arresting its slide. The Iran war is, from Beijing’s vantage point, exhibit A: a burst of aggression that has depleted American arsenals, alienated American allies, and disrupted global energy markets, all while China quietly positions itself to fill the vacuum when the flames die down. Why confront a declining power directly when you can simply wait for it to exhaust itself?

The Li Yuan column in the New York Times, published on the morning of the summit, documented how thoroughly Wang Huning’s thesis has saturated Chinese political culture. In January, a nationalistic Beijing think tank affiliated with Renmin University published a report on Trump’s first year back in office whose title dispensed with diplomatic niceties altogether: “Thank Trump.” The report called him “an accelerator of American political decay” and concluded with a sentence that belongs in an anthology of imperial epitaphs: “What we hear is the heavy and haunting toll of an empire’s evening bell.” Huang Jing, a professor at Shanghai International Studies University, was even blunter: “Only China can save Trump.” With midterms approaching, Huang argued, Trump needs Chinese purchases of soybeans and natural gas to play well in swing states. The dependency runs one way now, and both sides know it. But the most precise formulation came from Zongyuan Zoe Liu, an economist at the Council on Foreign Relations. Xi, she said, “is getting the United States he always wanted and the America he most feared at the same time.” The first half of that sentence is the patience strategy vindicated. The second half is the reason Beijing is not celebrating.

The Brookings assessment was similarly muted. The relationship, Brookings argued, is “defined more by an absence of friction than any affirmative agenda or deep dialogue.” Many Chinese analysts, Brookings noted, expect a snap back to a more competitive American China policy — either after the midterms or after Trump leaves office in 2029. The summit is, from Beijing’s perspective, a weather event to be survived rather than a strategic opportunity to be seized.

Michael Froman, who served as US trade representative under Obama, wrote last week what may serve as the summit’s epitaph: “Gone is any pretense of solving the major structural issues at the heart of the world’s most important bilateral relationship: China’s mercantilist economic model, its designs on absorbing Taiwan and its active support of U.S. adversaries such as Iran and Russia. The summit is about managing for stability, not solving outstanding concerns.”

Allen Carlson at Cornell delivered what may be the single most devastating assessment of any summit in the history of international relations: “The chance of anything of substance emerging from these talks is little more than zero.” And then the knife: “It is precisely this lack of substance that defines the summit’s purpose. Absent any real content, both men can declare victory without sacrificing much of anything. The summit itself is then the thing.”

Let that settle for a moment. The summit itself is then the thing. Not the trade deal. Not the Taiwan framework. Not the Iran cooperation. The meeting. The handshake. The photograph. The performance of statesmanship in the absence of statecraft. This is what the most important bilateral relationship on earth has been reduced to: a play in which both actors know their lines, the audience knows the plot, and the only question is whether the staging is convincing enough to sustain the illusion for another six months.

To shake or not to shake, that is the question

May 14, 2026. Two men who need each other more than either will admit.

The TACO framework — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — which I have been developing across these essays, achieves its fullest expression in the dynamics of this summit. Not because the framework is cleverer than the situation warrants, but because the situation is operating in all four modes simultaneously, and neither side can afford to acknowledge that fact.

Threaten: The threats are real and ongoing. The United States maintains tariffs on Chinese goods — reduced from their 2025 peaks but still substantial. It continues semiconductor export controls that Chinese officials regard as an attempt to strangle China’s technological development. It has sanctioned Chinese oil refineries and shipping companies for trading with Iran. It recently added and then removed Chinese technology companies from the Pentagon’s blacklist in a display of policy incoherence that confused allies and adversaries alike. Meanwhile, China maintains its rare earth export controls — paused but not lifted, a threat held in suspension rather than deployed. It continues military pressure on Taiwan, with operations around the island at elevated levels. And it invoked, for the first time in its history, a “blocking rule” directing Chinese companies not to comply with American sanctions — a legal instrument that signals Beijing’s willingness to escalate the sanctions war into a direct confrontation over corporate jurisdiction. The New York Times reported on the eve of the summit that Beijing’s regulatory posture has shifted from reactive to offensive: new rules give Chinese regulators the power to investigate corporate records, interrogate employees, and bar companies or executives from leaving China if they are found to be helping shift supply chains out of the country. “China is signaling more strongly that they are locked and loaded,” said Andrew Gilholm at Control Risks. The message to every multinational doing business in both countries is now explicit: comply with American sanctions and face Chinese punishment, or comply with Chinese law and face American sanctions. Sean Stein of the US-China Business Council called it a “hot stove approach” — Beijing demonstrating that American actions will produce immediate burns.

Accommodate: Both sides are accommodating by showing up. This sounds trivial but it is not. A presidential visit to Beijing — the first in nearly a decade — is an enormous concession of attention, prestige, and political capital. Trump is travelling to China in the middle of a war that is not going well, at a moment when his domestic political position is complicated by midterm anxieties and the lingering fallout of the Supreme Court’s IEEPA ruling. Xi is receiving a president whose administration has sanctioned Chinese companies, restricted Chinese technology access, and launched a war that has disrupted Chinese energy supplies. The mutual accommodation of the visit itself is the first and perhaps most important deliverable of the summit, because it signals to markets, allies, and domestic audiences that the relationship has not collapsed. Everything else is footnotes.

Capitulate: The capitulations will be carefully curated to look like victories. China will buy soybeans. It will probably buy Boeings — the 500-aircraft deal has been telegraphed so aggressively that failing to announce it would itself be a diplomatic event. The United States will probably extend the trade truce, announce the Board of Trade, and pocket whatever agricultural commitments Beijing offers. Neither side will frame these outcomes as concessions, because the entire art of summit diplomacy is the transformation of capitulation into narrative. You do not surrender soybeans. You “strengthen bilateral agricultural cooperation.” You do not cave on Boeings. You “demonstrate confidence in commercial aviation partnerships.” The language is the product. The language is always the product.

Obfuscate: This is where the summit earns its keep. Both leaders need to leave Beijing with stories their domestic audiences can accept. Trump needs to show that his visit produced economic results — deals, purchases, commitments — that justify the trip and demonstrate that his China policy is working. Xi needs to show that the visit validates China’s position as a co-equal power, that the trade truce is stable, and that the American president came to Beijing and treated Chinese concerns with respect. Neither story needs to be complete. Both stories need to be plausible. The space between complete and plausible is where obfuscation lives, and it is a very large space.

Four issues sit outside the transactional framework and carry genuine strategic risk. They are the rocks beneath the surface of the choreographed performance, and any one of them could turn the Beijing Opera from theatre into drama.

Taiwan is the first and most dangerous. The Centre for Strategic and International Studies in Washington has identified the specific fear: that Xi will extract a shift in American declaratory language, moving from “does not support” Taiwan independence to “opposes” Taiwan independence. This sounds like a grammatical quibble. It is not. The current formulation — “does not support” — is a passive construction that leaves American policy ambiguous. The proposed formulation — “opposes” — is an active commitment that implies the United States would work against Taiwanese independence, not merely decline to encourage it. For Taiwan, the difference is the difference between a friend who won’t help you move and a friend who actively prevents you from moving.

Rubio confirmed that Taiwan would be discussed, adding that “both countries understand that it is in neither one of our interests to see anything destabilizing happen in that part of the world.” This is the language of managed ambiguity — the same language that has governed the Taiwan question since 1979 — and its continued use is itself a signal that the administration intends to avoid a dramatic shift. But Trump’s pattern in one-on-one meetings with authoritarian leaders is not a pattern of careful adherence to scripted language. Trump, at his most characteristic, is a man who believes that personal rapport can override institutional positions, and that the warmth of a handshake can substitute for the precision of a communiqué. Taipei has reason to be nervous.

And Taipei’s nervousness is not theoretical. On Friday — three days before the summit — a bipartisan group of eight senators sent Trump a letter urging him to move forward with a $14 billion arms package for Taiwan that the administration has held up for months. The letter’s existence is itself remarkable, but the detail that makes it devastating is this: administration officials told lawmakers involved in the approval process that the White House directed the hold to ensure that Trump had a successful meeting with Xi. “American support for Taiwan is not up for negotiation,” the senators wrote, in a sentence that would not need to be written if American support for Taiwan were not, in fact, being negotiated. Taiwan’s legislature, for its part, approved a $25 billion special defence budget last week — a domestic political achievement that required overcoming real opposition — and is now waiting for the American weapons that the American president has decided to withhold as a gesture of goodwill toward the country that has threatened to take Taiwan by force. The irony is not subtle. It is not meant to be.

Iran is the second rock. The administration wants Beijing to use its influence with Tehran to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. This is a reasonable ask — China is Iran’s largest oil customer, and Beijing has demonstrated some willingness to engage, hosting Iranian Foreign Minister Araghchi in the week before the summit. But the analysts who study China most closely are nearly unanimous in their assessment that Beijing does not plan to deliver much on Iran. China’s engagement is calibrated to look helpful without being decisive. The analogy, offered by multiple sources, is North Korea: even on an issue directly adjacent to China’s borders, Beijing rarely puts real pressure on Pyongyang. China’s energy buffer — 1.4 billion barrels of strategic reserves, overland pipelines from Russia and Central Asia, rapid electrification — means the Hormuz crisis, while inconvenient, is not existential for Beijing. The bigger concern for China, as one analyst noted, is “the secondary and tertiary effects from this conflict” — a war-driven global slowdown that hits the Southeast Asian and European consumers that Chinese exports depend on.

Rare earths are the third rock, and they are the one that connects the summit most directly to the ongoing war. Every analyst I have consulted describes rare earth controls as China’s most potent lever — a weapon that does not need to be fired to be effective, because its mere existence constrains American military planning. The pause on Chinese export restrictions is expected to continue through the summit, because Beijing gains more from the implied threat than from the actual restriction. But the underlying dependency is unchanged: the United States cannot replenish the weapons it fired in Iran without Chinese cooperation, and the Pentagon’s 2027 deadline for eliminating Chinese rare earths from its supply chain is, by universal assessment, unachievable. The summit will produce reassuring language about supply chain cooperation. The underlying structural dependency will be politely unmentioned.

And then there is the fourth rock — the one that nobody saw coming and that may, in retrospect, dwarf the other three.

Reports have surfaced that Trump and Xi are considering a deal to allow China to invest up to $1 trillion in the United States, largely to build factories on American soil. A trillion dollars. The figure is so large that it resists comprehension — it exceeds the total direct investment made in the United States by any single country since the Declaration of Independence. Oren Cass of American Compass, a conservative economic think tank and therefore not a voice that can be dismissed as reflexively anti-Trump, called it “an unforced error of world-historic proportions.”

The logic, from Trump’s perspective, is characteristic: the problem with China is a bad deal, and the remedy for a bad deal is a better deal. Factories on American soil mean American jobs, American headlines, and the appearance of a president who turned the world’s most dangerous economic rival into the world’s most generous investor. It is, in the TACO framework, the ultimate accommodation dressed as victory.

The problem is that China has already demonstrated, with clinical precision, what happens when it invests in another country’s industrial base. Cass cites the case of Tesla — a company I have been writing about in a separate series and in which I hold a personal interest as a shareholder. China lured Musk to Shanghai with generous subsidies, a sweetheart deal on premium industrial land, and fast-tracked regulatory approvals. Chinese engineers learned Tesla’s innovations. Then they went home to competitors. Beijing withdrew the subsidies. By 2025, Tesla was getting crushed in China, its market share below five percent. Cass calls it running the playbook to perfection. The question is whether the United States can run that playbook in reverse. The answer, as Cass argues and as the structure of the Chinese state ensures, is that it cannot. Chinese companies operating in the United States will share no technology that the Chinese Communist Party does not want shared. They will, however, have what amounts to a hall pass to gather data, absorb methods, and operate with the backing of a state that has no obligation to pursue profit and virtually unlimited capacity to subsidize below-market pricing.

This is not paranoia. It is doctrine. A textbook used by the People’s Liberation Army states that Xi has emphasized that China’s system is “fundamentally incompatible with the West” and that the resulting struggle will be “long, complicated and sometimes even very sharp.” The textbook’s recommended strategy is explicit: “We must gain a grip on foreign government leaders and their business elites by encouraging our companies to invest in their local economies.” That is the sentence that should be read aloud in every briefing room in Washington before the summit begins. It will not be.

Michael Kovrig — the Canadian diplomat who was himself detained by China for over a thousand days in retaliation for Canada’s arrest of Huawei executive Meng Wanzhou, and who now writes about China from a perspective that combines scholarly rigour with personal authority — published an essay in Foreign Affairs this week titled “Trump’s China Trap.” His argument is that individually, each Western leader’s visit to Beijing may be defensible and may even serve the visiting country’s rational self-interest. But in aggregate, the visits produce “a political and propaganda bonanza for Beijing.” By accepting the terms Xi sets and performing in his authoritarian pageantry, visiting leaders gratify what Kovrig identifies as Xi’s deepest yearning: to be recognized as the world’s foremost strongman.

Kovrig’s analysis is sharp, and it cuts both ways. If every visit to Beijing validates the Chinese system, then every visit must also be weighed against the cost of that validation. The calculus is not simple. Not going to Beijing also has costs — in lost commercial opportunities, in diplomatic drift, in the absence of channels that might prevent miscalculation. The summit is simultaneously a trap and a necessity, which is the essential paradox of great-power engagement and the reason that diplomats, from Keenleyside’s era to the present, have always preferred the imperfect conversation to the eloquent silence.

But a trillion-dollar investment deal is not an imperfect conversation. It is a structural transformation — the kind of decision that, once made, cannot be unmade. Kovrig warns about the propaganda value of a visit. Cass warns about the economic value of an invitation. And the PLA textbook suggests that from Beijing’s perspective, the invitation is not a concession from China but a weapon aimed at America, deployed with the patient confidence of a power that believes its rival is declining and need only be encouraged to accelerate the process.

There is another audience for this performance, and it is neither Chinese nor American. It is the rest of the world — the middle powers that live in the space between the two giants and have learned, over the past decade, that neither giant can be relied upon to act in anyone’s interest but its own.

Richard Heydarian, the Filipino political scientist now at Oxford, described their posture this week with a phrase that deserves to outlive the summit: “fifty shades of hedging.” Poland is buying South Korean tanks. Australia is buying Japanese warships. Canada is sending uranium to India. India is offering cruise missiles to Vietnam. Brazil is building military transport planes for the UAE. Every one of these deals was sealed in recent weeks. Every one represents a country that has concluded it cannot depend on the American security architecture and is quietly building alternatives.

My own prime minister, Mark Carney, received a standing ovation in Davos earlier this year for a speech that declared: “We are in the midst of a rupture, not a transition.” It is a line that European and Asian officials echo privately, even when their public language remains carefully deferential. Carney himself has learned how Xi manages middle powers. In their first meeting, in South Korea, Xi spent the opening ten-plus minutes setting ground rules for the relationship. Carney’s summary of Xi’s message was characteristically Canadian in its diplomatic restraint: “No surprises. If you really care about something, be clear.” Then, with the understatement of a man who had just been lectured by the leader of the world’s second-largest economy: “I mean, he didn’t say it this way, but I interpret it. Don’t lecture me in public. Bring issues to me directly.” Xi, it seems, reserves the right to lecture others — he confronted Trudeau at the G20 in Bali over a leaked readout — but does not care to receive the favour in return.

The fear, expressed across the region and reported extensively by the New York Times this week, is not merely that the summit will fail — it is that it will succeed in ways that make middle powers less safe. A concession on Taiwan emboldens Beijing’s territorial claims elsewhere. A troop drawdown in Asia to fund the Iran war weakens collective deterrence. A grand bargain on trade that ignores the interests of smaller economies leaves those economies exposed. Japan’s Prime Minister Takaichi has been crisscrossing Asia promoting military cooperation, but carefully — officials in Tokyo worried that her security tour might be seen as “a version of Mark Carney,” which in the current diplomatic climate is apparently the worst thing a Japanese prime minister can be compared to.

The middle powers are hedging because they have concluded — correctly, in my Canadian estimation — that the Beijing Opera is being performed for an audience of two, and that the rest of the world is not in the theatre. They are in the parking lot, checking whether their cars still start.

Let me close with a prediction, offered with the confidence appropriate to a man watching from Toronto, which is to say, very little confidence but a certain amount of pattern recognition.

The summit will be announced as a success by both sides. Boeing will announce an order. Agricultural commitments will be made. The Board of Trade will be established, or at least named. Trump will declare the relationship “the best it’s ever been” or some variant thereof. Xi will express satisfaction with “the general direction of dialogue.” Both leaders will agree to meet again — Xi in Washington, Trump at APEC in Shenzhen — and the meeting cycle will continue, producing its own momentum, which is a different thing from producing results.

Taiwan language will not change dramatically, because the bureaucratic machinery on the American side — the State Department, the National Security Council, the Taiwan desk — will have prepared language that Trump can read without conceding too much, and because Xi, who is many things but not reckless, will accept the appearance of progress over the risk of a rupture. Iran will be discussed and both sides will claim cooperation, while the Strait of Hormuz remains functionally compromised by forces that no summit can control. Rare earths will be politely mentioned and politely deferred.

And the Beijing Opera will end as Beijing Operas always end: with the audience applauding the performance while the stage crew begins dismantling the set for the next show.

The performance, I suspect, will be good. It usually is. These two men — for all their differences in temperament, ideology, and governing style — share a theatrical instinct and an understanding that in the age of global media, the image is the policy. The question that will not be answered in Beijing, the question that will not be answered until the set has been struck and the motorcade has departed and the soybeans have been shipped and the Boeings have been ordered (or not), is whether any of it mattered.

That question belongs to Part V, which will be written after the curtain falls.
