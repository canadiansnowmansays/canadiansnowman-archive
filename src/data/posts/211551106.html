I introduced the stacking problem in Part I of this series as a narrow, almost technical observation about Abdul El-Sayed’s Michigan Senate campaign: that his combination of Medicare for All, abolishing ICE, and cutting off military aid to Israel wasn’t dangerous because any single plank was radioactive on its own, but because stacking three positions of very different polling popularity into one bundle handed Republicans an efficient attack surface — no distortion required, just reading the platform back verbatim. Four weeks and several more nominating contests later, that observation holds up well enough to widen into something closer to a general rule. It’s worth testing it against a broader set of 2026 nominees to see where it holds, where it doesn’t, and what that tells the party about message discipline heading into November.

The Bundle, Confirmed

Start with what actually happened to El-Sayed’s own bundle in practice, because the attacks that materialized after his primary win are close to a controlled experiment in exactly the dynamic Part I predicted. The National Republican Senatorial Committee’s digital ad rolled all three planks into a single fifteen-second package — “abolish ICE, implement Medicare for All, and defund the police,” with a line about a “Bernie Sanders-backed campaign” layered on for good measure — followed within weeks by conservative outlets circulating a rally clip of El-Sayed declaring, unprompted, “I’m the only one for Medicare for all. I’m the only one that will abolish ICE,” to a cheering crowd waving matching signs. By early August, the opposition research had moved to more personal terrain entirely, with reporting that El-Sayed’s own wife’s psychiatry practice doesn’t accept Medicare, an attack line that only works because “Medicare for All” was already established as his signature issue.

None of these attacks required distortion. El-Sayed said the words himself, on camera, at a podium, in front of “Fight Oligarchy” signage — which is precisely the vulnerability Part I flagged before any of it had aired. The lesson isn’t that Democrats should hide their positions. It’s that a candidate who volunteers the full stack, unprompted and in the most quotable possible phrasing, is doing his opponent’s ad-cutting for him.

The Candidates Who Didn’t Stack

The more useful test of the theory is negative: are there 2026 nominees running with equally progressive substance who haven’t handed Republicans the same gift, and if so, what did they do differently?

North Carolina’s Roy Cooper is the clearest case. A former two-term governor with a moderate, southern political brand, Cooper has run a campaign built almost entirely around his gubernatorial record — infrastructure, education funding, a pragmatic reputation built over eight years in Raleigh — rather than around a national platform of stacked progressive commitments. That’s partly a function of who Cooper is rather than a deliberate tactical choice; he was never going to run as an insurgent. But the effect is the same regardless of motive: there’s no fifteen-second ad to cut, because there’s no bundle of unpopular-with-swing-voters positions sitting there waiting to be assembled.

Georgia’s Jon Ossoff offers a more interesting case, because he’s not a moderate by reputation — he’s a first-term senator who beat an incumbent in a 2020 special election most Democrats considered a long shot, and who’s spent his term building a record on manufacturing investment and Georgia-specific economic issues rather than national culture-war planks. Ossoff is the only Democratic senator up in 2026 defending a seat in a state Trump carried in 2024, which makes him the single best test case in the entire cycle for whether progressive substance survives contact with a genuinely hostile electorate. His approach so far has been to localize almost everything — jobs, healthcare costs, infrastructure spending specific to Georgia counties — while largely declining to lead with the kind of national symbolic fights that generated El-Sayed’s most quotable rally lines.

Then there’s Texas, where the Democratic Senate primary pitted Representative Jasmine Crockett’s confrontational national profile against state Representative James Talarico’s more deliberately moderate, faith-inflected appeal. Reuters’ own primary coverage noted the calculation plainly: Crockett might excite primary voters more, but analysts judged she’d have a harder time in a general election than Talarico, precisely because her national brand, built substantially on viral clashes with Republican colleagues, carries the same bundling risk El-Sayed’s rally lines did. Talarico’s approach — a seminarian’s register, a deliberately lower national temperature — is close to a Texas-flavored version of the Whitmer-Beshear “talk like a normal human being” instinct this series examined in Part V.

Why the Order Matters as Much as the Content

Here’s where the theory needs sharpening rather than just confirming, because a purely content-based reading of the stacking problem runs into an immediate complication: Ro Khanna, examined in Part III of this series, holds a position on Israel aid that’s substantively close to El-Sayed’s — he’s publicly argued for redirecting military spending away from Israel toward domestic priorities, in almost identical language about a forty-five-billion-dollar defense budget. Khanna hasn’t paid anything like the same political price for it. The difference isn’t the position. It’s the sequencing and the venue.

El-Sayed volunteered his full stack, unprompted, at a rally, in the most viral possible phrasing, months before a contested primary where every word was being clipped by opposing campaigns in real time. Khanna’s Israel position surfaces mainly in response to direct questioning from interviewers like ABC’s Jonathan Karl, embedded within a broader “Team America” economic message that leads with a wealth tax and a jobs plan rather than foreign policy. Both men hold what a hostile audience would call a radical position on military aid. Only one of them has built an opposition research file out of it, because only one of them made it the first line of his own campaign advertisement.

This is a genuinely useful distinction for the party to internalize, because it cuts against both of the lazy conclusions available here. It’s not that progressive substance is simply unelectable in swing states — Cooper, Ossoff, and Khanna all hold plenty of positions well to the left of where the median swing voter sits, and none of them are getting cut into fifteen-second attack ads the way El-Sayed has been. And it’s not that message discipline alone can rescue any bundle of positions regardless of content — Talarico’s caution wouldn’t have saved a Crockett-style national brand any more than a calmer delivery would have neutralized El-Sayed’s specific combination of abolish-ICE-and-cut-Israel-aid, which polls badly as a pair even when either plank alone might survive. What actually determines whether a bundle becomes a weapon is the interaction of all three factors at once: how many low-polling positions get stacked together, how unprompted and quotable the phrasing is, and how much of the messaging happens in a controlled setting versus a rally moment built for virality.

The Vocabulary Connection

It’s worth tying this back explicitly to Part II, because the two arguments reinforce each other more than they might first appear to. Vocabulary reclamation — Buttigieg’s “freedom,” El-Sayed’s own capitalism argument, Khanna’s “Team America” — works by taking a word or concept the opposing party claims exclusive ownership of and demonstrating, through repetition and reframing, that the substance underneath it already belongs to the other side. That’s a slow, cumulative rhetorical project, and it depends on discipline: saying the same reframed argument consistently, in controlled settings, until it displaces the old association.

Stacking does the opposite. It takes several genuinely popular or at least defensible individual positions and compresses them into a single, high-velocity soundbite optimized for a friendly room rather than a skeptical one. A rally line that gets a standing ovation from committed supporters is, definitionally, not built for the swing voter who’s going to encounter it later as a fifteen-second attack ad stripped of context and applause. The same candidate can be doing vocabulary reclamation and stacking simultaneously without realizing they’re pulling against each other — patiently building “I’m a real capitalist” in one interview while handing opponents “abolish ICE, defund the police, Medicare for All” in the next rally, undoing months of careful reframing with thirty seconds of unguarded enthusiasm.

That tension is, I’d argue, the single most important internal contradiction this series has surfaced so far. The party has real, talented people doing the patient work of reclaiming contested vocabulary — Buttigieg, Khanna, Sand, Whitmer, Beshear, each examined in earlier installments. It also has candidates who, often in the same election cycle and sometimes in the same week, undercut that work by treating a primary rally as a venue for maximum stacking rather than message discipline. Both instincts come from the same underlying frustration — a base that’s tired of playing defense and wants its candidates to say the quiet part loudly — but only one of the two approaches survives contact with a general election audience that hasn’t already made up its mind.

What This Means Heading Into November

El-Sayed is the nominee now regardless of any of this; the primary’s over, and the general election against Mike Rogers will be the actual test of whether Michigan’s electorate punishes the bundle the way the sixty million dollars in primary spending predicted it would. But the broader pattern matters for every Democrat still running a competitive primary or gearing up for a general this fall. The stacking problem was never an argument for ideological retreat, and this series has been careful, across every essay so far, not to make that argument. It’s an argument for discipline in sequencing and venue — the same discipline Khanna is applying to a nearly identical Israel position, the same discipline Ossoff is applying to a Georgia electorate that offers zero margin for error, the same discipline Cooper never had to learn to apply because his political brand was built somewhere else entirely.

Loyal opposition, in the Westminster sense this series keeps returning to, isn’t about having the most positions or the fewest. It’s about knowing which fights to pick in public, in what order, and in front of which audience — a discipline Washington’s national leadership, per Part IV of this series, has struggled to enforce even on its own candidates, let alone teach them.
