On June 16, 2015, Donald Trump descended a golden escalator in Trump Tower on Fifth Avenue in New York City and announced his candidacy for the presidency of the United States. He was accompanied by a crowd that was later reported to include paid actors. He gave a speech that included the assertion that Mexico was sending rapists across the border. He declared his intention to build a wall.

Within forty-eight hours, NBC had cancelled The Celebrity Apprentice. Within a week, Univision had dropped the Miss Universe pageant, which Trump co-owned. Macy’s, which had carried a line of Trump menswear, announced it was discontinuing the product. NASCAR moved its awards banquet from a Trump property. The Professional Golfers Association moved a tournament from Trump National Doral. ESPN moved a college football awards ceremony.

The escalator descent, which Trump and his supporters experienced as a political launch, was experienced by the commercial partners of the Trump Organization as a brand emergency. The name that had been a commercial asset — a thing that commanded licensing fees and management contracts and premium pricing in luxury markets — had become, in the space of a single speech, a political symbol. And political symbols, unlike commercial brands, divide markets rather than expanding them.

The brand had always had its detractors. The bankruptcies, the litigation, the television persona that some found compelling and others found repellent — none of this had prevented the licensing model from functioning, because the detractors were diffuse and the supporters were numerous and the name carried enough aspirational weight to sustain the commercial logic. The escalator speech changed the arithmetic. It gave the detractors a specific, documented, undeniable reason to avoid the brand, and it gave that reason a political charge that made avoidance not just a consumer preference but a statement.

The statement, once made by NBC and Univision and Macy’s and NASCAR, was available to every licensing partner, every hotel guest, every resident of a Trump-branded building, every consumer of Trump-branded products. The name now meant something specific in a way it had not meant before, and what it meant was contested, and contested names are bad for business.

The global retreat from the Trump brand that followed the 2015 announcement and accelerated through the 2016 election and the subsequent presidency is worth documenting in some detail, because it establishes the baseline against which the monument project — the Kennedy Center, the Institute of Peace, the arch, the currency — must be understood.

In Panama City, the Trump Ocean Club International Hotel and Tower — a sixty-three storey property whose developers had signed a Trump licensing agreement in 2006 — became the site of a protracted legal battle when, in 2018, the building’s majority owners sought to remove the Trump Organization from its management role. The dispute involved allegations of financial mismanagement, inflated fees, and the straightforward commercial reality that the Trump name, post-2016, was functioning as a liability in the Central American luxury market. The Trump Organization resisted the removal through litigation. Eventually, the name came off.

In New York, the Trump SoHo — a forty-six storey luxury hotel and condominium in lower Manhattan, developed through a licensing arrangement that had involved considerable legal controversy from its inception — was rebranded as the Dominick Hotel in 2017. The rebranding was announced with a studied avoidance of any reference to the name being removed, in the way that corporate communications tend to avoid acknowledging the obvious when the obvious is embarrassing.

In Toronto, as we examined in Part III, the name came off in 2017 and the building became the St. Regis. In Vancouver, as we examined in Part IV, the name came off in 2021 and the building became Paradox Vancouver.

The pattern across these properties is consistent: licensing agreements that had made commercial sense in the pre-2015 environment became commercial liabilities in the post-2015 environment, and the owners of the underlying assets — the people who had paid for the buildings and the hotels and the condominiums — made the rational calculation that the cost of maintaining the association exceeded the remaining value of the association, and they terminated the arrangement.

Trump fought some of these terminations. He accepted others. The distinguishing variable, as we noted in Part II and Parts III and IV, was the presence or absence of institutional leverage. Where Trump had legal mechanisms to resist — management contracts with enforceable terms, litigation strategies that could delay the inevitable — he used them. Where the commercial logic was simply too unfavourable and the buyout price was acceptable, he took the money.

The name was a commodity. Commodities respond to market forces. The market, post-2015, had spoken.

The international dimension of the brand collapse deserves particular attention, because it reveals something about the name’s value that the domestic story partially obscures.

In the United States, the Trump brand’s post-2015 trajectory was complicated by the political valence of the name — by the fact that for a substantial portion of the American population, the name had become not a liability but a loyalty signal, a marker of tribal affiliation that generated its own commercial value within the specific market of Trump supporters. This is the market that bought the trading cards, the gold sneakers, the $59.99 Bible, the Trump cologne, the MAGA merchandise in all its iterations. The brand lost the broad market and found, within the narrow market of its most committed supporters, a revenue stream that was smaller but in some ways more reliable, because it was not buying a product but expressing an identity.

Internationally, this compensating dynamic was largely absent. In the markets where Trump-branded properties had operated — Panama, Toronto, Vancouver, the Philippines, where a Trump-branded tower in Manila opened in 2017 and operated under the brand for a period before the political complications became commercially unmanageable — the name carried no tribal loyalty premium. It was a pure commercial signal, and the signal had deteriorated.

The Angus Reid poll of 2015 that found fifty-six percent of Canadians wanting the Trump name removed from the Toronto and Vancouver towers was not a poll of political partisans. It was a representative sample of Canadian public opinion, and Canadian public opinion, which had never had a particular investment in the Trump mythology, responded to the escalator speech and its sequels as a straightforward reputational signal: this name now carries associations that make us uncomfortable, and we would prefer not to have it on buildings in our cities.

The market listened. Eventually, the buildings agreed.

There is a broader question embedded in the brand collapse story that this series has been approaching obliquely and should now address directly: what is the relationship between the commercial failure of the Trump brand and the political success of the Trump project?

The answer is that they are, in a precise sense, the same phenomenon viewed from different angles.

The escalator speech that destroyed the commercial brand was the founding act of the political brand. The qualities that made the name toxic to Macy’s and NBC and Univision — the willingness to say what the establishment had decided should not be said, the indifference to the social and commercial consequences of that saying, the projection of a combative identity that divided rather than united — were precisely the qualities that made the name compelling to the political constituency that sent Trump to the White House in 2016 and returned him there in 2024.

The brand did not become toxic. The brand bifurcated. One portion of the market found the new associations repellent. Another portion found them essential. The commercial licensing model, which required a broad market to function, collapsed because it could not survive the bifurcation. The political brand, which required only a plurality rather than a majority, thrived.

This bifurcation explains, better than any other single factor, the transition from the commercial naming project to the monument project that has been the subject of the last several essays in this series. The commercial model was dead. The broad market was gone. The licensing fees from international luxury properties were going with it. What remained was the narrow market — the committed supporters, the tribal loyalists, the buyers of gold sneakers and $59.99 Bibles — and the political office that, once held, provided a new kind of leverage entirely.

The leverage of office is not commercial. It is institutional. The President of the United States cannot license his name to the Kennedy Center in the way that a real estate developer licenses his name to a hotel. But the President of the United States can appoint the board of the Kennedy Center, and the board can vote to rename it, and the letters can go up in a day. The President of the United States cannot sell his brand to the Bureau of Engraving and Printing in the way that a cologne manufacturer sells a fragrance to a department store. But the President of the United States can have his Treasury Secretary hold up a mock-up of an illegal $250 bill at a White House press briefing and describe it as something there is nothing untoward about.

The commercial brand needed buyers. The political brand needs only the office.

And the office, for the moment, is held.

The story of the brand collapse has one more chapter that this essay should record, because it happened in Washington and it happened early and it established, before any of the later and larger naming acts, exactly what the administration’s relationship to the name’s placement on public institutions would be.

In the first weeks of Trump’s second term, workers appeared at the facade of the United States Institute of Peace and added lettering to the building. The new letters spelled out a name.

They had also, by that point, been appearing in other locations around the capital. On signage. On official materials. On the websites of federal agencies. The name was migrating from the commercial world — from the towers and hotels and products that the market had been quietly rejecting for a decade — into the institutional world, where the market has no jurisdiction and the rejection mechanisms are courts rather than consumers.

The commercial brand had lost the broad market. The institutional project was acquiring a new surface area, measured not in square footage of hotel lobby but in square footage of marble federal building, linear feet of arch over a ceremonial bridge, denominations of proposed currency, square inches of national parks pass.

The name that Macy’s had dropped and NBC had cancelled and the Toronto and Vancouver property owners had bought out was being written, with accelerating confidence and diminishing concern for legal constraint, on the institutions of the American republic.

The market had said no.

The republic was still deciding.

Donald Trump has spent a lifetime writing his name on things. Towers. Golf courses. Hotels. A university. A Bible. A perfume called Success. A peace institute he gutted before the paint on the letters dried. A performing arts centre dedicated to a murdered president he has spent his entire career trying to impress.

The name is always the same: gold where possible, large where mandatory, permanent where permitted, and imposed where neither permission nor permanence was ever his to grant.

This series is about what that compulsion reveals — psychologically, politically, historically, and occasionally comically. It is not a flattering portrait.

Then again, neither is a loading dock on Bay Street.
