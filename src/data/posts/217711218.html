There’s a bitter irony sitting at the root of French colonization in Vietnam, and it’s worth stating plainly before telling the rest of the story: the Nguyen dynasty that France would eventually conquer owed its own existence, in part, to French help. Nguyen Anh — the future Emperor Gia Long, whose unification of Vietnam in 1802 closed out the long dynastic history I traced in Part IV — had relied on French Catholic missionary and mercenary assistance during his campaign against the Tay Son rebels. The relationship that assistance created between the Nguyen court and French Catholic interests in Vietnam would, within a few decades of Gia Long’s death, become the pretext for the conquest of the country his French allies had helped him win.

The Missionary Wedge

French Catholic missionary activity in Vietnam predates Gia Long’s reign by well over a century — Jesuit missionaries, most famously Alexandre de Rhodes, had been active in Vietnam since the early seventeenth century, and it was de Rhodes and other missionaries who developed Quốc Ngữ, the Latin-alphabet-based writing system that would eventually replace Chinese characters as Vietnam’s standard script — a linguistic legacy of missionary activity that outlasted French colonialism itself and remains how Vietnamese is written today.

Gia Long, whatever debts he owed to French assistance, was relatively tolerant of continued missionary activity during his reign. His successors were not. Emperor Minh Mang, who ruled from 1820 to 1841, viewed Catholic missionary activity as a genuine threat to Confucian social order and Nguyen dynastic authority, and pursued increasingly severe persecution of Vietnamese Catholics and foreign missionaries alike — executions of missionaries and Vietnamese converts became a recurring feature of Nguyen policy under Minh Mang and his successors, Thieu Tri and Tu Duc.

This persecution gave France — and to a lesser extent Spain, whose missionaries were also active in the region — the pretext it wanted. French colonial and commercial interests had been eyeing Vietnam and the broader Indochinese peninsula for decades, both as a market and as a strategic foothold to compete with British colonial expansion elsewhere in Asia. The persecution of French missionaries provided a humanitarian justification for military intervention that domestic French political opinion could be rallied around, even as the underlying motivations were considerably more commercial and strategic.

It’s worth pausing on the broader regional context that made this kind of pretext-driven intervention so viable by the mid-nineteenth century. Britain had already forced open China through the Opium Wars, established colonial control over India, and was steadily expanding into Burma; the Dutch held the East Indies; Spain held the Philippines. France, having lost most of its earlier North American and Indian colonial holdings, was looking for a comparable foothold in Asia to avoid being left out of what French policymakers and commercial interests increasingly viewed as an inevitable and accelerating carve-up of the region among European powers. Vietnam, with its extensive coastline, its agricultural wealth, and — crucially — its already-established, if currently persecuted, community of French missionaries and Vietnamese Catholic converts providing a ready-made network of local contacts and information, was a natural target. Napoleon III’s Second Empire, eager for prestige projects that could bolster domestic support for his regime, proved considerably more willing to authorize costly overseas military campaigns than the more cautious July Monarchy that preceded it, and the timing of the Da Nang expedition in 1858 reflects this shift in French domestic political appetite as much as it reflects any sudden escalation in missionary persecution itself, which had in fact been ongoing intermittently for decades before France finally acted.

The Conquest

French military intervention began in earnest in 1858, when a combined French and Spanish force attacked Da Nang, ostensibly in response to the execution of Spanish missionaries, beginning a campaign that would unfold in stages over the following three decades. The initial assault on Da Nang stalled, but French forces redirected south and captured Saigon in 1859, establishing a foothold in the Mekong Delta region that would become the core of French Cochinchina.

By 1862, the Nguyen court, under Emperor Tu Duc, was forced to sign the Treaty of Saigon, ceding three provinces in the south to French control outright and granting France other significant concessions, including free practice of Catholicism throughout Vietnam. This was the opening wound.

Over the following two decades, French control expanded steadily — additional provinces were annexed in 1867, extending French Cochinchina across the entire Mekong Delta, and by the 1880s, France had turned its attention to the north, using a series of military campaigns and diplomatic pressure to bring Annam (central Vietnam) and Tonkin (the north) under French protectorate status, formalized through a sequence of treaties in 1883 and 1884 that left the Nguyen emperor as a nominal, ceremonial ruler stripped of any genuine sovereign authority.

By 1887, France had consolidated its holdings in Vietnam alongside its protectorate over Cambodia into a single administrative entity: French Indochina, or Indochine — later expanded to include Laos as well. Vietnam itself was administered as three separate units under this structure — Cochinchina in the south as a direct colony, and Annam and Tonkin as protectorates retaining a fig leaf of Nguyen imperial authority — a tripartite division that would have lasting consequences for how the country experienced and responded to colonial rule, since each region developed a somewhat different relationship to French administration and, later, to the nationalist movements that arose against it.

What Colonial Rule Actually Looked Like

French colonial administration in Indochina followed a pattern familiar from European colonialism elsewhere, adapted to specific Vietnamese conditions. The core economic logic was extraction: Vietnam’s agricultural output, particularly rice from the Mekong and Red River Deltas, along with coal, rubber, and other raw materials, was organized to serve French commercial interests and export markets rather than domestic Vietnamese development. Large plantations, particularly rubber plantations established in the early twentieth century, relied on brutal forced or heavily coerced labour systems that produced staggering human costs — worker mortality rates on some rubber plantations in the interwar period were extraordinarily high, a fact that later Vietnamese nationalist and communist propaganda would invoke, with considerable justification, as evidence of colonialism’s fundamental cruelty.

France did build infrastructure — railways, ports, some administrative and educational institutions — but this infrastructure was overwhelmingly designed to serve extraction and colonial administration rather than genuine Vietnamese development, and access to French-language education, the pathway to any meaningful position within the colonial administrative hierarchy, was tightly restricted, producing a small, Western-educated Vietnamese elite alongside a vast, largely excluded peasant majority.

One of the more consequential and, in retrospect, double-edged decisions of French colonial administration was its promotion of Quốc Ngữ, the Latin-alphabet script that missionaries like Alexandre de Rhodes had developed two centuries earlier, as the standard written language for Vietnamese instruction, in place of the Chinese-character-based chữ Hán and chữ Nôm systems that had been in use for the preceding millennium. French administrators favoured Quốc Ngữ partly because it was easier for French officials themselves to learn and partly because it served to cut Vietnamese elites off from the classical Chinese scholarly tradition that had underpinned centuries of Confucian mandarinate education, weakening one potential source of cultural resistance rooted in shared literary heritage with China. The unintended long-term consequence, though, was to make written Vietnamese dramatically more accessible to a much broader swath of the population than the old character-based systems, which had required years of specialized education to master. By the early twentieth century, this more accessible writing system would become an essential tool for the spread of nationalist and revolutionary political literature — including material produced by Ho Chi Minh’s own movement — in a way French colonial administrators had never intended or anticipated when they first promoted it as a tool of administrative convenience and cultural severance.

Colonial taxation policy compounded the economic extraction I’ve already described. The French administration imposed monopolies on the production and sale of salt, alcohol, and opium — the last of these particularly notorious, since colonial authorities actively cultivated opium consumption among the Vietnamese population as a revenue source, running government-operated opium dens and processing facilities that generated a substantial share of Indochina’s colonial budget throughout the early twentieth century. This was colonialism functioning, quite literally, by profiting from addiction among the colonized population, and it became one of the most frequently cited grievances in later Vietnamese nationalist and communist critiques of French rule, for obvious reasons.

This dynamic — a narrow colonial-educated elite, some of whom would become collaborators and some of whom would become the very leaders of anti-colonial resistance, precisely because their French education gave them exposure to European Enlightenment and revolutionary political thought — is a pattern common across many colonial contexts, and it would prove decisive in Vietnam specifically, because the man who would eventually lead Vietnam’s successful anti-colonial movement, Ho Chi Minh, was a product of exactly this system: colonial-educated, exposed to French political thought, and radicalized by direct experience of the gap between French republican ideals and French colonial practice.

Early Resistance, and Why It Failed

Vietnamese resistance to French rule didn’t wait for Ho Chi Minh. From the earliest years of conquest, a movement known as the Can Vuong (”Aid the King”) sought to rally resistance around the nominal authority of the Nguyen emperor, with limited and ultimately unsuccessful results through the 1880s and 1890s. The movement was launched in the name of the young Emperor Ham Nghi, who fled the French-controlled court in 1885 and issued an edict calling on Vietnamese scholars and loyalists to rise up against French rule — a call that produced scattered guerrilla resistance across parts of Annam and Tonkin, led by regional scholar-officials rather than any unified military command. But the movement lacked the organization, weaponry, and unified leadership to meaningfully challenge French military superiority; it fragmented into localized, uncoordinated bands that French forces were able to isolate and defeat piecemeal over more than a decade, and its final significant leader, Phan Dinh Phung, held out in mountainous terrain in Ha Tinh province until his death in 1895, with remaining resistance collapsing shortly after.

A subsequent generation of nationalist reformers in the early twentieth century took a different approach, having absorbed the lesson that the Can Vuong’s backward-looking appeal to traditional monarchical loyalty had been a strategic dead end against a modern industrial power. Phan Boi Chau, the most significant figure of this generation, looked instead to a rapidly modernizing Japan — which had just stunned the world by defeating Russia in the 1904-05 Russo-Japanese War, becoming the first Asian power in the modern era to defeat a European one in open conflict — as a model for how an Asian nation could modernize quickly enough to resist Western domination. Phan Boi Chau organized a “Go East” movement encouraging young Vietnamese to study in Japan, and briefly attempted to organize Vietnamese resistance with Japanese material and political support. This effort collapsed by 1909, when Japan, seeking closer diplomatic relations with France, expelled Phan Boi Chau and other Vietnamese activists from Japanese territory — an early and instructive lesson, for later Vietnamese revolutionaries, in the unreliability of relying on the goodwill of another Asian power whose own strategic interests might not permanently align with Vietnamese independence. Both movements, in their different ways, failed to produce an organization capable of seriously challenging French colonial control, hampered by French surveillance, limited organizational capacity, and the fundamental military and technological gap between colonial resistance movements and the French colonial state.

What these early movements lacked, and what would eventually distinguish the movement Ho Chi Minh built, was a combination of ideological coherence, organizational discipline modeled on the emerging international communist movement, and — critically — a strategic patience willing to accept decades of gradual organization-building rather than seeking rapid, dramatic confrontation. That’s the story of the next installment in this series: Ho Chi Minh’s years in Paris, his exposure to and adoption of Marxist-Leninist political thought, and the slow, patient construction of the movement that would eventually succeed where Can Vuong and Phan Boi Chau’s efforts had not.

It’s worth closing this chapter by returning to the theme that’s run through this entire series so far. Every previous foreign incursion into Vietnam this series has covered — the thousand years of Chinese rule, three Mongol invasions, twenty years of Ming reoccupation — was eventually met and eventually repelled by tactics rooted in geographic knowledge, patience, and asymmetric resistance against superior numbers. French colonial conquest, arriving by sea with industrial military technology in the mid-nineteenth century, represented something categorically different: a threat the old doctrine, on its own, couldn’t meet. It would take a new kind of movement, built on a new kind of ideological and organizational foundation, to eventually do to France what Ngo Quyen had done to the Southern Han and Tran Hung Dao had done to the Mongols. That movement’s origins begin, appropriately enough, not in Vietnam at all, but in Paris.
