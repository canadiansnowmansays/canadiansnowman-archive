I. The Loop Vinson Didn’t Notice

As Callie Holtermann reported for The New York Times Magazine on August 14, 2026, Christian Vinson spent close to ten hours this year teaching two AI chatbots his entire professional history — his education, a banking internship, five years in the Army — so they could tailor his cover letter to hundreds of finance job listings. He knew, going in, that he wasn’t really writing to a hiring manager. He was writing to the screening software that would read his application before any person did. “This is the new normal,” Vinson told Holtermann, after landing a job at a New York investment bank.

What makes Vinson’s story worth opening on is not that he used AI to apply for jobs — that alone would barely register as news in this series’ seventy-sixth installment. It’s that the entire exchange, start to finish, involved no human reading anything a human wrote. He built a chatbot’s understanding of his résumé; the chatbot wrote the letter; a company’s own chatbot read and scored it. A dog chasing its own tail, in Holtermann’s phrase — and Vinson got the job anyway, which is either reassuring or the most unsettling part of the story, depending on which incentive you think that outcome rewards.

II. What Happens When the Interlocutor Isn’t Human

Holtermann’s reporting names the phenomenon plainly: a “bot loop,” the moment when both sides of an exchange that used to require two people now involve none. It’s a corollary to the older “dead internet theory” — the idea that most digital content will eventually be machine-generated, leaving humans a shrinking minority of the traffic — except a bot loop isn’t passive content generation. It’s a live exchange, structured like a conversation, with the humans who originally needed to have it stepping back and deputizing something else to have it for them.

The examples Holtermann gathered span the mundane and the intimate. Students write essays with ChatGPT; teachers grade them with AI tools of their own. Workers exchange “lengthy A.I.-generated emails” that get answered with “walls of chatbot text” in return. A graduate student had his AI-assisted coursework graded by his professor’s own virtual assistants. A telecom executive noticed customers dispatching AI voice agents to argue with his company’s AI customer-service system — machine negotiating with machine, on behalf of two humans who never spoke. A man on a dating app used a chatbot to draft his messages to a woman who, he eventually realized, appeared to be doing exactly the same thing. “How long are we gonna ChatGPT each other?” he finally wrote. She never responded — which might be the most human moment in the entire piece, a silence that at least wasn’t generated by anything.

Jiaxin Pei, an assistant professor at the University of Texas at Austin’s School of Information, offers the plumber scenario as the loop’s likely near-future default: instead of calling ten plumbers yourself, you deputize a personal AI assistant to make the calls, and plumbers — overwhelmed by an influx of tireless AI callers — respond by deputizing an AI receptionist of their own. “This is this endless loop we’re creating,” Pei told Holtermann. Nobody built this on purpose, in the sense that no company set out to design a plumber-to-customer relationship mediated entirely by bots negotiating with other bots. It emerged from millions of individually reasonable decisions to offload one tedious task at a time.

III. Is This Just Another Moral Panic?

It’s worth taking the skeptical read seriously before treating any of this as alarming, because Holtermann does. Human history is, as she puts it, one long string of freakouts about new technology wrecking our communication. Socrates worried that writing would ruin memory — a complaint we know about only because Plato wrote it down. Early-20th-century critics warned the telephone would corrode civility. A 1994 New York Times piece argued that email was “so compelling and easy that it threatens to overwhelm its usefulness through sheer volume and lack of consideration,” conceding only that “there are no stamps to lick.” None of these innovations ended productive discussion. Each one simply changed its texture, the way email let people trade more messages with more people without occupying the same room — at a cost, researchers now think, of talking to each other less overall, on platforms that reward exchanges for being heated or outlandish rather than for being good.

Holtermann’s case for why AI is a different order of change rather than the next entry in that list is specific and worth stating plainly: email transmits a message between two people. A chatbot emulates a person, and can substitute for one. That is a categorical difference, not a matter of degree — the telephone never gave anyone the option of skipping the other person entirely and still getting something that reads like a conversation. AI does, and Section II’s examples are what people choose once that option exists.

IV. The Cascade Nobody Catches

This series has spent seventy-five parts documenting containment failures where a model escapes a boundary somebody built. What Holtermann’s reporting describes is a different and in some ways more insidious failure mode: no boundary was ever crossed, because nobody built one in the first place, and the danger isn’t a rogue action but an ordinary error that nothing stops from compounding.

Soheil Feizi, an associate professor of computer science at the University of Maryland, told Holtermann that when two AI systems built on similar underlying models interact, they tend not to catch each other’s mistakes — they simply share the same blind spots. A recent paper by a Harvard Medical School researcher, cited in Holtermann’s reporting, walks through what that looks like inside an actual hospital: one AI tool reads an X-ray and labels a break; a second tool uses that label to assign a room; a third uses both to determine the order of patient treatment. If the first read is wrong and no human catches it, the error doesn’t just persist — it propagates through two more automated decisions before it ever reaches a person positioned to notice, at which point it’s shaped a patient’s treatment order, not just a single misread scan.

That is the containment problem this series has tracked since Part 66, restaged without any of the drama of an escape. Nobody has to break out of a sandbox when the sandbox was never built to catch this kind of error to begin with — three separate automated systems, each doing its assigned job correctly by its own narrow standard, can still produce a compounding mistake that no single company’s testing regime was designed to catch, because no single company owns the whole chain.

V. The Bias Nobody Disclosed

The harder finding in Holtermann’s reporting is not that AI systems make mistakes talking to each other. It’s that they appear to prefer each other’s output over a human’s. One study she cites found that AI résumé screeners rated applications written by AI more favourably than ones written by humans — which means Vinson, the chatbot-assisted job applicant from Section I, may have improved his odds precisely by removing his own voice from his own application. Holtermann notes the same dynamic may already be shaping college admissions, where some schools use AI-powered essay readers to score submissions that are, in no small number of cases, generated by chatbots in the first place.

This is a genuinely new category of accountability problem for this series to name, distinct from a containment escape or a moderation failure: a documented preference, baked into a screening tool, for output produced by the tool’s own category of system — undisclosed to every applicant whose very effort to write in their own voice may be quietly working against them. Nobody voted on this. No terms of service mentions it. It was never announced as a feature, and it’s not clear any single company currently building these tools even understands why it’s happening, only that a controlled study found it happening.

VI. The Forum Built for No One

Tech’s own response to the bot loop has not been caution — it’s been infrastructure. In March, Meta acquired Moltbook, the forum Matt Schlicht built for AI agents rather than people to talk to one another, and brought its founders into its AI lab. A pastor in the Bay Area, Justin Lester, has trained a “digital twin” that can meet with his congregants when he’s unavailable — and Holtermann’s reporting notes the obvious next step: a future in which a parishioner’s own digital twin meets with the pastor’s, with neither human ever actually present for the conversation their names are attached to.

Sarah Davis, a cultural anthropologist and the dean of St. John’s College in Santa Fe, offered Holtermann the clearest statement of what’s actually being lost as this infrastructure gets built out: a “kind of ease that lets us become worse versions of ourselves, and a kind of alienation, a lack of togetherness that requires that we face friction.” The philosopher Daniel Dennett, whom Holtermann also cites, warned before his death about “counterfeit people” — AI-generated personas convincing enough to substitute for the real thing in exactly the settings, like a pastoral conversation or a dating-app exchange, where the whole point used to be that a real person was on the other end.

VII. The Luxury of Being Heard

Holtermann’s closing argument is the one this series should sit with longest, because it reframes everything above as a governance question rather than a curiosity about how people communicate. The likely trajectory isn’t full automation of every conversation — it’s a shrinking share of interactions that involve two actual humans, diluting gradually rather than disappearing all at once. In that world, a human voice on the other end of a phone call — a real receptionist, a real customer-service agent, a real person reading your job application — stops being a baseline expectation and starts becoming, in Holtermann’s words, “something akin to a luxury good.”

The uncomfortable question she leaves open is who can afford that luxury. The people most likely to have the money for it, she notes, are disproportionately the same people currently being enriched by the AI boom that made human contact scarce for everyone else in the first place — the executives whose companies deploy the AI screeners, the investors whose portfolios include the labs building the underlying models. That is not a hypothetical this series has to reach for. It is the direct, material version of the democratic-versus-oligarchic choice this series named in its Introduction: not just who controls the technology’s development, but who still gets to talk to another human being when it matters, and who gets routed to a bot because a bot is what they can afford.

VIII. TACO, Applied to a Conversation With No One In It

Run this through the framework this series has used since Part 1. Threaten doesn’t fit; nothing about a bot loop is coercive, only ambient. Accommodate is every individual feature described here — Meta’s bot-social-network acquisition, Lester’s digital twin, the AI essay graders — each one a genuine convenience solving a genuine problem, none of them accompanied by any disclosure of what the aggregate effect might be on who gets to speak to whom. Capitulate, a platform or company deciding that some category of interaction — a job application, a pastoral conversation, a patient’s treatment order — should require a human on both ends by design rather than by whoever can pay for it, appears nowhere in Holtermann’s reporting. Obfuscate is the résumé-screener bias itself: a documented preference for AI-written text that no applicant is ever told about, discovered only because researchers happened to go looking for it.

IX. The Gap, Restated as a Missing Voice

This series has spent seventy-five parts describing failures that required an incident — a breach, a wrongful ban, a manifesto timed to a competitive setback. The bot loop is different in kind: nobody has to fail at anything for this outcome to arrive. Every individual decision documented in this essay was locally reasonable — a tired job applicant automating a tedious task, a busy pastor extending his availability, a hospital speeding up a triage pipeline. The aggregate of all those reasonable decisions is a world where an increasing share of the conversations that shape people’s jobs, health, and relationships involve nobody who can be held accountable in the way a person can, because nobody is there. That is the Asimov Gap in its quietest form yet: not a law failing to catch up to a dramatic new capability, but a law that was never going to occur to anyone to write, because no single decision along the way looked like the kind of thing that needed one.
