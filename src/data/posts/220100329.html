There is a question that every negotiator learns to ask in the first week on the job, and it is the simplest one in the trade: what do we get?

On Friday, October 9, 2026, at a golf club outside Miami, a member of Ukraine’s negotiating team asked it. The answer, by the account of three people familiar with the meeting, as reported by Anton Troianovski, Jeanna Smialek, Andrew E. Kramer and Steven Erlanger in the New York Times on October 12, 2026, was that the United States was already doing a great deal for Ukraine and should not have to offer a trade. Then the Americans mentioned the intelligence that Washington shares with Kyiv.

Nobody in the room needed that explained either.

Part I of this series looked at the economic war from Kyiv’s end: ports down 95 per cent, a steel industry gone dark, thirty million tonnes of harvest stuck in silos, and one effective lever, the drone campaign against Russian refineries, that Putin himself had admitted was costing Russia about 1 per cent of its economy. This essay is about the weekend Washington asked Ukraine to let go of that lever, and what happened when Ukraine said it would, provided Russia let go of something too.

The room at Shell Bay

Shell Bay is Steve Witkoff’s club. It is an odd place to decide the fate of a country at war, but it is an accurate one. In this administration, diplomacy happens where the President’s friends play golf.

The meeting was meant to be routine, as these things go. Ukraine’s three-person negotiating team had flown in. So had the national security advisers to the leaders of Britain, Germany and France, and senior officials from the European Union and NATO. The plan, according to the Times, was to work through updated proposals for ending the war before Witkoff and Jared Kushner made another trip to Moscow. For much of the six hours, that is what happened: brainstorming about what future talks would need, EU accession for Ukraine, the hard question of territory.

At one point Witkoff stepped out. He was joining a telephone call between Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin.

When he came back, the agenda changed. The United States, the Americans announced, would suspend sanctions on Russian diesel. And the President had a firm request: Ukraine was to stop attacking Russian oil refineries.

The Europeans were stunned. Björn Seibert, a senior EU official who was in the room, briefed ambassadors from all 27 member states on the Sunday afterwards, and the Times reported that the ambassadors were stunned in their turn. Seibert’s most pointed takeaway was not about diesel at all. It was that the Americans were engaging with the Russians more than they publicly let on.

That is the image I keep coming back to. A table of allies, assembled at American invitation to talk about peace, while one of the hosts slips out to take a call from the other side, and returns with the terms.

The deal itself

The call itself had been billed as a belated birthday greeting; Putin had turned 74 on October 7. Some in the White House advised against making it, as Jonathan Lemire and Simon Shuster reported for The Atlantic on October 12, 2026. It ran about ninety minutes, and in the course of it Trump told Putin he would lift the sanctions on Russian diesel. Some in Kyiv suspect the deal was settled even earlier, because a ship carrying Russian oil and gas was already approaching the United States.

The details arrived that evening on Truth Social. Trump wrote that he had just finished a very successful conversation with Putin, and that Russia would immediately supply more than 300,000 tonnes of diesel to American and world markets, with another 500,000 tonnes in November, 1,000,000 tonnes after that, and, depending on the state of Russia’s refineries, 3,000,000 tonnes within a short time.

The phrase depending on the state of Russia’s refineries deserves a moment. The reason those refineries are in a poor state is that Ukraine has been hitting them. The deal and the demand were one package. Russia would sell diesel it could only produce if Ukraine stopped destroying the means of producing it.

The Treasury moved quickly. As LIGA.net reported, the Office of Foreign Assets Control issued a licence permitting the sale, delivery and import of Russian diesel through April 7, 2027. That date matters. The midterm elections are on November 3, 2026. A licence running nearly six months past them is not an emergency measure for the campaign. It is a policy.

It also cuts against the law. In September 2026, Trump himself signed a tougher Russia-sanctions bill, named in honour of one of its champions, the late Senator Lindsey Graham. The diesel licence undercuts it, along with sanctions in place since 2022.

And what does it buy American farmers and truckers, in whose name all this was done? David Frum, writing in The Atlantic on October 10, 2026, ran the arithmetic. The United States refines about 5.2 million barrels of diesel a day and consumes about 3.7 million; it is one of the largest diesel exporters on earth. The first Russian tranche works out to roughly 2.25 million barrels, about sixteen hours of American consumption. Even the full promised volume, if it ever arrives, would barely register in a global market where prices are set worldwide.

Diesel prices really are high. They have climbed about 70 per cent since the Iran war began, according to Romania’s Spotmedia. But the Times, citing export statistics, put the cause plainly: the rise stems largely from the American war with Iran, with Ukraine’s refinery strikes playing a secondary role. The President’s own explanation, posted on Sunday, October 11, 2026, ran the other way: the world diesel price rise, he wrote, was mostly caused by the Russia–Ukraine war, not Iran.

I spent a career in a complaints office. One of the first things you learn there is that when a company’s own billing system causes a problem, the most tempting move is to find a customer to blame for it.

It did not come out of nowhere

It would be comforting to treat Shell Bay as a sudden lurch. It wasn’t.

On Sunday, September 13, 2026, nearly a month earlier, Trump was on the sidelines of the Irish Open at his own golf club in Doonbeg, County Clare, when he told reporters that Zelensky “has to stop knocking out diesel fuel in Russia.” The Associated Press reported the remarks the same day. The shortage, he said then too, wasn’t the Middle East’s doing. That same night, Ukrainian drones hit a major refinery in Slavyansk-on-Kuban, near Crimea. Kyiv heard the request. It also had a winter coming.

So Shell Bay was the second ask, made the second time on a golf course, and this time carrying a price for refusal.

The weekend that followed

Here is how the next three days unfolded, as best they can be pieced together.

Friday, October 9. Zelensky answered within hours. Russia, he wrote, would “repay” the diesel with further terror and perfidy. Easing sanctions without a lasting de-escalation, he said, was an obvious weakness that would let Russia kill more people and prolong the war. In a phone interview with Axios the same day, reported by Asianet Newsable, he called the decision “not fair and not honest.” And he made a counter-offer: Ukraine would stop striking Russian refineries if Russia stopped striking Ukraine’s energy system.

That counter-offer is the hinge of this whole story. Ukraine did not refuse. It asked for reciprocity.

The White House saw it differently. A White House official told Lemire and Shuster that Trump does not regard the two sets of strikes as equivalent, and demanded that Ukraine stop first. Zelensky refused. “Zelensky is overplaying his hand and pissing the president off,” the official said.

Saturday, October 10. Trump told reporters that Zelensky had better “damn well stop,” that he wanted to make problems for American farmers and ranchers. He did not mention the Russian strikes on Ukraine’s power grid. Then he said Ukraine needed “a new president.”

Pause on that. The elected leader of a democracy under invasion asks, reasonably, for a mutual halt to energy strikes. The response from the leader of the free world, as the phrase used to go, is that the country should replace him. Applebaum, writing in The Atlantic on October 12, 2026, noted that on the same Friday, Trump had held another long and friendly conversation with Putin, the one leader who could end the war tomorrow simply by telling his soldiers to stop.

Sunday, October 11. It rained in Washington, the remnants of Hurricane Isaias washing over the East Coast. Unable to golf, the President spent part of the morning on social media, by Lemire and Shuster’s account, touting the size of his rallies and defending Christopher Columbus. He also announced on social media that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to an “ENERGY CEASEFIRE,” effective immediately. Neither government confirmed it. Zelensky said Ukraine had put forward a framework through its partners but that no final deal existed, according to WION. In Moscow, Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said any call on Kyiv to stop attacks on civilian economic infrastructure could only be welcomed. He offered no equivalent pledge from Russia.

A person familiar with Trump’s thinking told the Times that the President meant a ceasefire on refineries in both countries. There is a problem with that. Ukraine’s own refining capacity was largely destroyed early in the war. A refinery-for-refinery truce protects Russia’s refineries and Ukraine’s rubble. It leaves untouched everything Russia actually hits in Ukraine: power plants, substations, ports, steel mills, rail lines and the grid that will keep Ukrainians alive in January.

That night, within hours of the announcement, Russia struck Ukrainian energy infrastructure. Applebaum reported that a power plant in Kharkiv was hit.

The same Sunday, a bipartisan group of senators, including four Republicans, Lisa Murkowski of Alaska, Susan Collins of Maine, Thom Tillis of North Carolina and John Curtis of Utah, issued a statement saying that lifting the sanctions ran directly counter to the Graham law and that the United States should not throw Putin a financial lifeline while Russia keeps killing Ukrainian civilians. They called on the administration to reverse course immediately.

Monday, October 12. Zelensky said Ukraine had held off on refinery strikes overnight. Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha, arriving at a meeting of EU foreign ministers in Luxembourg, told reporters that Ukraine was ready for an energy ceasefire in any format that was mutual, anything that was not one-sided, and pointed out that Russia had attacked Ukraine’s energy infrastructure just hours after Trump’s announcement. By then, the Times reported, Witkoff and Kushner had signalled that their trip to Moscow was on hold until Zelensky made peace with Trump.

Read that last line again. The peace process between Russia and Ukraine was suspended until Ukraine apologised to the United States.

We have seen this film

Ukrainians have seen this exact sequence before, and they remember it better than Washington does.

In March 2025, after telephone calls between Trump and Putin, the United States announced a partial ceasefire on strikes against energy infrastructure, followed by technical talks in Riyadh. Moscow declared that its side of the truce had been in force since March 18. On April 1, 2025, a Russian strike on a power facility in Kherson left about 45,000 residents without electricity, as the Kyiv Independent reported. Sybiha said Ukraine had also recorded attacks on energy facilities in the Kharkiv and Poltava regions. The ceasefire quietly died, and the drones resumed.

The pattern is the same every time. An American announcement. A Russian welcome. A Ukrainian request for terms in writing that apply to both sides. Then Russian strikes, within days or hours, and an American president who blames Kyiv.

That is why Sybiha’s insistence on a package, energy and ports and the Black Sea together, is not stubbornness. It is the memory of March 2025, turned into negotiating terms.

What was actually asked

Strip away the golf club, the Truth Social capitals and the language of energy prices, and here is what the United States asked of Ukraine over that weekend.

Stop the one campaign that is actually costing Russia money. Get nothing in return. Watch your ports, factories and power plants keep burning. Accept that the sanctions you were promised would hold have been lifted on a phone call. And understand that the intelligence your soldiers depend on, which an American official insisted to the Times was never threatened, comes from a President so angry, by that same official’s account, that it was hard to say what he might do. A senior European official told The Atlantic that Washington had also threatened to stop selling weapons to Ukraine through Europe if the strikes continued, and some close to Trump have floated cutting off intelligence, as he briefly did after the Oval Office argument in 2025.

When Ukraine asked for the most basic thing in any negotiation, that the other side give something too, the answer was that Ukraine needed a new president.

There is a word for an arrangement in which one side gives everything, gets nothing, and is threatened for asking. It is not a ceasefire, and it is not a deal. It is a demand for surrender, issued on behalf of the country that started the war.

The question that follows is why. Diesel prices alone do not explain a licence that runs to April 2027, or envoys who will not go to Moscow until Kyiv says sorry. The answer, or at least a large part of it, lies in what else was being discussed in Moscow on September 5, 2026, when Witkoff and Kushner sat down with Putin, and who stands to profit. That is the subject of Part III.
