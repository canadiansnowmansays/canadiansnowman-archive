On Tuesday, October 13, 2026, Senator Elissa Slotkin of Michigan sat down with Vox’s Astead Herndon at the University of Chicago and said something unremarkable. If Democrats take Congress, she argued, they should hold the administration to account for its corruption, but selectively. Pick “signature cases,” she said. Go after the people and projects that are “super egregious.” Don’t make it the party’s whole personality. And if a few ambitious graduates think twice about signing on with the Trump administration in its final two years, so much the better.

Nobody noticed for five days. Then, on Sunday, October 18, the noticing began all at once, the way a flock of starlings turns, without any visible bird giving the order.

Senator Mike Lee of Utah called it disgraceful to threaten young Americans for serving their country. Ari Fleischer, George W. Bush’s old press secretary, pronounced the thinking terrible for the nation. Mike Davis, the Trump legal adviser, wrote it up for Fox News; Miranda Devine wrote it up for the New York Post. By Monday, October 19, The Federalist had promoted a congressional-oversight remark into a party platform for “throwing conservatives in prison.” And the President, asked about it outside the White House, called Slotkin a lowlife who was ruining her state, then summarised her remarks as a call for prosecutions to scare people out of politics.

She hadn’t mentioned prosecutions. She was describing a Democratic Congress facing a Republican White House, a situation in which Democrats would have subpoenas, hearings and television cameras, and no prosecutors at all.

The cleanest account of what happened next is Jonathan Chait’s, published in The Atlantic on October 21, 2026, under the headline “This Is Not a Cycle of Retribution.” Chait walks through the outrage, the omitted context, and the fallback argument that arrives once the first line of defence fails. That fallback argument is what this essay is about, because it is the one argument aimed directly at the premise of this series.

The Senator Who Was Already on the List

Before getting to the argument, one fact the outrage chorus left out, and which Chait’s piece doesn’t dwell on either.

Elissa Slotkin is not a hypothetical future prosecutor of Republicans. She is a current subject of a Republican administration’s investigation. On January 14, 2026, as Joey Cappelletti of the Associated Press reported for PBS NewsHour, Slotkin disclosed that the office of U.S. Attorney Jeanine Pirro had notified her she was under investigation. Her offence was organising a 90-second video in which she and other Democratic lawmakers, several of them veterans, reminded service members that they are obliged to refuse illegal orders. That is not a fringe legal theory. It is roughly what every officer is taught at commissioning. The President called the video seditious and noted on social media that sedition is punishable by death.

Slotkin is one of the six Democratic lawmakers who have sat on this series’ scorecard since the beginning. So the week’s spectacle comes down to this: a senator under federal criminal investigation for reciting the Uniform Code of Military Justice was denounced, by the people who opened that investigation, for threatening their side with legal consequences.

Stephen Leacock once wrote of a man who flung himself upon his horse and rode madly off in all directions. The modern Republican response to accountability is more disciplined than that. It rides off in exactly one direction, every time, and calls it self-defence.

The Argument That Sounds Like Wisdom

The first line of defence was that Slotkin was menacing innocent young people. When that didn’t survive contact with the transcript, the second line arrived, and it is a better argument, which is why it deserves a proper answer.

The Wall Street Journal’s editorial board accused Slotkin of essentially promising scorched-earth lawfare that would invite reprisals. In the board’s telling, the Biden-era prosecutions of Donald Trump started this; Trump is now retaliating in kind; Slotkin proposes yet another turn of the wheel; and down that road lies only bitterness. Jack Goldsmith, a senior Justice Department lawyer in the Bush administration, made a similar case on his Substack.

The appeal is obvious. It sounds even-handed. It casts the speaker as the adult in the room, above the brawl, warning both sides to put down the chairs. Canadians, who are constitutionally inclined to believe that most disputes are six of one and half a dozen of the other, find it especially soothing.

The trouble is that a cycle has a structural requirement. Both sides have to be doing the same thing. If one side is prosecuting crimes and the other is prosecuting critics, that isn’t a cycle. It’s a crime wave with a public relations department.

There is also a small matter of the Journal’s own record. In late November 2025, after a federal judge threw out the indictments of James Comey and Letitia James, the same editorial board ran a piece mocking the administration as “The Gang That Couldn’t Indict Straight,” arguing that Trump’s lawfare revenge tour had gone bust because officials cut corners in their rush for retribution, as Yahoo News summarised it at the time. The board knew, eleven months ago, that one side’s prosecutions were collapsing because they were built on nothing. It now proposes that the other side’s oversight would be more of the same.

Running the Test

Fortunately, this series doesn’t have to settle the question by temperament. In June, in Part XXVI (”The One That Was Real”), the John Bolton guilty plea gave us a working test for telling a real prosecution from a manufactured one. A real case predates the grievance. It is built by career prosecutors, often across more than one administration. It rests on the defendant’s own words and conduct. And it tends to end in a plea or a verdict. A manufactured case postdates the grievance, runs through handpicked prosecutors and favourable venues, and collapses on contact with a grand jury or a judge.

The “cycle” argument invites us to apply that test to both sides. Very well.

Jack Smith’s classified documents case. The matter began with the National Archives trying, for most of 2021, to recover records Trump had taken to Mar-a-Lago, and with a referral to the Justice Department in early 2022. Career national security prosecutors were working it months before Smith was appointed in November 2022. The evidence rested heavily on Trump’s own conduct: the subpoena he didn’t honour, the boxes moved after it arrived, and his own recorded voice describing a document he acknowledged he hadn’t declassified. It never reached a verdict because Judge Aileen Cannon dismissed it in July 2024 and the election ended it. Three of four criteria, and the fourth was lost to a judge and a calendar, not to a collapse of evidence.

Alvin Bragg’s hush-money case. Here the honest answer is less comfortable, and the series should say so. Chait himself calls it questionable. Bragg’s theory, turning a falsified-records misdemeanour into a felony by linking it to a federal campaign-finance violation, was a stretch that many lawyers who loathe Trump still describe as a reach. But even here the test cuts in an interesting direction. A Manhattan grand jury indicted, a jury Trump’s own lawyers helped select convicted on all 34 counts, and the investigation predated Trump’s 2022 campaign announcement. Joe Biden didn’t direct it, couldn’t have directed it, and a local district attorney is not the President’s to command. A weak case brought by an elected local prosecutor is a weak case. It is not a presidential vendetta.

The other side of the ledger. Comey and James: indictments brought by an interim U.S. attorney installed in the Eastern District of Virginia after her predecessor declined to bring them, both dismissed in November 2025 on the ground that she had been unlawfully appointed, followed by failed attempts to indict James again. Lisa Cook: a mortgage-fraud referral that arrived only after Trump decided he wanted her seat on the Federal Reserve, followed, when the Supreme Court kept her in place, by a White House “committee of inquiry” (Part XXXI). Ilhan Omar: a home search 22 days before the midterms (Part XXXII). Judge Juan Merchan, who presided over the Bragg trial: a year-long grand jury inquiry into his daughter’s firm, narrowed eventually to a single $468 payment (Part XXVIII). Elissa Slotkin: an investigation for reminding soldiers of the law.

Every one of those postdates the grievance. Every one runs through loyalists. Several have already collapsed in front of judges. If the Biden-era cases and the Trump-era cases belong to the same cycle, it is a strange cycle in which one wheel is a wheel and the other is a drawing of a wheel on a napkin.

There is one Biden-era decision the “cycle” crowd can fairly hold up, and it isn’t a prosecution. It’s a pardon. In December 2024, after promising not to, Biden pardoned his son Hunter. That was a genuine abuse of the norm, and Democrats who want to campaign on accountability should own it rather than shuffle past it. But note what it was: a president shielding a family member from a case his own Justice Department had won. It was not a president directing prosecutors at his enemies. And the case Hunter Biden was pardoned out of brings us to our next witness.

The Prosecutor From the Other Side’s Case

Two days before Chait’s piece, on October 19, 2026, The Atlantic published an essay by Leo J. Wise titled “How Corruption Got This Bad.” Wise is worth listening to precisely because he is the last person the “cycle” theory can cast as a partisan. He spent twenty years prosecuting white-collar crime at the Justice Department. His first criminal trial was Enron. His last was Hunter Biden. He prosecuted Baltimore’s mayor, its police commissioner and its state’s attorney, and a vice chairman of the Joint Chiefs. He finished in the Public Integrity Section, the unit that handled corruption cases against officials nationwide, until it was effectively shut down in February 2025. Asked to help dismiss the corruption case against New York Mayor Eric Adams, he declined, as did nearly all his colleagues, and left.

A Biden-era prosecutor who convicted Joe Biden’s son is not part of anyone’s retribution machine.

Wise’s essay is not about Slotkin, but it supplies the half of the picture that the “cycle” argument never mentions. While this administration has been inventing crimes against its critics, the Supreme Court has spent nearly four decades making real corruption very hard to charge. Wise walks through it case by case. In McNally (1987) the Court gutted honest-services fraud until Congress restored it. In Sun-Diamond (1999) it held that lavish gifts to a cabinet secretary because of his office weren’t illegal. In Skilling (2010), a case Wise tried, it narrowed honest-services fraud to bribes and kickbacks. In McDonnell (2016) it decided that a governor arranging meetings and events for a benefactor’s company hadn’t performed “official acts.” In Snyder (2024) it ruled that a mayor accepting $13,000 from a company that won $1 million in city contracts had received a gratuity, which the statute didn’t cover. Several of those decisions were unanimous, which is Wise’s point. This isn’t left against right. It is, in his phrase, political elites protecting political elites.

And then Trump v. United States (2024), which put the President, alone among elected officials, largely beyond prosecution for corruption at all, and did it on constitutional grounds that Congress cannot simply legislate away.

Put Wise beside Chait and the shape of the thing becomes clear. Chait’s sharpest line is that Trump and his allies will keep investigating fake crimes and committing real ones, because Trump doesn’t believe in the rule of law. Wise explains why the second half of that sentence carries so little risk. The Qatari jet. The Emirati $500 million into the family crypto venture, followed months later by approval of advanced AI chip sales the previous administration had blocked. Wise asks the question every reader asks, “Isn’t that illegal?”, and answers, with the weariness of a man who used to turn cases away, that it very likely isn’t anymore.

Anne Applebaum’s October cover story for The Atlantic, “MAGA Is Making the World Safe for Corruption,” adds the scale: citing the New York Times, she reports that Trump earned $2.2 billion in the first year of his second term, much of it from cryptocurrency. That is what the Journal’s editorial board is asking a Democratic Congress not to look at too closely, lest it provoke reprisals.

Why Oversight Isn’t the Same Thing

This is where the “cycle” argument does its quietest damage. It treats every form of accountability as interchangeable with every other, so that a Senate subpoena and a midnight raid on a congresswoman’s home become two moves in the same game.

They aren’t. What Slotkin described was congressional oversight: hearings, document requests, testimony, public records. It is slow, it is noisy, and its ultimate sanction is embarrassment. Wise’s prescription is milder still. He wants Congress to rewrite the statutes the Court narrowed, to make gifts to officials because of their positions illegal again, to treat undisclosed conflicts as honest-services fraud, and to bar companies that invest in a president’s businesses from bidding on federal contracts. None of that puts anyone in prison. All of it would apply to Democrats as fully as to Republicans.

That’s the tell. Real accountability writes rules that bind whoever holds power next. Retribution writes rules that bind only whoever holds power now. A Democratic majority that passed Wise’s reforms would be handing a future Republican Congress the same tools. A Republican administration that indicts critics through unlawfully appointed prosecutors is handing its successors nothing except a precedent it assumes will never be turned around.

The Outrage as Campaign Material

Why the five-day delay, then? Why does a remark that drew no notice on October 13 become a national emergency on October 18, sixteen days before an election?

Mike Davis answered the question himself, in a post on X addressed to the senator, telling her she was rallying fractured Republicans right before the midterms. That is the most candid sentence anyone produced all week. The outrage isn’t a defence of young civil servants. It is turnout. The party that has spent two years filling this series’ scorecard needs its voters to believe they are the ones about to be hunted, and a measured remark about signature cases, stripped of its first half, will do nicely.

Chait closes by observing that Trump has two modes, flaunting his impunity and wallowing in victimhood, and that for now the second is more useful. This series has been documenting the first mode for thirty-three parts. Part XXXIV is a note that the second has arrived, right on schedule.

The cycle-of-retribution argument asks voters to imagine two wheels turning against each other. The record shows one wheel, turning steadily since January 2025, and one senator standing beside it, already caught in its spokes, suggesting that somebody might eventually check the brakes.
