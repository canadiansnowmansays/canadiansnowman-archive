By Stephen Gordon — CanadianSnowmanSays

June 1,thousands of Talarico supporters filled the room at an event hall in Plano in Collin County, Ken Paxton's home turf.

On the night of May 26, Ken Paxton stood before a crowd in Plano and declared: “Tonight we just sent a Texas-sized message to Washington.” He was right, though not in the way he intended. The message Texas sent was this: a four-term senator who voted with Donald Trump 99% of the time was not loyal enough; a scandal-plagued attorney general who was impeached by his own party, indicted for securities fraud, and divorced on biblical grounds was just right; and the Democrats, for the first time in a generation, might actually have a shot.

Paxton won the Republican runoff 64-36. It wasn’t close. Within hours, both the Cook Political Report and Larry Sabato’s Crystal Ball shifted the Texas Senate race from “Likely Republican” to “Leans Republican.” National Senate Republicans, who had spent months warning that Paxton could cost them the seat, lined up to embrace him anyway.

The general election is now set. Ken Paxton versus James Talarico. The attorney general versus the seminarian. The man Trump endorsed because loyalty matters more than electability, against the man Joe Rogan told to run for president because “we need someone who is actually a good person.”

This is the race that could decide control of the United States Senate. And to understand it, you need to know three people — not two.

The Man They Discarded

John Cornyn is a San Antonio native whose father flew B-17s in the Mighty Eighth Air Force during World War II. He studied journalism at Trinity University, earned a law degree from St. Mary’s, and — while already sitting on the bench — went back to school for a Master of Laws from the University of Virginia. He was elected a district judge in Bexar County at 32, won a seat on the Texas Supreme Court at 38, and in 1997 became the first Republican elected Texas Attorney General since Reconstruction.

He served in the United States Senate for twenty-three years. In that time, he was the primary sponsor of 119 bills signed into law. He authored the Justice for Victims of Trafficking Act, which passed the Senate unanimously. He championed the OPEN Government Act, the most significant reform of the Freedom of Information Act in a decade. He helped lead passage of the First Step Act, the landmark bipartisan criminal justice reform. He co-authored the bipartisan gun safety bill after the Uvalde massacre — at a school in his own state, twenty-one people dead, most of them children. He served as Republican Whip, the second-highest ranking position in the Senate conference, and chaired the National Republican Senatorial Committee through two election cycles. He sat on Finance, Judiciary, Intelligence, Foreign Relations, and Budget.

He was never indicted. Never impeached. Never had his divorce records unsealed by court order. He voted with Donald Trump more than 99% of the time.

None of it was enough. In 2023, Cornyn made the mistake of saying what everyone in his party knew to be true: “I don’t think President Trump understands that when you run in a general election, you have to appeal to voters beyond your base.” He also supported the bipartisan gun safety bill and acknowledged Biden’s victory in 2020. For those offenses — candour, decency, the exercise of independent judgment on behalf of twenty-one dead children — he was destroyed.

“There comes a time when you have to realize that despite your best efforts, you’ve been unsuccessful,” Cornyn said on election night. He did not mention Paxton’s name.

It was the quietest exit in modern Texas political history. And the most damning.

Ken Paxton and John Cornyn: the rap sheet and the résumé. Texas Republicans chose the one on the left.

The Trump Candidate

If you were designing a Senate candidate to maximize Democratic general election chances, you could hardly do better than Ken Paxton.

Start with the impeachment. In 2023, the Texas House — controlled by Paxton’s own party — impeached him for corruption and bribery related to his relationship with Austin real estate developer Nate Paul. The allegations included using the powers of the attorney general’s office to investigate Paul’s enemies, delay foreclosures on Paul’s properties, and interfere with police investigations. In return, according to impeachment testimony, Paul paid for Paxton’s home renovations and — in a detail that even a satirist would hesitate to invent — hired Paxton’s girlfriend so the two could meet in secret in Austin.

The Republican-controlled Senate acquitted him. Only two Republican senators voted to convict. Angela Paxton, a state senator and Ken’s wife of 38 years, attended the trial but was barred by Senate rules from voting or participating in deliberations. She sat and watched.

Two years later, in July 2025, Angela Paxton filed for divorce. “Today, after 38 years of marriage, I filed for divorce on biblical grounds,” she wrote on social media. The divorce records were initially sealed — unsealed only in December 2025 after media organizations sued, producing roughly 300 pages of documents revealing finances, property disputes, and the details of what “biblical grounds” meant.

Then there’s the securities fraud. Paxton was indicted in 2015 — his first year as attorney general — on felony charges of defrauding investors in a Dallas-area tech startup. The case dragged on for nine years before prosecutors agreed to drop the charges after a settlement. A separate federal investigation into bribery and corruption, prompted by whistleblower complaints from Paxton’s own former aides, was also eventually dropped. A State Bar of Texas attempt to sanction him over his effort to overturn the 2020 presidential election was overcome.

Trump endorsed him one week before the runoff, calling him “a true MAGA Warrior.” The endorsement was not really about Texas. Steve Bannon said the quiet part at full volume: “This is as much a vote of no confidence in John Thune as it is a vote of confidence in Ken Paxton.” Trump was furious that Senate Republicans had resisted eliminating the filibuster, refused to fire the parliamentarian, and called his $1.776 billion “anti-weaponization fund” a bad idea. So he punished them. A disciplinary action wrapped in populist language. A loyalty purge dressed up as a democratic mandate.

On election night, Paxton turned to the general: “My opponent is the most extreme radical the Democrats have ever nominated.”

He was talking about a seminarian.

The Seminarian

Talarico for Senate: Texas deserves better than crooked Ken Paxton

Here’s what the New York Times came to Austin to figure out: how does a Presbyterian seminary student with a progressive pastor and a campaign slogan about flipping tables become the Democratic nominee for United States Senate in Texas?

The answer starts with a grandfather. Jimmy Causey was the pastor of three Baptist churches in South Texas in the late 1960s. He found himself on the moderate side during what critics call the “fundamentalist takeover” of the Southern Baptist Convention. “The conservatives believed that the Bible was dictated by God to the writers of the Bible and that every word of it was to be taken as the literal word of God,” Causey, now 86, told the Times recently. “I just never could swallow that line.”

James Talarico was born in Round Rock, Texas. His mother left his biological father, an abusive alcoholic, when James was seven weeks old. She married Mark Talarico, who adopted him. Talarico graduated from the University of Texas, earned a Master of Education from Harvard, and became a middle school English teacher in San Antonio. In 2018, at 28, he ran for the Texas House. During the campaign, he walked 25 miles across the length of his district. Halfway through, he collapsed. He spent five days in the ICU, diagnosed with Type 1 diabetes. He won the election anyway.

His first 30-day supply of insulin cost $684. In 2021, he introduced legislation to cap insulin prices at $50 a month, standing at the podium and sharing his diagnosis publicly for the first time. The bill, with bipartisan support, became law. He later helped pass a $25 monthly cap.

None of this made him nationally famous. What made him famous was a two-hour conversation with Joe Rogan.

In September 2025, Talarico went on The Joe Rogan Experience and did something almost no Democratic politician had done before: he talked about Christianity as if he actually believed it, and as if it had political implications that cut left, not right. He argued that the story of the Annunciation — the angel visiting Mary — was about consent, and that the Bible does not establish a single Christian orthodoxy on abortion. He called Jesus “a radical feminist.” He said the Ten Commandments mandate in Texas schools was “unconstitutional, un-American, and un-Christian.”

Rogan, who endorsed Trump in 2024, told Talarico: “You need to run for president. We need someone who is actually a good person.”

The clip went everywhere. Talarico, who already had nearly a million TikTok followers, became one of the most-discussed Democrats in the country. He entered the Senate race, defeated Congresswoman Jasmine Crockett in the March primary, and delivered a victory speech that barely mentioned Trump: “We’re done being divided. We’re done being played.”

When Barack Obama came to Austin on May 12 to boost the campaign — lunch at a taco shop, the sort of thing former presidents do — it was confirmation that the national party saw Texas as genuinely in play.

The Pastor

Jim Rigby has led St. Andrew’s Presbyterian Church in Austin since the 1980s. He baptized Talarico as a toddler and married his parents. Talarico calls him “my pastor in every sense of the word.”

The Times spent a Sunday morning at St. Andrew’s. Rigby asked his congregation what came to mind when he mentioned the Apostle Paul. They cheerfully responded: “Villain!” “Homophobic!” “He’s a jerk.” In a sermon that also cited the Bhagavad Gita and the Buddha, Rigby nudged them to reconsider: “Aristotle and Plato, they were creeps, too, in modern times. But do we want to learn from our ancestors or not?”

Rigby does not use male pronouns for God. He does not use the word “Lord.” He does not much care for the word “God.” He uses it on occasion, he said, but he tries to use synonyms, because “it’s going to mean something different to everybody.” Since the 1990s, St. Andrew’s has used the Inclusive Bible, a feminist translation that refers to the first human in Genesis as an “earth creature” and uses “kindom” instead of “kingdom.”

Talarico draws a careful line. He uses “Lord” and “God” and male pronouns. He sees the resurrection as a historical event. He reads his father’s copy of the Revised Standard Version, not the Inclusive Bible. “I don’t believe in a progressive or conservative Christianity,” he told the Times. “I believe in a biblical Christianity.”

But the proximity to Rigby has given Republican operatives exactly the material they wanted. The National Republican Senatorial Committee clipped Talarico saying “God is nonbinary.” Jason Rapert, president of the National Association of Christian Lawmakers, called him “a very dangerous person.” Steve Toth, a conservative pastor who served with Talarico in the Texas House, called him “a demonic presence in the world.”

Talarico’s response to the NRSC attack was four words: “I approve this message.”

Here’s the thing about that response: it works because the attack is the sermon, and the sermon is the message, and the message is the campaign. When the NRSC clipped Talarico calling Jesus an immigrant and saying Christ is present in “the senior deprived of their Social Security benefits,” they distributed his best material to an audience he couldn’t otherwise reach. Every share was a free ad for a candidate whose entire theory of the case is that progressive Christianity is bigger than the Democratic base.

And the evidence is accumulating. Emma Borders, a Southern Baptist from East Texas who hadn’t attended church in more than a decade, saw a Talarico Easter sermon online and sobbed. She started attending St. Andrew’s last fall, then volunteered with the homeless ministry and the food pantry. “Now you couldn’t drag me out,” she said. Jim Hall, an MBA student, heard Talarico on Rogan and said he had “never heard Christianity described that way.” He brought his wife to St. Andrew’s, then his brother. Jonathan Westerfield contacted the campaign to find out where Talarico was preaching; when he learned the candidate wasn’t speaking that Sunday, he came to hear “the guy behind the guy” — Rigby. He brought his father, a pastor, who compared Talarico to Jimmy Carter.

St. Andrew’s in-person attendance has risen by about 50 people on an average Sunday over the past year. The church now has a budget surplus for the first time in decades, and possibly ever.

This is persuasion, not turnout. These are not people who were already in the Democratic coalition waiting to be activated. They are people who were outside the coalition — in some cases, outside any institution — and are being drawn in by a language that is simultaneously political and theological. It is the same dynamic we documented in the Fort Worth special election, where a 33-year-old machinist swung a Trump+17 district by 31 points. Not mobilization. Conversion.

The Numbers

May 29th poll, Talarico leads Paxton in first post-runoff poll of Senate race

The polling tells a story of a race that is genuinely competitive — and may be trending in an unexpected direction.

In January, Emerson College had Paxton and Talarico tied at 45.6%. In April, the Texas Politics Project at UT found Talarico ahead by 8 points, 42-34. The Barbara Jordan Center at Texas Southern University found a dead heat at 45-45. After the runoff, BetOnline had Paxton a slight favorite at -125 to Talarico’s -105 — essentially a coin flip. Polymarket had Talarico at 43%.

Talarico is polling ahead of where Beto O’Rourke stood at this stage of the 2018 Senate race. And the underlying numbers are worse for Republicans than the topline suggests. Republican primary turnout was notably soft: Paxton’s 880,000 votes in the runoff were significantly fewer than the 1.2 million Greg Abbott drew in 2022 when he won his gubernatorial primary outright with a similar margin. Democratic turnout, by contrast, surged. Nearly one in five voters remained undecided in general election matchups — a pool of persuadable voters that, given Paxton’s baggage, is likelier to break against him than for him.

The Republican operative’s nightmare scenario has not changed: if Paxton drags down the ticket in November, the GOP could lose some of the very congressional districts it redrew during Trump’s mid-decade gerrymander. The redistricting we documented in “When Democracy Fights Back” — the lines drawn to lock in Republican advantage for a decade — could be undermined by the party’s own nominee. The gerrymander, designed to be fireproof, may have met its accelerant.

Two Visions

On election night, the two nominees offered the country a preview of what November will look like.

Paxton: “My opponent is the most extreme radical the Democrats have ever nominated.”

Talarico: “The most corrupt politician in America just became the Republican nominee for the United States Senate right here in Texas. It’s disgusting what he’s put the state of Texas through, and Texans deserve better.”

One candidate is running against an ideology. The other is running against a record. One has to hope voters don’t Google his name. The other has to hope they Google his opponent’s.

Talarico’s strategic positioning is worth studying. He is not running as a progressive. He is not running against Trump. He is running as a Christian populist against a corrupt system, and he has calibrated his language so precisely that when conservatives attack his theology, they distribute his message. When the NRSC calls him a heretic, they tell voters he takes the Bible seriously enough to argue about it — which is more than most politicians can say.

Whether this can work in a state that hasn’t elected a Democrat to statewide office since 1994 remains an open question. Jamie Wilder, a seventh-generation Texan who has written critically of Talarico, acknowledged that some conservative Christians may be attracted to his conciliatory tone at first — but predicted that past comments, like asserting that “our trans community needs abortion care, too,” will eventually catch up with him. “That dog’s not going to hunt in Texas,” Wilder said.

Maybe. But the pews at St. Andrew’s are filling up. The polls are tightening. And the Republican nominee is a man whose own wife filed for divorce on biblical grounds — running against a man whose entire campaign is biblical grounds.

The résumé, the rap sheet, and the seminary. Texas in November.
