Dear Friend of the Committee,

As we head into the August recess, we wanted to take a moment to thank you personally for your continued support. It’s donors like you who make work like this week’s possible. We know times are tight — inflation, grocery prices, the usual — but your contributions have allowed us to stay laser-focused on the issues that matter most to the American electorate with 89 days left before the midterms. And so, on behalf of the entire Committee, we’re proud to share this quarter’s Impact Report.

THIS WEEK’S ACCOMPLISHMENTS

  • Convened a full Senate committee hearing on the origins of a pandemic that ended years ago, featuring a witness who had already testified extensively on the subject, under oath, on multiple prior occasions.

  • Released over 1,000 pages of a private citizen’s personal journal entries in the days ahead of that hearing — a genuinely impressive research effort, if it had been aimed at literally anything a voter mentioned wanting addressed this cycle.

  • Watched that witness decline to answer questions more than one hundred separate times, on the advice of counsel, in order to avoid a perjury trap built around a pardon he received precisely because you and people like you would do exactly this.

  • Held a formal committee vote — 8 to 5, straight down party lines — to declare the witness in contempt of Congress.

  • Devised a legal workaround to send that contempt finding directly to the Justice Department, specifically so it would not require the full Senate’s 60-vote threshold, which our own legal team has quietly acknowledged the resolution would not survive.

  • Asked the Vice President, in his separate constitutional role as President of the Senate, to personally certify the paperwork.

As the Pew Research Center found in its latest polling on 2026 midterm priorities, the percentage of Americans who listed “prosecuting Dr. Fauci” as an issue they’d like congressional candidates to address came in at zero. We want to be transparent about that number with you, our donors, because transparency is a core value of this Committee, and because zero is a genuinely difficult number to spin as “we did this because voters demanded it.” We are choosing instead to frame it as an opportunity: a full 100 percent of the electorate is currently available to be persuaded that this was worth doing, since none of them asked for it in the first place.

DONOR TIER REWARDS

As always, we like to recognize our supporters according to their level of commitment. This quarter’s tiers:

Bronze ($25 and up): Your name on a mailing list that will receive at least four more emails using the phrase “election integrity” before Labor Day.

Silver ($100 and up): Early access to next quarter’s hearing topic, pending a working group decision, likely to involve either another pardoned official or, time permitting, an unrelated culture-war subject better suited to a thirty-second ad.

Gold ($500 and up): A personally signed thank-you note from a committee member, drafted by staff, on a subject we will select once we’ve confirmed which one polls best in your specific media market.

Platinum ($1,000 and up): An invitation to a private call in which a senior committee aide explains, off the record, that this was never really about the 60 votes.

WHERE YOUR DOLLARS WENT

Committee staff hours logged on this hearing: Substantial. Committee staff hours logged on the affordability crisis Pew found voters actually rank first: We are still compiling this figure. Cost of releasing 1,000 pages of a private citizen’s journal: Considerable, in staff time alone. Return on investment, measured in convictions actually obtained: None yet, and — per multiple outside legal analysts, cited without enthusiasm by our own general counsel — possibly none ever, since a Senate floor vote was never secured and may not be attempted. Return on investment, measured in cable news segments generated: Outstanding. Genuinely, our best quarter yet on this metric.

A NOTE ON THE ACCUSED, FOR CONTEXT

We want to be even-handed here, because that is another core value of this Committee. In the interest of full disclosure, our research department has compiled the following footnote regarding the witness’s background, which we are including in the smallest legible print our design team could manage, as is standard practice for material that might otherwise distract from the main narrative:

Anthony Fauci, 85, led the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases across seven presidential administrations, both parties, without interruption. He is widely credited with steering the U.S. response to HIV/AIDS from confrontation toward inclusion, working with early activist groups to reform clinical trial access. He was a principal architect of PEPFAR, a program credited by public health researchers with saving an estimated 25 million lives globally. He has, by any conventional measure available to this Committee’s research staff, spent six decades doing the actual job the term “public service” was invented to describe. None of this appears anywhere else in this letter, because none of it is the point of this letter.

We return you now to our regularly scheduled programming.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS FROM DONORS

Q: Didn’t two of your own party’s former officials — Steve Bannon and Peter Navarro — go to prison on contempt-of-Congress charges under the previous administration?

A: We appreciate this question and want to assure you that the Committee sees no relevant parallel between those cases and this one, for reasons our legal team has not yet found the time to fully articulate, given how busy this week has been.

Q: If the contempt resolution likely can’t clear 60 votes on the Senate floor, and the Committee already knows this, what was actually accomplished here?

A: Please see “Cable News Segments Generated,” above.

Q: Is there a chance this makes the party look, to use the technical term, unserious, 89 days before an election that Pew says voters want focused on affordability?

A: The Committee does not comment on hypotheticals.

Q: Isn’t there some tension between calling this witness dangerously unaccountable and simultaneously admitting the resolution against him will likely never reach a Senate floor vote?

A: We prefer to think of it less as tension and more as a two-phase strategy, in which Phase One is the vote you saw this week, and Phase Two is a press release.

Q: Your own chairman said, on the record, that pursuing this “pains” him given the respect he holds for the witness. Can you clarify what, specifically, is causing the pain?

A: The Committee finds this question needlessly literal.

MEDIA HIGHLIGHTS

We’re pleased to report robust coverage across the political press this week, which our communications team has compiled below for donor review:

NBC News covered the party-line 8-5 vote and noted that the witness had declined to answer questions more than one hundred times during the underlying hearing — a statistic our team considers, candidly, one of our stronger visuals this quarter.

NPR walked through the procedural mechanics of the DOJ referral strategy in some detail, including the part where a full Senate vote would require 60 votes the resolution likely doesn’t have — coverage we’d describe as thorough, if not exactly flattering.

CBS News quoted the chairman’s own acknowledgment that “there might not be” a point to holding a full Senate vote at all, which our team has chosen to interpret as refreshing candor rather than, as some have suggested, an admission that the whole exercise was theater from the start.

And one commentator, writing for a network we won’t name here out of professional courtesy, described the entire proceeding as an election-year crusade rapidly descending into madness — a characterization the Committee firmly rejects, while also declining to identify which specific word in that sentence it disputes.

NEXT QUARTER’S GOALS

Building on this week’s momentum, the Committee is proud to preview several exciting initiatives for the recess and beyond: continued exploration of the journal-entry format as an investigative tool; a working group to identify additional pardoned private citizens who might be productively re-litigated on live television; and, pending donor interest, a possible sequel hearing, timed for maximum proximity to Election Day, on a subject we have not yet selected but which we can promise, in advance, will not appear anywhere on Pew’s list of what voters actually asked for.

We’re also pleased to announce a new internal metric, the Distraction Efficiency Ratio, calculated as cable segments generated divided by legislative days spent on actual affordability relief. This quarter’s DER, by our team’s own preliminary math, is the strongest the Committee has posted all cycle, and we intend to keep chasing that number right through the recess, the fall session, and, resources permitting, straight into November.

We could not do this work without you. Your contributions make it possible for this Committee to spend a full legislative week on a hearing zero percent of the electorate requested, achieve a vote that even our own side privately doubts will hold, and generate a news cycle that will be forgotten well before any of the actual midterm issues get resolved. That is, in the truest sense, public service — or at least the version of it that fits inside a fundraising email.
