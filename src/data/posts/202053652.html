I. The Special Day

On Sunday, June 14, 2026 — Donald Trump’s eightieth birthday, and the two-hundred-fiftieth anniversary of the United States Army — the war with Iran ended. Or was declared ended. Or was declared complete, pending finalization of documents, pending an electronic signature, pending a formal ceremony on Friday in Switzerland, pending the absence of any of the spoilers that have ended thirty-eight previous declarations that the war was nearly over.

Pakistan’s Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif announced it first, on X: “Following intensive talks, we are pleased to announce that the Peace Deal between the United States of America and Islamic Republic of Iran has been REACHED. Both sides have declared the immediate and permanent termination of military operations on all fronts, including in Lebanon.”

Trump followed on Truth Social, minutes later: “The Deal with the Islamic Republic of Iran is now complete. Congratulations to all! I hereby fully authorize the toll free opening of the Strait of Hormuz, and, simultaneously herewith, authorize the immediate removal of the United States Naval blockade. Ships of the World, start your engines. Let the oil flow!”

Iran’s deputy foreign minister confirmed, through state media, that the text had been finalized and would be signed Friday.

Abbas Araghchi (left), Iran's foreign minister, and Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf (right), parliamentary speaker and lead negotiator — the two men who negotiated the end of the war, and who were called traitors at home for it. Ghalibaf is expected to sign for Iran in Geneva on June 19. The New York Times and Getty Images

After one hundred and five days, three waves of strikes in the final week alone, a satellite-confirmed strike on a drinking water facility, thirteen American dead, roughly seventeen hundred Iranian civilian dead, three dead Indian sailors, more than thirty-six hundred dead in Lebanon, and a global energy crisis — the war that began on February 28 ended on Trump’s birthday, hours before a cage fight on the White House South Lawn.

And it is worth pausing here, before anything else, to let that land for what it is. The killing stops. After fourteen weeks, the bombs stop falling on Iran, the missiles stop falling on Bahrain and Kuwait and Jordan, the strikes stop falling on Beirut and Tyre. The Strait of Hormuz reopens. The blockade that starved a nation of its oil revenue and stranded Indian sailors in burning engine rooms is lifted. The Iranians who told Farnaz Fassihi “either fight or don’t fight, we are fed up” finally have their answer. The mothers in Tehran who fled north on jammed Caspian highways can come home. The villagers of Sirik can stop wondering whether the next precision bomb has their water tank in its sights. Whatever else is true about how this deal was reached and what it leaves unresolved, the most important fact about it is the simplest: people who would have died next week will now live. That is not a small thing. That is the only thing that finally matters.

And credit is due — genuinely, not grudgingly — to the people who made it happen. To the Pakistani mediators, Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif and the diplomats in Islamabad who shuttled between two parties that would not speak directly to each other, who absorbed the collapses and the recriminations and kept the channel open through thirty-eight false dawns. To the Qatari negotiators who flew to Tehran on the final Sunday morning to keep the thing from dying. To Oman, the quiet broker that has hosted these talks since before the war. To Iran’s negotiators, Araghchi and Ghalibaf, who are now being called traitors in their own parliament for the crime of ending a war. And yes — to Donald Trump, who wanted this conflict over and, whatever his motives and whatever the timing, used the leverage of the American presidency to bring both sides to a signature. A president who ends a war deserves to be acknowledged for ending it. The diplomacy was real. The mediators were tireless. The outcome, if it holds, is one that every person of good will should welcome.

So: congratulations to all, as the man himself put it. The guns are quiet. Let the record show this diary said so, plainly and without reservation, before it said anything else.

Now — because a war diary that stops asking questions the moment the news turns good is not a war diary but a press release — the observations.

II. The Timing

Trump’s Eightieth Birthday Bash

The deal was signed — or agreed, or completed, or whatever verb survives contact with the actual document — on Trump’s eightieth birthday.

This is not a coincidence in the way a rainstorm on a wedding day is a coincidence. Deals of this magnitude do not finalize themselves on arbitrary calendar dates. They finalize when both parties decide the moment is advantageous. And one of the two parties — the one hosting a UFC fight on his lawn, the one whose birthday coincides this year with the Army’s 250th, the one who has wanted this war over since it blew past its promised six-week timeline four months ago — had an overwhelming personal incentive to make the moment Sunday.

This series has documented, since February, the degree to which this war has been conducted around Trump’s psychological needs rather than any coherent strategic objective. The thirty-eight peace announcements. The “22 ships” he revealed because he “wanted to say it so badly.” The Kharg Island threat issued and retracted within the same Fox & Friends phone call. A war run on impulse, on the last voice in the room, on the need to appear to be winning.

A birthday deal fits the pattern exactly. And here is the part the pattern predicts: if Trump needed the deal more than Iran did — and the evidence suggests he did — then Iran held the leverage in the final hours. The party that can walk away is the party that sets the terms. Trump could not walk away from his own birthday.

As The Atlantic reported, Vivian Salama and Nancy Youssef reported Friday that a Persian Gulf official put it bluntly: “The Iranians are positioning to milk it.” Jon Alterman of the Center for Strategic and International Studies told them: “If the Iranians have their way, and I suspect they will, it will amount to less than meets the eye.” Iran’s view, Alterman said, “is that they’ve won. The region’s biggest power and the world’s biggest power came together to take them on, and they’re still standing.”

The man who needed a birthday present negotiated against a regime that had concluded it had already won. One does not need to be a cynic to guess how that allocates the spoils.

The mood, on the day, was unmistakable. Trump telephoned the New York Times's David Sanger from the White House residence on Sunday afternoon — a twenty-eight-minute call he placed himself, his family audible in the background gathering for a birthday dinner, the UFC fight on the South Lawn still ahead of him. He talked about the rain that might interrupt the fights. "This happens in wartime," he said. And in the same conversation, he floated a proposition that ought to have made more headlines than it did: if Iran failed to reach a final accord, the United States would become "the guardian of the Middle East" in return for twenty percent of the region's revenues. A nuclear umbrella, rented out by the percentage point. The post-war American security guarantee, reimagined as a protection racket, described down the phone by an eighty-year-old president between bites of birthday cake. It is the single most revealing thing he said all weekend, and he said it almost in passing.

III. What Is Actually in It

Strip away the announcements and here is the memorandum of understanding, as CNBC has reported and as confirmed consistently across Iranian state media, the Atlantic, the New York Times, and in outline by senior administration officials:

The fighting stops on all fronts, including Lebanon, for sixty days. Iran reopens the Strait of Hormuz. The United States lifts its naval blockade of Iranian ports. And then the hard part — Iran’s nuclear program, its enriched uranium stockpile, sanctions relief, the question of whether Iran may ever again enrich uranium on its own soil — is deferred to a sixty-day second-phase negotiation, during which the differences that caused this entire war remain precisely as unresolved as they were on February 27.

Iran reiterates its commitment not to develop or acquire a nuclear weapon.

It made that same commitment in 1970, when it signed the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty. It made it again in 2015, in the first paragraph of the JCPOA — the deal Trump called the worst in history, the deal he withdrew from in 2018, the deal whose collapse set in motion the chain of events that produced this war. The fifteen-year sunset clause that Trump denounced is, by the reporting, roughly the timeline of the framework now being celebrated.

The war was fought to replace a deal in which Iran promised not to build a bomb and accepted limits on enrichment, with a deal in which Iran promises not to build a bomb and defers the question of enrichment to later talks. Thirteen Americans died in the gap between those two sentences.

The enriched uranium itself — the actual material, the thing the war was ostensibly about — sits, by Trump’s own Saturday account, buried under the rubble of facilities the United States bombed. He has promised to retrieve and “downblend and destroy it, whether in Iran, or the United States.” Pete Hegseth, asked Sunday on CBS’s Face the Nation how exactly that would happen, said: “We’ll have all options on the table.” Which is the phrase a man uses when he does not have a plan.

There is one more detail worth holding up to the light. Trump spent the weekend celebrating that the Strait of Hormuz would be “permanently toll-free.” But the memorandum suspends tolls for sixty days, after which a regional dialogue is to determine the strait’s future — and, as Sanger noted in the Times, Iran had never charged tolls before the war at all. The president is celebrating, as a hard-won concession, the restoration of the way things were on February 27. This is the pattern of the entire deal in miniature: a return to the status quo ante, achieved at the cost of a war, sold as a triumph.

The people who have negotiated with Iran before are not impressed. As the New York Times also reported, Robert Malley, who led the American negotiating team for the 2015 accord, observed that the genuinely hard questions — the fate of the nuclear program, the disposition of the enriched uranium, the scope of sanctions relief — “almost certainly will be left for later, and will almost certainly be harder to resolve than prior to the war.” Harder than before the war. That is the verdict, in six words, on what one hundred and five days of bombing purchased.

IV. The Strike on the Birthday

Early on signing day, Israel struck Dahiya in Beirut and the Tyre district. The timing enraged Trump, who told Axios that Netanyahu has ‘no fucking judgment.’ Iran held its fire to save the deal.

The deal nearly died on the morning it was meant to be born.

Early Sunday — hours before the announcements — Israel struck the Dahiya neighborhood of Beirut, a Hezbollah stronghold, after accusing the group of firing across the border into northern Israel. Prime Minister Netanyahu and Defense Minister Katz issued a joint statement claiming the strike: “Israel will not tolerate fire into its territory.” Additional Israeli strikes hit the Tyre district. Hezbollah confirmed the death of one of its operatives.

The timing — on Trump’s birthday, on the planned signing day, at the precise moment the MOU was being finalized — enraged the President of the United States.

Trump told Axios’s Barak Ravid that Netanyahu has “no fucking judgement.” He said he was “so pissed off.” He said the strike “delayed the signing by a few hours.” Publicly, on Truth Social, he was only marginally more diplomatic: Israel “has the right to defend itself,” but the attack it was responding to “was very small and meaningless, nobody was hurt, injured, or killed, and should not disrupt this important process.” He called on “all sides” to “stand down.”

Iran’s lead negotiator, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, fired back not at Israel but at Washington: “If you neither have the will nor the ability to execute your commitments, talking about the continuation of the path is not possible.” The message was precise. You, the United States, cannot control your own ally. Why should we trust your guarantees?

And here is the moment that, more than any other, reveals where the leverage sat. As the Times of Israel reported, Iran was "incentivized not to attack Israel in response" to the Beirut strikes — incentivized, presumably, by the deal it was about to sign and the terms it was about to extract. A week earlier, under nearly identical circumstances, Iran had answered an Israeli strike on Dahiya with ballistic missiles on northern Israel. This week, it held its fire. It held its fire because holding its fire was worth more than retaliating.

That is a regime negotiating from strength. That is a regime that has learned exactly how much a birthday is worth.

V. The Man Who Was Not Invited

Netanyahu sent birthday wishes on X while Israel struck Beirut: ‘Happy birthday Donald… peace comes through strength.’ Israel is not a party to the MOU and remains deeply concerned.

Israel is not a party to the memorandum of understanding.

This is the detail that should command attention. The country that, alongside the United States, launched this war on February 28 — the country whose prime minister framed it as an existential struggle, whose air force struck Iranian nuclear facilities, whose intelligence services the Pentagon has spent the war describing as a growing espionage threat — is not a signatory to the agreement that ends it.

Netanyahu’s office stressed the point itself, even as it bristled at it: Israel is “not a party to the memorandum of understanding.” Defense Minister Katz had said Friday that, deal or no deal, Israel would not withdraw from the territory it occupies in Lebanon, Syria, and Gaza, and would retain the right to act independently against Iran’s nuclear program. The reported terms, according to the Times of Israel, have caused “profound concern in Israel,” where the MOU is widely seen as a capitulation.

So Netanyahu sent a birthday card.

"Happy birthday Donald," he wrote on X — as the Times of Israel noted — observing the birthday came at "an auspicious time" — "America celebrates 250 years of freedom, a great nation built on liberty and faith."And then the needle, wrapped in ribbon: a reminder that “peace comes through strength.” It was a birthday wish and a policy critique in a single post, addressed to a man who had, hours earlier, called him a name unprintable in a family newspaper but apparently quite printable in Axios.

The war began with the United States and Israel standing together. It ends with the United States signing a deal Israel calls capitulation, while Israel bombs Beirut on the signing day and the President of the United States seethes about his ally’s judgment to a reporter. Whatever else the birthday deal accomplished, it clarified that the alliance that started this war did not survive it intact.

VI. The Opposition at Home

It was not only Israel that recoiled.

Inside Iran, the conservative factions that have spent this war demanding escalation greeted the deal with fury. As the New York Times has reported, at rallies in Tehran and other cities over the weekend, crowds of hard-liners chanted "death to Araghchi" and "death to anyone who compromises, be it a minister or a general" — targeting both the foreign minister and Ghalibaf by name. FARS, the news agency affiliated with the Revolutionary Guards, reported protests outside the foreign ministry office in Mashhad, Iran's second city. Two conservative members of parliament publicly attacked the agreement. One, Amirhossein Sabeti, called for Araghchi to be impeached. Another, Mahmoud Nabivian, said in a television interview that under the deal "Iran will become a colony of America." Iranian state television, controlled by hard-liners, presented the fourteen-point agreement as Iran's surrender.

This is worth holding alongside the Israeli reaction, because it tells you something about the shape of the thing. A deal that Israel calls a capitulation to Iran, and that Iranian hardliners call a colonization by America, is either a genuine compromise that split the difference — or a document vague enough that everyone can project their fears onto it. Given that the actual contested questions have all been deferred to a sixty-day second phase, the latter seems more likely. The MOU resolves the shooting. It resolves almost nothing else.

In Washington, the more hawkish members of Trump’s own party have signaled they don’t want Iran left with wiggle room on enriched uranium or future enrichment rights. Those are precisely the questions the MOU leaves open. The coalition that will have to ratify whatever emerges from the sixty-day talks is already fracturing, on all three sides, before the ink is dry on the thing that merely sets up those talks.

VII. The Funeral

There is a date that has not yet drawn much notice, and it should.

Ayatollah Ali Khamenei — Iran’s supreme leader for more than thirty-five years, the man killed on February 28, on the first day of this war, in the opening U.S.-Israeli strikes — will be buried on July 9 in Mashhad, his birthplace. The funeral ceremonies, Iranian state media announced this week, will begin on July 4.

July 4. America’s two-hundred-fiftieth birthday.

While the United States marks two and a half centuries of independence — while the fireworks go up over the National Mall and the President, freshly eighty, presides over whatever spectacle the occasion produces — Iran will begin five days of mourning for the leader killed on the day this war began. The man whose death opened the conflict will be buried as America celebrates, his funeral and the nation’s birthday running on the same calendar, a coincidence of dates that no satirist would dare invent.

There is a symmetry here that the birthday deal only sharpens. The war began with one death on February 28 and is ending with one signature on June 19. In between: thousands of others, most of them anonymous, most of them poor, most of them — the water-tank villagers of Sirik, the Indian sailors on the Settebello, the eleven-year-old girl in Bahrain, the displaced of southern Lebanon — people who had no stake in Iran’s nuclear program or Trump’s birthday or Netanyahu’s coalition, and who paid for all three.

VIII. It’s OK, Continued

Last week, this diary made an argument in its title: Mr. President, It’s OK Not to Retaliate. The argument was that on the Tuesday the Apache went down, with the attribution uncertain and the crew safe, a measured non-response was available and was not chosen — that three waves of strikes followed an incident that may have been an accident, and that the wisest course was the one not taken.

This week, the argument extends. On Sunday morning, Israel struck Beirut on Trump’s birthday, and Iran — for once — did not retaliate. It held its fire. And because it held its fire, the deal survived, the strait will reopen, and the killing, for now, stops.

It was okay not to retaliate. Iran proved it. The regime that has spent this war demonstrating it knows exactly when enough is enough demonstrated it one final time, on the one morning it mattered most, and was rewarded with the deal it wanted on the terms it preferred.

The question this diary asked last week was whether Washington could learn the same lesson. The answer, it turns out, was more complicated than yes or no. Washington did not need to learn restraint, because Tehran supplied it. The birthday deal exists because Iran chose not to answer the Dahiya strike with missiles — because the party with the leverage also, this once, had the discipline.

Whether that discipline survives the sixty-day second phase — whether the deferred questions of enrichment and uranium and sanctions can be answered without the shooting resuming — is the subject of every essay this series has left to write. For tonight, the guns are quiet. The oil is flowing, or about to. A man turned eighty and got the present he wanted.

And somewhere in Sirik County, where a U.S. precision bomb put a hole through a water tank that read “Water is the pulse of life,” twenty thousand people who spent a June afternoon without drinking water in 96-degree heat will, perhaps, hear that the war is over, and wonder what it was for.

American dead: 13 service members killed.

War cost: Approximately $29 billion through May.

Iranian civilians dead: Approximately 1,700 from the opening campaign, plus uncounted casualties from the final week’s strikes.

Lebanese killed: Over 3,600 since March. More than one million displaced. Israel struck Dahiya and Tyre again on Sunday — the signing day — killing at least one Hezbollah operative.

Indian sailors dead: 3, aboard the Settebello. Three tankers with Indian crews struck in the final week.

The deal — what’s not in it: Israel, which is not a party to the memorandum of understanding. Any resolution of the enriched uranium stockpile, currently buried under rubble. Any answer to what happens after 60 days if the second phase fails.
