I. The Blog Post

As Anthropic disclosed on February 23, 2026 and as reported by Reuters, the company published a blog post accusing three Chinese start-ups — DeepSeek, Moonshot, and MiniMax — of running roughly 24,000 fraudulent accounts to generate more than 16 million conversations with Claude. The purpose, Anthropic said, was distillation: harvesting the outputs of a more powerful model to train a cheaper one to imitate it.

Anthropic did not describe this as competition. It described it as theft, at industrial scale, with national security consequences. The company warned that distilled capability could be repurposed toward bioweapons research or mass surveillance, since the safety guardrails built into a frontier model do not survive the distillation process. It called on lawmakers and rival labs to help build a coordinated response, warning that “the window to act is narrow.”

Four months later, on June 12, 2026 as reported by Reuters, the U.S. government ordered Anthropic to disable its two most advanced systems, Claude Fable 5 and Mythos, for all foreign nationals — a story this series told in full in Part 49. The order came by phone at 1:00 p.m. and by letter at 5:30 p.m. the same day. No threat was specified. No duration was given.

The juxtaposition is not a coincidence worth dwelling on for irony alone. It is the Asimov Gap in miniature: a company arguing that its technology is too dangerous to leave unguarded when foreign actors reach for it, while the same technology was, for eighteen days that June, too dangerous for its own government to let anyone reach for at all — including, apparently, Anthropic’s own explanation of why.

II. The Letter

As the New York Times reported on July 6, 2026, on June 10, 2026 — two days before that suspension — Anthropic sent a letter to Senators Tim Scott and Elizabeth Warren, who lead a Senate committee that was about to hold a hearing on artificial intelligence. This time the target was not a trio of scrappy start-ups but Alibaba, one of the largest technology companies in the world. Anthropic accused Alibaba of “brazenly” and “illicitly” copying its technology using roughly 24,000 fraudulent accounts — the same figure, notably, as the February complaint, though the letter framed it as a separate campaign.

“These distillation attacks are carried out illicitly, systematically and at industrial scale to harvest U.S. A.I. capabilities across frontier labs and repackage them as their own,” Anthropic told the senators.

Alibaba declined to comment. Ms. Warren declined to comment. Mr. Scott did not respond at all.

Nobody in a position to legislate said anything. Four months separate the February blog post from the June letter, and in that entire span, the only government action taken against any of the accused parties was, if anything, aimed the other way — restricting Anthropic’s own models rather than restricting the companies Anthropic says are stealing from it. If the goal of publicising an accusation this specific was to prompt legislative movement, the actual record of the spring says the accusation moved nothing except the news cycle.

III. The Teacher and the Student

What makes this arc worth a full essay, rather than a paragraph in a future round-up, is what the New York Times explained in its July 6, 2026 distillation primer made unavoidable: distillation is not new, not exotic, and not inherently wrongful. Geoffrey Hinton, who helped develop the technique at Google more than a decade ago, described it in the plainest possible terms — one model teaches, a second model learns to behave like it, at a fraction of the cost.

That is not theft. Companies distill their own models constantly, to make them cheaper to run. And when a lab open-sources a model, it is explicitly inviting others to learn from it. The entire architecture of the open-source AI movement — the one China has embraced almost completely, and the one Meta and Mistral have used to compete against Anthropic and OpenAI in the West — depends on distillation being legitimate.

The dispute is not over distillation itself. It is over distilling a proprietary model without permission from a system that forbids it in its terms of service. And here the Gap opens wide, because the answer to “is that illegal?” is, as one trade-secret litigator put it, genuinely unresolved. Copyright law does not obviously apply, since distillation copies behaviour rather than text. The Defend Trade Secrets Act might apply, but no court has said so. And even if a court did say so, enforcing that ruling against companies operating inside China would be, in the litigator’s words, “very challenging” — a diplomatic way of saying nearly impossible.

Congress has not passed a law that answers the question. It has not needed to try particularly hard, because — as this series has documented from Part 43 onward — Congress has shown limited appetite for legislating anything about AI with teeth, preferring hearings to statutes and letters to bills. A letter to two senators, however forcefully worded, is not a claim filed in any court. It is a press strategy wearing the costume of a legal one — which may be precisely why it was addressed to legislators rather than judges. A legal filing has to survive discovery. A letter to Congress only has to survive a news cycle.

IV. Musk’s Admission

The irony the series cannot skip past: as the Times noted in its distillation coverage, in April 2026, while testifying in the Oakland federal trial this series covered in its Oakland Transcript sub-series, Elon Musk was asked directly whether his company, xAI, had ever distilled OpenAI’s technology.

“Generally A.I. companies distill other A.I. companies,” Musk replied.

He did not deny it. He normalised it. And he was, on the substance, correct — every major lab in this industry has drawn on the outputs of a rival at some point in its history, exactly the way Anthropic’s own blog post implicitly concedes when it distinguishes “illicit” distillation from the ordinary kind. The dividing line between “how the industry works” and “an industrial-scale IP theft campaign requiring congressional action” turns out to depend entirely on who is doing the distilling, and where they are sitting when they do it.

That is not a legal distinction. It is a geopolitical one, dressed in legal language because legal language is what gets letters into the hands of senators. Musk’s own testimony matters here for a reason beyond irony: it is a direct admission, under oath, by a competitor of Anthropic’s, that the practice Anthropic has spent four months characterising as an existential national-security threat is also standard operating procedure inside American labs. If distillation were the bright legal line Anthropic’s letters imply, Musk’s answer would have been a confession. Instead it was a shrug — and nobody in the room treated it as anything else.

V. Alibaba’s Own Gap

There’s a second, quieter version of this same story happening inside Alibaba itself, and it is worth pausing on because it complicates the tidy narrative of a monolithic Chinese state-directed AI machine.

As Jason Hsu wrote in The New York Times on July 23, 2026, Alibaba’s Qwen had by then become the most downloaded A.I. model family on earth — developers had already used it to build more than 100,000 specialized models of their own. That openness is precisely what drew the distillation accusations in the first place. Qwen’s reach made Alibaba both a symbol of China’s open-weight strategy and, per Anthropic’s June letter to Senators Scott and Warren, an alleged beneficiary of harvested American data.

But openness has not made Alibaba money. The company reported $1.3 billion in AI-related revenue in the first quarter of 2026 — less than four percent of total revenue — against an announced $55 billion infrastructure spending plan. Several senior Qwen engineers, including lead engineer Lin Junyang, left the company this spring amid internal disputes over how to commercialise a product built on giving itself away. Alibaba has responded by quietly shifting toward proprietary, paid models, releasing three of them within days of each other in April.

In other words: Alibaba is discovering, from the inside, the same problem every open-source AI company eventually discovers — that a governance model built on radical openness and a business model built on recurring revenue pull in opposite directions, and something has to give. That tension is not a Chinese problem or an American one. It is the Gap itself, showing up on both sides of the Pacific, wearing different clothes. Anthropic accuses Alibaba of stealing its technology through Qwen’s openness; Alibaba’s own engineers are quietly concluding that the openness Anthropic is worried about was never a stable business model to begin with. Both companies, from opposite sides of a distillation complaint, are arriving at the same underlying fact: giving a model away and charging money for a model are not compatible strategies indefinitely, whichever government the company answers to.

Meanwhile, the Pentagon has placed Alibaba on a blacklist of firms it says support the Chinese military — a designation Alibaba disputes — layering a national-security judgment on top of a commercial one, in exactly the pattern this series tracked with Anthropic’s own supply-chain designation in Part 18 and Part 49. Two companies, on opposite sides of a distillation dispute, are each now also on the receiving end of a national-security designation from their own or a rival government, decided without the kind of adversarial process either would insist on for an ordinary legal claim.

VI. What the Builders Say the Fight Isn’t About

It would be convenient to end on the accusation. The more honest ending is the one the industry’s own engineers keep offering, unprompted, whenever a journalist asks whether distillation explains China’s progress.

“This narrative that all of the capabilities of these models are coming from Anthropic is not as true as people say it is,” said Charles O’Neill, head of model training at Baseten, discussing Z.ai’s GLM-5.2 — the model this series covered in Part 51 as evidence of how thin the American lead had become. As the New York Times reported in its coverage of the Chinese models closing the gap, distillation alone cannot produce a top-tier model; it requires the same enormous compute, the same engineering talent, and the same architectural innovation everyone else needs.

And Sara Hooker, chief executive of the AI research lab Adaption, offered the line that may age better than anything in Anthropic’s letter to Scott and Warren: distillation “won’t matter as much for the next era of A.I.” As labs move from single-response chatbots toward agents that operate across many steps and tools, the thing worth copying stops being a static set of outputs and becomes something closer to a live process — much harder to harvest by asking a chatbot sixteen million questions.

If that’s right, the entire distillation panic of early-to-mid 2026 may turn out to be a fight over the tactics of the last war, waged loudly, in public, with letters to senators, precisely because a new and harder-to-litigate one is already arriving. Anthropic will have spent a year building a public case against an adversary using a method that, by its own engineers’ competitors’ account, is already becoming obsolete as the actual unit of value in this industry — while the harder governance question, over who controls an agent’s live operating process rather than its static outputs, has yet to produce even a blog post.

The Gap, as ever, is not that nobody is paying attention. It’s that everyone paying attention is arguing about the wrong decade.
