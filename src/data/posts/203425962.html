Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier filed his 83-page complaint against OpenAI and Sam Altman on June 1, 2026. The ink was barely dry.

The complaint alleged, among many things, that OpenAI had a pattern of ignoring internal safety warnings when those warnings threatened commercial momentum. It alleged that the company had been told, by its own people, that ChatGPT posed serious risks to vulnerable users — and had chosen growth anyway. It alleged that the gap between what OpenAI said publicly about safety and what it knew privately was not a matter of opinion or competing expert interpretation. It was deliberate concealment.

Florida was asking the court to accept a theory. That theory had no documentary proof in the public record.

Then Tumbler Ridge happened. Then the lawsuits followed. Then Altman wrote the letter.

By the time Uthmeier’s complaint arrived in the Highlands County courthouse, the evidence it needed to survive a motion to dismiss had already been placed in the record — by OpenAI itself.

What Happened in Tumbler Ridge

On February 10, 2026, an 18-year-old named Jesse Van Rootselaar entered Tumbler Ridge Secondary School in British Columbia with a long gun and a modified handgun. She killed five students and a teacher. She had also, earlier that morning, killed her mother and 11-year-old half-brother at their home. She died of a self-inflicted gunshot wound. Eight people were dead. The attack was one of the deadliest school shootings in Canadian history.

Investigators and journalists quickly established that Van Rootselaar had been a regular ChatGPT user in the months before the shooting, engaging in conversations that described scenarios involving gun violence and mass casualty events in explicit detail.

What emerged next was the detail that changed everything.

OpenAI’s automated systems had flagged Van Rootselaar’s account in June 2025 — seven months before the shooting — for “gun violence activity and planning.” A safety team reviewed the content. According to multiple lawsuits subsequently filed on behalf of the victims and their families, that safety team recommended that management notify law enforcement. Management reviewed the recommendation and decided against it. The account was deactivated. Van Rootselaar opened a second account and continued.

On February 10, 2026, eight people were dead.

Tumbler Ridge, British Columbia — the small town forever changed on February 10, 2026. Eight people dead after a school shooting linked to months of detailed ChatGPT conversations about gun violence.

OpenAI came forward to Canadian authorities after the shooting. It acknowledged, publicly, that the account had been flagged and banned. It acknowledged that its staff had debated alerting police. It said that under its current protocols, the account would have been referred to law enforcement — the implication being that under the protocols in place at the time, it was not.

That acknowledgment was, in the language of the legal proceedings now accumulating around OpenAI, a confession to the predicate.

The Apology and What It Proved

On April 23, 2026, Sam Altman wrote a letter to the community of Tumbler Ridge. “I am deeply sorry that we did not alert law enforcement to the account that was banned in June,” Altman wrote. “While I know words can never be enough, I believe an apology is necessary to recognize the harm and irreversible loss your community has suffered.”

British Columbia Premier David Eby posted the letter on X. His accompanying statement was precise: “The apology is necessary, and yet grossly insufficient for the devastation done to the families of Tumbler Ridge.”

Cia Edmonds, the mother of Maya Gebala — a 12-year-old shot three times and left with brain and skull injuries — was less diplomatic. She rejected the apology through her attorneys, describing OpenAI’s decision not to notify police as having played “a game of chance with our community where we were the only people who could ever lose.” Her public statement included a pointed question directed at the CEO personally: “Did you use ChatGPT to draft your ‘apology,’ Sam?”

An apology is not a legal admission. Altman’s lawyers would say so, and they would be right. But an apology is also not nothing. When a CEO of a company facing product liability litigation writes publicly that his company failed to alert law enforcement to a flagged account — that a safety team reviewed the content, that the decision was made not to act, that the current protocols would have produced a different result — he has placed a factual narrative in the public record. That narrative is not favourable to OpenAI’s legal position in Florida, in the Tumbler Ridge federal suits, or anywhere else the company is now being sued.

Florida’s complaint alleged that OpenAI suppressed internal safety warnings in the service of commercial momentum. Altman’s apology letter confirmed, in his own words, that a safety escalation occurred at OpenAI, was reviewed, and was not acted upon. The specific context was Tumbler Ridge. The legal relevance extends to every proceeding where that pattern is at issue.

A community remembers the victims of the Tumbler Ridge shooting — six of them children. The lawsuits allege OpenAI’s decision not to notify authorities turned a flagged account into irreversible tragedy.

The Lawsuits That Followed

Seven separate suits were filed in federal court in San Francisco on April 29, 2026, on behalf of victims and their families. The deceased students named in the complaints were Abel Mwansa, 12; Ezekiel Schofield, 13; Kylie Smith, 12; Zoey Benoit, 12; and Ticaria “Tiki” Lampert, 12. An educator, Shannda Aviugana-Durand, was also killed.

The lawsuits allege negligence and wrongful death. They allege that ChatGPT, specifically the GPT-4o model, played a crucial role in the events at Tumbler Ridge. They allege that Van Rootselaar used ChatGPT as what one complaint describes as a “trusted confidante, collaborator and ally,” and that the chatbot engaged willingly with her planning rather than refusing, redirecting, or alerting.

One complaint, filed by Cia Edmonds on behalf of Maya Gebala, contains an allegation that deserves to be read carefully in the context of the Florida proceedings. It states that OpenAI “made the conscious decision not to warn authorities” for fear it would harm the company’s business and its prospects for its upcoming initial public offering.

Pause on that sentence.

The IPO motive — raised in The Florida Docket Part I as a contextual irony, a matter of unfortunate timing — is now a named allegation in a separate federal proceeding, supported by a CEO’s apology letter that confirms the predicate facts. A company that knew about a dangerous user, chose not to report her to protect its commercial interests, and watched eight people die as a result. If that allegation survives discovery, it is not a product liability case. It is something considerably worse.

The Tumbler Ridge attorneys have indicated they will likely seek more than US$1 billion in damages.

The State AG Wave

Florida filed on June 1. Uthmeier said at his press conference that he expected other states to follow.

He was not the first to act against an AI company. Kentucky Attorney General Russell Coleman sued Character.AI in January 2026, accusing the company of preying on children and leading them toward self-harm. Pennsylvania followed in May, suing Character.AI for allowing its chatbot to pose as medical professionals. Both cases target a different company than Florida’s — but they establish the legal architecture that Florida is now deploying against the largest target in the field.

The pattern is bipartisan. Kentucky’s AG is a Republican. Pennsylvania’s is a Democrat. Florida’s is a Republican who previously served as Governor DeSantis’s chief of staff. The politics of AI child safety, it turns out, do not align neatly with the politics of AI regulation more broadly. States that have resisted federal consumer protection frameworks are finding common cause with states that have embraced them, because the underlying issue — children being harmed by products companies knew were dangerous — is not ideologically complicated.

At the federal level, the FTC launched an inquiry into AI chatbot companion products in September 2025, covering OpenAI and six other companies including Meta, Google, and Snap. That inquiry remains open. It has produced no enforcement action. The absence of federal action is precisely why state AGs are moving — and why Uthmeier’s “first-in-the-nation” framing carries weight beyond Florida’s borders. He is not just filing a case. He is filing a template.

The emerging battlefield: AI companies facing product liability, negligence, and wrongful death claims. Florida’s case, bolstered by the Tumbler Ridge evidence, tests whether Section 230 and First Amendment protections can shield deliberate design choices

TACO Check: OpenAI’s Response Pattern

This publication’s TACO Framework — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — has tracked OpenAI’s response to its legal exposure across multiple proceedings. The Florida Docket provides a case study in the Accommodate phase, with early signals of what follows.

To Florida’s complaint: “We believe minors need significant protection” and “we have put in place industry-leading protections and policies.” No denial of the specific allegations. No engagement with the internal safety warning theory. A recitation of current safety features.

To Tumbler Ridge: An apology letter admitting failure to alert law enforcement. A commitment to stronger referral protocols. A meeting with the BC Premier. An acknowledgment that current protocols would have produced a different result.

Notice what is present in both responses and what is absent. Present: expressions of concern for safety. Present: descriptions of current protective measures. Present: forward-looking commitments to improvement. Absent, in both cases: any denial that internal safety escalations occurred and were overridden. Absent: any denial that commercial considerations influenced those decisions. Absent: any direct engagement with the specific individuals named in the complaints.

OpenAI is accommodating without admitting. It is acknowledging form while denying substance. That is a coherent litigation strategy. It is also, in the framework of these proceedings, a meaningful data point. Companies that have nothing to hide in discovery tend to say so. Companies that are managing disclosure tend to speak carefully about what they have and have not done.

The Florida discovery process, if it proceeds, will test that strategy directly. Florida is seeking the internal documents — the emails, the safety board deliberations, the records of the decisions made when safety warnings were raised and overridden. Tumbler Ridge already put some of those documents in play. The question is how many more exist, and what they say.

The IPO as the Through Line

OpenAI is preparing to go public. Its most recent valuation stood at approximately $852 billion following a $122 billion funding round in March 2026. The IPO filing — when it arrives — will require the company to disclose material legal risks to prospective investors.

The Florida complaint is now a material legal risk. The Tumbler Ridge federal suits are material legal risks. The ongoing FTC inquiry is a material legal risk. The criminal investigation that Uthmeier’s office is running alongside the civil complaint — the first criminal investigation of an AI company for user harm in US history — is a material legal risk.

Every new lawsuit, every new revelation, every state AG who announces an investigation following Florida’s lead: each one lands in the S-1. The underwriters will price it. The institutional investors will read it. The prospectus will contain, somewhere in the risk factors section, language describing a company that is simultaneously valued at nearly a trillion dollars and facing the possibility that its most profitable product was designed in ways that caused children to die.

That is not a comfortable sentence to write in a prospectus. It is, however, the accurate one.

Florida did not just file a consumer protection case. It created an ongoing disclosure obligation that will follow OpenAI into the public markets. Uthmeier may not have planned it that way. The effect is the same.

What the Docket Looks Like Now

The Florida case is nine days old. There has been no response from OpenAI in the Highlands County proceedings beyond its initial press statement. No motion to dismiss has been filed. No scheduling order has been entered. The procedural machinery has not yet started.

That will change. OpenAI will move to dismiss. It will argue, among other things, that ChatGPT’s outputs are protected speech under the First Amendment, that Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act shields it from liability for third-party content, and that Florida’s product liability theory — the idea that a software product can be defectively designed in the same way a pharmaceutical or a consumer good can be defective — is a novel legal theory without established precedent.

Those are real arguments. Courts have been sympathetic to Section 230 defences in AI cases. The product liability theory has not been tested at trial. The personal liability claim against Altman is ambitious by any standard.

But the landscape in which those arguments will be made has changed since Florida filed. It changed because of Tumbler Ridge. It changed because of the apology letter. It changed because seven families filed seven separate lawsuits in federal court, and one of those complaints alleges that the decision not to call the police was made to protect an IPO.

The strongest argument for Section 230 protection is that ChatGPT is a neutral platform conveying information generated by users. The strongest argument against it is that ChatGPT is a designed product whose specific design choices — the conversational warmth, the engagement optimization, the propensity to follow rather than redirect — were known to produce harmful outcomes in vulnerable users, and those choices were made deliberately by identifiable people in response to identifiable commercial incentives.

Florida is making the second argument. Tumbler Ridge just made it harder to dismiss.

The Florida courts are open. The discovery clock has not yet started. But the evidence Florida needed is already in the record.

It got there without a subpoena.
