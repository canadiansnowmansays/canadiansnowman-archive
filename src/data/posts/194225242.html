Part of the series: Israel/Palestine: The Original Sin and Its Compound Interest

By Stephen Gordon | CanadianSnowmanSays

More than 140 settler outposts have been set up without Israeli government approval since the 1990s

The maps that document the settlement project should be required viewing for anyone attempting to understand the current state of the Israel-Palestine conflict. They tell a story that unfolds across decades: settlements sparse in 1967, a handful of dots near Jerusalem and in the Jordan Valley; spreading by 1993; metastasized by 2000; and by the present day covering the territory like a rash, fragmented and pervasive, with the Palestinian population centres — Ramallah, Nablus, Hebron, Jericho — isolated in shrinking islands surrounded by Israeli-controlled land.

Look at those maps long enough and the political conclusion becomes unavoidable: the two-state solution, which requires a contiguous Palestinian state in the West Bank, is not merely difficult. It is physically impossible without a forced evacuation of Israeli settlers on a scale that no Israeli government in the current political environment could survive. The settlements have not complicated the peace process. They have ended it. They were designed to end it. And they have been funded, protected, and legally enabled by every Israeli government since 1967 — with the financial support of the United States government and the tax-deductible donations of American Jewish organizations — while the international community issued statements of concern that changed nothing.

This instalment is about how that happened. It is about the ideology behind it, the mechanics of it, the money that funded it, and the political protection that sustained it. It names names. The settlements are not an abstraction. They are the product of specific decisions made by specific people for specific reasons, and those reasons are not the security rationale that their defenders offer. They are the fulfillment of a religious and political program whose objective has always been the permanent Israeli control of the entire territory of biblical Israel, regardless of who was already living there.

In the first decade of the occupation, Israel moved carefully. Nearly thirty settlements were established in the West Bank — excluding East Jerusalem — housing roughly 4,500 people in total. The numbers were small enough to be dismissed as temporary, strategic, or reversible. They were none of those things.

The West Bank under early occupation. Settlements sparse, military zones already established. The infrastructure of permanent control was being assembled before most of the world noticed what was being built.

On land declared state property by administrative order — a legal mechanism that required no legislation and attracted little international attention — successive Likud governments expanded the project into something qualitatively different. Another hundred settlements were planted in densely populated Palestinian areas. The stated goal, documented in internal planning documents, was demographic and geographic: to create facts on the ground that would make any future challenge to Israeli dominance physically impossible.

A hundred settlements later. State land declarations converted Palestinian territory into Israeli permanence. Each triangle is a community. Each community is a precedent. The process had a name in the planning documents: thickening.

The Oslo II Accord of 1995 was supposed to be a framework for transition. Instead it institutionalised the geography of control. The West Bank — excluding Hebron — was divided into three administrative zones: Area A under Palestinian civil and security authority, Area B under Palestinian civil authority with Israeli security override, and Area C under full Israeli control. Area C comprises roughly sixty percent of the West Bank. It is where most of the settlements are. It is where most of the land is.

The Oslo II geography. Palestinian population centres in brown — islands in a sea of Israeli-administered blue. The Accord that was sold as a peace framework produced a map that looks like containment. Area C was never handed back.

In 1996, under political and international pressure, Israel announced it would stop establishing new settlements. It did not stop expanding them. Instead it adopted a new instrument: the illegal outpost. In theory, outposts are established without official sanction — built by settlers acting on their own initiative, outside the law. In practice, they receive government infrastructure, military protection, and retroactive legal recognition. Approximately one hundred new outposts were established across the West Bank under this arrangement. The illegality was the point — it provided deniability while the map continued to change.

Settlements and outposts, post-1996. Official settlements and illegal outposts — distinguished on the map, indistinguishable in practice. When the political cost of building settlements rose, Israel invented a category of settlement that didn’t officially exist.

In June 2002, the Israeli cabinet voted to construct the Separation Barrier. Israel described it as a security measure — a response to the suicide bombings of the Second Intifada, a wall between populations in conflict. The route of the barrier told a more precise story. It was drawn not along the 1967 Green Line — the internationally recognised boundary — but around the settlements, enclosing 81 of them inside its western embrace, including 11 neighbourhoods built in East Jerusalem. The barrier did not follow the conflict. It followed the real estate.

The Separation Barrier, 2002–present. Constructed barrier in solid black. Projected extensions in dashed black. The route was determined by settlement locations — enclosing 81 settlements inside the barrier’s arc. A security measure that happened to annex land. The wall is not the argument. The wall is the conclusion.

The Ideology: Greater Israel

The settlement enterprise is rooted in an ideology called Religious Zionism, which differs from the secular political Zionism of Israel’s founding generation in one critical respect: it regards the establishment of the State of Israel not as a political achievement but as the beginning of a divinely ordained process of redemption that requires Jewish sovereignty over the entire biblical land of Israel — including the West Bank, which the movement calls by its biblical names, Judea and Samaria.

In this theology, the 1967 conquest of the West Bank was not a military victory creating a security buffer. It was a divine gift creating an obligation. The obligation was to settle the land — to establish Jewish presence across every part of the biblical homeland — in fulfillment of God’s covenant with Abraham. Withdrawal from any part of the land was not merely a political mistake. It was a religious transgression. Oslo was not a peace process. It was a betrayal.

Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook, the first Ashkenazi Chief Rabbi of Mandatory Palestine, and his son Rabbi Zvi Yehuda Kook were the theological architects of this movement. The younger Kook’s sermon on the eve of Israel’s Independence Day in 1967 — in which he wept over the partition of the biblical land — was delivered three weeks before the Six Day War restored the territories to Israeli control. His students subsequently interpreted the war as divine confirmation that his tears had been heard. Gush Emunim — the Bloc of the Faithful — was founded by his disciples in 1974 and became the organized engine of the settlement enterprise.

This is not a fringe theology. It is the governing ideology of the most politically powerful movement in contemporary Israeli society — a movement whose representatives currently hold some of the most consequential positions in the Israeli government, including the Finance Ministry portfolio that directly controls settlement funding and the National Security Ministry portfolio that controls settler protection in the West Bank.

Bezalel Smotrich, the Finance Minister, has publicly stated that there is no such thing as a Palestinian people, that the West Bank is not occupied territory but the heartland of the Jewish people, and that the application of Israeli sovereignty to the entire West Bank is both inevitable and religiously mandated. Itamar Ben Gvir, the National Security Minister, has called for the expulsion of Arab citizens of Israel whom he considers disloyal and has spent his political career advocating positions that the Israeli Supreme Court has characterized as racist.

These are not the statements of politicians operating at the fringe of their political system. They are cabinet ministers in the government of the most right-wing coalition in Israeli history, exercising real power over the lives of millions of Palestinians.

The Mechanics: How Settlements Grow

Then and now. A West Bank settlement in its early form (left) — modest, suburban, unremarkable by design. Maaleh Adumim today (right) — 40,000 residents, tower blocks, a city. Over 700,000 settlers now live across 279 settlements and outposts, a population larger than Washington D.C. The argument that this is reversible has not been seriously made by anyone who has looked at a map.

The settlement enterprise operates through a combination of legal mechanisms, military orders, and extra-legal seizures that together amount to a systematic appropriation of Palestinian land.

The primary legal mechanism is the declaration of “state land” — a category of land that the Israeli military administration defines as belonging to the state rather than to private owners, and which is therefore available for settlement construction. The definition of state land has been progressively expanded by Israeli courts and military orders to encompass land that was previously communally owned, agriculturally used by Palestinian villages, or simply unregistered under Ottoman-era land law. B’Tselem, the Israeli human rights organization, has documented that approximately 26 percent of the West Bank has been declared state land and made available for settlement use.

Israeli-only bypass roads physically divide the West Bank

The second mechanism is the bypass road network — a system of roads built across the West Bank to connect settlements to Israel proper, which Palestinians are prohibited from using or are severely restricted in using. The road network physically divides Palestinian territory, cutting through agricultural land, separating villages from their fields and from each other, and making the West Bank progressively less navigable for its Palestinian inhabitants while making it progressively more integrated with Israel proper for its settler population.

The third mechanism is the outpost system. Israeli law technically requires government approval for new settlement construction. Settler activists have circumvented this requirement by establishing unauthorized “outposts” — illegal even under Israeli law — on West Bank hilltops, which are then progressively legalized, connected to road and utility infrastructure, and eventually recognized as official settlements. The Israeli government has retroactively legalized dozens of outposts that were established illegally, creating a system in which illegality is rewarded rather than penalized.

The fourth mechanism — and the most immediately violent — is settler terrorism. The term is used deliberately, because it is accurate. Israeli settlers in the West Bank have conducted a sustained campaign of violence against Palestinian civilians and property that the Israeli military has documented, the US State Department has documented, and the Israeli human rights organization Yesh Din has documented in meticulous annual reports. Olive trees — the agricultural inheritance of Palestinian families across generations — are uprooted by the thousands annually. Vehicles are torched. Homes are vandalized with Hebrew graffiti. Physical assaults on Palestinian farmers and shepherds are routine. And in the most extreme cases — Duma in 2015, where an 18-month-old Palestinian child was burned alive in an arson attack on his family’s home — settlers have committed acts that by any legal standard constitute terrorism and murder.

The Israeli military’s response to settler violence against Palestinians is, as documented by its own internal reports, systematically inadequate. Prosecutions are rare. Convictions are rarer. The message sent to the settler population — that violence against Palestinians will not be meaningfully punished — is not an accidental product of institutional failure. It is the predictable outcome of a political environment in which the settlers’ political allies hold the portfolios that control the prosecution of settler crimes.

The Money: Who Pays for This

The settlement enterprise is not a private initiative by ideologically motivated individuals. It is a state-funded project, subsidized at every level of Israeli government, that has received billions of dollars in support from the Israeli treasury over five decades.

Israeli settlers in the West Bank receive subsidized mortgages, subsidized infrastructure connections, preferential access to water from shared aquifers, free or heavily subsidized education, and security provided by the Israeli military at public expense. The Israeli government has established industrial zones in settlements, providing economic incentives for Israeli businesses to locate in occupied territory. Israeli universities have established campuses in settlements. Israeli cultural institutions operate in settlements.

The total financial transfer from the Israeli public to the settlement enterprise over five decades is difficult to calculate precisely because it is distributed across dozens of budget lines in multiple ministries. Israeli economists who have attempted the calculation — including researchers at Molad, a progressive Israeli think tank — have produced estimates in the range of tens of billions of dollars. The settlements are not economically self-sustaining communities of ideologically committed pioneers. They are heavily subsidized state projects whose economics depend entirely on continued government support.

Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich and National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir, key architects of accelerated settlement policy in the current government.

In the United States, donations to Israeli settler organizations are tax-deductible under American law because the settler organizations are structured as non-profits registered under the US tax code. American donors have contributed hundreds of millions of dollars to settler organizations and settlement infrastructure through this mechanism, effectively receiving a US government subsidy for contributions to a project that the US government officially describes as contrary to international law and an obstacle to peace. The contradiction has been noted by legal scholars and ignored by Congress.

The total American financial commitment to Israel — military aid, loan guarantees, diplomatic support — over the period of the occupation amounts to hundreds of billions of dollars. None of it has been conditioned on halting settlement construction, despite the fact that every American administration since 1967 has described settlements as an obstacle to peace, as contrary to international law, and as contrary to American interests in a negotiated solution.

The gap between American rhetoric on settlements and American action on settlements is the most precisely documented example of the broader gap between American stated values and American conduct that Part XII of this series will examine in full.

The Political Protection: Why Nothing Stops Them

In 1992, the Rabin government imposed a limited freeze on new settlement construction. It was the only significant settlement freeze in the history of the occupation. It lasted less than two years. It was reversed by Netanyahu’s first government in 1996.

Barack Obama, in his first term, demanded a settlement freeze as a precondition for resuming peace talks. Netanyahu refused. The United States eventually dropped the demand. Settlement construction continued throughout the Obama years. It accelerated during the Trump years, when the Trump administration reversed decades of US policy by declaring that settlements were not necessarily illegal under international law — a legal position rejected by the entire international community but welcomed enthusiastically by the Israeli right.

The Biden administration returned to the formal position that settlements are illegal and an obstacle to peace. It imposed sanctions on a small number of individual settlers responsible for specific acts of violence. It continued delivering $3.8 billion per year in military aid to Israel without conditioning that aid on any halt to settlement construction.

The pattern is consistent across administrations: American rhetoric opposes settlements, American policy enables them. The explanation is not complicated. The domestic political cost to any American administration of genuinely pressuring Israel on settlements — of conditioning military aid, of supporting UN resolutions, of refusing Security Council vetoes — exceeds the foreign policy benefit. The Israel lobby, the evangelical Christian Zionist constituency, the bipartisan congressional support for the US-Israel relationship, and the genuine strategic value that the US-Israel relationship provides for American regional interests all combine to make meaningful pressure on Israel domestically impossible.

The most precise illustration of how October 7th accelerated what was already underway comes from Peace Now’s tracking of Israeli government approval of new settlements. In 2020: zero new settlements approved. In 2021: zero. In 2022: zero. In 2023 — the year of October 7th: nine. In 2024: five. In 2025: fifty-four. Fifty-four new settlements approved in a single year. The shackles did not just come off after October 7th. They were incinerated. What had been a project conducted with at least the appearance of restraint became an open, state-sanctioned land rush operating at a scale the occupation had never previously attempted. Netanyahu signed a settlement project that the United States had opposed for years because it would effectively bisect the West Bank, making a Palestinian state physically unimaginable. When asked why, he was explicit: “We are going to fulfill our promise that there will be no Palestinian state. This place belongs to us.”

We will return to this in Part XII. The point here is that the settlements have grown because they have been politically protected — by Israeli governments that fund them, by American governments that enable them, and by an international community that condemns them in resolutions that are vetoed before they can be enforced.

What 700,000 Settlers Means

There are now over 700,000 Israeli settlers living in 279 settlements and outposts in the West Bank and East Jerusalem. For context: that is larger than the population of Washington D.C. It is larger than the population of Boston. It is a city-sized population embedded across the territory of a future Palestinian state, organized in communities with their own infrastructure, their own institutions, their own political representation, and their own deeply held conviction that the land they live on is theirs by divine right.

Removing them — which a viable two-state solution would require — is not a technical challenge. It is a political impossibility under current Israeli conditions. The Israeli right has made clear that it would resist evacuation by force. The Israeli military — which includes many soldiers who are themselves settlers or settlement sympathizers — would be ordered to remove their own communities. The political coalition that could authorize such an evacuation does not exist in the current Israeli Knesset. It has not existed for decades.

This is the mechanism by which the settlement enterprise has achieved its strategic objective. It has not annexed the West Bank formally — formal annexation would trigger legal and political consequences that Israel has wished to avoid. It has instead created, through fifty-eight years of continuous settlement construction, a physical reality on the ground that makes the alternative to annexation — a viable Palestinian state — structurally impossible.

The Palestinians watching this process for fifty-eight years have described it accurately: it is slow annexation. It proceeds settlement by settlement, outpost by outpost, dunham by dunham — the Arabic unit of land measurement — toward a conclusion that has been visible for decades and that the international community has been unable or unwilling to prevent.

The conclusion is the permanent Israeli control of the entire West Bank under a system in which the Palestinian population has neither the rights of Israeli citizens nor the sovereignty of an independent state. It is, by the most careful analysis of international human rights law, apartheid. It is, by any honest moral reckoning, a catastrophe whose acceleration is directly connected to the catastrophe unfolding in Gaza.

You cannot imprison a people for fifty-eight years and then express surprise when they explode.
