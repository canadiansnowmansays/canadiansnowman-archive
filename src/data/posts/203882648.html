Constance had been saving what she thought of his farewell season for the last night. On Sunday morning she found herself getting in the car. He held the door open. They sat in the lobby for forty minutes. She told him what she had been saving.

Constance Hay has attended the final performance of every Elsinore Festival season for fifty years.

She attended the first one she saw when she was twenty-eight and newly arrived in Elsinore from Guelph, which was where she was from and where she had lived until the particular combination of circumstances and decisions that she has never fully detailed to anyone brought her to Wellington Street, where she has remained ever since. She sat in the second row, left of centre, because she had been told by the woman who sold her the ticket that it was the best seat in the house, and she has sat in the same seat for fifty years because she has found no evidence to contradict this assessment.

She was on the original committee, in 1985, that proposed the Community Ticket program. She was at the meeting, in 1987, at which it was approved. She has redeemed hers every year since.

She knew Alistair when he was twenty-six years old and auditioning for a part he did not get but which he handled with the composure of a young man who understood that a part you did not get was information rather than a verdict. She thought then that he would be significant, and she has never revised this assessment, and she is aware that the season ending on Saturday night is the last one she will watch him end.

She has been saving what she thinks of his farewell season for the last night.

She has not shared it with anyone. Not with her grandson, who drove her to three performances this year and who waited in the car with the patience of a young man who has learned that waiting for Constance is worthwhile. Not with Reverend Faye, with whom she has tea every Tuesday and who she trusts with more than most. Not with Norman, who she has known for twenty-three years and who she would trust with the cabinet contents if she had a cabinet.

She has written it, in sections, in her narrow spiral notebook, over the course of the season. Each entry begins with the date and the production and one sentence about the play itself. Then one sentence about what the play said in this moment, in this season, in this theatre. Then, since July, one additional sentence that is about Alistair specifically — about what she has observed of the man who has been directing plays in Elsinore for nineteen years and who has done so with a quality of conviction that she has found, over those years, to be the thing she most respects and most worries about.

The worry is not about his skill. The worry is about what happens to a person when the thing they have given themselves to most completely is also the thing they are leaving.

She has been watching for it all season. She has written what she has seen.

The final performance was A Midsummer Night’s Dream.

Alistair had programmed it deliberately, for the end. He had spent the season with Beckett and Shakespeare’s tragedies and Something Rotten! and the weight of the farewell, and he had wanted to end it with a comedy — not a comedy of absurdity or a comedy of criticism, but Shakespeare’s most generous comedy, the one about love and transformation and the strange places that serious things lead you through before you come out the other side. He had said this to his stage manager in August. He had not said it publicly. He does not say things publicly that he has not thought through completely, and he has never thought through everything he has done in this theatre completely, because the theatre does not permit that kind of certainty.

Constance arrived at seven. She found her seat. She sat down. She opened her notebook to the last written page and looked at what she had written there, which was the entry from the previous Saturday’s Othello, and then she closed the notebook and put it in her bag and did not take it out again until after the show.

She watched the play. She watched Oberon and Titania and the lovers and Bottom the weaver transformed and the confusion of the forest and the unconfusion at the end, the reconciling and the restoring and the giving back of what had been taken, and the fairies’ blessing of the house.

She watched Alistair’s work in the play — not the play itself but the choices in the play, which is where a director’s work actually lives, in the moments you cannot quite see directly but can only see the results of: a pause held slightly longer than expected, a piece of business that makes a familiar speech suddenly new, a quality of attention in the actors that tells you someone has been in the room with them for months insisting on precision.

She sat with it.

At the curtain call, the company was on stage and the audience was standing, which they did for every final performance, and Alistair was in the wings, which is where he always was at curtain calls because he considered the work to belong to the actors and not to himself, and Constance knew this about him, and she watched the place where he was not visible and thought about what it meant that he had been making these choices for nineteen years without standing on the stage to take credit for them.

She thought about the talent buried in the ground. She thought about what Reverend Faye had said about it.

She thought that Alistair had not buried his talent. He had put it into the work, every season, in full, and had stood in the wings while the work took the curtain call, which was the correct relationship between the director and the work, which is to say the relationship in which the work is the point and the person is the means.

She wrote three sentences in her notebook on the drive home, by the light of her phone.

The next morning was Sunday. She came to the Festival for the unlocking of the main entrance, which Alistair did himself every morning during the season, which was a habit from his first year when he had been too anxious to let anyone else do it and which had become, over nineteen years, a ritual that the building had come to expect.

He was there at eight-fifteen with his key and his coat — not the good coat, which was for civic occasions; a different one, older, that he wore in the theatre — and when he saw Constance on the steps he stopped.

He said: “Constance.”

She said: “Good morning, Alistair.”

He said he had not expected anyone to be there.

She said she had not expected to come. She said she had been home and had found herself getting into the car, which happened occasionally, and that the car had brought her here.

He unlocked the door. He held it open.

They went inside.

They sat in the lobby, in two of the chairs set out for pre-show audience gathering, and they talked for forty minutes, which is longer than Constance usually talks to anyone at one sitting and which she will account for by saying only that the occasion warranted it.

She told him what she thought of the farewell season.

She told him what she had been saving.

She told him about the entry she had written in her notebook after King Lear in June, and the entry after Waiting for Godot in July, and the entry after the production she had seen in September that she had not expected and which had been, she said, extraordinary — which was her word for the highest available response to a work of art, and which she had used on seven occasions in fifty years of attending this theatre, and which he received with the expression of a man who knows what the word costs and is trying not to show what it is worth to him.

She told him that the season had been what she had hoped it would be. She told him that nineteen years was not a short time but that it had not felt like a long one, which was either a tribute to the work or to the life, and that she thought it was both, and that she was glad he had titled it This Rough Magic, which was the right title.

He asked her why it was the right title.

He titled the farewell season This Rough Magic, which is what Prospero says before he breaks his staff. He stood in the wings at every curtain call, because the work belonged to the actors. He put down the staff. The island is still the island.

She said because Prospero puts down the staff, which is an act of choice — not of loss, not of defeat, not of the world taking something from you, but of you deciding that the power you have held has been used as well as it can be used in the time available to you, and that it is time to put it down and let the island be the island without the magic.

She said: “You have used it well.”

He said nothing for a moment.

Then he said: “Thank you, Constance.”

She said: “You’re welcome.”

She got up. She adjusted her coat. She went home.

He sat in the lobby for a while after she left. His stage manager, who had arrived at nine and found him there, did not ask what he was thinking. She had learned, in eight years, when not to ask.

The Moonbeam Chronicle does not usually describe private conversations that were not offered for publication. This one was offered. Constance told me what she had said in the lobby on Sunday morning, in three sentences, on the telephone the following Thursday. She said I could write about it.

I asked her why she was telling me.

She said: “Because it happened, and the Chronicle keeps the record, and some things should be in the record.”

I said: “What three sentences would you want me to quote?”

She thought for a moment.

She said: “You have used it well. The island is the island. The swans don’t need the magic.”

I said I thought those were good sentences.

She said she thought so too.

She hung up.

A Note for the Record

The farewell is a particular kind of moment. It requires the person leaving to do two things at once: to acknowledge that what they have built belongs to them and also to the people and the place that held it, and to find a way to give it back without losing the part that belongs to them.

Alistair named his farewell season after a speech in which Prospero breaks his staff and buries his books and releases Ariel and invites the audience to clap their hands to free him from the island. It is an ending in which the magician gives up the magic and becomes a man again, which Shakespeare understood was not a diminishment but a restoration.

To the south, the question of ending has been handled differently. People who have held positions of significant power have in several cases refused to acknowledge that the power belonged to the position rather than to themselves. The staff has not been put down. The magic has been claimed as personal property. The island has been told that the magic is what the island is, and that without the magic the island is nothing.

Constance Hay told Alistair that the island is the island. She said the swans don’t need the magic. She said this as a compliment, which it was — the compliment of telling someone that what they built does not depend on their continued presence for its existence, which is the highest thing you can say about a person’s work.

Alistair put down the staff.

The island is still the island.

The new artistic director arrives in spring. Gerald has the handover documents. Constance has fifty years of final performances in her notebook. The swans will be back on the south bank in April.
