The Affordable Care Act offered states a deal in 2010: Expand Medicaid to cover adults earning up to 138% of the federal poverty level (about $20,783 for an individual in 2024). The federal government would pay 100% of the costs at first, phasing down to 90%. States would pay at most 10%.

It was close to free money to cover the poorest Americans. All states had to do was say yes.

As of 2025, ten states still say no:

Alabama

Florida

Georgia

Kansas

Mississippi

South Carolina

Tennessee

Texas

Wisconsin

Wyoming

What do these states have in common?

Eight of the ten have Republican trifectas (governor + legislature). In the other two, Kansas and Wisconsin, Democratic governors want expansion and Republican legislatures block it

Several have among the highest uninsured rates in America, including Texas, the highest of all

Several, particularly in the South, have among the worst health outcomes (life expectancy, maternal mortality)

Most have suffered rural hospital closures

All have low-income residents who would gain coverage from expansion

This is Part 8 of Code Blue, our investigation into America’s 80-year healthcare failure. We’ve documented broken promises (Parts 1-2), followed the money (Part 3), examined international models (Part 4), described the bipartisan compromise nobody wants (Part 5), exposed the lobbying apparatus (Part 6), and showed how Medicare already proves single-payer works (Part 7).

Now we examine the cruelest irony of American healthcare: the states that need help most refuse to accept it, even when Washington pays 90% of the bill, and their residents pay with their lives.

That’s not an accident. That’s a policy choice.

Life Expectancy: The Ultimate Measure

When states refuse Medicaid expansion, people die. Not metaphorically. Actually die. Earlier. From treatable causes.

The best evidence comes from a study by Sarah Miller, Sean Altekruse, Norman Johnson and Laura Wherry, which linked federal survey data to death records. It found that Medicaid expansion saved the lives of about 19,200 adults aged 55 to 64 between 2014 and 2017. Over the same years, about 15,600 older adults in non-expansion states died prematurely because their states refused (Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, “Medicaid Expansion Has Saved at Least 19,000 Lives, New Research Finds,” November 6, 2019).

The state-by-state map points the same way, though coverage is only one of many causes. CDC’s state life tables for 2022 (NCHS, December 4, 2025):

Highest life expectancy:

Hawaii: 80.0 years

Massachusetts: 79.8 years

New Jersey: 79.6 years

New York: 79.5 years

Lowest life expectancy:

West Virginia: 72.2 years

Mississippi: 72.6 years

Kentucky: 73.6 years

Alabama, Louisiana, Oklahoma and Tennessee: 73.8 years each

Every state on the highest list expanded Medicaid. The lowest list is the South and Appalachia, which includes the non-expansion holdouts Mississippi, Alabama and Tennessee, but also West Virginia, Kentucky and Louisiana, which did expand. Poverty, smoking, obesity, overdoses and distance from care all play a part. Medicaid can’t fix all of that. But it is the one lever a state legislature can pull this year.

Mississippi residents now live 7.4 years less than Hawaii residents on average. Some of that is poverty and chronic disease. Some of it is healthcare access.

The Rural Hospital Crisis

Since 2010, 154 rural hospitals have closed or stopped providing inpatient care in the United States: 86 complete closures and 68 conversions to outpatient or other services, according to the University of North Carolina’s Cecil G. Sheps Center for Health Services Research, which keeps the national count.

Where?

Rural hospital closures and conversions since 2010, selected states (Sheps Center list, as of 2026):

Texas: 22

Tennessee: 14

Missouri: 10 (expanded Medicaid only in October 2021)

Alabama: 8

Georgia: 8

Kansas: 8

North Carolina: 8 (expanded only in December 2023)

Oklahoma: 8 (expanded only in July 2021)

Florida: 5

Notice a pattern? Texas and Tennessee, the two states with the most, never expanded Medicaid. Every other state on this list either never expanded or waited until 2021 or later.

Why do rural hospitals close?

Many reasons: shrinking rural populations, staff shortages, low payment rates. But uncompensated care is a big one. When patients can’t pay, hospitals eat the cost. When too many patients can’t pay, hospitals go under.

Medicaid expansion would have covered many of those patients. Federal money would have paid for much of their care. More rural hospitals would have stayed open.

Instead, Republican legislatures, and in most of these states Republican governors, refused expansion, rural hospitals closed, and rural residents now travel much farther for emergency care, if they can afford to go at all.

The Human Cost

Kansas, 2015:

Mercy Hospital Independence, serving a town of about 9,000 people, closed on October 10, 2015, according to Catholic Health World (October 1, 2015). Mercy cited declining population, trouble recruiting physicians, shrinking reimbursement and years of losses. It also cited the state’s refusal to expand Medicaid as one factor.

In 2017, the Kansas legislature finally passed expansion. Governor Sam Brownback (Republican) vetoed it, and the House override attempt fell three votes short (High Plains Public Radio, April 5, 2017).

Kansas Governor Laura Kelly (Democrat), in office since 2019: Repeatedly asked the Republican-controlled legislature to expand Medicaid. Repeatedly rejected.

Kansas has lost eight rural hospitals to closure or conversion since 2010, Independence among them, by the Sheps Center count.

Tennessee, 2017–2024:

Three rural hospitals that closed in the past decade:

Copper Basin Medical Center (Polk County), 2017

Jamestown Regional Medical Center (Fentress County, the county’s only hospital), 2019

Jellico Regional Hospital (Campbell County), which closed briefly in 2021 and then for good in 2024, with a notice taped to the doors (WPLN, May 27, 2026)

Tennessee has had more rural closures and conversions since 2010 than any state except Texas.

Tennessee’s legislature killed Governor Bill Haslam’s (Republican) compromise plan, Insure Tennessee, in 2015. His successor, Governor Bill Lee (Republican), has not pursued expansion.

Reality: Federal government pays 90% of costs. Tennessee would pay 10%. But Tennessee has preferred no expansion to 90% federally funded coverage.

The Maternal Mortality Crisis

The United States has the highest maternal mortality rate among high-income nations: 18.6 deaths per 100,000 live births in 2023, down from 22.3 in 2022 (CDC, as reported by AHA News, February 6, 2025).

But that’s an average. In much of the South, it’s far worse.

Maternal mortality rates (deaths per 100,000 live births, 2018–2022; CDC data compiled by KFF; U.S. 23.2):

Non-expansion states:

Tennessee: 41.1

Mississippi: 39.1

Alabama: 38.6

South Carolina: 32.3

Georgia: 32.1

Texas: 28.2

Southern states that did expand:

Arkansas: 38.3 (expanded 2014)

Louisiana: 37.3 (expanded 2016)

Other expansion states:

California: 10.5

Massachusetts: 16.4

Oregon: 16.6

Tennessee’s rate is nearly four times California’s.

Why? Poverty, chronic illness, and distance from care all matter, which is why Arkansas and Louisiana remain high despite expanding. But lack of coverage before pregnancy, lack of postpartum care, and the loss of rural obstetric services make it worse. Those are exactly the gaps Medicaid expansion fills.

Texas, specifically:

Texas has the highest uninsured rate in America: 16.8% of the population, about one Texan in six.

Texas maternal mortality rate: 28.2 per 100,000 live births (2018–2022), nearly triple California’s rate.

Texas Governor Greg Abbott (Republican): Has long opposed Medicaid expansion, arguing it would cost too much.

Cost of non-expansion to Texas:

The highest uninsured rate in the country

22 rural hospitals closed or converted since 2010

Billions in uncompensated care costs shifted to hospitals, emergency rooms and local taxpayers

Cost of expansion to Texas state budget:

Federal government pays 90%

Texas would pay 10%

Net result: Texas forgoes billions of dollars a year in federal funding to avoid paying a 10% share. The difference is made up through hospital cost-shifting (higher prices for everyone else), rural hospital closures, and preventable deaths.

The Politics of Cruelty

Let’s be clear about what’s happening: Republican legislatures, backed in eight of these states by Republican governors, are refusing federal money to cover their poorest residents because they don’t want to participate in “Obamacare.”

That’s it. That’s the reason.

Not cost — the federal government pays 90%.

Not effectiveness — expansion states have lower uninsured rates, and research links expansion to fewer deaths.

Not public opinion — every time voters in a red state have been asked whether to adopt expansion, they have said yes (see below).

It’s political theater. And people are dying for it.

The Stalling After Refusing

Here’s the twist: Even when the pressure becomes overwhelming, these states find ways to delay or dilute.

Georgia Governor Brian Kemp (Republican):

Refused full Medicaid expansion.

Instead created Georgia Pathways, which covers adults only up to the poverty line, and only if they meet a work requirement or qualify for an exemption. Enrollment has remained low (KFF, “How Many Uninsured Are in the Coverage Gap,” July 27, 2026).

Translation: Georgia turned down the 90% federal match for full expansion in favor of a smaller program built around paperwork.

Missouri Governor Mike Parson (Republican):

2020: Missouri voters passed Medicaid expansion by ballot initiative, Amendment 2 (53% yes, August 2020).

May 2021: The Republican legislature passed a budget without money for it, and Parson withdrew the state’s expansion plan.

July 22, 2021: The Missouri Supreme Court unanimously upheld the amendment (Ballotpedia News, July 26, 2021).

October 1, 2021: Coverage finally began, three months after the July 1 start date the voters had written into the constitution.

Result:

About 275,000 Missourians became eligible (NPR, July 22, 2021)

The state’s legislators had to accept what its voters had already decided

No apology for the delay.

The Ballot Initiative Strategy

When governors and legislatures refuse, voters sometimes override them.

States where voters passed Medicaid expansion by ballot initiative:

Maine (2017): 59% yes (Republican governor)

Idaho (2018): 61% yes (Republican trifecta state)

Nebraska (2018): 53% yes (Republican governor, officially nonpartisan legislature)

Utah (2018): 53% yes (Republican trifecta state)

Oklahoma (2020): 51% yes (Republican trifecta state)

Missouri (2020): 53% yes (Republican trifecta state)

South Dakota (2022): 56% yes (Republican trifecta state)

Pattern: Even in deep red states, even in states with Republican trifectas, voters have approved Medicaid expansion every time it was put to them for adoption.

But most of the holdouts don’t allow it. Texas, Tennessee, Georgia, Alabama, South Carolina, Kansas and Wisconsin have no citizen-initiated statewide ballot measures. Mississippi had one until its Supreme Court invalidated the process in May 2021, which ended a Medicaid expansion petition drive. Wyoming allows citizen-initiated statutes, but no expansion measure has reached its ballot. Florida does allow citizen initiatives, but amendments need 60% of the vote. After Governor Ron DeSantis signed H.B. 1205 in May 2025, restricting petition drives, the campaign Florida Decides Healthcare moved its target from 2026 to 2028 (CBS News Miami, September 25, 2025).

So Republican legislatures can ignore public opinion for years.

The County-Level Picture

Life expectancy isn’t just lower in some states. It’s dramatically lower in specific counties, particularly poor rural counties where hospital closures hit hardest.

The most detailed county study, by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (“Inequalities in Life Expectancy Among US Counties, 1980 to 2014,” JAMA Internal Medicine, May 2017), found a gap of about 20 years between the longest-lived and shortest-lived counties in 2014:

Summit County, Colorado: 86.8 years (wealthy ski-resort county)

Oglala Lakota County, South Dakota: 66.8 years (Pine Ridge Reservation, extreme poverty)

Some of that is wealth. Some is education. Some is environment.

But access to healthcare is a factor. And Medicaid expansion directly affects healthcare access.

The smoking gun:

The clearest evidence isn’t a single pair of towns. It is the mortality study above. Researchers compared expansion and non-expansion states before and after 2014, among older low-income adults who were otherwise similar. Expansion cut their death rates. Refusal cost about 15,600 lives in four years.

North Carolina, a state that changed its mind:

North Carolina’s Republican legislature passed Medicaid expansion in 2023, and Governor Roy Cooper (Democrat) signed it. Coverage began December 1, 2023.

By 2024, North Carolina’s uninsured rate was 8.6%, below its non-expansion neighbors Tennessee (9.7%), South Carolina (9.1%) and Georgia (12.0%).

The experiment is running in real time. We can see what happens when states expand versus refuse.

The Excuses Don’t Hold Up

Excuse 1: “We can’t afford it.”

Reality: Federal government pays 90% of costs. States pay 10%. But states save money on:

Uncompensated care costs (state and local funds that help hospitals cover unpaid care)

Mental health and substance-use treatment that the state would otherwise fund itself

Care for prisoners and other groups the state now pays for in full

Studies in several expansion states have found these savings, plus new tax revenue, offset much of the state’s 10% share.

Excuse 2: “It would blow up our budget.”

Reality: No state has repealed expansion because of cost. Arkansas has kept it since 2014, under governors of both parties.

Excuse 3: “Medicaid is broken, why expand a broken system?”

Reality: Medicaid costs far less per enrollee than private insurance, largely because it pays providers less. Its enrollees rate it well. In KFF’s 2023 consumer survey, 83% rated their coverage excellent or good, slightly ahead of people with employer insurance (80%). The “broken” complaints come mostly from providers upset about reimbursement rates. But hospitals still treat Medicaid patients, because even lower rates are better than no payment.

Excuse 4: “It’s a handout to lazy people who won’t work.”

Reality:

64% of working-age adults on Medicaid (excluding those on disability benefits or Medicare) work full or part time. Most of the rest are caregiving, ill or disabled, or in school (KFF, “Understanding the Intersection of Medicaid and Work: An Update,” May 30, 2025)

Many Medicaid recipients work but earn too little to afford private insurance

Medicaid work requirements have been tried. In Arkansas in 2018, nearly 17,000 people lost coverage in the last three months of the year alone, with no measurable gain in employment (New England Journal of Medicine, “Medicaid Work Requirements — Results from the First Year in Arkansas,” 2019)

The real reason: Ideology over outcomes. Refusing “Obamacare” matters more than covering constituents.

What Expansion Actually Delivers

Louisiana: Expanded Medicaid on July 1, 2016, under Governor John Bel Edwards (Democrat), after years of refusal under his Republican predecessor, Bobby Jindal.

Results:

Hundreds of thousands of Louisianans gained coverage

The Sheps Center lists only three rural hospital closures or conversions in Louisiana since 2005

State budget impact: the federal government pays 90%

Louisiana’s maternal mortality remains among the worst in the country. Coverage is necessary, not sufficient.

Virginia: Expanded Medicaid on January 1, 2019, after years of refusal. The Republican-controlled General Assembly passed expansion in its 2018 budget, with votes from both parties, and Governor Ralph Northam (Democrat) signed it.

Results:

Hundreds of thousands of Virginians gained coverage

The Sheps Center lists one rural hospital conversion in Virginia since 2005, and no complete closures

Arkansas: Expanded in 2014 under Governor Mike Beebe (Democrat) and a Republican legislature, through a “private option” that used Medicaid money to buy private plans. When Republicans took full control in 2015, Governor Asa Hutchinson kept the program, renamed it, and in 2018 added a work requirement. That requirement removed more than 18,000 people before a federal judge blocked it in March 2019.

Current status: Arkansas still has expansion, under Republican control, more than a decade on.

The 2025 Law That Changes the Math

The holdouts are no longer the only threat to Medicaid. On July 4, 2025, President Trump signed the budget reconciliation law Republicans called the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. It makes the biggest changes to Medicaid since the ACA (KFF, “Health Provisions in the 2025 Federal Budget Reconciliation Law”):

Work requirements: Expansion adults aged 19 to 64 must document about 80 hours a month of work, school or other qualifying activity, with exemptions for groups such as parents of children 13 and under and the medically frail. States must start by December 31, 2026, though the federal government can grant delays to the end of 2028. CBO estimates this alone leaves 5.3 million more people uninsured in 2034.

Update, October 2026: In June, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services issued a rule saying people with serious conditions such as cancer or H.I.V. would also have to prove the illness “significantly impairs” their ability to work before being exempted as “medically frail”. On October 29, Judge Richard G. Stearns of the U.S. District Court in Massachusetts blocked that rule in the 25 states and the District of Columbia that sued, finding that the administration had no authority to narrow the five groups Congress defined as medically frail (The New York Times, “Court Blocks Strict New Rule for Medicaid Work Requirements”, October 29, 2026). The ruling covers only the states that sued. The work requirement itself still takes effect in January, and CBO expects it to leave about five million people uninsured, many of them working but unable to manage the paperwork to prove it.

Six-month renewals: Expansion adults must re-prove eligibility every six months instead of every year, beginning with renewals on or after December 31, 2026.

Provider taxes: States can no longer create or raise the taxes on hospitals and other providers that help fund their share of Medicaid. In expansion states, the permitted rate falls from 6% to 3.5% between fiscal 2028 and 2032.

State-directed payments: Extra payments to hospitals are capped at 100% of Medicare rates in expansion states and 110% in non-expansion states.

Cost-sharing: Expansion adults between 100% and 138% of poverty can be charged up to $35 per service from October 2028.

The bonus disappears: The extra federal funding the American Rescue Plan offered states that newly expand ended on January 1, 2026.

A rural fund: A $50 billion Rural Health Transformation Program, spread over fiscal 2026 to 2030, which every state can apply to.

CBO estimates that the law cuts federal health spending by more than $1 trillion over ten years and leaves about 10 million more Americans uninsured by 2034.

The irony is hard to miss. The law asks more of the states that said yes than of the ones that said no. The work requirements, the six-month paperwork and the tighter provider-tax limits fall on expansion states. Non-expansion states are spared them, and keep a higher cap on hospital payments. The incentive to join has shrunk. And the $50 billion rural fund is small next to more than $1 trillion in federal health cuts.

The Question

Why do states that need healthcare most refuse to expand it?

The answer isn’t economic. It’s not pragmatic. It’s not based on public opinion.

It’s ideological purity. It’s tribal politics. It’s refusing to participate in “Obamacare” even when refusing kills your own constituents.

Republican legislators in these ten states, and the Republican governors in eight of them, would rather let rural hospitals close, let maternal mortality stay high, and let residents die from treatable conditions than accept 90% federally funded Medicaid expansion.

Because that would mean admitting the ACA does something good.

And that’s apparently unthinkable.
