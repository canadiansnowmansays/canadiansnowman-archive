Nine days. That was how long the Florida Docket sat in Highlands County Circuit Court before anyone touched the merits. On June 1, 2026, Attorney General James Uthmeier filed his 83-page complaint against OpenAI and Sam Altman personally. By the time this series last checked in, no motion to dismiss had been filed, no scheduling order had been entered, and the machinery of the case had not yet started.

It has started now. It just isn’t the machinery anyone expected.

On July 2, 2026, OpenAI filed a notice of removal, moving the case out of Highlands County and into the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Florida, Fort Pierce Division. Florida responded within days, asking the federal judge to send it right back. As of this writing, that judge has not ruled. The Florida Docket’s next chapter is not about sycophancy, product design, or Sam Altman’s state of mind. It is about which courthouse gets to ask those questions in the first place.

The Argument for Leaving

OpenAI’s removal notice rests on a single federal thread woven into an otherwise state-law complaint: the Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act, COPPA. Florida’s complaint alleges, among its ten counts, that OpenAI collected data from minors without meaningful parental oversight — an allegation that touches the same conduct COPPA was written to regulate. OpenAI’s theory is straightforward removal doctrine: when a state-law claim necessarily turns on a substantial, disputed question of federal law, a defendant can pull the case into federal court even though the plaintiff never filed a federal claim. It is not a frivolous argument. It is also not a slam dunk, and the fact that OpenAI reached for it says something about how it wants this fight conducted.

Federal court offers OpenAI several things state court in Highlands County does not. A federal judge, appointed for life, insulated from the political currents of a state where the Attorney General and the Governor have spent the better part of two years building a national profile around confronting technology companies. Federal procedural rules, which in some respects are less permissive of the kind of broad-based consumer-protection theory Florida is running. And — not incidentally — a slower, more heavily litigated path to any actual discovery, which is precisely the terrain where the Tumbler Ridge cases have already done Florida’s work for it. Every month the case spends litigating forum is a month OpenAI does not have to produce the internal safety-board documents Florida wants.

The Argument for Sending It Back

Florida’s remand motion argues the obvious: this is not a COPPA case. It is a Florida Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act case, brought under a state statute, alleging state-law causes of action — negligence, strict liability, fraudulent misrepresentation, public nuisance — that a state attorney general is entitled to bring in state court under a state consumer-protection framework built for exactly this purpose. The reference to data collection from minors is one allegation among many, not the spine of the complaint, and a defendant does not get to manufacture federal jurisdiction by pointing at a single sentence in an 83-page filing and declaring the whole case federalized. Courts have sent weaker removals back before. The well-pleaded complaint rule, in its ordinary application, favors the plaintiff’s chosen forum unless the federal question is unavoidable — and Florida’s lawyers are betting that ten counts of state law survive nine of ten being read the way Florida wrote them.

Why the Judge Matters Here

The judge who inherits this decision is Aileen Cannon, whose name Floridians and, by now, most of the country will recognize from a different docket entirely — she presided over the federal prosecution of Donald Trump’s handling of classified documents at Mar-a-Lago, a case she ultimately dismissed on grounds that drew sharp criticism from legal commentators across the ideological spectrum and an eventual reversal on appeal. That history tells you nothing directly about how she will rule on a removal motion in an unrelated products-liability case. It tells you a great deal about the scrutiny any ruling she issues here will receive, and about how little room she has to make a decision that looks anything other than carefully reasoned regardless of which way it goes. A quick remand looks like deference to a Republican state AG’s preferred forum in a case against a company the administration in Washington has been notably uninterested in regulating. A grant of removal looks like a federal judge with a complicated recent history pulling a high-profile state enforcement action into her own courtroom. Neither optic is one Cannon can be indifferent to, and it would not be surprising if this motion takes longer to resolve than the underlying merits question would suggest it should.

What Isn’t Moving While This Sits

The criminal investigation Uthmeier opened in April — into whether OpenAI bears responsibility for the 2025 Florida State University shooting — continues, according to Uthmeier’s own public statements, but has produced no public update since the civil complaint was filed. Grand jury proceedings, if any are underway, are secret by design; there is genuinely nothing to report here except that the absence of news is not the same as the absence of activity. No motion to dismiss has been filed in the civil case, and none can meaningfully be briefed while the forum question sits open — a defendant does not typically want to argue the merits of a complaint in a court it is simultaneously arguing lacks jurisdiction to hear it.

The Wider Field Moved Around the Standstill

While Florida’s own case sat in procedural limbo, three other developments landed in quick succession that bear directly on how any eventual Florida ruling — state or federal — will be read.

On June 8, OpenAI confidentially filed initial paperwork with the Securities and Exchange Commission targeting a public listing at a valuation reported to approach one trillion dollars. Four days later, on June 12, a coalition of forty-two state attorneys general, led by New York’s Letitia James, served OpenAI with a subpoena reaching further than Florida’s complaint ever did: advertising practices, user engagement and retention design, handling of consumer and health data, treatment of minors and seniors, and — named specifically — “model sycophancy,” the behavioral property of chatbots agreeing with and amplifying whatever a user brings to them rather than redirecting away from it. That subpoena is not litigation. It carries no damages, no injunction, no criminal exposure. But it is now a disclosure obligation OpenAI’s own securities lawyers must account for in whatever registration statement eventually goes public, and it demonstrates that Florida’s theory — that OpenAI’s design choices, not merely its content moderation, are the legally relevant conduct — has already spread to forty-one other state capitals without a single one of them having filed a lawsuit yet.

Then, on July 7, a government entered the field in a different country entirely. British Columbia’s Attorney General, Niki Sharma, announced that the province has retained outside counsel — in both British Columbia and California — to explore its own suit against OpenAI over the Tumbler Ridge shooting. Sharma’s language was pointed: British Columbians, she said, should not be left “bearing the costs of corporate wrongdoings,” and her office wants accountability for what she described as a missed opportunity to prevent the deaths of eight people, including six children. This is not one of the seven federal wrongful-death suits already filed by victims’ families in California — it is a sovereign government weighing whether to become a plaintiff on its own behalf, the same structural move Uthmeier made in Florida, translated into a different jurisdiction and a different legal system entirely.

Put together, these are three separate governments — Florida, a coalition of forty-two states, and now a Canadian province — independently arriving at the same conclusion: that the conduct worth suing over is not any single tragedy in isolation, but a pattern of design choices that produced multiple tragedies across multiple jurisdictions. Florida got there first. It is no longer alone, and it is no longer the most procedurally advanced of the three; the multistate subpoena and the B.C. announcement both moved forward in the weeks Florida’s own case spent arguing about which courthouse should hear it.

What to Watch

Judge Cannon’s ruling on remand is the single event that will determine this case’s shape for the next year. A remand sends the case back to Highlands County, where Florida’s UDAP theory gets to be tested on terrain built for exactly that kind of claim, likely faster and with a jury pool Uthmeier’s office would consider friendlier to the state’s framing. A denial keeps it in federal court, where COPPA’s specific requirements could end up doing more work than Florida’s broader deceptive-practices theory, and where OpenAI’s lawyers clearly believe they have better odds.

Either way, the case that looked nine days old and untouched when this series last checked in is no longer standing still. It has simply moved the fight one level up, from what OpenAI knew and when, to who gets to ask.
