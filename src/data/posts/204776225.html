We come now to the part of the series where the symptoms have been catalogued, the accountability assigned, and the question becomes the practical one that every reader has been quietly asking since the first essay: all right, but what do we do when the house is an oven? And the answer that leaps to every North American mind — just turn on the air conditioning — turns out, on inspection, to be the beginning of an argument rather than the end of one. There is no cleaner illustration of the gap this series is about than the fight, currently underway across Europe, over a machine that Americans regard as plumbing and the French regard as a referendum on civilization.

Let me set the scene, because it is genuinely one of the more revealing political episodes of the year. During the heat that opened this series, as France was recording its hottest day in history and some eighteen hundred schools were forced to close, the far-right leader Marine Le Pen went on television and delivered a line of pure populist clarity: “Air conditioning saves lives.” She called it absurd that the majority of French schools and hospitals still lacked it, and promised that if she ever reached the presidency she would install AC units across the country. Éric Ciotti, the rightist mayor of Nice, joined her in calling for a “grand national plan” for air conditioning. And the French left and centre, rather than dismissing this, found themselves in the uncomfortable position of having to respond to it — because, of course, air conditioning does save lives, and everyone knew it, and the people sweating in those classrooms were not in a philosophical mood.

Now, watch what happens next, because it is subtle and important. The Green Party leader, Marine Tondelier, did not say no. She said — and I am paraphrasing only slightly — yes, but. Yes to AC in schools and hospitals, but it cannot be the only answer; the real problem is that French buildings are poorly insulated, and the durable solution is to fix the buildings, not to bolt cooling units onto structures that leak heat like a sieve. This is, I want to stress, almost certainly the correct answer. It is also, as political rhetoric, a catastrophe — because “air conditioning saves lives” fits on a placard and “we must pursue a comprehensive thermal-renovation strategy for the building stock” fits on a doctoral thesis. We have met this asymmetry before, in the essay on the carbon tax that died: the true answer is complicated and the false-or-partial answer is simple, and simple wins the afternoon.

But the AC fight is richer than a mere contest of slogans, because air conditioning is genuinely, almost poetically, a trap — the purest example in all of climate policy of a solution that deepens the problem it solves. Run the logic. The planet warms, so you turn on the air conditioner. The air conditioner draws electricity, and unless that electricity is clean, generating it emits more carbon, which warms the planet, which makes you run the air conditioner harder. Worse, the units themselves are filled with refrigerant gases that are potent greenhouse agents in their own right, and they leak. And worst of all, the machine cools your room by pumping the heat out of it — into the street, into the city, so that every air conditioner is a small device for making the outdoors hotter in order to make the indoors cooler. A city full of them is a city actively heating its own air while each resident retreats from the result. It is the house built for winter rendered as a verb: a frantic, energy-hungry, collective effort to hold off a heat that the effort itself intensifies.

And this is not a marginal effect, a rounding error in the global energy account. It is becoming one of the central facts of the world’s electricity system. The International Energy Agency projects that the number of air conditioners in the world will grow from roughly 1.6 billion today to 5.6 billion by 2050 — which works out, the agency notes drily, to about ten new air conditioners sold every second for the next thirty years. Cooling already consumes around a tenth of all the electricity generated on Earth, and energy demand from air conditioning is on track to roughly triple by mid-century, requiring new generating capacity equivalent to the entire current electrical output of the United States, the European Union, and Japan combined. To put that in a frame that may startle readers of my Asimov Gap series, where I have written so much about the power hunger of artificial intelligence: the IEA reckons that the extra electricity demand from residential air conditioning by 2035 will be more than three times the extra demand from all the world’s computer data centres. We have all been worrying about the AI server farm. The air conditioner is the bigger story, and almost no one is watching it.

Here is where the French position, which is so easy for a sweating North American to mock, deserves more respect than it usually gets. The instinctive French resistance to air conditioning is not merely Gallic stubbornness or a romance with suffering, though there is some of both. It contains a real intuition: that adapting to climate change by each household privately air-conditioning its own bubble is a kind of surrender dressed as a solution — that it abandons the shared project of keeping the commons livable in favour of a thousand individual retreats, and that it does so using more of the very energy that caused the trouble. The French have a word, in effect, for the suspicion that AC is climate policy’s equivalent of bailing a boat with a bucket that has a hole in it. They are not entirely wrong.

And yet. And yet the people in those eighteen hundred closed schools are real, and the elderly who die in these heat waves are real — France has not forgotten the catastrophe of August 2003, when a heat wave killed some fifteen thousand people in that country alone, many of them old, many of them alone, in a nation that prided itself on caring for both. There is something almost obscene about telling a grandmother sweltering in a top-floor Paris apartment that her discomfort is a necessary stand for the thermal commons. Le Pen’s brutal little slogan has the force it has precisely because it is, at the level of the individual human body, true. Air conditioning saves lives. The grandmother should have it. The argument is over.

But before I resolve the French fight, I want to widen the lens, because the European argument over whether to have air conditioning is, viewed globally, a quarrel among the comfortable. The truly enormous moral question is not whether France should cool its schools but who, on a warming planet, gets to be cool at all. Today some 3.5 billion people live in the hot regions of the world, and only about fifteen per cent of them own an air conditioner. More than eighty per cent of the projected growth in cooling demand between now and 2050 will come from the emerging and developing economies — from India, Indonesia, sub-Saharan Africa, the places that did the least to cause the warming and will feel its heat most savagely. And the access is brutally unequal: in much of East Asia, a quarter of low-income households own a cooling unit against three-quarters of the rich. This is the part of the cooling story that should trouble a Western conscience most. We in the temperate, wealthy north are debating air conditioning as a lifestyle and an emissions question. For billions of people nearer the equator, it is becoming a question of survival, and most of them cannot afford the machine that would provide it. The heat does not distribute itself by guilt. It falls hardest on those with the least protection from it, which is the oldest and ugliest pattern in the whole climate story.

So which is it? Here, I think, is the honest resolution, and it is the one Tondelier was reaching for before the slogan ran her over. The French wrinkle that makes this almost tractable is that most of France’s electricity comes from nuclear power, which means — unusually — that the country can run a great deal of air conditioning without burning much additional carbon to do it. France is one of the few places on Earth where “just turn it on” is not automatically a climate betrayal, because the grid behind the switch is already largely decarbonized. Which reframes the entire fight: the question was never really air conditioning versus virtue. It was always air conditioning on a dirty grid versus air conditioning on a clean one — and the way out of the trap is not to deny people cooling but to clean the power that cools them, and to fix the buildings so they need less of it.

I should enter one sobering caveat here, because the heat wave that opened this series supplied it in real time. During the worst of the June heat, France had to throttle back a nuclear plant near Toulouse because the water of the Garonne, drawn on to cool the reactor, had itself grown too hot to do the job — and at the very same moment, surging demand for cooling caused nearly a hundred and twenty thousand French homes to lose power as the grid strained. There is a bitter lesson in that pairing. The clean nuclear grid that is supposed to power France’s air conditioning can be partly disabled by the very heat that makes the air conditioning necessary, precisely when it is needed most. A heat wave does not politely confine itself to the demand side of the ledger; it attacks the supply side too, warming the rivers that cool the reactors, sagging the transmission lines, buckling the infrastructure. So even the relatively happy French case — clean power behind the switch — is not the simple escape it appears. The heat comes for the solution as well as the problem. That is a theme this series keeps returning to, and the grid is merely its latest instance.

There is, as it happens, a single technology that threads this needle better than any other, and it deserves a mention because it is the closest thing this series has to good news: the heat pump. A heat pump is, in essence, an air conditioner that runs both directions — it cools in summer and heats in winter, at two or three times the efficiency of the systems it replaces, and it runs on electricity, which means that as the grid gets cleaner, so does your comfort, automatically, without your having to do anything. It is the rare device that answers both halves of the problem at once: it keeps the grandmother cool in July and warm in January, and it does so with a fraction of the energy and, on a clean grid, a fraction of the carbon. If there is a piece of hardware that belongs in the house we are going to have to build — the subject of the final essay in this series — it is this one. It is, you might say, the appliance for a house that has to handle both the old winter and the new summer.

That is the whole climate problem in a single appliance, which is why I have given it an entire essay. The air conditioner is not the enemy and it is not the saviour. It is a test of whether we can do two things at once — keep people alive in the heat and stop making the heat worse — at a moment when our politics can barely manage one thing at a time and rewards whoever promises the simpler half. Le Pen promised the cooling. Tondelier promised the renovation. The correct answer was both, powered cleanly, and “both, powered cleanly” has never once won an afternoon against “air conditioning saves lives.”

Dior, I should note, has reached her own settlement with this question. She has located the single air-conditioning vent in our house and lies upon it with the air of a creature who has read the entire policy debate and concluded that it does not concern her. She may be the only one of us thinking clearly.
