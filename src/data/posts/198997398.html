The Six Countries: The U.S. government proposed sending one man to all of them

The criminal indictment was only one instrument in the orchestra of coercion the government assembled against Kilmar Abrego Garcia. There was also the question of where to send him — and the answer changed depending on whether he was willing to cooperate with his own persecution.

To understand the coercion, you have to understand the legal architecture. Abrego Garcia’s 2019 withholding of removal barred the government from deporting him to El Salvador. It did not bar deportation altogether — it barred deportation to one specific country. In theory, the government could deport him to any other nation willing to accept him. This created a peculiar leverage: the government could threaten him with deportation to hostile, unfamiliar, or dangerous countries while simultaneously offering a more palatable destination — if he would only cooperate. The plea deal and the deportation threat were two instruments playing the same note: submit, or suffer.

Abrego Garcia pleaded not guilty to the smuggling charges. He was held in custody in Tennessee. As Al Jazeera reported, his own lawyers feared that if he were released from criminal detention, ICE would immediately re-detain him — which is exactly what happened. The government had constructed a legal corridor with no exit: criminal custody in Tennessee or immigration custody under ICE. Either way, he was behind walls. Either way, the government maintained control.

Kilmar Abrego Garcia arrives at a federal court in Tennessee on February 26, 2026 [George Walker IV/AP Photo]

On the Thursday before his scheduled release from pretrial detention in August 2025, the government presented a deal. It went like this: plead guilty to the felony smuggling charges. Accept deportation. The destination would be Costa Rica — a country that had agreed to accept him, grant him refugee status, and guarantee he would not be returned to El Salvador. The Costa Rican Ministry of Governance, Police, and Public Security had provided a letter ensuring that Abrego Garcia could live freely in the country with some form of legal status. The condition: he had to stay in jail through the weekend and accept the plea on Monday.

His lawyers communicated the offer but declined to agree to keeping him incarcerated over the weekend. They would present it to their client and let him decide.

As the Tennessee Lookout reported, Abrego Garcia was released on Friday, August 22, 2025, from a detention facility in Putnam County, Tennessee. He walked out the door — photographed by the press, flanked by supporters — to return to his family in Maryland. For the first time since March, he was not behind glass, not behind bars, not behind a border. He was, for a few hours, free.

Within minutes — minutes — of his release, a representative of Immigration and Customs Enforcement informed his attorneys that the government intended to deport him to Uganda and ordered him to report to ICE’s Baltimore field office on Monday.

Uganda. A country he had never visited. A country he had no connection to. A country on the other side of the world from his family in Maryland, his wife who is an American citizen, his children who are American citizens. A country where his safety and liberty would be, as his lawyers wrote, “under threat.” A country chosen, it appears, for no reason other than that it was not Costa Rica and was therefore punitive.

The sequence is worth dwelling on, because the sequence is the evidence. Thursday: Costa Rica. Freedom and safety, if he would only confess to a crime his lawyers say he did not commit. Friday: release from criminal custody, followed within minutes by the Uganda threat. Friday evening: the government informed Abrego Garcia that he had until Monday morning to accept the plea deal and the Costa Rica deportation, “or else that offer will be off the table forever.”

His lawyers wrote: “There can be only one interpretation of these events: the DOJ and ICE are using their collective powers to force Mr. Abrego to choose between a guilty plea followed by relative safety, or rendition to Uganda, where his safety and liberty would be under threat.” As TIME reported, the ultimatum was delivered with a deadline: Monday morning, or the Costa Rica offer disappears forever. The Thursday-Friday-Monday timeline was not accidental. It was designed to compress the decision window, to force a choice under pressure, to make the plea deal look like the only rational option. Plead guilty and go to Costa Rica, or refuse and go to Uganda. Confess or be exiled. Those were the terms offered by the Department of Justice of the United States of America to a man whom a federal judge had already found was wrongfully deported.

Abrego Garcia refused the deal.

Consider what that refusal means. He had spent three months in CECOT. He had been beaten, starved, sleep-deprived, and threatened with gang violence. He had lost thirty-one pounds. He had been separated from his wife and children for months. He was being offered a path to Costa Rica — a safe country, a country willing to give him refugee status, a country with beaches and a functioning democracy and no gangs hunting his family. All he had to do was say he was guilty of a crime he did not commit. And he said no. He said no because he was innocent, and because the rule of law meant something to him, and because — as Lincoln advised — he had put his feet in the right place and intended to stand firm.

On Monday, ICE arrested him at the Baltimore field office. Secretary of Homeland Security Kristi Noem announced the arrest on the department’s official social media account, calling him “a MS-13 gang member, human trafficker, wife beater, child predator.” No charges supported any of those labels. No court had found any of them to be true. The Department of Homeland Security was using its official communications platform to convict a man in public whom it could not convict in court. The social media post was shared and amplified by administration allies, creating a public narrative of dangerousness that had no basis in any judicial finding. The man they were describing — the gang member, the trafficker, the predator — was a sheet metal apprentice who had just walked out of a Tennessee jail to return to his wife and three children in Prince George’s County.

He was placed in immigration detention. A federal judge ordered his release. He was transferred to a facility in Virginia, then to Moshannon Valley, Pennsylvania — one of the most common sites for Maryland residents in immigration detention, since Maryland lawmakers had effectively outlawed long-term immigration detention in the state in 2021. The government announced it would deport him to Liberia.

Why Liberia? As the Baltimore Banner reported, a DOJ attorney wrote that “Liberia is a thriving democracy and one of the United States’s closest partners on the African continent.” It had provided “assurances about the treatment of people sent there.” Abrego Garcia had no ties to Liberia. He had no ties to any country in Africa. He did not speak any language spoken in Liberia. He had no family there, no community there, no prospect of employment or support there. His lawyers noted that Costa Rica remained willing to accept him with refugee protections — and that the Costa Rican government had signed a broader agreement with the United States to accept deportees who could not legally be returned to their home countries. The path was clear. The Trump administration insisted on Liberia.

The geography of the coercion tells its own story. El Salvador — illegal. Costa Rica — offered as a reward for a guilty plea, then withdrawn. Uganda — threatened within minutes of his release. Ghana — proposed and abandoned. Eswatini — proposed and abandoned. Liberia — insisted upon despite no connection, no plan, and no logic. Six countries across three continents, proposed for the deportation of one man, in a case where the government’s own lawyers admitted the original deportation was an error. If you plotted these destinations on a map — and I have — the pattern is not strategic. It is punitive. Each new country was further from anything Abrego Garcia knew, further from his family, further from any place where he might rebuild a life. The destinations were not chosen for him. They were chosen against him.

Judge Xinis, who had been presiding over the Maryland civil case since March, had seen enough. She noted that ICE had proposed El Salvador, then Uganda, then Ghana, then Eswatini, then Liberia — all without any concrete plan to execute any of them. As CBS Baltimore reported, her assessment was devastating in its plainness: “One empty threat after another to remove him to countries in Africa with no real chance of success.” She prohibited ICE from deporting or re-detaining Abrego Garcia, noting that the agency had no real plan — only threats.

In December 2025, a federal judge ordered Abrego Garcia released from immigration detention. He went home to Prince George’s County for Christmas — his first holiday with his family since March. His wife Jennifer spoke at a rally the following spring, describing the homecoming as “incredibly emotional.” In February 2026, another judge ruled that ICE could not re-detain him. For the first time in nearly a year, the government’s grip on Kilmar Abrego Garcia loosened.

His lawyer Simon Sandoval-Moshenberg, who had been part of a team of more than thirty attorneys defending one construction worker against the full weight of the federal government, made a statement to Maryland Matters that captures the absurdity of the government’s position better than any legal brief: “Our argument isn’t that he can’t be deported. Our argument is that he must be deported. But he must be deported to Costa Rica, because that’s the country that’s offered him safety. It’s been the U.S. government that’s keeping him in this country.”

Let that settle. The government that spent fifteen months trying to get rid of this man was the reason he was still here. Thirty lawyers defending one sheet metal worker. The government arguing with itself about where to send a man they could have sent to Costa Rica months ago, while paying lawyers on both sides with taxpayer money. The cruelty is obvious. The waste is staggering. And the absurdity — a government insisting on Liberia while Costa Rica waits with a welcome mat and a refugee certificate — is the kind of detail that would make Stephen Leacock set down his pen and say, “I couldn’t have invented this.”

The man the government called a dangerous gang member, a human trafficker, a predator — they couldn’t find a country willing to take him, they couldn’t sustain the charges against him, and they couldn’t even explain why they wouldn’t send him to the one country that had volunteered to receive him with protections.

And then, on May 22, 2026, the indictment fell.
