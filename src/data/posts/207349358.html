Stephen Gordon July 31, 2026

Markwayne Mullin took over the Department of Homeland Security promising the opposite of Kristi Noem. That is not spin on my part — it is what he told Congress, on the record, before he was ever confirmed. He called Noem’s requirement that she personally sign off on any contract over $100,000 what it was: micromanaging. He said ICE officers would come off the “front lines” and that the agency would lean on judicial warrants rather than the sweeping, made-for-television operations that defined her tenure. He said, more than once, that he did not want DHS in the headlines every single day the way it had been under her.

Nine days into the job, according to reporting at the time, he had already delivered on some of it. The contract policy changed. Some of the highest-visibility, Minneapolis-style citywide sweeps wound down. Republicans who had grown uneasy with Noem’s approach — including members who broadly support the administration’s immigration agenda — pushed Mullin to go further still, to review everything Noem had touched and refocus the agency on people who actually posed a public-safety risk rather than the broad dragnets that had produced Renee Good’s and Alex Pretti’s deaths in the first place.

I want to give Mullin’s early moves their due, because this series is not interested in pretending nothing changed. Something did change. The posture softened. The theatre of daily crisis, the kind that made Noem a fixture of cable news for the worst reasons, receded. That is a real and measurable shift, and it would be dishonest to write a series about accountability and pretend a genuine change in approach did not occur.

Beyond the contract policy, Mullin has also opened a review of immigration detention warehouse projects across the country — the kind of unglamorous, back-office change that rarely makes headlines but that Republican allies on the Hill say represents genuine seriousness, distinct from Noem’s preference for headline-driven operations. GOP Rep. Carlos Gimenez, who sits on the House committee overseeing DHS, put the ask to Mullin directly: review everything done under Noem, and be the agent of change Republicans wanted but did not get from her. Separately, Rep. Brian Fitzpatrick, a moderate Republican, introduced a bipartisan bill with Democratic Rep. Thomas Suozzi that would go considerably further than anything Mullin has done unilaterally — requiring agents to remove their masks in most encounters and mandating judicial warrants for criminal arrests, adopting reforms Democrats have pushed since the earliest days of the DHS funding standoff that followed Good’s and Pretti’s deaths. That a bill like this exists at all, with a Republican co-sponsor, tells you something about how far the political center has moved since January. That it exists as legislation rather than as settled DHS policy tells you it has not moved far enough to be adopted voluntarily.

Where the Promise Started to Slip

But six months is long enough to see where a promise gives way to instinct, and by early summer the gap between what Mullin said in his confirmation hearing and what he was actually doing had opened wide enough to notice.

Asked directly whether ICE agents could be stationed near polling places ahead of the midterms — a question he had already faced and deflected once during his own confirmation process — Mullin declined again to rule it out. Instead, in a Fox News interview, he pivoted to a broader argument about citizenship verification of voter rolls, telling the interviewer that DHS was actively reviewing voter information to ensure election integrity, without naming which states or which specific rolls he meant. It is worth noticing the rhetorical move here: a direct question about federal immigration agents’ physical presence at polling places was answered with a general claim about data review that nobody had asked about. That is not transparency. It is redirection, delivered by a man who had promised, under oath, that he wanted less controversy, not more careful management of it.

In late May, Mullin went further, telling Fox News he was drawing up plans to halt international flight processing at airports in cities he characterized as uncooperative “sanctuary cities” — a direct response to weekend protests outside a Newark detention facility, where a sitting U.S. senator was pepper-sprayed during an encounter with immigration agents. A former Obama-era DHS official’s assessment of the idea was blunt: of all the administration’s bad ideas, this one ranked. The practical effect, critics noted, would not touch immigration enforcement in any meaningful way. It would simply strand travelers and disrupt airlines serving cities the administration considered politically unfriendly — a policy whose stated justification did not survive even cursory scrutiny, proposed anyway, on television, by the man who had promised fewer headlines.

Then, in early June, came the moment that crystallized the gap most vividly, not because of what Mullin did but because of what happened to him in public. In a House committee hearing on departmental funding, Rep. Al Green of Texas called Mullin a racist to his face and told him to shut up. Mullin, visibly startled, absorbed it. It is worth noting who was delivering that accusation: Green has a documented history of aggressive floor confrontations, including being ejected from two consecutive presidential addresses to Congress, and had recently lost his own Democratic primary to a younger challenger — hardly a neutral or dispassionate witness. I raise that context because fairness requires it, not because it changes what the moment revealed. Whatever one thinks of Green’s temperament, the substance of his accusation landed in a committee room because the underlying distrust of DHS’s conduct toward immigrants and citizens alike was already sitting there, ready to be voiced by whoever next lost patience. The exchange made headlines for its raw hostility rather than its substance, but it is worth noting what it represented structurally: a member of Congress in an open committee hearing, on camera, expressing precisely the kind of institutional distrust toward the new DHS secretary that had defined the entire Noem era — proof that the change in personality at the top of the department had not, by June, translated into a change in how the department was perceived by the people meant to oversee it.

The Counter-Narrative Worth Taking Seriously

I do not want to present only one side of this. There is a genuine argument, made by conservative commentators and some administration allies, that Mullin’s approach represents real, substantive reform — that a quieter, more targeted enforcement posture focused on people with felony charges or convictions is not theatre-with-better-manners but an actual course correction, and a defensible one. One recent piece defending the administration’s approach argued explicitly that competent enforcement does not require theatrics, and credited Mullin with pursuing removals through consistency rather than spectacle after the controversies of the Noem era forced DHS to adjust its posture. That argument deserves to be represented here, not dismissed, because it reflects a real strand of opinion among people who support strict immigration enforcement but were genuinely uncomfortable with how Noem carried it out.

Where that argument runs into trouble, for the purposes of this series, is not on enforcement philosophy — reasonable people disagree about how aggressively immigration law should be enforced, and that disagreement is not what this series is about. It runs into trouble on the specific, narrower question this series has been tracking since Part I: accountability for the agents who use lethal force, and transparency about who they are and what they did. Nothing in Mullin’s tenure has changed the posture on Good’s car, on Pretti’s still-unnamed shooters, or on the DOJ investigations Part IV described. Those are not questions of enforcement philosophy. They are questions of whether the government answers for the people it has already killed, and on that specific axis, quieter enforcement and continued opacity are not in tension with each other. They can, and apparently do, coexist comfortably in the same department.

The Mask Changed. The Machinery Didn’t.

That is the through-line worth holding onto as this series moves toward its final parts. Mullin is, by every available account, a genuinely different personality running DHS than Noem was — calmer, more careful with his public statements, less inclined to reach immediately for a term like domestic terrorism before any evidence exists. But the department he runs still has not returned Renee Good’s car. It still has not named Alex Pretti’s shooters. It still has not answered, in any way Minnesota’s own investigators consider adequate, for what happened in January. A different face at the podium was never going to be the same thing as a different set of answers, and six months into Mullin’s tenure, the answers remain exactly where Noem left them: unspoken.

The next part of this series steps back from any single official to take the full measure of what this administration’s immigration enforcement has actually cost in lives — not just Good and Pretti, but the fuller ledger of deaths this series has referenced along the way, gathered in one place.
