This series began with a fire.

It is concluding with a funeral.

On Monday, May 19, 2026, two young men opened fire outside the Islamic Center of San Diego, killing three people. The shooters had expressed hatred for many groups — law enforcement officials said they had been radicalized by online vitriol and conspiracy theories — but it was Muslims they chose to target. Within hours, Laura Loomer, the Trump adviser whose trajectory from platform bans to White House access Part IX traced, called for the deportation of all Muslims and described them as an “invasive species.” She also sought to tie the mosque itself to the September 11 attacks, citing a connection that the 9/11 Commission had investigated and found innocent two decades ago.

A twelve-year-old boy who attended school on the mosque campus told his mother he felt unwanted. Then he said something else: “If this were to happen to anyone else, everyone would make a much bigger deal about it.”

He is twelve. He already understands the asterisk.

Families outside the Islamic Center of San Diego where shooters killed three people last week.Credit...John Francis Peters for The New York Times

In 2008, three men burned a mosque in Columbia, Tennessee. In 2026, the congressman whose district contains that mosque told its congregation — and the forty thousand Muslim Americans in Nashville, and every other Muslim in the country — that they do not belong in American society. The Speaker of the House said the only problem was the phrasing.

The fire and the post are separated by eighteen years. They are connected by the same mechanism — the same five-element template that this series has traced from the Puritans’ Boston Common through the Know-Nothing movement’s burning convents, through the federal government’s seizure of Mormon church assets, through the State Department’s obstruction of Jewish refugee visas, through the internment of Japanese Americans, through the surveillance apparatus built around American mosques after September 11, through the political normalization of explicit anti-Muslim bigotry in the decade that followed.

The mechanism is not new. What is new, in 2026, is that it is operating at full capacity — with the permission structure intact, the penalty structure dismantled, the person at the top either issuing the license or declining to revoke it, and a war providing the same cover that wars have always provided for the domestic persecution of communities associated, however remotely, with the external enemy.

This final part asks the question the series has been building toward: what would it actually mean to remove the asterisk? What would honoring the First Amendment’s promise — fully, without the fine print — require?

What the Counter-Tradition Has Built

Every chapter of this series has contained, alongside the history of persecution, a counter-tradition — the communities and individuals who refused the template, built something different, and sometimes succeeded in changing the terms of the argument.

Roger Williams, founder of Rhode Island and champion of conscience liberty — a cornerstone of the counter-tradition that refused the exception clause.

Roger Williams built a colony on the principle of separated conscience. It became Rhode Island. The Quakers who returned to Boston Common knowing they would be hanged made the cost of the hangings politically unsustainable. Anne Hutchinson’s insistence on procedural accountability — which law, specifically, have I violated? — established a standard that later civil liberties arguments would draw on. The Catholics and Mormons and Jews and Japanese Americans who went to court against governments that persecuted them did not always win. When they won, they established precedents. When they lost, they created records — documented the persecution, named the mechanism, made it visible.

Fred Korematsu spent sixty years fighting a conviction that resulted from remaining in the city where he was born. He filed amicus briefs in the Guantanamo cases in 2004, at the age of eighty-four, arguing that the military necessity exception being invoked to justify indefinite detention was the same exception that had been used to justify his internment. He died in 2005. The Supreme Court formally acknowledged that Korematsu had been “gravely wrong” in 2018. He did not live to see it. His advocacy made it possible.

The American Civil Liberties Union challenged the PATRIOT Act’s surveillance provisions through years of litigation that produced partial results, modified specific practices, and established procedural protections in specific contexts. The Council on American-Islamic Relations has documented anti-Muslim incidents systematically since 1994, creating the evidentiary record that civil rights litigation and congressional advocacy require. The lawyers who represented clients on the No-Fly List navigated a legal system designed to make their work impossible and occasionally forced the government to revise its procedures.

The counter-tradition is not a story of triumph. It is a story of incremental, contested, frequently reversed progress made by people who understood that the work would not be completed in their lifetimes and did it anyway.

The Legal Tools That Exist

The First Amendment’s religion clauses have been interpreted, over two and a half centuries, to produce a body of law that is more protective of religious freedom than it was in 1789 — but less protective than the text, read plainly, would seem to require.

The Reynolds belief-practice distinction has been qualified but not overruled. The Sherbert v. Verner decision of 1963 established that the government cannot substantially burden religious practice without a compelling interest and the use of the least restrictive means — a standard that, applied consistently, would require significant justification for many of the post-9/11 surveillance practices. Employment Division v. Smith in 1990 significantly weakened that protection, holding that neutral laws of general applicability can burden religious practice without triggering strict scrutiny, even if the burden falls disproportionately on a specific religious community. The Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993 attempted to restore the Sherbert standard legislatively. Its scope has been contested in court ever since.

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment applies to religious discrimination by government. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibits religious discrimination in employment. The Fair Housing Act prohibits it in housing. These legal tools exist. Their enforcement has been uneven — more robust for some communities than others, more effective in some periods than others.

What the legal tools cannot do, without consistent political will to deploy and enforce them, is close the gap between the promise and the practice. Laws that are on the books but not enforced, constitutional protections that are acknowledged in principle but not applied in practice, are the institutional form of the asterisk — the formal existence of the promise alongside the functional operation of its exception.

The Political Requirements

Removing the asterisk permanently — or as permanently as any political achievement can be — requires more than litigation. It requires a political environment in which religious bigotry carries genuine penalties at every level of the political system.

The penalty structure that partially constrained explicit anti-Muslim bigotry in mainstream politics before 2010 was not a sufficient protection. It was too easily dismantled, too dependent on the goodwill of specific political leaders, too susceptible to the discovery that the political market rewarded what it was supposed to penalize. What it failed to be was structural — embedded in institutional rules and consequences rather than in the discretionary judgments of party leaders.

The Republican Party’s removal of Steve King’s committee assignments in 2019 was the penalty structure working as designed. The failure to apply the same standard to Randy Fine, Andy Ogles, and the broader pattern of explicit anti-Muslim political speech in the years that followed was the penalty structure’s collapse. The difference between the two was not a change in the party’s formal rules. It was a change in the political calculation of the people at the top — a determination that the electoral benefits of tolerating explicit bigotry exceeded the institutional costs of the rebuke.

Reversing that calculation requires making the institutional costs real again — through primary challenges, through electoral consequences, through the sustained pressure of constituencies that include religious freedom as a genuine political priority rather than a rhetorical one. It requires the political equivalent of what Roger Williams built: institutions designed around principle rather than around the preferences of whoever currently controls them.

It also requires something that no law can mandate and no institution can guarantee: the willingness of members of the dominant culture to understand the exception clause as their problem, not only the problem of the communities it targets.

The Solidarity Requirement

Diverse faith communities standing together. The political coalitions that have, at key moments, expanded the scope of the promise.

Every chapter of this series has contained examples of people outside the targeted community who chose to intervene — who understood that the exception clause’s application to a specific community was a threat to the principle that protected everyone.

James Madison intervened on behalf of Baptist ministers in Virginia not because he was a Baptist but because he understood that the logic of religious establishment, once accepted, could be turned against any community. The abolitionists who supported Mormon civil liberties in the 1880s — a small and not particularly popular group — understood that a federal government empowered to dissolve a church and seize its assets could dissolve and seize anyone’s. The Protestant ministers who testified against the NYPD Demographics Unit understood that a government empowered to map a religious community for security purposes could map any religious community.

The solidarity is not altruism in the ordinary sense. It is the recognition that the principle at stake — the First Amendment’s actual promise, without the asterisk — is indivisible. A religious freedom that applies only to the religions that the dominant culture approves of is not religious freedom. It is a privilege, conditioned on continued approval, revocable at the discretion of whoever currently holds the power to revoke it.

That recognition has produced coalition at various points in American history — coalitions that have partially enforced the promise, expanded its scope, and occasionally closed part of the gap between the document and the practice. The coalitions have never been permanent. The work has never been finished. The asterisk has always found its way back.

The question for 2026 is whether the current moment — with a war providing the cover, the penalty structure dismantled, and the exception clause operating explicitly in the statements of elected officials — will produce the coalition that the counter-tradition requires, or whether the template will complete another cycle unchallenged.

The Promise, Read Without the Fine Print

The First Amendment says: “Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof.”

Read without the fine print, it means this: the government will not tell you which religion is correct. It will not establish a state church. It will not penalize you for the religion you practice, the mosque you attend, the church you join, the synagogue you belong to, or the absence of any of these. It will not require you to prove that your religious community is loyal before allowing you to build a house of worship where zoning permits. It will not map your religious community as a security environment on the assumption that your religion makes you a threat. It will not place you on a watchlist because of the mosque you attend. It will not require you to swear that your religious institution has no influence on your civic conduct as a condition of political participation.

That is what the promise says, read without the fine print.

It has never been fully honored. The Quakers on Boston Common knew it had not been honored. Fred Korematsu knew it. The passengers of the MS St. Louis knew it — they were not asking for the promise to be expanded; they were asking for existing law to be applied. The congregation in Columbia, Tennessee, that rebuilt their mosque and kept their plans secret to avoid a backlash, knows it.

The promise has been extended, incrementally, in every generation. Women received the vote. Black Americans received the legal recognition of their citizenship that the Fourteenth Amendment had promised a century before. The university quotas came down. The Japanese American internment was formally acknowledged as a constitutional wrong. The belief-practice distinction has been partially qualified. The surveillance apparatus has been partially constrained.

The extension has never been complete. The asterisk has never been fully removed. The question of whether the promise applies to everyone has been asked in every generation and answered, in every generation, with a partial yes that left someone outside.

The Work That Remains

Interfaith solidarity in prayer — the recognition that the First Amendment’s protection is indivisible.

This series has examined ten chapters of the same story. The story is not over. It will not be over when the current chapter ends — when the war in Iran concludes, when Andy Ogles loses an election or retires, when the specific political moment that produced his post recedes into history.

The template does not require a specific war or a specific politician. It requires a dominant religious culture willing to define belonging in terms that exclude, a political system willing to permit or enable that definition, and a targeted community whose resources for resistance are less than the resources being deployed against it. Those conditions have been met, repeatedly, throughout American history, and they will be met again.

As this series was being published — as Part IX described the permission structure at full capacity, the penalty structure dismantled, the license issued from the top — the template produced a body count in San Diego.

The Islamic Center of San Diego had been adding security features for years. Tall metal fences. Cameras. The mosque’s imam, Taha Hussane, said what the series has documented in analytical terms and what he understood in lived ones: “Looking at what’s happening, the madness of this world and the dehumanization of the Muslim community, you do expect something like this.” The Council on American-Islamic Relations recorded more civil rights complaints in 2025 than in any year since it began tracking them in 1996. The Islamophobia Index — a measure of public endorsement of anti-Muslim stereotypes — rose steadily from 2018 to 2025, with a sharp increase after 2022. A congressional subcommittee held a hearing earlier this month on the dangers of “the rise of Shariah law in America.”

The template does not require a specific shooter. It requires the five elements this series has identified: a dominant culture willing to define belonging in terms that exclude, a political system willing to permit that definition, a permission structure that licenses the expression, a penalty structure whose removal makes the expression cost-free, and a war that provides the cover. All five were in place on Monday morning in San Diego. The shooters supplied the sixth element — the one the template makes statistically inevitable but never specifically predicts.

Vice President Vance condemned the attack. He said he did not know “a single person who would say anything other than” that the violence was reprehensible. He may not know Laura Loomer’s timeline, which was saying quite a lot of other things. He may not read the congressional hearing transcripts, or the posts of the congressman from Tennessee whose district contains a mosque that was burned in 2008. The vice president’s statement was the George W. Bush play — the “Islam is Peace” move, the reassurance that the promise still holds. The difference between 2001 and 2026 is the number of people in the vice president’s own political coalition who have made clear, explicitly, that they do not agree.

What changes, from generation to generation, is the cost of meeting them. Roger Williams made expulsion from Massachusetts Bay more expensive than it had been before him. The Quakers made the hangings more expensive than the Puritan leadership expected. Fred Korematsu made the next Korematsu slightly more expensive, even if only by ensuring that the court that validated it would eventually have to acknowledge that it had been wrong. The Muslim civil liberties lawyers who challenged the No-Fly List made the government revise its procedures, not by winning comprehensively but by making the status quo expensive enough that revision was preferable.

This series has been written from Toronto — from outside the document, with the distance that provides. The distance is, as I have said, a form of clarity. But it is also a limitation. I am not the person being asked to prove my loyalty. I am not calculating whether the cost of speaking exceeds the cost of silence. I am not the congregation that rebuilt its mosque and kept the plans quiet. I am examining the mechanism from the outside, which means I can describe it but cannot, by that description alone, do anything about it.

What can be done about it is the work of the people inside the document — the people for whom the promise is not a historical artifact but a living claim, whose status as Americans is what the exception clause is designed to put in question. Their work is harder than mine. It always has been. It has also, repeatedly and incompletely, moved the line.

The promise says for everyone.

Whether it means it is a question that each generation answers by what it does — or fails to do — while the answer is still possible to change.
