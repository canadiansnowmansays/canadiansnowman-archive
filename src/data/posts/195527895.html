January 6, 2021 Capitol Riot: Crowd clashing with police or breaching barriers

Editor’s Note — May 4, 2026

This essay was written before Monday.

On May 4, 2026 — nine days after a gunman stormed the Washington Hilton security checkpoint — a second armed man exchanged fire with Secret Service agents near the Washington Monument. A teenage bystander was hit in the crossfire. Vice President Vance’s motorcade passed the scene minutes later. The suspect, identified as Michael Marx, was hospitalized and placed in custody.

Two incidents. Nine days apart.

Part I of this series opened with a premise: that political violence in America is no longer exceptional. That it has become structural. That the interval between incidents was shrinking.

We did not expect to be proven right this quickly.

The longer ledger that follows was compiled before today’s shooting. It does not need to be revised. It only needs to be read with the understanding that by the time you reach the final entry, the list may already be incomplete.

It would be convenient if this were a story about Donald Trump.

Convenient because Trump is singular, and singular problems imply singular solutions. Remove the man, change the dynamic, restore the norm. That’s the comforting logic, and it is wrong.

The crisis of political violence in America is not about one target. It is about a culture that is producing shooters, bombers, and attackers across the entire political spectrum, targeting officials at every level of government, with a frequency that would constitute a national emergency in any other developed democracy on earth.

Let us compile the ledger. Not exhaustively — exhaustive would require a document longer than this series — but sufficiently to establish the scope.

The Republican Side of the Ledger

2017 Congressional Baseball Shooting (James Hodgkinson/Steve Scalise): Crime scene at the Alexandria field with police tape, responders, and players.

June 14, 2017. A man named James Hodgkinson opened fire on a group of Republican members of Congress practising for the annual congressional baseball game at a field in Alexandria, Virginia. He had a rifle and a handgun. He had asked a bystander, before the shooting, whether the people on the field were Republicans or Democrats. Representative Steve Scalise of Louisiana was shot in the hip and nearly died. Four others were wounded. Hodgkinson was killed by Capitol Police.

The motive was unambiguous. Hodgkinson was a left-wing activist who had volunteered for Bernie Sanders’s 2016 presidential campaign and had posted extensively on social media about his hatred of Republicans. He had a list of Republican members of Congress in his pocket.

September 10, 2025. Charlie Kirk, the conservative activist and founder of Turning Point USA, was shot and killed by a sniper while answering a student’s question at an outdoor event at Utah Valley University in Orem, Utah. He was 31 years old, a father of two, and a confidant of President Trump. The fatal shot was fired from a rooftop approximately 175 yards away. Video of the killing spread across social media within minutes, exposing millions of people to the gruesome footage.

Tyler Robinson, 22, surrendered to police the following day after his parents recognized him from images released by investigators. The ammunition found with his weapon was engraved with taunting messages. Robinson has been charged with aggravated murder. Prosecutors are seeking the death penalty.

At the Washington Hilton on Saturday, April 25, 2026, Erika Kirk — Charlie’s widow — was a guest of Fox News when Cole Tomas Allen stormed the security checkpoint with guns and knives. She crawled beneath her table as Secret Service agents swarmed the ballroom. She was comforted by a Fox anchor while the chaos unfolded around her. One year after her husband was murdered for his politics, she found herself sheltering from gunfire at a political event.

That detail alone should be sufficient to end any debate about whether America has a political violence problem.

The Democratic Side of the Ledger

Paul Pelosi Attack (2022)

October 28, 2022. A man broke into the San Francisco home of Paul Pelosi, the husband of then-Speaker Nancy Pelosi, and attacked him with a hammer. Pelosi suffered a fractured skull and injuries to his hands and right arm. The attacker had zip ties and duct tape. He was looking for the Speaker. He told police he intended to hold her hostage and break her kneecaps.

June 14, 2025. In the early hours of a Saturday morning, Vance Luther Boelter — a 57-year-old man from rural Green Isle, Minnesota — disguised himself as a police officer, equipped himself with firearms and body armor, drove a fake squad car to the home of Minnesota state senator John Hoffman in Champlin, and shot Hoffman and his wife Yvette when they opened the door. Both survived. Yvette Hoffman later wrote that her husband had taken nine bullet hits and she had taken eight.

Boelter then drove to the nearby Brooklyn Park home of Melissa Hortman, the former Speaker and current leader of the state House Democratic caucus. When police arrived for a safety check, they found Boelter at the Hortmans’ front door, dressed in body armor, with emergency lights flashing on his vehicle. He fired into the home, killing Hortman, 55, and her husband Mark. Their golden retriever Gilbert was gravely wounded and later euthanized. In Boelter’s car, investigators found a hit list of approximately 70 names — abortion providers, pro-choice activists, and Democratic lawmakers in Minnesota and other states, including Governor Walz, Senators Klobuchar and Smith, and Representatives Ilhan Omar and Rashida Tlaib. It was the largest manhunt in Minnesota history.

In April 2025, the home of Governor Josh Shapiro of Pennsylvania was the target of an arson attack. The governor and his family were asleep inside. The attack was, like so many entries on this ledger, treated as a discrete incident rather than a data point in a trend.

A Kamala Harris campaign office in Arizona was hit by gunfire during the 2024 election cycle. No one was injured. The story lasted approximately one news cycle.

The Bipartisan Ledger

January 6, 2021. A mob of Trump supporters, incited by the president’s rhetoric and his refusal to accept the results of the 2020 election, stormed the United States Capitol. Approximately 150 police officers were injured. Some were beaten with flagpoles. Others were crushed in doorways. One officer was sprayed with a chemical agent and later died. Several rioters died as well. Members of Congress from both parties sheltered in secure locations while the building was overrun.

January 6th occupies a peculiar position in American memory. For some, it was an insurrection — an attempt to overthrow democratic governance. For others, it was a protest that got out of hand, or a justified response to a stolen election, or a false-flag operation, depending on how deep into the conspiracy ecosystem one is willing to wade. What it was, indisputably, was an act of mass political violence directed at the legislative branch of the United States government while it was in the process of certifying a presidential election.

The fact that this event remains subject to partisan interpretation — that a significant portion of the American public regards it as something other than an attack on democracy — is itself a data point in the ledger.

July 13, 2024. A gunman opened fire at a Trump rally in Butler, Pennsylvania, wounding the president’s ear and killing a bystander who had shielded his family. September 15, 2024. A second man was intercepted with a rifle at a Trump golf course in Florida. April 25, 2026. Cole Tomas Allen stormed the Washington Hilton during the White House Correspondents’ Association Dinner, armed with guns and knives, in what prosecutors have charged as an attempted assassination of the president. A Secret Service agent was shot. May 4, 2026. Michael Marx exchanged fire with Secret Service agents near the Washington Monument; a teenage bystander was wounded.

Four incidents targeting one president in twenty-two months.

The Pattern Above the Pattern

Now step back from the individual entries and look at the ledger as a whole.

A congressional baseball practice. A Speaker’s husband. A state lawmaker and her spouse. A governor’s home. A campaign office. A conservative activist. A president — four times. The Capitol itself. Members of Congress from both parties facing threats at a rate that has, according to the Capitol Police, increased by several hundred percent over the past decade.

This is not a partisan phenomenon. The instinct to assign blame to one side is powerful and, in specific cases, often warranted. The baseball shooting was left-wing. January 6th was right-wing. The Kirk assassination was carried out by a young man whose politics had veered left. The Hortman assassination was carried out by a right-wing extremist with a hit list. The Pelosi attack was motivated by right-wing conspiracy theories.

But the meta-pattern — the pattern above the pattern — is not left or right. It is a culture that has normalized the idea that political opponents are existential threats who deserve to be eliminated. The specific ideology of the person pulling the trigger varies. The underlying permission structure — the sense that violence is an acceptable response to political disagreement — is shared.

From Canada, this is perhaps the most visible and the most terrifying aspect of what is happening south of the border. It is not that one side is violent and the other is peaceful. It is that the entire temperature of American political life has risen to a point where violence has become a recurring feature of the system rather than an aberration within it.

The Broken Taboo

There is a term for this in political science. It is called “the breakdown of the taboo against political violence.” Every functioning democracy depends on an unwritten agreement that political disputes will be resolved through institutional means — elections, courts, legislatures, protests — and not through force. That agreement is not encoded in law. It is encoded in culture. It survives only as long as enough people believe in it.

The ledger suggests that the agreement is fracturing. Not evenly. Not symmetrically. Not in a way that permits easy equivalence between left and right. But fracturing nonetheless, across a broad enough front that the question is no longer whether the taboo will hold but how much further it can erode before the damage becomes irreversible.

Threats against members of Congress have skyrocketed. Elected officials at the state and local level report intimidation campaigns that were unthinkable a generation ago. School board members receive death threats over library books. Election workers have been driven from their positions by harassment. Poll workers wear body armour.

And a president — the most prominent political figure in the country — has now survived four violent incidents in less than two years, a rate of targeting that is historically unprecedented and that the political system has begun to metabolize as normal, the way one metabolizes a chronic illness rather than treating it as an emergency.

When the interval between assassination attempts is measured in days rather than decades, the taboo is not fracturing. It has already broken.
