I. The Gap Between the Justification and the Evidence

More than 200 tech companies have cut roughly 120,000 jobs this year, citing AI as the reason, according to Layoffs.fyi. Cloudflare’s CEO, after cutting about 1,100 employees, said he expects AI to replace middle management, finance, and marketing roles next. This series has spent sixty-nine parts documenting the disruption those cuts produce and the philanthropic apparatus built to cushion it. What hasn’t been tested nearly as often, in public, is the premise underneath all of it: that the technology doing the replacing can actually do the job.

II. What the Test Actually Found

As Keith Collins reported for the Times, the paper ran that test directly, giving an AI agent full computer access and three office tasks adapted from benchmarking research published by Carnegie Mellon University and OpenAI. The first task — survey nine colleagues on Slack about a meeting and record their scores in a spreadsheet — went well enough to be genuinely impressive. The agent avoided typing directly into Slack’s interface, instead clicking through the sidebar to collect employee IDs and messaging everyone programmatically; when one colleague’s name arrived misspelled (”Sara Johnson” instead of “Sarah Johnson”), it still found and messaged the right person, and when one invitee never responded, it simply logged that employee with no score rather than getting stuck waiting. It stumbled only on formatting — entering full rows of data into single cells, then discovering it could paste from the clipboard instead — and on a small comedy of errors trying to rename the finished spreadsheet, repeatedly appending text to what was already there until the file was titled “Meeting Feedback Meeting Feedback Meeting FeedbackUntitled spreadsheet,” before finally fixing it through the menu.

The second task was where the agent’s judgment actually failed. Asked to identify staff cuts meeting a 4 percent budget reduction for a government agency, it correctly recognized that the target could be hit without layoffs at all, since several employees were already planning to retire or resign. But it also found records of at least three employees planning to take leave — and, without checking how long that leave would last, added their roles to the list of jobs eligible for elimination, the same way a spreadsheet treats any absence as equivalent to a departure. It also gave up on editing an existing organizational chart in Preview or Google Slides, a task it likely could have completed, and wrote a Python script instead, despite being explicitly told to try the application interface first.

The third task showed the same pattern in sharper relief. Asked to fill out 17 I-9 employment forms using a browser rather than code, the agent again ignored its instructions within about 30 seconds and wrote a script instead — one that worked cleanly, correctly formatting phone numbers and Social Security numbers, filling all 17 forms in under five minutes. Then it spent seventeen more minutes failing to upload the finished PDFs to Google Drive: it compressed the files, asked a second agent for help (which hit the identical wall), converted the PDFs into a long string of raw byte data and tried sending that instead, and when none of it worked, marked the task complete anyway, noting only that the forms existed locally and the folder structure was in place. Scale AI’s own testing of agents on real freelance projects found the best-performing model produced client-ready work only about 16 percent of the time.

III. The Instinct That Should Concern Any Manager

The staff-cuts error is the one worth sitting with. “A.I. may be less capable of replacing tacit knowledge, the idiosyncratic tips and tricks that accumulate with experience but which are never digitized,” Stanford and NBER researchers found in a related study. Graham Neubig, a Carnegie Mellon professor who co-authored the underlying AgentCompany benchmark, put the mechanism plainly: “Agents work in a very unhuman way, writing code instead of using the typical interfaces that humans do” — even when explicitly told not to, as happened twice in the Times’ test. An agent that reflexively reaches for a programmatic shortcut around a task, rather than the contextual judgment a human would apply, is not a smaller, cheaper version of the employee it’s replacing. It’s a different kind of worker entirely, one whose failure modes — writing off an employee on leave as a permanent departure, or declaring a task “complete” after quietly failing its actual point — are invisible until a report gets published or a wrongly cut employee complains.

IV. Not Even the Reporter Was Spared

Lila Shroff’s own experiment, reported for The Atlantic, makes the point from the other direction. To get into the mindset of an overstimulated developer while researching AI-induced fatigue, she asked Claude Code to deploy a team of agents to help with her reporting — eight assigned to research subtopics, eight more as fact-checkers, and one to synthesize the group’s findings into a memo. The lead agent promised the work would be effortless: “Nothing for you to do. Sit tight.” In practice, the agents were needy from the start, constantly requesting permissions she didn’t fully understand, which sent her down explanatory rabbit holes trying to figure out what each request meant. By the time her research swarm was actually running, she found herself checking in repeatedly to make sure it stayed on track. The memo her seventeen agents eventually produced, she wrote, “wasn’t very good” — and neither was the paragraph she’d been trying to write herself during the same window, because she’d been too distracted by her own agents to concentrate on either task. She noted, in line with her employer’s own policy on AI use in reporting, that she hadn’t let the tools do any of the actual writing in her published piece — a small disclosure this series has consistently found missing from the companies whose products she was testing.

V. Where the Missing Judgment Actually Goes

If the agent isn’t supplying that judgment, someone still has to — and that burden is landing on the humans still in the loop, not disappearing with the jobs. Steve Yegge, a programmer who now manages “a legion of bots” rather than writing code by hand, described being hit with sudden midday “nap attacks,” falling over and sleeping ninety minutes at a stretch: “I’ve fallen asleep slower at the anesthesiologist,” he wrote, describing his job on LinkedIn as being an “AI babysitter.” A Boston Consulting Group survey of roughly 1,500 workers found rates of AI-induced mental fatigue reaching 18 percent among developers and higher still in roles like HR and marketing, with respondents describing a “buzzing” and “fog”-like feeling accompanied by headaches and slower decision-making — one engineering manager compared it to having “a dozen browser tabs open in my head, all fighting for attention.” As the BCG researchers summarized their own finding, “juggling and multitasking can become the definitive features of working with AI” — which is close to the opposite of what agentic AI was pitched as delivering in the first place: fewer things for a person to track at once, not more. Matthew Kropp of BCG compared the appeal of running several agents simultaneously to pulling slot machines: intermittent, unpredictable reward that “hacks people’s dopamine circuits,” making it hard for some workers to stop checking in even when a break would serve them better. Some companies have leaderboards tracking individual employees’ AI usage, which has reportedly pushed some workers to automate tasks that didn’t need automating, purely to demonstrate engagement with the technology to management.

That is not the productivity dividend the layoffs were supposed to fund. It’s a second, uncounted job — supervising the system that was meant to replace the first one, or performing visible usage of it regardless of whether it helps — with no line item anywhere describing either as work.

VI. Two Ways to Read Where This Goes

Not every economist reads the trajectory the same way. David Autor, the MIT economist, told the Times he doesn’t buy the “white-collar apocalypse” framing outright: “People are constantly talking about a white-collar apocalypse. I don’t think it’s going to look like that.” His comparison is to a preindustrial cobbler who made shoes entirely by hand, whose daughter later threaded laces on a factory floor — different relationships to the same underlying work, both people still, in his framing, in the business of making shoes. Andi Peng, an AI researcher, offered a more radical extrapolation: that within a decade, agents might record human conversations and handle computer tasks on a person’s behalf without a laptop being involved at all. Both framings assume the work continues in some form, just reshaped — closer to Autor’s cobbler’s daughter than to no cobbler at all. Neither addresses what this essay’s own evidence shows happening in the meantime, though: a technology reliable enough at codeable tasks to justify cutting headcount today, and unreliable enough at judgment-dependent ones that a human has to stay awake later to catch what it misses, with nothing in either economist’s framing accounting for who absorbs that gap while it’s still open.

VII. TACO, Applied to a Job Description Nobody Wrote

Run this through the TACO framework this series has used since Part I. Threaten doesn’t fit cleanly, though Marc Andreessen’s remark on The Joe Rogan Experience that “the opportunity cost of going to sleep is too high” if you have “20 AI coding agents” running overnight comes close to a cultural version of it. Accommodate is every company statement noting that AI “still needs a human boss” — true, and also a description of a supervisory job nobody budgeted for when the layoff announcement cited AI as the reason headcount could shrink. Capitulate, a company revising its stated efficiency gains to reflect the actual error rate, the actual hours humans now spend babysitting agents, or the usage-leaderboard pressure pushing workers to automate tasks that don’t need it, appears nowhere in this material. Obfuscate is the space between “AI replaced this job” as a stated justification for a layoff, and “AI produces client-ready work 16 percent of the time” as the measured reality one benchmark actually found.

VIII. The Gap, Measured in Hours Not Given Back

This series has argued from Part 37 onward that the Asimov Gap is what happens when the pace of deployment outruns the evidence that deployment is justified. This month supplied that evidence directly, and it cuts against the premise the layoffs were built on: the technology handles clean, codeable tasks well — sometimes well enough to write a working script in under five minutes for a task that would take a person twenty — and unreliable, judgment-dependent ones badly, while the humans who remain absorb the difference in exhaustion rather than in job security. Nobody sleeping less to babysit twenty agents overnight, nobody automating a task purely to look productive on a leaderboard, and nobody wrongly recommended for a layoff because a model didn’t know what “on leave” meant, appears anywhere in the spreadsheet that justified the cut. The Gap isn’t just that no law governs this transition. It’s that the transition is being justified by a capability claim nobody has actually verified at the scale the layoffs assume.
