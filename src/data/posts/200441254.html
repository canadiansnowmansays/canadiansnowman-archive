The God Bless the USA Bible, as rendered by AI — which is fitting, since the product itself is a rendering: a $12 King James text wearing a $59.99 costume. (AI-generated image)

In April 2024, Donald Trump launched a Bible.

Not a new translation. Not a scholarly edition with historical commentary. Not a devotional text with reflections by its endorser on the spiritual principles that have guided his life — a genre that, given the documented arc of that life, might have presented certain editorial challenges. What Trump launched was the God Bless the USA Bible: a King James text packaged with the United States Constitution, the Bill of Rights, the Declaration of Independence, the Pledge of Allegiance, and a handwritten version of the chorus of Lee Greenwood’s patriotic anthem “God Bless the USA.”

It retailed at $59.99.

“Happy Holy Week!” Trump posted on Truth Social when he announced it. “It’s my favourite book.”

The Bible is the best-selling book in the history of human publishing. It is available in virtually every bookshop, supermarket, hotel room, and online retailer in the English-speaking world, in editions ranging from a few dollars to the kind of leather-bound heirloom that sits on mantlepieces for generations. The King James Version specifically — the translation that Trump’s Bible uses — has been in continuous publication since 1611 and is available in perfectly serviceable editions for approximately twelve dollars.

The Trump Bible costs $59.99.

The difference between twelve dollars and $59.99 is not a better Bible. The text is identical. The difference is the name on the cover and everything that name is understood, by the people who pay the premium, to represent. The God Bless the USA Bible is not a religious product. It is the licensing model — the same model we examined in Part II, the same model that put the name on the towers and the hotels and the university — applied to scripture.

God’s word, endorsed by a man facing multiple felony indictments at the time of the launch, packaged with the founding documents of the republic, retailing at $59.99.

This is the branded life in its most concentrated form. And it tells you, if you are paying attention, everything about what the name has become, and everything about the market for it.

The arc of Trump’s consumer product career — from the buildings and hotels of the 1980s through the branded goods of the 2000s to the explicitly political merchandise of the 2020s — is a study in the progressive commodification of a single human identity, and the progressive narrowing of the market that will pay for that identity.

At the peak of the commercial brand, in the 1980s and 1990s, the Trump name had genuine crossover appeal. It meant something to aspirational buyers across a broad demographic range — people who had not yet arrived but intended to, people who associated gold lobbies and assertive wealth with their own ambitions, people who were buying not just a building or a hotel room but a proximity to a version of success that the culture was, in that particular decade, actively celebrating. The brand was expansive. It could be licensed broadly because it carried broadly understood associations.

Donald Trump launches Trump Steaks at The Sharper Image, 2007 — a steak sold through a gadget store, a sentence that made no more sense at the time.

By the 2000s, the commercial product extensions — the steaks, the vodka, the board game, the cologne, the university — were already revealing a narrowing. Trump Steaks sold through the Sharper Image. Trump Vodka discontinued in the American market, surviving in Israel as a kosher spirit. Trump: The Game, a board game about accumulating wealth, discontinued after modest sales. “Success by Trump” cologne, the name itself a challenge to any consumer attempting to maintain a straight face at the department store counter.

Each of these products was selling the same thing the buildings had sold: proximity to a mythology. The buyer of Trump cologne was not buying a better fragrance. The buyer of Trump Steaks was not buying a better piece of beef. They were buying the association — the implicit claim that the purchase connected them, however tenuously, to the world the name represented.

The market for that association proved, in each case, to be smaller than the brand’s promoters had anticipated. Not because the products were necessarily inferior — some of them were perfectly adequate in their categories — but because the association they were selling was, outside a specific demographic range, not something most consumers wanted to buy.

After 2016, that demographic range became the entirety of the market. And within that range, it became extraordinarily lucrative.

The Trump trading cards deserve a paragraph of their own, because they represent a kind of distilled perfection of the post-2016 branded life that rewards examination.

The Trump Digital Trading Cards, December 2022: $99 for the image of the man in the cape. There is nothing behind the card. That is the product.

Launched in December 2022, the Trump Digital Trading Cards were non-fungible tokens — digital collectibles sold on a blockchain platform — depicting Trump in a variety of heroic and fantastical poses: as a superhero, as an astronaut, as a cowboy, in a series of images that combined the aesthetic of a 1980s action figure catalogue with the visual grammar of authoritarian political iconography. They sold at $99 each. The first series sold out within hours, generating approximately $4.5 million. The series was later found to have used stock images in some of the designs, which is the kind of detail that, in a context of any other consumer product launch, might have prompted a recall or at minimum an apology. It prompted neither.

“There has never been anything like this,” Trump said in the video announcing the cards, which opened with Trump intoning “America needs a superhero” before the camera pulled back to reveal him in a cape.

This is technically true. There has never been anything quite like a former president selling digital trading cards depicting himself as a caped superhero to supporters who paid $99 each for the privilege, shortly after announcing his third campaign for the presidency. The novelty is not in dispute.

What the trading cards reveal, stripped of their surrealism, is the endpoint of the personal branding project. The buildings had the name on them because the name was supposed to certify quality. The trading cards have the name on them because the name is the entire content. There is nothing behind the card except the image of the man in the cape. There is no building, no hotel room, no steak, no educational credential, no product of any kind. There is only the face and the name and the $99 and the transaction that confirms, for the buyer, their membership in the community of people who believe in the face and the name.

This is not commerce in the ordinary sense. It is closer to devotional practice. The purchase of a Trump trading card is not materially different, in its psychological function, from the purchase of a religious icon — it is the acquisition of a physical token that represents and affirms an allegiance. The $99 is not the price of a product. It is a tithe.

The sneakers complete the picture.

The Never Surrender High Tops, launched at Sneaker Con Philadelphia, February 2024. $399. Gold. Of course they are.

In February 2024, at Sneaker Con in Philadelphia — an event dedicated to the buying, selling, and celebration of athletic footwear as a cultural artifact — Trump appeared to launch his personal line of sneakers. He was, at the time, facing four separate criminal indictments, a civil fraud verdict in New York, and a campaign trail that required the kind of sustained public performance that most seventy-seven-year-olds do not attempt.

He launched sneakers.

The Trump branded sneakers — retailing at $399 — were gold. High-tops, metallic gold, with an American flag on the ankle and the letter T on the toe. They were called “Never Surrender High Tops,” a name chosen to reference his ongoing legal situation in a way that his supporters would recognize and respond to. The name was not subtle. The sneakers were not subtle. The colour — the specific, unmistakable, Trump Tower lobby gold — was emphatically not subtle.

They sold out in hours.

The gold sneakers are the physical manifestation of everything this series has been arguing about the name and its relationship to the colour it travels with. The buildings are gold because gold announces significance to those who cannot otherwise demonstrate it. The sneakers are gold because gold is the colour of the brand and the brand is the man and the man needs, in every medium and on every surface available to him, to be the most visible thing in the room.

At $399 a pair, the sneakers were not selling footwear. They were selling a statement. The statement was: I wear these, therefore I am with him, therefore I believe in the mythology, therefore I am part of something larger than myself that is defined by this name and this colour and this man.

The sneakers are gold.

Of course they are.

The full catalogue of the branded life, assembled in one place, produces an effect that no individual item in it quite achieves alone.

Trump Steaks. Trump Vodka. Trump: The Game. Trump cologne — “Success by Trump.” Trump University. Trump Ice bottled water. Trump Mortgage, launched in 2006 and dissolved in 2007 after the housing market collapsed. Trump Airlines, which acquired the Eastern Air Lines Shuttle in 1988 and defaulted on its loans in 1990, the planes eventually repossessed by creditors. Trump Magazine. The Trump Network, a multi-level marketing scheme selling nutritional supplements. The digital trading cards. The gold sneakers. The God Bless the USA Bible at $59.99. The Trump-branded watch line. The Trump-branded fragrance line. The Trump-branded menswear line.

Assembled together, they constitute something that is not quite a business empire and not quite a personality cult and not quite a political movement, but partakes of all three. Each product is an iteration of the same proposition: the name is the value, the value is the name, and the buyer’s willingness to pay for the name is both the product and the proof.

The failures in this catalogue — and there are many — are not incidental to the pattern. They are the pattern. The brand survives the failures because the failures are absorbed into the mythology: the man who keeps trying, who never surrenders (see: sneakers), who builds and loses and builds again, whose reversals are proof of his resilience rather than evidence of his limitations. The bankruptcy filings — six of them, for various Trump corporate entities — become, in the brand mythology, not failures but pivots. The man who filed for Chapter 11 is the man who knows how to use the system. The man whose airline was repossessed is the man who reached for the sky.

This is the final and most sophisticated iteration of the licensing model: the name licenses not quality, not expertise, not a proven operational track record, but a story. The story is about a man who wants things very much, in gold, in large, and will not stop wanting them regardless of what the courts or the creditors or the voters or the federal judges say.

The story is compelling to its audience. The audience is willing to pay $59.99 for a Bible, $399 for gold sneakers, $99 for a digital image of the man in a cape.

The story has been running for fifty years and shows no sign of resolution.

Which is, if you think about it, exactly the point.

There is one more product in the branded life that this essay should close with, because it is the most recent and in some ways the most clarifying.

The Trump $250 bill.

Not yet legal. Requiring an act of Congress to exist. Breaking a prohibition on living persons appearing on currency that has held since 1866, when lawmakers first enshrined what George Washington had practiced as a principle — that the republic’s money belongs to the republic, not to whoever happens to be president when it is printed.

The proposed $250 bill mock-up, displayed at the White House. Serial number PT45474547DT1. The republic's money, at last, with a name on it

The design has been prepared. The Treasury Secretary confirmed it at a White House press briefing, holding up the mock-up like a product launch — gold ground, Trump’s portrait, Trump’s signature, “250” in large numerals for the anniversary, “AMERICA” across the face in the style of a branding exercise rather than a currency note.

The serial number on the mock-up is PT45474547DT1.

DT1.

Donald Trump. First.

The justification offered for the bill is the 250th anniversary of American independence — the semiquincentennial of 1776, the year the republic declared itself into existence and began the long, contested, imperfect project of self-governance that it is still conducting. The $250 denomination was chosen, its proponents explain, because 250 matches the anniversary. The timing places Trump on the currency that commemorates the revolution.

This justification contains, at its core, a profound historical inversion that deserves to be stated plainly.

The revolution of 1776 was fought, in significant part, against a king whose face was on everything — on the coins in every colonist’s pocket, on the royal seal above every official document, on the signage of every institution that operated in his name. The founders’ objection to George III was not merely about taxation without representation, though it was certainly about that. It was about the principle that a free people should not be governed by a man who had confused himself with the state — who treated the institutions of government as extensions of his personal authority rather than servants of the public that had created them. The revolution was, among other things, a rejection of the equation of the leader with the republic.

George Washington understood this with unusual precision, because he was the man most tempted by the opposite equation and most deliberately chose against it. He declined to be called king when the title was offered. He declined to put his face on the currency when the opportunity existed. He established the norm of the two-term presidency and the voluntary relinquishment of power specifically because he understood that the republic’s health depended on the clear separation between the person who held its offices and the institutions those offices served. The republic was not George Washington. George Washington served the republic. When his service was complete, he went home.

The $250 bill, proposed to commemorate the 250th anniversary of the revolution that established these principles, proposes to put the face of a living president on the currency of the republic he currently leads — breaking the prohibition that Washington established by practice in 1797 and that Congress codified in law in 1866. It uses 1776 as its occasion while reversing 1776’s most fundamental principle.

The name is on the money.

It is not yet legal money. It is a mock-up of money that would require a change in the law to become money, a change that Congress has not yet made, a change that breaks with a hundred and sixty years of deliberate American practice and two hundred and fifty years of the principle that practice was designed to protect.

But the design is ready.

The name is on it.

Of course it is.

Donald Trump has spent a lifetime writing his name on things. Towers. Golf courses. Hotels. A university. A Bible. A perfume called Success. A peace institute he gutted before the paint on the letters dried. A performing arts centre dedicated to a murdered president he has spent his entire career trying to impress.

The name is always the same: gold where possible, large where mandatory, permanent where permitted, and imposed where neither permission nor permanence was ever his to grant.

This series is about what that compulsion reveals — psychologically, politically, historically, and occasionally comically. It is not a flattering portrait.

Then again, neither is a loading dock on Bay Street.
