On July 28th, Vice President JD Vance — who has spent recent months as one of the administration’s more vocal critics of Israel, and who reportedly fumed earlier this month over what he characterized as an Israeli government-funded influence campaign targeting him personally — sat down in the Oval Office with Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. Afterward, a senior Israeli official, briefing reporters on the meeting, characterized the conversation as “good” and “honest,” and then, notably, declined to elaborate further.

This is not a sentence. This is a diplomatic crime scene with the chalk outline already drawn. Two adjectives, no verbs, no elaboration — and an entire relationship’s worth of tension folded into the negative space where a real description should be. In the spirit of public service, the Bureau of Undisclosed Elaboration offers the following authoritative glossary, so that citizens may translate their own diplomatic briefings going forward.

“Good” — adj. Nobody raised their voice loudly enough to be heard through the door. Distinct from “productive,” which implies something was accomplished, and from “warm,” which implies anyone involved was glad to be there. “Good” is the diplomatic equivalent of a doctor saying your test results are “not concerning,” which is not at all the same as saying they are fine.

“Honest” — adj. At least one party said something true and uncomfortable, and the other party did not storm out. This is, by any real standard, a low bar — most disagreements between colleagues clear it before lunch — but between a vice president who’s been publicly sniping at your government’s influence operations and a prime minister whose government has been running one, “honest” without a follow-up description is doing an enormous amount of quiet, exhausted labor.

“Characterized” — v. Chosen carefully, by someone whose job depends on the choosing. A “senior official” does not simply describe a meeting; he characterizes it, the way a lawyer characterizes a client’s whereabouts on the night in question. The verb itself is a tell.

“Without elaborating further” — adv. phrase. The single most informative four words in the entire briefing. When a diplomat volunteers two adjectives and then declines every opportunity to build on them, the silence is not an absence of information. It is the information. Ask any parent who has heard a teenager describe a party as “fine” and correctly concluded that something happened at that party.

“Frank exchange of views” — n. phr. (Not used this week, but held in reserve.) One step up from “honest.” Typically deployed when someone actually raised their voice, though rarely loud enough for the transcript.

“Constructive” — adj. Nobody signed anything, but nobody was asked to leave either. A meeting can be constructive the way a demolition can be constructive — technically building toward something, eventually, if you squint.

“We remain aligned on the fundamentals” — clause. We disagree on several fundamentals and have decided, for the cameras, to call them details instead.

“A senior official, speaking on condition of anonymity” — n. phr. Someone important enough to know exactly what was said, and cautious enough to make sure their name isn’t attached to the two adjectives they were willing to offer. The condition of anonymity is itself a form of elaboration: it tells you the honest part of “honest” was sharp enough that nobody wanted their name near it.

“We had a very direct conversation” — clause. (Held in reserve, not yet deployed this cycle, but a known escalation from “honest.”) Typically surfaces after “good and honest” has failed to hold for more than a news cycle, and someone needs a slightly more serious-sounding phrase for the same unspecified disagreement.

“There is no daylight between us” — clause. A phrase reserved for moments when there is, in fact, visible and measurable daylight between the two governments, and everyone in the briefing room knows it, and saying so anyway is understood by both sides as a kind of professional courtesy.

“Ironclad” — adj. Modifies “commitment,” almost exclusively, and almost exclusively in briefings issued shortly after someone has publicly wondered aloud whether the commitment is anything of the sort.

A SHORT HISTORY OF THE GENRE

None of this vocabulary is new, which is precisely why it’s worth learning to read. Diplomatic euphemism around the U.S.-Israel relationship has a long, almost liturgical history — decades of “candid,” “constructive,” and “close as ever,” deployed across administrations of both parties, regardless of what was actually happening in the room. What makes this week’s entry notable isn’t the vocabulary. It’s the contrast between the vocabulary and everything surrounding it: a vice president who has been unusually willing, by the standards of the office, to publicly grumble about an ally’s influence operations aimed at him personally, sitting across from a prime minister whose own government had, separately, been floating a desire to reduce its dependence on American money altogether. Two parties positioning for more independence from each other, in public, in the same news cycle as a meeting quietly filed under “good and honest.”

Ordinarily, when the underlying relationship is stable, the bland language is just habit — diplomats being diplomats, filling air with pleasantries because that is the job. It is when the underlying relationship is visibly straining that the same bland language starts to do real work, holding a lid on something that both sides have already, separately, told the public they’d like to renegotiate.

THE CONTEXT THE GLOSSARY IS QUIETLY CARRYING

None of this happens in a vacuum, which is precisely why the vagueness is doing so much work. Vance has spent weeks positioning himself as unusually willing, for a vice president, to publicly needle an ally — grumbling about influence campaigns aimed at his own office, a posture that is rare enough among American vice presidents that it counts as news whenever he does it. Netanyahu, for his part, arrived in Washington with an Israeli official separately noting the prime minister’s stated desire to wean the country off American aid — an odd thing to want to be quoted saying to the country currently supplying that aid, unless the quote itself was the message.

Put those two facts on either side of “good and honest, without elaborating further,” and the sentence stops being bland. It becomes the diplomatic equivalent of two people leaving a tense family dinner and telling the neighbors it was “nice to catch up.” Everyone in earshot understands exactly how much information is being withheld, and exactly why it’s being withheld with such polish.

A NOTE FOR FUTURE BRIEFINGS

The Bureau recommends the following standardized disclosure form for future senior officials wishing to characterize sensitive meetings without elaborating further:

☐ Good ☐ Honest ☐ Frank ☐ Constructive ☐ All of the above, which is another way of saying none of the above

Please initial one box. Elaboration remains optional, which is to say, forbidden.

WHY THE VAGUENESS IS THE STORY

There is an instinct, reading a briefing like this one, to wish for more detail — to want the senior official to have simply said what was said in the room, so the public could judge for itself whether “good and honest” was doing honest work or covering for something worse. But the vagueness itself is the more reliable signal. Officials elaborate freely when a meeting went well and they want credit for it; they elaborate freely, in a different register, when a meeting went badly and they want to control the damage. The genuinely uncomfortable middle ground — where something real and unflattering was said, but nobody involved is ready to have it attributed to them by name — is where the two-adjective, no-elaboration briefing lives. It is, in its own small way, more informative than a transcript would be, because a transcript can be spun. Silence, chosen this deliberately, mostly just sits there, daring you to notice it.

Two allies edging toward more distance from each other, describing the edging as “good” and “honest,” and declining, twice, to say anything further: that is not a diplomatic non-event. That is two governments agreeing, without saying so, that some things are better managed in adjectives than in sentences.
