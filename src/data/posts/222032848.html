Part 91 of this series covered the theology underneath tech messianism: Thiel’s private comments about the Antichrist, Andreessen’s Manifesto naming “existential risk” and “tech ethics” as enemies of progress. This entry covers an argument published the same week that reaches many of the same figures from an entirely different direction — not religion, but a specific, dateable cultural shift the writer traces back to how America chose to exit the pandemic.

The numbers that frame the whole argument

As David Wallace-Wells wrote in a New York Times newsletter column on September 16, 2026, the AI panic of mid-September is genuinely new, but the underlying risk estimates driving it are not. A large 2022 industry survey found AI researchers themselves estimated, on average, a 16 percent chance of human extinction or loss of control to AI; by 2024, that average had risen to 18.3 percent. Those numbers predate every incident this series has covered since Part 93 — which means the events of this summer didn’t create the fear so much as finally make the public notice a fear the industry had already been quietly living with for years. Wallace-Wells names the paradox this series has circled without ever stating quite this plainly: why would people who believe, on average, that their work carries close to a one-in-five chance of ending humanity be racing each other to build it faster rather than slower? Public opinion, in the meantime, has moved further than this series’ own account of the Times/Siena poll in Part 96 fully captured: Americans who think AI is moving too fast now outnumber those who think it’s moving too slow by roughly 33 to 1, and polling Wallace-Wells cites shows 68 percent support for an outright ban on superintelligence against just 25 percent opposition — a far more lopsided split than the near-even Republican divide this series documented in Part 96’s own account of the same underlying debate. The direction of that opinion is not new either. In a survey of 2,000 American adults taken between September 29 and October 5, 2025, the Future of Life Institute found that 64 percent said superhuman A.I. should not be developed until it is proven safe, or never, and only 5 percent favoured developing it “as quickly as possible.” The institute’s one-sentence Statement on Superintelligence, calling for “a prohibition on the development of superintelligence, not lifted before there is (1) broad scientific consensus that it will be done safely and controllably, and (2) strong public buy-in,” showed more than 76,000 signatures in early October 2026.

Why nothing happened despite four years of warning

The strangest part of the story, on Wallace-Wells’ account, isn’t that AI executives kept warning about catastrophe. It’s that almost nobody outside the industry treated those warnings as calling for the kind of response society still knows how to mount. He credits Kelsey Piper with a point this series’ own Part 103 arrived at independently while explaining recursive self-improvement: losing control of AI was never an accident the industry stumbled into. It was, on this reading, close to the plan from the start, since many of the biggest projected gains sit on the far side of exactly the RSI dynamic Part 103 spent an entire essay unpacking. Legal scholars Gabriel Weil and Lina Khan — the same Khan already quoted in Part 105’s account of Kayyem’s argument — have separately pointed out that existing law, however imperfectly suited to the technology, was never actually applied at scale to any of the incidents this series has documented. Wallace-Wells’ verdict on the four years since ChatGPT’s release is a phrase worth sitting with: “learned helplessness” — not a society that concluded regulation was impossible, but one that stopped asking the question at all, treating AI’s trajectory as closer to a natural process unfolding than a set of choices specific people were making.

The argument that actually explains it: Covid

Wallace-Wells’ central claim is the one that makes this essay worth its own place in this series rather than a footnote to Part 96 or 100: the reflexive, unquestioned acceleration this series has documented across the AI industry isn’t really an AI-specific phenomenon. It’s the AI-industry expression of a broader, dateable cultural turn against caution generally, one he traces to the specific way America exited the pandemic emergency. The critique of pandemic-era caution carried an unmistakable right-wing charge at first — Republican governors embarrassed by how strictly they’d locked down, plutocrats citing regulatory excess as their reason for swinging toward Trump — but Wallace-Wells is careful to document it as genuinely bipartisan: it was the Biden administration that formally declared the pandemic emergency over and quietly redefined infection levels once colour-coded as extremely high as benignly low. The same anti-safetyism shows up, in his account, across markets that have nothing to do with AI at all — the SPAC and NFT booms, crypto’s journey from regulatory pariah to bank-and-administration darling, the explosive growth of sports betting and prediction markets despite real evidence of gambling-driven bankruptcy and domestic harm. Nate Silver’s framing, which Wallace-Wells borrows directly, gives the whole pattern a memorable shorthand: the triumph of the risk-embracing gamblers of “the river” over the caution-minded “village.” Silver set out the distinction in his 2024 book On the Edge: The Art of Risking Everything (Penguin Press), written as a sympathetic tour of the river’s poker players, venture capitalists and effective altruists; Wallace-Wells uses it to describe a country that let the river set the terms.

That framing is what lets Wallace-Wells land, independently, on nearly the same figures and material this series already covered in Part 91’s account of tech messianism. He cites Andreessen’s own description of the shift as a declaration of independence not just from DEI and pandemic restrictions but from a longer, stronger liberal paternalism — the identical Manifesto Part 91 already quoted naming “existential risk” as an enemy. He cites Thiel’s high-profile lectures describing promises of safety and social protection as a sign of “the coming of the Antichrist” — the same Antichrist framing this series covered at length in Part 91’s own account of tech theology, where it surfaced in Thiel’s remarks about Greta Thunberg. That two separate writers, working independently from entirely different starting points — one from AI-industry reporting, one from a broader post-Covid cultural argument — converged on the identical two men and nearly identical language within the same week is itself a data point this series should take seriously: whatever is happening to AI safety culture specifically is downstream of something considerably larger than the AI industry’s own internal debates.

The river’s campaign budget

This essay is posted on November 3, 2026, the day of the American midterm elections, and the river has not left the outcome to chance. The super PAC Leading the Future, founded in August 2025 with backing from Andreessen Horowitz and OpenAI’s president, Greg Brockman, had drawn more than $125 million from OpenAI and Andreessen Horowitz figures by February 2026, Maria Curi reported for Axios. Its most visible target was Alex Bores, the New York assemblyman who co-wrote the state’s RAISE Act on frontier-model safety and then ran for Congress in Manhattan’s 12th District. By mid-June, Whitney Curry Wimbish reported in The American Prospect, the network had spent $7.6 million against him. On June 23, Bores lost the Democratic primary to Micah Lasher, Nicholas Fandos reported for The New York Times. Andreessen’s manifesto named “existential risk” an enemy; his firm’s money has since been spent against a legislator who wrote that risk into law.

The money does not run only one way, and this series has a disclosed interest in saying so. Anthropic, whose model co-writes these essays, gave $20 million in February to Public First Action, a group linked to one Democratic and one Republican super PAC that back candidates supporting A.I. safeguards, according to the same Axios report. The company said “vast resources have flowed to political organizations that oppose these efforts.” Either way, the question Wallace-Wells poses is being settled partly by the people with the most at stake in the answer.

The village has been answering in its own fashion, and closer to home. Pew Research Center reported on September 22, 2026, from a survey of 10,548 adults taken between July 20 and August 9, that 54 percent of Americans now say data centres are mostly bad for the environment, up from 39 percent in January, and that half say they push up household energy costs, up from 38 percent. Local opposition to the buildings has become one of the few places where the caution Wallace-Wells describes as lost is being exercised in public, at council meetings, by people who will have to live next to the result.

The choice this essay leaves standing

Wallace-Wells is careful to note the countervailing evidence his own thesis has to explain away — drug use and teen pregnancy are both down, and the MAHA movement around Robert F. Kennedy Jr., whose department is the subject of Part 100’s medical-AI essay, argues, in its own way, against underregulation rather than for it. But his closing argument is the one this essay should end on, because it reframes the entire premise this series has operated under since its earliest parts: safety and progress were never actually a binary choice, however convenient that framing has been for the people asking the public to accept one at the expense of the other. “We don’t have to choose between progress and safety,” he writes. “More to the point: in a democracy, or something close to it, we shouldn’t let others choose for us.” That is close to the thesis this series’ own title has been gesturing toward all along — the gap between what these systems can do and what any accountable structure has required of them — stated, for once, as a choice still available rather than a foregone conclusion.
