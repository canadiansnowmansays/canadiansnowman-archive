Quebec separatism has a poet’s vocabulary. Western separatism has an accountant’s. Its founding documents are freight tariffs, royalty schedules and equalization formulas, and its great cry of grievance was printed on a bumper sticker. People cheated in the ledger feel it as sharply as people insulted in their language, but it changes what the movement is for. You can satisfy a ledger. Keep that in mind as Albertans vote on October 19, 2026, on whether to begin the process of voting on whether to leave.

Born Without the Keys

The western complaint is older than the oil. Sir John A. Macdonald’s National Policy tariffs of 1879 protected factories in Ontario and Quebec and obliged prairie farmers to buy their ploughs from those factories at protected prices while selling their wheat on an unprotected world market. The Crow’s Nest Pass Agreement of September 6, 1897 was the first great western bargain with Ottawa: in exchange for public money to build a line through the Rockies, the Canadian Pacific Railway agreed to haul prairie grain east and farm machinery west at reduced rates “forever.” Forever lasted until the Western Grain Transportation Act of 1983 began raising the rates and the Chrétien government abolished the subsidy in 1995.

Then there was the matter of the land itself. Ontario, Quebec and the Maritime provinces entered Confederation owning their Crown lands and natural resources. Manitoba in 1870, and Alberta and Saskatchewan when they were created on September 1, 1905, did not. Ottawa kept them. Alberta was a province in name and a federal estate in fact for a quarter-century, until Premier John Brownlee signed the Natural Resources Transfer Agreement on December 14, 1929, taking effect on October 1, 1930, as Stacy F. Kaufeld recounts in “Born Unequal: Alberta and the Natural Resources Transfer Agreement” for the Legal Archives Society of Alberta. The political scientist Tom Flanagan has called it Alberta’s real constitution. Every modern Alberta fight with Ottawa is, at bottom, about who controls what came out of the ground after 1930.

Oil and the Man From Calgary

In 1947 the Leduc No. 1 well came in south of Edmonton. By 1973, when the Arab oil embargo quadrupled world prices, Alberta was the country’s energy cupboard, and Pierre Trudeau’s government decided the windfall belonged partly to the country. Ottawa held domestic prices below world levels and imposed an export tax on Alberta crude.

Peter Lougheed, a Calgary lawyer who had ended thirty-six years of Social Credit rule in 1971, called the export tax, in a Canadian Club speech on September 14, 1973, “the most discriminatory action taken by a federal government against a particular province” in Canadian history, as the Wikipedia biography of Peter Lougheed records. Then he raised Alberta’s royalties. Lougheed was never a separatist. He was the first western premier to understand that the province held a lever, and that the lever was a valve.

October 28, 1980

He pulled it seven years later. On October 28, 1980, Finance Minister Allan MacEachen’s budget introduced the National Energy Program. It aimed at 50 per cent Canadian ownership of oil and gas production by 1990, kept domestic prices below world prices, and created a new Petroleum and Gas Revenue Tax, as the Wikipedia article “National Energy Program” summarizes. To Alberta, it was Ottawa reaching into a provincial resource for the second time in seven years.

On October 30, Lougheed went on television and announced that Alberta would cut oil production by 60,000 barrels a day in stages over nine months beginning in April 1981, forcing the country to buy more imported oil, and suspended two oil sands projects. It was a provincial strike, and it worked. In September 1981 Lougheed and Trudeau signed a revenue-sharing agreement and were photographed toasting it with champagne, a picture Lougheed later named among his greatest regrets, according to CBC News‘s longform feature “Lougheed, Trudeau and the notorious NEP,” published October 24, 2020.

The same feature lays out the wreckage, not all of it Ottawa’s doing, since a global recession and punishing interest rates arrived at once. Drilling rigs in Alberta fell from about 550 in 1980 to 120 by 1982, unemployment passed 10 per cent, and the population shrank for the first time since the Second World War. The economist Robert Mansell has estimated the NEP cost Alberta roughly $100 billion. The bumper sticker read “Let the eastern bastards freeze in the dark.” Brian Mulroney’s Western Accord on Energy ended the programme on June 1, 1985. The memory has never been dismantled.

The Concept

The NEP produced the West’s first real separatist moment, and its fastest collapse. Elmer Knutson’s Western Canada Federation, West-Fed, filled halls that winter; in December 1980 more than 650 people braved the weather to hear him in Eckville, as the CBC Digital Archives feature “In the ‘70s and ‘80s, some wanted Alberta to separate from Canada” shows. A Canada West Foundation survey in 1981 found 49 per cent of western respondents agreeing that the West got so few benefits from Canada it might as well go it alone. That measured a sentiment, not a vote.

On February 17, 1982, the sentiment briefly became a seat. Gordon Kesler of the Western Canada Concept won the provincial by-election in Olds-Didsbury with 4,015 votes, 42.2 per cent, beating the Social Credit and Progressive Conservative candidates, as the Wikipedia biography of Gordon Kesler details. His causes ran heavily to opposing bilingualism and the metric system, which tells you something about the anger. In the general election that November, Lougheed won 75 of 79 seats. The Western Canada Concept took 11.8 per cent and no seats; Kesler, running in Highwood, finished a distant second. His legislative career lasted about nine and a half months. Lougheed had shown that a premier who fought Ottawa hard enough could swallow the separatists whole.

The West Wants In

The next wave did not ask to leave. A Vancouver assembly in May 1987 led to a founding convention in Winnipeg, where Preston Manning, son of the Social Credit premier Ernest Manning, was named leader of the new Reform Party on November 1, 1987, as the Wikipedia article “Reform Party of Canada” sets out. Its 1988 slogan was four words long: “The West wants in.”

It is the most honest summary of western separatism ever written, by people who were not separatists. Reform wanted a Triple-E Senate, fiscal restraint and an end to Ottawa’s preoccupation with Quebec. It won 52 seats in 1993 and 60 in 1997, becoming the Official Opposition. The slogan’s author, Reform’s young chief policy officer Stephen Harper, became prime minister in 2006, as the Wikipedia biography of Stephen Harper notes. The West did not get out. It got in, all the way to 24 Sussex.

Between those two points came the firewall letter. On January 24, 2001, after Jean Chrétien’s third majority, Harper, Tom Flanagan, Ted Morton, Rainer Knopff, Andrew Crooks and Ken Boessenkool wrote to Premier Ralph Klein urging him to “build firewalls around Alberta”: a provincial pension plan to replace the Canada Pension Plan, a provincial police force to replace the RCMP, provincial collection of income tax, provincial control of health care and Senate reform. The National Post published it on January 27, 2001, as the Wikipedia article “Alberta Agenda” records. Klein filed it. A quarter-century later it would come back almost word for word.

Wexit, and the Equalization Vote

When Justin Trudeau, son of the author of the NEP, was re-elected with a minority on October 21, 2019, and shut out of Alberta and Saskatchewan, Peter Downing founded Wexit Alberta within weeks; an Ipsos poll that autumn found 33 per cent of Albertans interested in secession. Rebranded in September 2020 as the Maverick Party, it won 35,178 votes nationally and no seats in the 2021 federal election, and Elections Canada deregistered it on February 28, 2025, for failing to file its financial returns, according to the Wikipedia article “Maverick Party”.

Jason Kenney, who had promised Albertans a “fair deal,” gave them a referendum. On October 18, 2021, voters were asked whether section 36(2) of the Constitution Act, 1982, the federal commitment to the principle of equalization, should be removed. The yes side won 61.7 per cent, 642,501 votes to 399,169, according to Elections Alberta‘s “Referendum Results” page, on a turnout of about 39 per cent.

It changed nothing, and everyone knew it would. One province cannot amend the Constitution; equalization is paid from federal revenues, not cheques written by Alberta; and Trudeau noted, with some relish, that the formula had last been updated when Kenney sat in Harper’s cabinet. The legislature passed a motion on November 18, 2021, demanding negotiations; Ottawa, in 2023, moved to lock the existing formula in until 2029. The equalization referendum was the purest example of the genre: a vote held to be photographed holding the result.

Sovereignty Within

Danielle Smith won the United Conservative leadership in October 2022 on the firewall programme with a harder edge. Her Alberta Sovereignty Within a United Canada Act was introduced on November 29, 2022 and received royal assent on December 15, 2022, as the Wikipedia article on the Act records. It lets the legislature direct provincial bodies not to enforce a federal measure it deems harmful. It was first invoked in late November 2023 against Ottawa’s Clean Electricity Regulations, as David Baxter reported for Global News in “Alberta uses Sovereignty Act for 1st time. What happens now?” on November 28, 2023. “Within a United Canada” is the West wants in, rendered in statute.

Then came the Alberta Next panel, announced on June 24, 2025, which toured the province asking about a provincial pension plan, a provincial police force, immigration and constitutional reform. The firewall letter had become a road show. Its $2-million report, released the Friday before Christmas 2025, showed in its own survey data majorities opposed to the pension plan and the police force, as David Climenhaga wrote in “Danielle Smith’s Alberta Next Panel Report Lands with a Whimper” for The Tyee on December 22, 2025.

The Petitions

The Alberta Prosperity Project, founded in 2021 and led by Mitch Sylvestre, a Bonnyville businessman who also chaired the local UCP constituency association, advocated independence “within or outside Canada,” a phrase to puzzle a geographer. In 2025 the government’s Bill 54 lowered the threshold for citizen-initiated referendums to 10 per cent of the votes cast in the previous election. Sylvestre’s Stay Free Alberta campaign delivered 301,062 signatures to Elections Alberta, against a threshold of 177,732, as Matthew Black reported in the Edmonton Journal on May 4, 2026, in “Stay Free Alberta delivers 301K signatures to Edmonton in support of separation”. A rival remain-in-Canada petition led by former deputy premier Thomas Lukaszuk gathered more than 400,000.

On May 13, 2026, Justice Shaina Leonard of the Court of King’s Bench quashed the separatist petition for failing to consult Treaty First Nations, a ruling Part VI will return to. On May 21, Smith put her own question on the October ballot anyway, borrowing Lukaszuk’s language and saying she would personally vote to stay, as the Red Deer Advocate reported in “Alberta Premier Danielle Smith commits to separation referendum” on May 21, 2026. On June 29, Justice Alice Woolley of the Court of Appeal let Elections Alberta finish counting Stay Free’s signatures but barred the result from going to the minister, as Kevin Sabo reported for the Stettler Independent. The count was declared successful in late July. By then Smith’s question was already on the ballot.

That question, one of ten Albertans will answer on October 19, asks whether Alberta should “remain a province of Canada” or whether the government should “commence the legal process required under the Canadian Constitution to hold a binding provincial referendum” on separation, as the Wikipedia article “2026 Alberta independence referendum” quotes it. A referendum on a referendum on a negotiation. If ever a man in a Red Deer Tim Hortons were entitled to look up from his double-double and say “Again? Must be Tuesday,” it is now.

Washington Calls

The new ingredient is American attention. Alberta Prosperity Project representatives, among them Dennis Modry and the lawyer Jeffrey Rath, met State Department officials on April 22, September 29 and December 16, 2025, as NBC News reported in “Canadian separatists optimistic after meetings with Trump officials” in February 2026. At Davos in January 2026, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told the podcaster Jack Posobiec that Alberta was a natural partner for the United States and that Albertans were very independent people, as Axios reported in “Albertan separatists court Trump while he feuds with Canada” on January 29, 2026. Part VII will return to it. Brown suggests the trade fight with Washington has hardened Albertans’ attachment to Canada.

What the Polls Say

Janet Brown Opinion Research, polling 1,200 Albertans for CBC News between September 8 and 21, 2026, found 72 per cent would vote to remain and 23 per cent to start the separation process, against 67 and 27 in April; among UCP supporters, though, 53 per cent favoured separation. Research Co. had 74 to 22 in late August, and Éric Grenier’s Alberta Referendum Poll Tracker at The Writ put remain at just over two-thirds as of September 25.

The most revealing figure came from the Angus Reid Institute on August 14, 2026, which found a narrower 61 to 33 but asked the separation side why. Forty-one per cent of them said their vote was about “sending a message to Ottawa,” according to the Angus Reid Institute‘s “Alberta vote: ‘Stay’ outweighs ‘Leave’ 2:1”. Asked a plain question about leaving Canada, only 30 per cent said yes.

Two in five of the people voting to begin leaving do not want to leave. They want to be heard, which is to say they want in.

The Valve

The pattern is almost embarrassingly regular. Western separatist sentiment spiked after the 1973 export tax, higher after the NEP, again after the Liberal shutout of 2019, and more modestly now. Each time the trigger was an Ottawa that looked hostile to oil. Each time the movement either collapsed (West-Fed, the Western Canada Concept, Wexit) or was absorbed by a premier or party that turned it into leverage (Lougheed, Reform, Kenney, Smith). And each time the West got something: the 1981 revenue deal, the Western Accord, a prime minister from Calgary, a Sovereignty Act, a ballot question.

That is the argument of this series, and the West makes it more cleanly than Quebec. Quebec’s sovereigntists have at least wanted a country. Alberta’s loudest voices have mostly wanted a better deal from the one they have, and found that the most effective way to ask is to threaten to leave. The danger every bluffer knows is that someone eventually calls. On October 19, if the polls hold, three in four Albertans will say the West still wants in. Whose land Alberta would take with it if it ever went is the subject of Part VI.
