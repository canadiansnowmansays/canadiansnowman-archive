Nvidia announced it would back one of the largest data centers ever built — a Piketon, Ohio site with a total project cost that could run as high as $500 billion — with up to $105 billion of its own money. The site will be leased to OpenAI. It will run exclusively on Nvidia chips. And Jensen Huang, Nvidia’s chief executive, went out of his way in the announcement to insist that none of this constitutes the circular financing this series has spent three prior entries documenting across the rest of the industry.

I want to note, briefly, the image that accompanied this particular announcement, because it says something the press release doesn’t. Photographs from the Piketon groundbreaking show excavators hoisting an American flag between their raised buckets, SoftBank Group and SB Energy branding on stage banners, construction crews standing beside a genuinely rural Ohio backdrop — grass, power lines, a modest industrial skyline in the distance. It is the aesthetic register of a factory opening from an earlier American industrial era, deliberately invoked for a project that is, functionally, a half-trillion-dollar financial instrument wearing a hard hat. That framing is not an accident. A groundbreaking ceremony with an American flag and heavy machinery photographs as regional economic revival. A circular financing arrangement between a chipmaker, a conglomerate subsidiary, and an AI lab photographs as nothing at all, which is presumably why nobody hoists a balance sheet between two excavator buckets.

As Kalley Huang reported for The New York Times on August 17, 2026, the mechanics of the deal are these: Nvidia will guarantee “lease and power payments” at the Ohio site — money that flows to SB Energy, the SoftBank subsidiary building, owning, and operating the facility. Jensen Huang’s own justification for the guarantee, delivered in a company blog post, was refreshingly candid about the underlying problem the whole industry is now structurally organized around: AI labs, in his words, are growing faster than their balance sheets and long-term credit profiles can support. That is not spin. It is an accurate description of a sector whose compute demand has outrun its ability to finance that demand through ordinary means — which is precisely why Nvidia, Microsoft, and a rotating cast of banks and asset managers keep having to invent new financing structures to paper over the gap.

The Sentence Doing the Denying

Huang’s specific rebuttal to the circularity charge is worth quoting in full, because it is doing more work than its length suggests: OpenAI, he said, will pay the lease. That is true as far as it goes, and it is also almost entirely beside the point. The mechanism this series flagged in Part III — Nvidia investing billions in OpenAI, which then spends billions back on Nvidia’s own products — does not require that OpenAI literally never write a check with its own money. It requires that the ultimate destination of the capital, regardless of how many hands it passes through and which entity’s balance sheet technically records the payment, ends up back at the company that financed the arrangement in the first place. Here, per the Times’ own reporting, the site will use only Nvidia’s chips. Nvidia is financing up to $105 billion of the infrastructure. OpenAI’s 20-year lease payments — once the facility actually comes online, starting in 2028 — flow through SB Energy to cover costs that are substantially chip costs, on chips Nvidia sold into the project. Money originates at Nvidia, travels through a lease structure, and returns to Nvidia as chip revenue, with OpenAI’s balance sheet serving as the pass-through in the middle. Huang’s defense is that the pass-through is real — OpenAI genuinely owes the lease payments and genuinely has to make them. That is true, and it is also exactly what a circular arrangement looks like when the company benefiting from the circularity wants a technically accurate sentence to describe it as something else.

The Deal Doubled in Two Weeks — Or Did It Halve?

There is a detail in the Times’ reporting that deserves more attention than the headline number gets: Nvidia and OpenAI had reportedly discussed a considerably larger figure — up to $250 billion in financial backing for this same Ohio project — before Monday’s announcement settled on $105 billion instead. A number moving from a quarter-trillion dollars down to just over a hundred billion, for the same facility, in the span of roughly three weeks, is not a rounding adjustment. It suggests either that Nvidia’s appetite for direct exposure to a single site cooled considerably in a very short window, or that the financing structure got reorganized to route more of the risk through other parties — SoftBank, other lenders, possibly the $500 billion Nvidia-led consortium this series covered in Part I — in ways the public announcement doesn’t fully disclose. Nvidia’s $105 billion is explicitly tied to covering 4.25 of the site’s eight total gigawatts of planned capacity, with room to extend to the rest later. That is a company keeping itself a door to walk back through, not a company that has fully committed to the scale of the project it’s attached its name to.

Eight Gigawatts, Six Million Households, One Company’s Chips

The physical scale here is worth sitting with on its own terms, separate from the financing dispute. Eight gigawatts of capacity is, per the Times, roughly enough electricity to power six million American households — a single data center campus, built to run one company’s models, consuming power on the scale of a mid-sized American state’s entire residential grid. This is the same category of concern this series traced through Part IV’s data-center-tax fights in Virginia and New York, except at a scale that makes those state-level tussles look like the opening skirmishes they actually were. A facility this size does not get built quietly, and it does not get powered without someone — Ohio ratepayers, the regional grid operator, ultimately some combination of both — absorbing costs that a $105 billion chip-financing arrangement has no mechanism to account for. Nvidia’s blog post is about guaranteeing lease payments to a data center operator. It says nothing about who guarantees the power grid can actually deliver eight gigawatts to rural Ohio without raising bills for everyone else drawing from the same lines.

The $30 Billion Nobody’s Forgotten About

It’s worth remembering, because Monday’s announcement rather conveniently doesn’t, that this isn’t Nvidia’s first investment in OpenAI — the company had already put $30 billion into OpenAI directly before this latest arrangement, a fact the Times notes almost in passing but which materially changes how the “OpenAI will pay the lease” defense should be read. Nvidia is not a disinterested financier extending credit to an arm’s-length customer. It is an equity investor in OpenAI, financing the construction of a facility that will exclusively run its own chips, guaranteeing the lease payments OpenAI will make back through that facility, with OpenAI’s ability to make good on any of it partly underwritten by capital Nvidia already put into the company. Untangling where Nvidia’s interest as a chip vendor ends and its interest as a shareholder begins is, at this point, not really possible from outside the deal — which is very likely the point. A company that owns a piece of its own customer, finances that customer’s infrastructure, and supplies the hardware that infrastructure runs on has structured itself to profit from the arrangement working, largely regardless of whether the underlying AI business the infrastructure supports ever turns the profit the valuation assumes it will.

The SoftBank Angle Nobody’s Naming

There’s a piece of this deal that the coverage treats as incidental but that this series has reason to look at more closely: SB Energy, the entity that will actually own and operate the Ohio facility, is a subsidiary of SoftBank — the same conglomerate whose founder, Masayoshi Son, has spent much of the past two years assembling an AI infrastructure portfolio through a tangle of vehicles (Stargate, various chip and data-center joint ventures) that makes SoftBank one of the least transparent major players in this entire buildout. Nvidia’s announcement includes a separate $1.5 billion direct investment in SB Energy itself, layered on top of the $105 billion lease guarantee — meaning Nvidia now holds simultaneous financial interests in the chip customer (OpenAI), the facility operator (SB Energy/SoftBank), and, through its own balance sheet, in the chips themselves. That is three points of exposure to the same underlying bet, wearing three different corporate names, disclosed across two separate blog posts published on the same afternoon. Untangling SoftBank’s own leverage — what SB Energy borrowed against to take on an operating role of this size, and what happens to that debt if OpenAI’s own finances wobble before 2028 — would require a level of disclosure none of the parties involved have offered, and the Times’ reporting, thorough as it is on the Nvidia-OpenAI relationship, doesn’t attempt it either. That gap is worth flagging on its own, because a financing structure this dependent on a single opaque conglomerate’s balance sheet is a fourth point of failure this series hasn’t had occasion to examine yet.

What “Not Circular” Actually Protects

I don’t think Jensen Huang is lying, exactly, when he says this deal isn’t circular — I think he’s using a definition of circularity narrow enough to be technically defensible while missing what actually worries the analysts and short-sellers this series has already covered. The concern was never that no real payment obligations exist between these companies. Real leases, real chip orders, and real equity stakes are all present and all enforceable. The concern is that the entire structure is optimized to make every participant’s revenue and asset figures look robust simultaneously, using the same pool of capital counted multiple times as it moves between related parties — Nvidia’s investment becomes OpenAI’s infrastructure becomes Nvidia’s chip revenue becomes, eventually, the basis for Nvidia’s own soaring valuation, which then supports the next round of financing to the next data center. Whether that structure collapses under its own complexity or simply keeps working as long as AI demand keeps growing to meet it is the actual question. Nvidia’s insistence that the lease “isn’t circular” answers a much smaller question than that one, and answers it in a way that happens to be true just often enough to keep the larger question from getting asked in the room where the $105 billion check gets signed.
