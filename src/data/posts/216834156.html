Part XVIII covered Anthropic’s own march toward what could be the largest IPO in history. This entry covers the market underneath that listing showing its first real cracks — not in Anthropic’s own numbers, but in the infrastructure companies whose IPOs were supposed to fund the physical buildout Anthropic and its rivals depend on.

The deals that stalled, and why

As The New York Times’ Maureen Farrell reported on September 21, 2026, the same public backlash against data centres this series has tracked since Part IX — the recall elections, the moratorium bills, the cross-partisan local opposition — has now reached Wall Street directly. SB Energy, the SoftBank subsidiary behind what would be the world’s largest data centre project, delayed its planned September IPO after bankers struggled to find enough buyers willing to meet the company’s sought valuation of $50 billion or more. IFR’s Amy Or reported on September 25, 2026 that the company had filed its documents on September 1, had planned to start its roadshow in the week of September 21 for a raise of about $5 billion, and had lined up $3 billion from Nvidia: a $1.5 billion prepayment for shares at a 10 percent discount and a further $1.5 billion private placement at the IPO price. Even an anchor commitment of that size from Nvidia did not close the gap between what SB Energy asked and what investors offered.

Holtec, a nuclear-energy company positioning itself to power AI data centres, paused its own IPO indefinitely, citing directly what its own release called “the uncertainty of data center development” — an unusually candid admission from a company that had been seeking a $10 billion valuation on Nasdaq. The pause became a withdrawal within days. Renaissance Capital reported on September 25, 2026 that Holtec Nuclear had formally pulled its offering, which had sought $825 million by selling 50 million shares at $15 to $18 under the ticker HNUC, from a company with $560 million in revenue over the twelve months to June 30, 2026. Aggreko, a power supplier counting AI data centres among its key customers, slowed its own offering as well, for reasons Farrell’s reporting attributes partly to data-centre-specific anxiety and partly to rising interest rates more broadly. Aggreko filed publicly to list on the New York Stock Exchange and then sat on it.

The rates point deserves more weight than a passing clause. Benzinga reported on October 4, 2026 that the 10-year Treasury yield had reached 5.29 percent on September 29, its highest level in 19 years, and that the smart-ring maker Oura had postponed a $2.2 billion listing the same day. A data-centre developer is, at bottom, a long-dated bet financed with borrowed money, and the price of that money rose sharply in the same weeks these deals were being marketed.

The SB Energy case is the most instructive of the three, because its own numbers illustrate exactly the kind of leverage and forward-looking optimism this series’ Part III and Part V coverage has already flagged as characteristic of the sector generally. When it tried to list, the company had yet to put a single data centre into full operation, yet projected a revenue backlog of $439 billion over roughly 20 years beginning in 2028, almost entirely from the Ohio project this series’ own Part V already covered in detail — the same project backed by Jensen Huang’s pledge, quoted directly in that earlier essay, that Nvidia would spend as much as $105 billion backstopping OpenAI’s lease obligations there. SB Energy’s own response to investor scepticism is worth noting for what it reveals about where the real product-market trust in this sector sits: rather than defending its own numbers directly, the company brought OpenAI’s chief financial officer and head of infrastructure onto an investor call to vouch for the Ohio project’s merits — asking its own most important tenant to do the persuading its own executives apparently couldn’t.

The Ohio campus also needs its own power, and that power has its own approval clock. Heatmap’s Jael Holzman reported on August 21, 2026 that the Trump administration was on course to permit a 9.2-gigawatt gas-fired plant for the Pike County site, financed by SB Energy and Nvidia, in about seven months, with the Army Corps of Engineers conducting a lighter Environmental Assessment rather than a full Environmental Impact Statement and a review scheduled to conclude on December 23, 2026. Investors asked to value SB Energy’s 20-year backlog were being asked, in part, to price a power plant that did not yet have its federal permit.

The one listing moving forward regardless

Set against three delayed offerings, Anthropic’s own IPO — the subject of this series’ Part XVIII — stayed, per Farrell’s reporting, on target for autumn 2026, a discrepancy worth sitting with rather than resolving too quickly. One plausible explanation is simply that Anthropic is a company with genuine, verifiable revenue ($100 billion annualized, per this series’ own XVIII coverage) rather than a data-centre developer asking investors to trust a 20-year backlog projection from a facility that isn’t built yet. Another is that Anthropic’s own safety rhetoric, whatever its sincerity, has bought it a different kind of investor trust than a pure infrastructure play can access — the “prudence over profit” framing this series’ Asimov Gap companion thread has already scrutinised at length in Parts 99 and 105. Whichever explanation carries more weight, the practical result was the same: the AI industry’s own most visible, most scrutinised IPO kept moving while the physical infrastructure that industry depends on had a noticeably harder time convincing investors it was a safe bet. Reuters, reporting on Anthropic’s confidential prospectus on September 28, said the listing was likely to come after the November 3 midterm elections.

Farrell’s reporting is careful not to overstate what this delay actually signals about the broader boom. Firms including PIMCO still estimate more than $5 trillion in infrastructure spending will be needed by 2030, and only two publicly traded companies — Equinix and Digital Realty Trust — focus solely on data centres, meaning September’s roughly 1-to-2-percent share-price dip for both tells this series comparatively little about the sector’s actual health. Tomasz Tunguz, a venture investor at Theory Ventures, offers the bull case directly: “The scope of what they’re embarking on is just enormous and so they need to tap all the major capital markets” — meaning the delays reflect a temporary pricing negotiation between developers and sceptical investors, not a fundamental reassessment of demand. Farrell also named several more experienced operators preparing to list, Vantage Data Centers, CyrusOne and Switch among them, though on different clocks. DatacenterDynamics, following Bloomberg, reported on August 10, 2026 that Switch had filed confidentially and could list as soon as November. Reuters’ Echo Wang reported on August 10, 2026 that CyrusOne was preparing an offering of about $5 billion for as early as 2027. Vantage, according to SiliconANGLE’s summary of Reuters reporting on August 13, 2026, was in early talks about an IPO at a valuation of roughly $100 billion or an outright sale, with a listing possible in 2027.

Nscale was to have been the first real test. The London-based company filed publicly on September 18, 2026, to list on the New York Stock Exchange under the ticker NSCL, Renaissance Capital reported that day, with an estimated $2 billion raise, $163 million in revenue over the twelve months to June 30, and multi-year agreements with Microsoft and Anthropic together worth up to roughly $88 billion. The filing made Anthropic a customer of the very infrastructure class whose listings were stalling. Fortune’s Jim Edwards reported on September 21, 2026 that Nscale was seeking a valuation of about $35 billion despite a $1 billion net loss on $140.6 million of revenue in the first half of 2026, with cost of revenue exceeding revenue. Its management had earlier identified “substantial doubt about the Company’s ability to continue as a going concern,” a concern eased only when Nvidia supplied $3.1 billion through convertible loan notes on September 15. Nscale’s offering became the one the market was waiting on.

What this adds to the picture this series has already assembled

This series’ own Part IX documented the local political cost of the data-centre boom — recall elections, tax-break backlash, the specific councilman in Independence, Missouri who lost his seat over a $6.26 billion break. Farrell’s reporting shows that same backlash beginning to price itself directly into capital markets rather than remaining confined to city council chambers and state legislatures. That is a meaningfully different kind of pressure than anything this series has tracked in the Capex Ledger thread so far — not regulation, not a tax law, but investors themselves declining to write the check at the valuation developers are asking for. Whether that hesitation compounds into something structurally significant, or resolves itself the moment Nscale’s pricing test comes back favourable, is the question September left open.

Where it stands now

It did not resolve. Through October the three stalled offerings stayed stalled, the pricing test never happened, and the gap between what the infrastructure companies were asking and what investors would pay stayed open.

Erin Griffith and Lauren Hirsch reported for The New York Times on 26 October that Holtec had withdrawn, and that SB Energy and Aggreko had both delayed their plans to go public. Aggreko therefore never priced on the New York Stock Exchange, and SB Energy has announced no new date. Oura, whose postponement this part treated as a rates story rather than a data-centre one, is still postponed.

Nscale never priced. Instead, Microsoft cancelled a deal with it. That matters more than a delayed listing, because this part recorded Nscale’s multi-year agreements with Microsoft and Anthropic as being worth up to roughly $88 billion together — the number underneath a $35 billion valuation sought by a company with $140.6 million of first-half revenue and a going-concern warning eased only by Nvidia’s $3.1 billion in convertible notes.

The weather around all of this turned as well. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index has fallen 26 per cent from its June peak, which is a bear market. Gartner has predicted that 80 per cent of “neoclouds” — the class Nscale belongs to — could be forced out of business. Ken Griffin of Citadel has said A.I. investors are “almost certainly” in a bubble. And Elon Musk, a day after his own company closed a $40 billion debt round, posted that “debt markets have almost entirely shut off access to money for any company that doesn’t have substantial revenue independent of AI data centers.” That is the sentence this part was reaching for in September, written by someone who had just proved the exception to it.

The listing that kept moving did move. On 26 October Anthropic published its finances and filed for an offering that could raise more than $100 billion, with Goldman Sachs as lead-left bank and Morgan Stanley as stabilisation agent. The contrast held exactly: the infrastructure companies could not find buyers at their asking price, and the model company filed anyway. One qualification, though, and it is the first crack in the asymmetry — after the slowdown in its own growth became visible, some investors began discussing a valuation of $1.5 trillion to $1.7 trillion rather than the $2 trillion that had been assumed. Parts XL and XLI take that story forward.
