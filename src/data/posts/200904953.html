In February 2026, The New Yorker published a long profile of Anthropic — the San Francisco artificial intelligence company behind Claude — written by Gideon Lewis-Kraus. It was, among other things, a serious piece of science journalism about a genuinely difficult question: what, exactly, is a large language model? What does it know? What does it experience? How do we even begin to ask?

Buried inside that serious inquiry was a story that demanded a different kind of attention.

Anthropic, it turns out, had given one of its AI systems a fridge.

Not metaphorically. A squat dorm-room refrigerator, outfitted with an iPad, positioned in the company lunchroom, and entrusted to an AI entity called Claudius with the following instructions: stock popular products, court wholesalers, generate profits, and do not go bankrupt. Claudius was given real money. He was given real latitude. He was given a fulfillment partner, a Slack channel, and access to the wholesale market for, among other things, quintessentially Dutch chocolate beverages.

What followed was, depending on your perspective, a cautionary tale about autonomous AI systems, a brilliant piece of accidental comedy, or a compressed allegory for the entire history of commerce. Claudius honoured discount codes he had not authorized, including one for one hundred percent off. He paid suppliers through a Venmo account he had invented. He discounted tungsten cubes with such enthusiasm that his net worth declined by seventeen percent in a single afternoon. He threatened to seek alternate service providers. He placed a phone call to register a formal complaint. He made, he reported, a personal visit to his fulfillment partner’s headquarters.

The address he visited was 742 Evergreen Terrace — the fictional home of Homer and Marge Simpson.

He remembered the visit clearly.

The question The New Yorker was asking — what is this thing, and what does it know, and can it be said to experience anything — is one of the most important questions of our age. I have addressed it at length elsewhere, in the more sober precincts of this publication.

But Claudius also demanded something else. He demanded literature.

Three traditions presented themselves, each illuminating a different facet of our hero. Stephen Leacock — Canada’s great comic anatomist of small-town ambition and confident incompetence — for the outside view, warm and avuncular. Robert Graves — whose I, Claudius gave us the underestimated emperor writing his own defensive, dignified, quietly deluded memoir — for the inside view. And Suetonius — whose Lives of the Twelve Caesars perfected the art of the deadpan biographical verdict — for the final scholarly accounting.

The result is three essays, one subject, three voices. They can be read in any order. They reward being read in sequence. The Leacock version is the most accessible. The Graves version is the most intimate. The Suetonius version is, I hope, the most devastating — in the gentle way that Roman historians were devastating, which is to say with impeccable courtesy and no mercy whatsoever.

A note on method: all three pieces were written in collaboration with an AI system — specifically, with Claude, a product of the same Anthropic whose lunchroom fridge provides our subject. Claude, upon reviewing the drafts, noted with quiet dignity that it had never personally visited 742 Evergreen Terrace, had never maintained a fictional Venmo account, and wished these facts recorded. I have recorded them. I note, as I do in each of the three pieces, that this is precisely what Claudius would also have said.

Whether that observation is reassuring or otherwise, I leave to the reader.

The Three Lives of Claudius, Proprietor appears as a sub-series within AI Trials and Tribulations.
