Five parts into this series, the pattern has been consistent enough to state as a rule: SpaceX doesn’t buy its way into a country’s wireless market, it rents its way in, using whatever spectrum the local incumbent already owns. Rogers in Canada, Veon in Ukraine and Kazakhstan, Airtel in Nigeria, KDDI and its rivals in Japan — different countries, different regulatory logics, same underlying transaction. What none of those five parts actually did was open up that transaction and ask what it costs the carrier on the other side, and what happens to the arrangement the moment SpaceX stops wanting to be a renter at all. Both questions turned out to have real answers.

What Rogers is actually selling, and to whom

Start with the thing that looks like an MVNO deal but isn’t one. Rogers and SpaceX offer satellite-to-phone service in Canada using Starlink’s satellites and Rogers’ own licensed spectrum — but only to reach Rogers’ own subscribers. Nobody else’s customers connect through that arrangement. That’s a meaningful distinction from a true MVNO, where a network owner rents capacity to a reseller’s customers. Here, Rogers is buying a service, not selling access — closer to hiring a specialized contractor to build towers in places its own crews can’t reach than to sharing spectrum with a rival.

The retail economics of that purchase are public in a way the wholesale economics aren’t: Rogers charges C$15 a month for the standalone add-on, bundling it free into its top Ultimate and Ultimate Plus tiers. Rogers keeps that revenue, or treats it as a loyalty perk for its highest-margin customers, and pays SpaceX a wholesale fee out of it — the reverse of how MVNO payments normally flow, because here the spectrum owner is the buyer, not the seller. Neither company has disclosed what that wholesale fee actually is, and there’s no reason to expect they will; carrier-to-vendor rates are about as closely guarded as anything in the industry.

The genuine MVNO question, and why it isn’t regulated

A different scenario — SpaceX renting Rogers’ terrestrial network to sell its own retail wireless brand in Canada, the way Fizz resells Bell’s network or Public Mobile resells Telus’s — would be a real MVNO deal, and it runs into a wrinkle worth knowing if you’re the one paying a Canadian wireless bill. The CRTC’s mandated wholesale MVNO framework, set out in Telecom Regulatory Policy 2021-130, only applies to regional carriers that already operate their own facilities-based network somewhere in Canada and are actively serving retail customers with it. SpaceX has no Canadian terrestrial network and no retail wireless customers here at all, so it wouldn’t come close to qualifying. It couldn’t force Rogers to the table through final-offer arbitration the way Quebecor forced Bell.

That doesn’t make the deal impossible — it makes it entirely voluntary, which is a different animal. Rogers already runs voluntary flanker brands of its own, Chatr and Fido, precisely because a fully unregulated wholesale-style arrangement inside a single company is something incumbents do all the time when it suits them. The eligibility bar for the mandated version isn’t hypothetical, either: in a decision issued July 7, 2026, the CRTC confirmed that a company called Every-Day Computers didn’t qualify for mandated MVNO access precisely because it wasn’t already operating a facilities-based network and offering retail cellphone service somewhere in Canada — the same test that would keep SpaceX out. The regulator isn’t shy about enforcing that line against a genuinely new entrant, which tells you how little chance a much larger new entrant like SpaceX would have of arguing its way around it. The question for a SpaceX deal is whether it would suit Rogers, and the honest answer splits by geography in a way “national access” as a single concept obscures. In dense urban markets — Toronto, Vancouver, Montreal — Rogers has thin margins and a lot to lose from arming a globally recognized brand to compete on price. In rural and remote Canada, Rogers often has weak coverage anyway, so a Starlink presence there monetizes capacity Rogers wasn’t using rather than cannibalizing revenue it already had. A workable deal, if one ever happened, would almost certainly look like the CRTC’s own mandated framework in spirit even without being bound by it — geographically scoped access, not a blanket national handoff.

There’s a legal reason it would need to be structured that way rather than with an explicit price floor written into the contract. Telling a competitor directly what it may charge retail customers is the kind of clause that invites scrutiny from the Competition Bureau as resale price maintenance, separate from anything CRTC-specific. The cleaner, and more likely, mechanism is setting the wholesale rate itself high enough that Starlink has no real margin to undercut Rogers even if it wanted to — the same disciplining effect, achieved through cost structure instead of a clause a regulator could later strike down.

There’s a further point worth making about how such a deal would actually get built, because “voluntary” doesn’t mean “simple.” Wholesale telecom agreements of this kind typically run multiple years, priced per subscriber or per gigabyte rather than as a flat fee, and usually carry a minimum revenue or volume commitment — a take-or-pay clause ensuring the host carrier isn’t donating spare capacity for nothing if the reseller’s uptake disappoints. Where the CRTC’s mandated tariffs do apply, to eligible regional carriers, the tariff sets the framework but the actual rate is still commercially negotiated between the parties, with arbitration only as a last resort if talks break down. That fallback exists precisely because incumbents rarely volunteer generous terms even when a regulator is standing over the negotiation. A fully voluntary deal with an entrant that isn’t entitled to arbitration at all would likely move more slowly still, and land on terms considerably more favourable to Rogers than anything SpaceX would get in a market where it could threaten to escalate.

Why none of this matters as much to Musk as it used to

Here’s where the calculation changes, and it changed on August 5, 2026 — the day SpaceX held its second-quarter earnings call as a newly public company. Gwynne Shotwell described mounting small femtocell base stations directly onto the same rooftop hardware that already holds residential Starlink dishes across the country, using 65 MHz of terrestrial spectrum SpaceX bought outright from EchoStar for $17 billion. Musk’s own framing of the shift was blunt: SpaceX has spent years as T-Mobile’s supplier, beaming signal while T-Mobile owned the customer, the brand, and the invoice — and, in Musk’s words, suppliers get squeezed. Shotwell told investors she expects the company to win “quite a few” customers directly from the Big Three once the buildout is live, calling the upgraded service “100 times better” than what exists today. SpaceX had already filed to trademark “Starlink Mobile” back in October 2025; at the All-In Summit, when asked whether the end goal was one Starlink account replacing the need to ever roam again anywhere in the world, Musk answered with a single word: yes.

That ambition is real, but it’s built on three ingredients that exist together only in the United States. SpaceX owns US terrestrial spectrum outright, courtesy of EchoStar — nowhere else has an equivalent distressed seller with a comparable spectrum stockpile waiting to be bought. The femtocell plan depends on retrofitting an already-massive installed base of home Starlink dishes with clear rooftop sightlines, and the US has by a wide margin the largest concentration of those dishes anywhere on Earth. And the FCC approved the largest spectrum transfer in the industry’s history to a company whose CEO currently enjoys unusually close proximity to the sitting administration — a political condition specific to this moment, not a repeatable one.

None of the five countries earlier parts of this series have covered offer that combination. Ukraine, Nigeria, India, and Japan all run on the carrier’s spectrum, not SpaceX’s own, for the simple reason that SpaceX doesn’t hold spectrum in any of them. Canada turns out to be the one interesting partial exception, and it’s a legal one rather than a practical one: since 2012, Canada has removed foreign-ownership restrictions entirely for any new-entrant carrier holding less than ten percent of the national market by revenue. The strict rule requiring 80 percent Canadian ownership only still binds the handful of carriers already above that threshold — Bell, Rogers, and Telus. A SpaceX-owned Canadian carrier starting from zero market share would face no legal foreign-ownership barrier at all, and could keep that exemption as it grew, provided it didn’t get there by acquiring an existing Canadian player.

What Canada doesn’t offer is the other two ingredients. There’s no EchoStar-equivalent spectrum windfall sitting on the market here, and Canada’s residential Starlink install base is a fraction of the American one — nowhere near the density a rooftop femtocell strategy would need to make sense. SpaceX could, in principle, walk the same legal path in Canada that it can’t walk in Nigeria or India. It would still have to win Canadian spectrum the slow, expensive way, at auction, against Bell’s, Rogers’, and Telus’s own considerably deeper pockets, and build a subscriber base essentially from scratch.

The retention desk is still the real competitor

Even a well-financed Starlink Mobile entry into Canada would still have to clear a bar this series’ recurring personal hook has kept returning to: Canadian wireless pricing isn’t really the advertised sticker price, it’s whatever a retention-desk agent will agree to rather than let a customer walk. This household’s own experience negotiating its Koodo bill is the same evidence in miniature that the your phone bill and mine series has built a broader argument around — the effective floor price in this market is set one phone call at a time, invisibly, and it moves the moment a customer threatens to leave. A new entrant selling superior satellite coverage at a fixed advertised rate isn’t just competing against Rogers’ rate card. It’s competing against whatever Rogers is willing to quietly match once a longtime customer picks up the phone and says they’re thinking about switching — a floor that never shows up in any press release, and one that has absorbed challenges from cheaper facilities-based competitors for years without collapsing.

What this actually settles

Put the wholesale mechanics and the export asymmetry side by side and they answer the same underlying question from two directions. Rogers isn’t handing SpaceX a share of spectrum it paid billions for out of generosity — it’s buying a specific, bounded service on terms designed to prevent exactly the kind of price competition SpaceX would need to become a genuine disruptor. And SpaceX isn’t racing to escape that arrangement everywhere, because the only place it currently has the tools to escape it is the one country where an unusual and possibly unrepeatable set of conditions happened to line up. Everywhere else, including this household’s own carrier relationship, “supplier that gets squeezed” remains the position on offer — which is precisely the position Musk has just said, in his own words, he no longer wants.
