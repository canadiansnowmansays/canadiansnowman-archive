Most cities that get conquered by Alexander the Great, sacked by Genghis Khan’s armies, and fought over by a rotating cast of Persian, Turkic, and Arab empires across two and a half thousand years do not, as a rule, come out the other side as one of the most celebrated urban centers in the history of Islamic civilization. Samarkand did exactly that, and the reason has less to do with luck than with geography and function: Samarkand sits at one of the genuinely unavoidable crossroads of Central Asian trade, at the meeting point of routes running from China in the east, India in the south, Persia in the west, and the steppe in the north, in the fertile Zeravshan river valley that made it one of the few places in the region capable of supporting a genuinely large settled population rather than a nomadic one. Conquerors could and did burn Samarkand to the ground on multiple occasions. None of them could relocate the crossroads itself, which meant the city — or a rebuilt version of it nearby — kept reasserting its usefulness to whoever held it next, generation after generation, empire after empire.

The city’s recorded history stretches back further than most Silk Road settlements, predating even the Han Dynasty’s opening of the route through the Hexi Corridor. Founded as Afrasiab, the ancient core of Samarkand, sometime around the sixth or seventh century BCE by Sogdian settlers, the city was already a substantial urban center by the time Alexander the Great’s army arrived in 329 BCE during his campaign into Central Asia. Alexander took the city — ancient sources describe fierce resistance and a bloody sack — and used it as a base during his brutal, multi-year campaign to subdue Sogdiana, a campaign remembered in Greek sources partly for its military difficulty and partly for the marriage of Alexander to the Sogdian noblewoman Roxana, a diplomatic match intended to help stabilize a region that had proven far harder to pacify than the Persian territories Alexander had already conquered. Macedonian garrisons and Greek settlers remained in the region after Alexander’s death, leaving behind a Hellenistic cultural residue in Central Asia that would linger, in fragments of art and coinage, for centuries.

It was under the Sogdians themselves, though, rather than under any of the various imperial powers that claimed nominal sovereignty over them, that Samarkand found its true calling, and it’s worth pausing on the Sogdians specifically because they are, in a real sense, the unsung logistical backbone of the entire Silk Road’s operation during its classical and early medieval peak. Sogdiana was never a unified empire in the way Han China, Parthia, or Rome were; it was a collection of city-states — Samarkand foremost among them, alongside Bukhara, Penjikent, and others — that never achieved political unification and, for long stretches of their history, existed as tributaries or nominal subjects of whichever larger empire happened to be dominant in the region at a given moment: Achaemenid Persia, then Alexander’s successors, then various Central Asian confederations, then eventually the Islamic caliphates. What the Sogdians lacked in political unity, though, they made up for overwhelmingly in commercial reach. Sogdian merchants operated trading colonies stretching from the Byzantine frontier in the west to the Chinese capital in the east, and Sogdian became, for centuries, the practical lingua franca of Silk Road commerce — the language in which a Bactrian merchant and a Chinese customs official might actually conduct business, regardless of which larger empire either of them nominally answered to. Sogdian trading networks moved silk, but also silver, musk, slaves, and an enormous range of other goods, and Sogdian merchant communities established permanent residential quarters in Chinese cities along the route, some of which have been excavated by archaeologists and reveal distinctly Sogdian religious practices, including Zoroastrian funerary customs, persisting for generations far from their Central Asian homeland.

The physical and documentary evidence of Samarkand’s Sogdian commercial network is unusually rich by ancient standards, thanks largely to a chance discovery: a cache of letters, now known as the Ancient Sogdian Letters, dating to around 313 CE and found in a watchtower ruin near Dunhuang, written by Sogdian merchants living in China back to business contacts and family in Samarkand. These letters — private, unpolished, and never intended for anyone beyond their original recipients — offer a startlingly direct window into how Silk Road commerce actually functioned at the ground level, including one merchant’s grim account of chaos and abandonment following the fall of the Chinese capital at Luoyang, and complaints about debts, stranded goods, and disrupted business that read, across seventeen centuries, as entirely familiar to anyone who has ever run a small trading operation dependent on political stability holding in a distant market. It’s a rare case of Silk Road history told not through the court records of the great empires that get most of the historiographical attention, but through the actual voices of the merchants whose accumulated individual transactions were what made the entire network function in practice.

Between the Sogdian merchant era and the Mongol catastrophe, Samarkand passed under Arab Muslim control, conquered by the Umayyad general Qutayba ibn Muslim in a campaign completed around 712 CE, and this transition marks the point at which the city’s population began a gradual conversion to Islam and its commercial networks began integrating into the vastly larger trading world of the early Islamic caliphates, stretching from Spain to the Indus. It also made Samarkand the place where papermaking entered the Islamic world — whether through Chinese prisoners taken at the Battle of Talas in 751 CE, as several later Arabic sources claim, or through more gradual commercial contact, as some modern scholars argue. A later installment of this series takes up that question properly. What belongs here is the outcome: Samarkand paper became renowned across the Islamic world for its quality, the city’s workshops supplied a scholarly and administrative culture that could not have functioned on parchment alone, and Samarkand’s name stayed attached to fine paper for centuries after the technology had spread onward to Baghdad and beyond. The crossroads did what crossroads do.

The Islamic centuries also transformed the city’s physical and institutional character in ways that outlasted the Arab conquest itself. Samarkand under the Samanids, a Persian dynasty ruling Transoxiana in the ninth and tenth centuries from a capital at nearby Bukhara, became part of a genuine Persian cultural renaissance — the period in which New Persian emerged as a literary language written in Arabic script, producing the poetry of Rudaki and setting the conditions for Ferdowsi’s Shahnameh a generation later. The Samanid period is also when Central Asian Islamic scholarship reached its first great height: Ibn Sina, whose medical and philosophical works would be standard references in European universities for five hundred years, was born near Bukhara in 980 CE and educated in the Samanid libraries. That a region which had been conquered by Arab armies only two and a half centuries earlier was by this point producing scholarship of that calibre says something about how thoroughly, and how quickly, the crossroads position converted into intellectual capital once the trade routes were operating under a single legal and commercial framework.

Before leaving the Sogdian period, it is worth pausing on the physical evidence, because Samarkand has produced one of the more remarkable pieces of Silk Road art anywhere on the route. Excavations at Afrasiab, the ancient city mound, uncovered in 1965 a set of seventh-century wall paintings from what appears to have been an audience hall in the residence of a Sogdian ruler, and their subject matter is essentially a diplomatic group portrait of the known world. The murals depict, across their four walls, ambassadors and delegations arriving from multiple directions — Chinese figures with distinctly Tang court dress and hairstyles, Korean envoys identifiable by their feathered caps, Turkic warriors, Indian figures — converging on the Sogdian ruler. Whatever the paintings’ precise political occasion, the message the commissioning ruler wanted communicated is unmistakable: this is a court that the entire continent comes to. It is worth noting how unusual that self-presentation is. A Chinese or Persian imperial painting of foreign envoys would frame them as tribute-bearers submitting to a superior power. The Afrasiab murals read more like a merchant’s ledger of valuable relationships, which is roughly what a Sogdian city-state’s foreign policy actually amounted to.

Sogdian commercial practice, reconstructed from contracts and letters recovered at Turfan, Dunhuang, and the Sogdian hill town of Mount Mugh, was correspondingly sophisticated. Sogdian merchants used written partnership agreements, employed agents who travelled while the capital-holder stayed home, extended credit across enormous distances, and maintained family networks in which brothers or cousins were stationed at nodes along the route so that goods and information could move through trusted hands the entire way. Marriage contracts recovered from Mount Mugh include provisions on property and divorce that indicate Sogdian women held a legal position with more independence than their contemporaries in several neighbouring societies, and a number of surviving documents show women operating as parties to commercial transactions in their own right. This was a genuinely mercantile civilization — not a state that happened to trade, but a network of cities whose institutions, law, and family structure had been shaped around the demands of long-distance commerce over generations.

Samarkand’s most catastrophic moment arrived in 1220, when the armies of Genghis Khan, in the course of the Mongol conquest of the Khwarazmian Empire that then controlled the city, besieged and sacked it with a thoroughness that ancient and medieval sources across multiple traditions describe in genuinely harrowing terms — mass killing of the population, wholesale destruction of the city’s irrigation infrastructure, and a depopulation severe enough that the surviving Persian chronicler Juvaini, writing a generation later, described the region’s agricultural land reverting to something closer to desert for years afterward. This is worth sitting with, because it’s a useful corrective to any romanticized notion that the Mongol unification of the Silk Road under a single political authority — which this series will discuss in more detail in a later installment on the Pax Mongolica — was some kind of bloodless administrative achievement. It was preceded, in city after city across Central Asia, by conquests of extraordinary brutality, and Samarkand’s later medieval flourishing happened not because the Mongol conquest was gentle, but because the city’s underlying commercial value to whoever controlled it was durable enough to survive even a catastrophe on this scale.

Samarkand’s most famous architectural and cultural flowering came later still, under Timur — known in the West as Tamerlane — who made the city his imperial capital beginning in the 1370s and used the enormous wealth extracted from his conquests across Persia, India, and the Middle East to rebuild Samarkand into one of the most architecturally spectacular cities of the medieval Islamic world. Timur’s building projects, and those of his descendants, produced the monumental blue-tiled domes and towering madrasas of the Registan complex that still define Samarkand’s skyline today, along with the astronomical observatory built by his grandson Ulugh Beg, whose remarkably precise star catalogue, compiled in the 1420s, remained among the most accurate available anywhere in the world for roughly two centuries afterward. Timur’s Samarkand was, in a real sense, built on the same underlying logic that had sustained the city since its Sogdian merchant heyday: a ruler who controlled the crossroads could tax, redirect, and ultimately monopolize an enormous share of the wealth passing between China, India, Persia, and the Mediterranean, and could then spend that wealth on architecture and scholarship extravagant enough to announce, to every rival power in the region, exactly how much the crossroads was worth.

There is a particular irony worth noting in the fact that Timur, whose military campaigns across Persia, the Caucasus, India, and the Middle East were themselves conducted with a brutality that later chroniclers compared explicitly to Genghis Khan’s — the sack of Delhi in 1398 alone reportedly left the city’s population decimated and its streets impassable with corpses — chose to spend the resulting wealth building a capital dedicated, at least in its architectural ambitions, to scholarship, astronomy, and religious monumentality rather than mere display. His grandson Ulugh Beg carried this further, ruling Samarkand as a genuine scholar-king who personally participated in the astronomical observations conducted at his observatory, and whose resulting star catalogue corrected errors in the older Greek astronomical tables that had gone unchallenged for over a millennium. Ulugh Beg’s reign ended badly — he was overthrown and assassinated by his own son in 1449, a reminder that intellectual accomplishment bought remarkably little protection in Timurid succession politics — but the observatory’s foundations, rediscovered by archaeologists in 1908 buried beneath centuries of accumulated soil, remain visible in Samarkand today, a physical trace of the brief window in which the city functioned as one of the most serious centers of astronomical research anywhere on earth.

What Samarkand’s long, violent, repeatedly interrupted history illustrates, more clearly than almost any other single city along the route, is the difference between political control of the Silk Road and economic control of it. Empires conquered Samarkand, garrisoned it, taxed it, occasionally razed it to the ground, and were in every case eventually replaced by some other empire that did the same thing. The city itself, or the rebuilt version that rose in its place, kept recovering its function as a trading crossroads regardless of whose flag flew over it, because the underlying geography — the fertile valley, the position between four major civilizational zones — never changed no matter how many times the political map did. Empires are, comparatively, temporary. Crossroads, it turns out, are not, and Samarkand’s real achievement across two and a half millennia was simply refusing to become irrelevant, no matter how many times it was given every reason to.
