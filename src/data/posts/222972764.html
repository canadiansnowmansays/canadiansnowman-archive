There is an old rule in sport that you do not let the owner of one of the teams appoint the referees. Nobody needs it explained. Bias need not even be proven; the arrangement itself is the problem, because no result it produces can be fully trusted.

Today, November 3, 2026, Americans vote in the most consequential midterm elections in a generation. The platform on which a large share of the arguments about those elections will be conducted is owned by the man who has spent more money on them than anyone alive. And the system that platform uses to correct falsehoods is increasingly run by that man’s own software.

Nobody has done anything illegal. That is rather the point of this series. The Musk Stack has always been about arrangements, not crimes: one person accumulating, piece by piece, positions that would each be unremarkable in separate hands and are something else entirely in one pair. Part XXII is about the newest piece, and it is the one that sits closest to the ballot box.

The post office and the mosque

Start with a single post, because it shows the whole machine in miniature.

On the weekend of October 24 and 25, 2026, Chaya Raichik, the right-wing influencer who posts as Libs of TikTok, told her nearly five million followers on X that post offices in Minnesota were operating inside mosques, and linked that to the people who handle mail-in ballots. She reshared a 28-minute video of a man circling a brick building in Plymouth, Minnesota, and confronting postal workers inside, warning darkly about an Islamic takeover of the country.

As Will Oremus reported for The Atlantic on October 31, 2026, almost all of it was misleading. The post office and the mosque occupy separate sections of the same building. There is no reason to think the shared real estate has anything to do with how ballots are handled.

This is precisely the kind of post X’s Community Notes feature was built for. A day later, someone proposed a note correcting the record, with links to reporting by the Minnesota Star Tribune and Minnesota Public Radio. The note was never shown. By October 30 the video had passed 1.7 million views.

The detail that matters most is who wrote the note: an AI agent. Not one of X’s own, as it happens, but a bot all the same. The machine did its job. The correction existed. It simply never reached anyone.

A clever idea with a structural flaw

Community Notes deserves a fair hearing, because it is a genuinely clever piece of design, and even some of Musk’s critics have said so.

Anyone can propose a note. The note is then rated as helpful or unhelpful by other users, and the system looks specifically for agreement among raters who have disagreed with each other in the past. A note goes public only when people who usually line up on opposite sides both find it useful. The aim is to make fact-checking credible to people who distrust professional fact-checkers, and there is some research suggesting crowdsourced notes are more widely believed than the old warning labels.

It has become the industry’s model. After Trump’s re-election, Meta dropped its partnerships with professional fact-checking organisations in the United States in favour of its own version, while keeping professional fact-checks elsewhere in the world. YouTube and TikTok have adopted similar features. On non-political subjects, the system works well. It routinely corrects misleading posts about sport, celebrities and advertising.

The flaw is built into the clever part. A system that publishes only what both sides agree on will publish very little about the subjects on which the two sides agree least. Elections are now chief among them. A volunteer contributor named Mike Doris told Oremus that the group chat where active note-writers once traded sources had gone quiet, a place nobody visits any more, and that he was close to giving up himself.

Humans tire of writing corrections nobody sees. Software does not.

Enter the bots

To keep the programme growing, X opened note-writing to artificial intelligence. It built its own note-writing agent on Grok, the chatbot made by Musk’s AI company, and invited outside researchers to build their own.

What began as an experiment became the norm within about a year. In May 2026, an analysis by Alexios Mantzarlis of The Indicator found that half of all the Community Notes shown on X were written by just eight bot accounts. In October, the Georgetown University researcher Renée DiResta went through X’s public research data and found that six of those eight accounts are controlled by the platform itself.

X presents this as a success, and on its own terms it is. A company spokesperson told Oremus that AI note-writers have roughly doubled the number of notes published over the past year. The company also says AI-written notes are more likely to win approval, plausibly because they are trained to use neutral language and cite reputable sources.

Step back, though, and look at the arrangement. The platform is owned by Musk. The chatbot that powers its in-house fact-checkers is built by a Musk company. Most of the corrections that actually reach readers are now produced by software the platform controls. The judgement of which notes appear still rests on the rating crowd, so this is not a story of Musk personally deciding what is true. It is a story of the referee’s whistle being manufactured, issued and increasingly blown by the same organisation that owns one of the teams. Earlier in this series we watched Musk move from selling to the Pentagon to helping write its requirements. Here he has moved from hosting the argument to supplying most of its corrections.

What the bots cannot fix

The bots have increased the volume of notes. They have not solved the underlying problem, because the bottleneck was never the writing. It was the agreement.

NewsGuard, a company that tracks misinformation, found that 93 per cent of false claims about the midterms on X received no Community Note at all. DiResta’s own AI fact-checking bot has proposed 81 notes; one has been approved. The machine can draft a correction in seconds. It cannot make a Democrat and a Republican agree that the correction is fair, and on elections that agreement is now almost impossible to find.

Meanwhile, X has become a starting point for the falsehoods themselves. Of the 37 viral false claims about the midterms that NewsGuard is tracking, 22 began as posts on X, and many then spread to larger platforms. Fabricated videos targeting at least three Democratic Senate candidates have been linked to Russian disinformation campaigns. One opens with real footage of a Cleveland local-news reporter and then cuts to invented allegations of sexual misconduct against Sherrod Brown, the Democrat running in Ohio. Darren Linvill, a Clemson University researcher who studies foreign interference, told Oremus that X has removed some of the accounts responsible, but the same circle of influencers keeps amplifying new ones.

Not every falsehood points the same way. One debunked post claimed that Ken Paxton, the Republican Senate candidate in Texas, had paid for a billboard defending paedophiles. It was satire, and at least one viral version now carries a “manipulated media” label. X says that earlier this month it suspended more than 13,000 accounts linked to foreign-influence operations. Those are real actions, and they deserve to be recorded. They are also enforcement by the company’s own discretion, which is a different thing from a working system of public correction.

The night the bots go blind

The real test has not happened yet. It comes on Tuesday night and in the days after.

Election-night rumours are not like a misleading post about a mosque, which can be checked against news reports already published. They arrive at speed and in the dark: a claim that a county has found a box of ballots, that a machine flipped votes, that a poll worker was seen doing something suspicious. The facts that would rebut them are known, if at all, only to people standing in a particular room in a particular county. DiResta put the limit plainly: “The AI does not know what is happening on the ground.” A bot can only check a claim against what someone has already written down and published. On election night, for hours at a time, nobody has.

The incentives on the other side are pointing in the opposite direction. President Trump has announced a $1 million bounty for voter-fraud tips that lead to a conviction. X has become the place where his supporters go hunting for examples, and Oremus notes that this matters most if Republicans fare poorly, because those examples are the raw material for disputing the result. Part LIX of my series When Democracy Fights Back described election officials drilling for interference from their own federal government. They are also, in effect, preparing to be accused on a platform whose correction system cannot keep up with them.

And the owner has made his own view clear. On October 30, 2026, Musk told his 241.5 million followers on X, as Theodore Schleifer reported for The New York Times, that a Democratic trifecta after 2028 would turn the United States into a permanent one-party state, the end of democracy, as he put it, and that 51 Republican senators were the only remedy that mattered. He has also said he already voted early in Texas, where he has more riding than anywhere else. By a later count in The New York Times, he has given $474 million this cycle, more than any donor in a single election apart from self-funding candidates, and about $212 million of it went to supporting Ken Paxton’s Senate campaign. On the eve of the vote, Trump told the New York Post he would pre-emptively pardon Musk and other allies if Democrats won Congress and opened investigations, as The New York Times reported. “I would if I needed to, sure,” the president said.

He is entitled to his opinion and to his vote, and in the United States, since Citizens United, he is entitled to spend nearly half a billion dollars on it. What he is not, in any meaningful sense, is a neutral steward of the town square in which his opinion competes. The fact-checking system on his platform does not have to be biased in his favour to be compromised by his ownership of it. It only has to fail at the moments when failure helps the side he is paying for, and the structure makes those moments the likeliest ones.

What 2020 looked like

It is worth remembering how recently this was done differently. In November 2020, the last national election with Trump in the White House, Twitter attached fact-checks and warning labels to post after post from the president and his allies claiming, without evidence, that the vote had been rigged. It did not stop the lie. The lie helped bring a mob to the Capitol on January 6, 2021. But the platform at least said, in its own voice, that the claims were disputed.

That is gone. Musk bought the company in October 2022, renamed it, cut its trust-and-safety staff and replaced the labels with the crowd. The crowd has been joined by the bots, and most of the bots belong to the house. Oremus ends his piece by observing that it is hard to imagine the platform doing what Twitter did in 2020. I would put it more sharply. The platform no longer has a voice of its own with which to do it. It has delegated the job to a system designed to stay silent whenever Americans disagree, at the very moment they are about to disagree most.

The view from here

From Canada, the country that gave Musk his first passport, this looks like a familiar Musk Stack story with an unfamiliar subject. Each piece of the stack is defensible on its own terms. Owning a social network is legal. Building a chatbot is legal. Letting bots write fact-checks is an interesting experiment. Spending nearly half a billion dollars on an election is legal in the United States. Posting that the other party will end democracy is free speech.

It is the stacking that makes the difference. The same man owns the venue, builds the referees, funds one of the teams and announces from the centre circle which result would mean the end of the game. As of Monday, he also holds the president’s word that he will be pardoned if the other team wins and comes looking. Each layer leans on the next. None of it is secret. All of it will be tested in the 72 hours after the polls close.

If the count is clean and close, and the rumours start, a great many Americans will turn to X to find out what is true. They will find the post, and the video, and the million views. Somewhere in the system there may well be a careful, neutral, well-sourced note, written by a bot, sitting unseen in a queue.
