Ships clustered near the Strait of Hormuz during the 2026 conflict. China watched forty days of American operations here — and took notes

China pressed Iran to accept the ceasefire. That detail, buried in the diplomatic coverage of the Hormuz war’s conclusion, is the one that matters most for Taiwan.

Beijing spent forty days watching the United States military operate under fire — its logistics, its munitions consumption, its political pain threshold, its president’s behaviour under economic pressure. China supplied missile propellant precursor to Iran during the conflict. Chinese satellite imagery of American military movements circulated through intelligence channels. China vetoed the United Nations Security Council resolution on Hormuz, providing Iran with diplomatic cover while extracting maximum intelligence value from every day of the war.

And then, at the moment of its choosing, China pressed for a ceasefire. Not because Beijing wanted to save the United States from an embarrassing defeat. Because the experiment was complete. The data was in. The model had been validated.

China now has something it did not have before the Iran war: a proof of concept.

What Beijing learned in forty days can be stated with precision, because the Iran war was, from China’s perspective, a controlled experiment in the application of supply chain leverage against American military and political will.

It learned Trump’s pain threshold. Forty days. Forty-percent gas prices. A Pew poll showing Americans cared more about fuel costs than military casualties. A ceasefire concluded without Iran conceding significant ground. The number — forty days — is now a planning parameter.

It learned the limits of American military reach. The United States possesses unrivalled military power in the abstract. In the specific — operating against drone swarms, replenishing precision munitions, sustaining a naval campaign in a contested strait — the constraints are real and measurable. The Patriot and THAAD batteries, the Tomahawk inventories, the carrier group logistics: all of this was observed, tested, and catalogued over forty days of actual combat operations. These are the same systems essential to Pacific deterrence. They are now depleted. Replenishment takes months.

It learned that economic pain moves faster than military calculation. The stock market reacted within days of the Hormuz closure. Gas prices followed within a week. The political pressure on the White House built within two weeks. The ceasefire came at forty days. The military campaign, had it been prosecuted to conclusion, would have taken longer. The economy would not wait.

It learned that a weaker power can force a stronger power to blink if it controls a chokepoint that the global economy cannot afford to lose. This is not a new principle. It is a very old one. What the Iran war did was confirm that the principle applies to Donald Trump’s America in 2026 — not in theory, not in a war game, but in fact.

The application to Taiwan is not analogical. It is direct.

The Strait of Hormuz carries one-fifth of the world’s oil. Iran’s disruption forced a superpower ceasefire in forty days. Beijing was watching.

The Strait of Hormuz carries roughly one-fifth of the world’s oil. A Chinese blockade of Taiwan would interrupt more than one-third of the world’s microchip production. The economic comparison is not oil versus chips. It is recession versus civilisational infrastructure failure. The chips that TSMC produces are not a commodity in the way oil is a commodity. They are the substrate on which the entire digital economy runs — the AI systems, the financial networks, the military hardware, the medical devices, the communications infrastructure of every advanced economy on earth.

If Iran closing the Strait of Hormuz produced forty-percent gas prices and a superpower ceasefire in forty days, the arithmetic of a Taiwan blockade suggests consequences of an entirely different order of magnitude. Chris Miller’s estimate — trillions of dollars, half a decade to rebuild — is not alarmism. It is conservative engineering. You cannot replace thirty years of institutional knowledge and precision manufacturing culture in five years. You cannot replace it at all in the window that matters.

The Taiwan Strait — where a Chinese blockade would threaten more than one-third of global microchip production. Chips, not oil: the substrate of the modern world.

China has run this calculation. China has now watched a smaller version of it play out in real time. The Iran war did not just provide a model. It provided confidence. Beijing’s planners now know that supply chain leverage works against the United States at this moment in history, with this president, under these economic conditions. The theoretical has become empirical.

There is one more thing China learned from the Hormuz war, and it is perhaps the most important of all.

Donald Trump went to Beijing on May 14, 2026, six weeks after the Iran ceasefire. He arrived having just concluded a war he did not win, with a depleted munitions inventory, a rattled stock market, and a domestic political environment in which Americans have demonstrated, in a Pew poll, that they prioritise gas prices over military casualties. He brought with him the CEOs of Apple, Tesla, and NVIDIA — the men who run the companies most dependent on Taiwanese chips, there not to defend Taiwan’s democracy but to negotiate market access in China. He arrived, as one analyst put it, much diminished.

Xi Jinping received him with full state protocol. The Great Hall of the People, the red carpet, the honour guard, the children waving flags. And then, in the first round of talks, Xi placed Taiwan at the centre of everything. He called it “the most important issue” in the bilateral relationship. He warned that mishandling it would produce “clashes and even conflicts.” He said that Taiwan independence and cross-strait peace are “as irreconcilable as fire and water.” He invoked the Thucydides Trap — the historical pattern in which a rising power and a ruling power collide — and asked whether America and China could avoid it. The question was not academic. It was an offer: accommodate China on Taiwan, and the collision need not happen.

Trump and Xi handshake. “The most important issue,” Xi called Taiwan. Trump later described it as “a place” and signalled he was “not looking to have somebody go independent.

What Xi knew, entering that summit, was that he had empirical confirmation of Trump’s economic pain threshold — established not by intelligence assessment but by forty days of observed American behaviour under pressure. He knew the munitions stockpiles were depleted. He knew the political calculus in Washington. He knew that Trump had told an interviewer, months before the Iran war even began, that Xi’s intentions toward Taiwan were “up to him.”

And he knew what happened at the summit. Trump told Fox News afterward that he is “not looking to have somebody go independent” and that “the last thing we need right now is a war that’s 9,500 miles away.” He waffled on a fourteen-billion-dollar arms package for Taiwan. He dismissed the 1982 Six Assurances — the policy under which the United States pledged not to consult with Beijing about Taiwan arms sales — as outdated. He called Taiwan “a place, because nobody knows how to define it.” He speculated that China would not move on Taiwan while he is in office but “might” afterward.

Wang Yi, China’s foreign minister, told state media after the summit that Beijing “sensed” that Trump “understands China’s position.” The White House readout did not mention Taiwan at all.

That phrase — “up to him” — now carries weight that it did not carry when Trump first said it. It carried strategic ambiguity then. It carries something closer to an invitation now.

The question that the proof of concept raises — and that the Formosa series must confront directly — is what China will do with what it has learned.

Inside a TSMC cleanroom in Taiwan. The island produces the advanced chips that power AI, finance, militaries, and the entire digital economy. A prize Beijing wants intact.

The answer is almost certainly not an immediate military assault. Military force against Taiwan remains enormously costly even for the aggressor, and Beijing’s preferred outcome has always been absorption rather than destruction. A war that reduced TSMC’s facilities to rubble would achieve the political objective while eliminating the economic prize. That is not what Beijing wants.

Bonnie Glaser and Amanda Hsiao, writing in Foreign Affairs days before the Beijing summit, made the case that Beijing is playing a long game. Their article — “Why China Waits” — argues that China believes time is on its side, that its growing military and economic power will eventually pressure Taiwan into accommodation without requiring the catastrophic costs of invasion, and that the massive purges in the People’s Liberation Army suggest Xi is not prepared to use force in the near future. The costs of failure, Glaser argues, are too high, including the threat to the CCP’s own legitimacy. Beijing is watching the 2028 Taiwanese presidential election as the next inflection point, and it is watching the opposition-controlled legislature constrain Lai’s room to manoeuvre.

What Beijing wants — and what the Iran war proof of concept moves closer to being achievable — is a negotiated capitulation. A Taiwan that, facing a credible blockade threat backed by empirical evidence of American pain thresholds, concludes that resistance is futile and accommodation is the least bad option. A KMT government, perhaps, that accepts terms the DPP would never accept. A Washington that finds the economic argument for de-escalation more persuasive than the democratic argument for resistance.

The Iran war did not make this outcome inevitable. But it made it more plausible than it was forty days ago. It gave Beijing a model, a timeline, and a diminished American president who has now returned from a summit in which he publicly wavered on the arms sales, dismissed the legal commitments, and called the island whose future is at stake “a place.”

Vali Nasr, the foreign policy scholar, said of Iran after the ceasefire: “Controlling the strait is now Iran’s vital strategic asset. It’s more important than their nuclear program.”

Substitute Taiwan Strait for Hormuz. Substitute chips for oil. The sentence writes itself — and it is not a sentence that offers much comfort to the twenty-three million people who live on the island the sentence is about.

Chinese naval forces on exercise. The Iran war gave Beijing empirical data on American pain thresholds — and confidence that time, leverage, and controlled pressure may achieve what invasion cannot.
