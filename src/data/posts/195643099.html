The Ronald V. Dellums Federal Building in Oakland, California, where jury selection began on April 27, 2026, for the Musk v. OpenAI trial.

On the morning of April 27, 2026, two things happened simultaneously in the world of artificial intelligence governance.

In Oakland, California, jury selection began in the federal courthouse named after Ronald Dellums — a Democratic Socialist congressman who once said the answer to the question “what inhibits life and growth?” was corporate corruption and corporate power that controls the wealth and dominates people’s lives. Nine jurors were being assembled to hear Elon Musk’s claim that Sam Altman and OpenAI had betrayed the nonprofit founding promise that distinguished OpenAI from every other commercially-motivated AI lab. The trial had been eleven years in the making. The company at its centre is now valued at $852 billion.

Across the country, in a simultaneous announcement timed with the precision of people who understand that the news cycle has a short memory, Microsoft and OpenAI restructured their partnership agreement. They eliminated exclusivity. They capped revenue sharing. And they deleted the AGI clause — the single contractual provision that would have required both parties to pause and determine what their obligations were to each other, and implicitly to the world, if and when artificial general intelligence arrived.

The clause is gone. The jury is seated. The founding charter is in court. The last governance mechanism is in the bin.

That is the morning of April 27, 2026.

What the Trial Is Actually About

Sam Altman, central figure in the lawsuit over OpenAI’s shift from nonprofit founding principles.

Musk’s lawsuit, stripped of the personalities and the theatre, is a governance lawsuit. It asks a simple question: when a company makes a public promise about how it will be structured and what it will prioritize, and then breaks that promise, who has standing to object?

The answer OpenAI and its lawyers have given is: not Elon Musk. The answer Musk’s lawyers have given is: someone has to. The answer the Farrow-Marantz investigation gave, and the answer this series has been building toward for thirty parts, is that the question of who has standing to object is precisely the governance gap — and that no existing structure answers it satisfactorily.

The four charges heading into trial are unjust enrichment, fraud, constructive fraud, and breach of charitable trust. The remedies Musk is seeking include forcing the return of profits from the for-profit conversion to OpenAI’s charitable foundation, stripping Altman and co-founder Greg Brockman of their positions, and reversing the structural shift entirely. The damages figure his lawyers have put to the court is $134 billion.

Elon Musk arriving at the courthouse during the OpenAI trial proceedings.

Set aside whether Musk’s motives are pure. They are not. He has his own AI company, xAI, which competes directly with OpenAI. He has personal grievances that predate the lawsuit by years. He was held liable last month by a separate jury for defrauding investors in his Twitter acquisition. The man bringing a case about honesty in corporate governance has his own honesty problems.

None of that makes the underlying question less important. The founding promise was real. The people who took pay cuts to join OpenAI believed it. The regulators who did not intervene because OpenAI was a nonprofit relied on it. The public, which was told this company was different from Google and Meta and Microsoft because it was not driven by shareholder returns, was told something that was true when it was said and became progressively less true in ways that were never disclosed.

Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers — who also presided over Epic Games v. Apple, and who has a demonstrated tolerance for complex technology cases with governance implications — said during a pre-trial hearing that she believes the case merited a trial because “it’s about whether a jury believes the people who will testify and whether they are credible.” That framing is exactly right, and it goes well beyond the specific claims. Credibility is what the entire AI governance debate is about. The question of whether the people building transformative technology can be believed when they describe what they are doing and why is the question this series has been asking since Part 1.

What the AGI Clause Deletion Actually Means

The AGI clause was not a governance mechanism anyone would have designed from scratch. It was a contractual artifact — a provision inserted into a commercial partnership agreement to handle a contingency so significant that neither party could fully anticipate its implications. If OpenAI declared that it had achieved artificial general intelligence, Microsoft’s IP rights to future models would change. The revenue-sharing structure would change. The entire relationship would be subject to renegotiation.

The clause mattered not because it was well-designed but because it was something. It was an acknowledgment, written into a binding legal agreement between two of the most powerful technology companies in the world, that the arrival of AGI was a threshold event that required a deliberate response. It said: if this thing happens, we stop and we decide what it means.

That clause no longer exists. As of the morning of April 27, 2026, Microsoft and OpenAI have agreed that AGI — should it arrive — is a commercial event like any other, to be managed within the existing terms of a technology licensing agreement. The revenue sharing continues through 2030. The IP license runs through 2032. Whether or not the technology achieves a capability that its own creators have described as potentially civilizational, the paperwork stays the same.

There is an additional detail worth noting. Under the revised agreement, OpenAI can no longer unilaterally declare the achievement of AGI. That determination now requires an independent expert panel. This sounds like an improvement in governance. It is, in the narrowest technical sense. But the independent panel’s role is to verify the commercial milestone, not to trigger any broader accountability to the public or to any democratic institution. The panel answers to the parties to the contract. It does not answer to Congress, to any regulatory body, to the people who will live with the consequences, or to the jury currently assembling in Oakland to hear a case about whether OpenAI’s previous promises meant anything.

The AGI clause was weak governance. Its deletion is the absence of governance. These are different things.

The Atlantic’s Question

In an Atlantic piece published on April 27 — the same morning the trial opened and the AGI clause disappeared — The Atlantic posed the question that frames this entire moment: what happens if Trump seizes AI companies?

The piece by Matteo Wong and Lila Shroff is not a warning about imminent nationalization. It is a careful map of the governance vacuum — an accounting of all the levers the executive branch already holds, all the ways soft nationalization is already underway, and the fundamental impossibility at the centre of the current moment. It deserves to be read in full. But its most important contribution to this series is not any single finding. It is the shape of the problem it describes.

Three futures, none of them good.

The first is full nationalization — top researchers from across the AI companies forced to work out of SCIFs in the Pentagon basement, reporting to Pete Hegseth, focused primarily on defence applications. Wong and Shroff are clear: this is almost certainly illegal under the Constitution, which generally prevents the government from seizing private property without compensation, and the government is unlikely to produce the trillions of dollars the industry is collectively worth. Full nationalization is the nightmare scenario. It is probably not the actual scenario.

The Pentagon in Washington, D.C., site of agreements with major AI companies for classified deployments announced shortly after the trial opened.

The second is utility regulation — the government treating AI the way it treats electricity and water, imposing requirements around service reliability, preventing companies from charging more than cost, mandating a baseline level of capability for all customers. Sam Altman has himself described a future in which “intelligence is a utility like electricity or water and people buy it from us on a meter.” Jensen Huang of Nvidia has said every country should have AI as part of its infrastructure. This framing serves the companies’ own interests — being classified as a service provider is, as the era of social media demonstrated, an excellent way to avoid liability for harmful outputs — but it is the scenario most likely to be presented as governance progress while delivering very little of it. Utility regulation tells you how much OpenAI can charge. It does not tell you what it can build.

The third is soft nationalization. And here is the thing: it is not coming. It is already here.

On May 1, 2026 — four days after the trial opened and the AGI clause was deleted — the Pentagon made the roster official. Defense Department CTO Emil Michael announced agreements with seven AI companies authorizing deployment of their technology across classified networks for what the contracts describe as "lawful operational use": Google, OpenAI, Nvidia, Microsoft, Amazon Web Services, SpaceX — which recently merged with Elon Musk's xAI — and Reflection, a startup developing open-weight models. Every major frontier lab except Anthropic signed the language Anthropic refused. Anthropic remains blacklisted. Its Mythos model, Michael told CNBC the same morning, is "a separate national security moment" — a government-wide emergency that the NSA is reportedly already responding to by using the model the Pentagon officially considers a supply chain risk. The same government that blacklisted Anthropic for refusing to remove its red lines against autonomous weapons and domestic surveillance is using Anthropic's most dangerous model anyway, while the other six companies have agreed in writing that the Pentagon can use their technology for any lawful purpose it chooses. Emil Michael's summary of this situation: "Those are negotiable based on what they are with all the companies, and they have different views on that." Soft nationalization, governance by improvisation, and the race to the bottom — all confirmed in a single CNBC interview, on the same morning that Elon Musk was on the witness stand in Oakland arguing about a nonprofit's founding promise.

Pentagon CTO Emil Michael confirmed May 1, 2026 that Anthropic remains blacklisted while its Mythos model is being treated as a government-wide national security emergency. The NSA is reportedly using the model anyway. “Those are negotiable,” Michael said of the guardrails. That sentence is the state of AI governance in America. CNBC, May 1, 2026.

Four days later, the picture sharpened further. Anthropic's Mythos — the model the Pentagon officially considers a supply chain risk — is being used by the military in Maven, the system that analyzes intelligence and suggests targets for airstrikes in the war in Iran. The NSA has also used it to assess vulnerabilities in government software. The same model the government blacklisted is being used to help decide who gets bombed. Meanwhile, David Sacks — the White House AI czar who spearheaded the administration's deregulation efforts — has left his role. Susie Wiles and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent have stepped in. The people now shaping US AI policy are a chief of staff and a Treasury Secretary managing a dispute between the Pentagon and a company whose model is simultaneously blacklisted and in use on the battlefield.

The Trump administration has taken a ten percent stake in Intel. The Army has established a new detachment for senior tech leaders; its first four recruits are executives from Meta, Palantir, and OpenAI. OpenAI has appointed retired NSA director Paul Nakasone to its board. OpenAI engineers are being deployed to work alongside the military. The Defence Production Act — the Cold War law that Hegseth threatened Anthropic with during the Pentagon standoff examined in Part 25 — was invoked by Trump last week to fund new energy projects, and multiple sources confirm it is being discussed again in the context of AI following the Mythos announcement. Google is negotiating its own Pentagon contract for classified settings.

This is soft nationalization: a hybrid in which the government is close enough to the industry to share its interests and distant enough from democratic oversight to ignore the public’s. The companies are not controlled. They are captured. The distinction matters because captured companies retain the appearance of private governance — the boards, the safety teams, the constitutional documents, the voluntary commitments — while operating under a set of incentives that are determined by their relationship with state power rather than by any of those structures.

Dario Amodei’s warning.

Dario Amodei, Anthropic CEO, whose public warnings about surveillance capabilities underscore the governance vacuum.

There is a detail in the Wong-Shroff piece that deserves to stand on its own. Anthropic’s CEO has publicly warned of a near future in which a powerful AI scans billions of conversations from millions of people to gauge public sentiment, detect pockets of disloyalty forming, and stamp them out before they grow. He is describing a surveillance state architecture enabled by the technology his own company is building. He is not describing a hypothetical. He is describing a capability that exists, that his own models are approaching, and that the Pentagon — the same Pentagon that designated his company a supply-chain risk when it declined to remove its red lines — is seeking access to.

This is the governance problem in its starkest form. The CEO of the safety-focused lab is warning about the precise misuse that his technology makes possible, while his technology is being sought by the government he is warning about, while that government is simultaneously threatening his company’s existence and negotiating its return to favour. Amodei took a trip to the White House last week. President Trump said a Pentagon deal with Anthropic might still be on the table.

The red lines are negotiable. The warnings are on the record. The technology is being built regardless.

The Manhattan Project they didn’t build.

Wong and Shroff close their piece with the most clarifying comparison available. The Manhattan Project coordinated the efforts of scientists, private companies, and America’s leaders under a governance structure — imperfect, secretive, ultimately world-altering in ways its creators did not fully anticipate — but a structure nonetheless. Decisions were made by people with defined accountability. Resources were allocated by a process with political oversight. The outcome was public, in the sense that the existence of the weapon became known and its use was a decision made by elected officials.

What we have instead, as Wong and Shroff put it, is the equivalent of Boeing and DuPont racing each other to develop the atomic bomb while Hegseth and Trump led the military.

There is no coordinating structure. There is a race. The competitors in the race are simultaneously the primary source of safety assurances, the primary lobbyists against meaningful regulation, and the primary contractors for the military applications of the technology they are also warning about. The government overseeing the race is the same government threatening to seize the competitors, deploying their executives inside the military, and invoking Cold War emergency powers to accelerate infrastructure development.

Wong and Shroff call the passage between these outcomes a Scylla and Charybdis — the tremendous concentration of power in government or in a small cadre of companies, with no navigable channel between them. The political dysfunction of the current administration, they note, complicates everything: entrusting the future of AI to Altman and Amodei or to Trump and Hegseth are two very different and similarly disastrous outcomes. The Wall Street Journal reported the same week that the US and China are considering official discussions about AI safety at an upcoming Trump-Xi summit — the first suggestion of anything resembling the nonproliferation framework that Mundie and Friedman described as urgently necessary in Part 25.
Whether those talks produce anything is a separate question.

This is where the Asimov Gap has been pointing since Part 1. The gap is not simply the absence of regulation. It is the absence of any legitimate governance architecture — any structure with democratic accountability, enforceable standards, and the authority to make binding decisions about how this technology is developed and deployed. The morning of April 27, 2026, is not the beginning of that absence. It is the moment it became impossible to deny.

The Courthouse and the Charter

The courthouse in Oakland where the Musk v. Altman trial is being heard was built in 1993. It is named for a man who believed corporate power was the primary obstacle to human flourishing. On its first day of trial, the building is hosting a legal dispute about whether a corporation honoured a public promise. Outside, a protest is planned. Its theme, according to the organizers, is “everyone sucks here.”

They are not wrong. Musk is not a reliable champion of nonprofit governance. Altman is not a reliable narrator of his own intentions. The judge will make the final determination on liability, with the jury serving in an advisory role — which means the most consequential corporate governance case in the history of the AI industry will ultimately be decided by one person, unelected, applying existing contract law to a situation that existing contract law was not designed to govern.

This is the state of AI oversight in April 2026. A nonprofit’s founding charter is being litigated under commercial fraud law. The last AGI governance clause has been deleted on the morning the trial opens. The federal government is simultaneously threatening nationalization and embedding its executives inside the companies it might nationalize. The safety-focused lab founded as an alternative to OpenAI has accepted $40 billion from Google. The man who founded OpenAI as a nonprofit is being sued by one of his co-founders for breaking the promise that distinguished it from every commercial alternative.

Asimov’s robots operated under explicit laws, publicly known and auditable. Everyone in his fictional universe understood the constraints. The drama came from edge cases in the rules, not from confusion about whether rules existed.

We have arrived at the edge case. The rules do not exist. The morning of April 27, 2026, is not the beginning of a governance crisis. It is the moment the governance crisis became impossible to deny.
