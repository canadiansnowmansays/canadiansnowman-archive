Sam Altman, CEO of OpenAI — the central figure in the governance drama that created Anthropic.

In November 2023, OpenAI’s board voted to fire Sam Altman. In the five days that followed — before the employee resignations, before Microsoft intervened, before Altman was reinstated — the board seriously discussed merging OpenAI with Anthropic and handing the company to Dario Amodei.

This disclosure, which emerged from Ilya Sutskever’s testimony in the Musk v. Altman trial, is the most compressed summary of the AI governance crisis available. Anthropic was founded in 2020 because Amodei and six colleagues did not trust OpenAI’s governance structure or leadership. Three years later, when that governance structure failed most publicly, the board that fired Altman considered asking Amodei to lead the company those failures had produced.

The Little Brother was, for a brief window, the proposed solution to the problem that created it.

Dario Amodei, CEO of Anthropic — the founder who left OpenAI over governance and safety concerns.

It did not happen. Altman was reinstated. Anthropic remained independent. The two companies resumed their parallel trajectories — building the same technology, for the same customers, with the same funding sources, while maintaining, for marketing purposes, distinct identities.

That marketing distinction is the subject of this essay.

The Dresser Memo

In April 2026, Matteo Wong from The Atlantic obtained an internal OpenAI memo written by an executive named Dresser. The memo’s assessment of Anthropic was blunt: “Their story is built on fear. Our positive message will win over time.”

The memo was not intended for public consumption. It was strategy — a competitive analysis of how Anthropic positions itself and how OpenAI should respond. The framing was dismissive: Anthropic trades on anxiety about AI risk, OpenAI trades on AI’s promise, and the promise will eventually beat the fear.

What the memo does not acknowledge — what it cannot acknowledge, because it would require OpenAI to describe its own history honestly — is why Anthropic’s story is built on fear.

Anthropic exists because six of OpenAI’s most senior researchers and executives became afraid. They were afraid of the governance structure Altman had built. They were afraid of the commercial pressures reshaping OpenAI from a safety-focused nonprofit into a product-driven company. They were afraid that the organization they had joined to prevent the catastrophic development of AI was becoming the kind of organization they had joined to counterbalance.

They left. They built a new organization explicitly around the proposition that safety had to be structural — not a value statement on a website, but a governance commitment enforced by the organization’s founding architecture.

That proposition is Anthropic’s product. It is, in the Dresser memo’s language, a story built on fear. But the fear it is built on is specific and documented. It is the fear of what happens when you trust the governance of transformative AI to the judgment of one person, operating without adequate external accountability, in a structure that proves unable to enforce its own stated commitments.

That fear, as three weeks of federal testimony confirmed from multiple witnesses under oath, was correct.

The Mirror

The public marketing contrast between OpenAI and Anthropic that the Dresser memo sought to exploit

By May 2026, the distinction between OpenAI and Anthropic that the Dresser memo was designed to exploit had become structurally difficult to defend.

Anthropic’s revenue rate had hit $30 billion — apparently surpassing OpenAI’s. Its valuation, in some private market estimates, had crossed $1 trillion. Google had committed $40 billion. Amazon had contributed billions more. The safety-focused alternative to OpenAI was now one of the most heavily capitalized companies in the history of technology, funded primarily by two of the largest technology conglomerates in the world.

The copy pattern that defines the competitive relationship is worth noting. Claude Code launched; OpenAI launched Codex. Anthropic published its Constitutional AI safety framework; OpenAI launched a safety campaign. Anthropic withheld Mythos; the White House began discussing pre-release government vetting. Anthropic refused Pentagon terms; the other six major frontier labs signed. In each case, Anthropic acts and OpenAI responds — or the government responds — or the industry responds. The Little Brother sets the pace.

And yet. The Pentagon blacklisted Anthropic for refusing to remove its red lines against autonomous weapons and domestic surveillance. The same Pentagon is using Anthropic’s Mythos model to suggest targets for airstrikes in the war in Iran. The NSA is using it to assess vulnerabilities in government software. The same government that designated Anthropic a supply chain risk is using its most dangerous model on active military operations.

Meanwhile, Google — which committed $40 billion to the safety-focused alternative to OpenAI — is simultaneously negotiating its own Pentagon contract for classified network deployment. The money funding Anthropic’s independence is the money that underwrites the military applications Anthropic’s founding principles were designed to prevent.

Wong’s closing line on the competitive dynamics is the sharpest sentence written about the Little Brother relationship: “There hasn’t been any real innovation in the business models of Silicon Valley.” The safety-focused lab and the commercial behemoth have converged. The mirror is the product.

The Colossus Detail

One disclosure from the trial’s third week belongs in this essay because it is the clearest available proof of the convergence.

SpaceX built the Colossus 1 data center — more than 220,000 Nvidia GPUs, one of the largest AI computing facilities ever constructed. It was built for xAI, Musk’s AI company, which merged with SpaceX. Anthropic has announced it will use all of Colossus 1’s compute capacity.

The company Musk built to compete with OpenAI and Anthropic is renting its flagship data center to Anthropic — because xAI has no competitive model capable of using the infrastructure at the scale it was built for. The man suing Sam Altman for betraying OpenAI’s founding promise is simultaneously the landlord of the safety-focused alternative to OpenAI.

Rows of servers inside xAI’s Colossus supercluster — the massive GPU facility Anthropic is renting from SpaceX at $1.25 billion per month.

The financial terms, disclosed in Anthropic’s pre-IPO filings in May 2026, put a number on the arrangement: Anthropic is paying SpaceX $1.25 billion per month over three years to rent compute capacity. The safety-focused alternative to OpenAI is writing its largest cheque to the company owned by the man who sued OpenAI for betraying its founding promise — a man who lost that lawsuit three days before SpaceX filed the largest IPO prospectus in history.

The AI industry’s founding feuds have produced a situation in which the plaintiff’s company is renting its infrastructure to the defendant’s most significant competitor, while that competitor was simultaneously considered as the proposed solution to the defendant’s governance failures. There is no clean narrative available. There is only the structure.

The Sacks Departure and What It Means

David Sacks — the White House AI czar who built the Trump administration’s deregulatory AI framework — departed his role in the weeks before the trial concluded. Susie Wiles and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent stepped in. Neither has a technology background. The people now shaping US AI policy are a chief of staff and a Treasury Secretary managing a dispute between the Pentagon and a company whose model is simultaneously blacklisted and in use on the battlefield.

This matters for the Little Brother argument because Sacks’s departure removed the person most ideologically committed to the proposition that AI governance should be left entirely to the market. His replacements are political operators. Political operators follow incentives. The incentives are pointing toward governance: the Mythos announcement, the White House’s interest in pre-release vetting, the 80 percent of AI insiders who now support regulation, up from 20 percent the previous year.

Paul Tudor Jones put the industry’s position plainly on CNBC: the leader of one of the companies told him he was surprised the industry wasn’t regulated yet. The Lieu bill is still about deepfakes. But the 20-to-80 percent swing in a single year is the most significant governance signal of the trial period — and it belongs in the Little Brother analysis because both Anthropic and OpenAI are now, publicly, part of the 80 percent.

What that support looks like in practice is measurable. OpenAI doubled its federal lobbying spend to $1 million in the first quarter of 2026 and is opening a showroom called the Workshop blocks from the White House — its chief global affairs officer quoting Hamilton about it being “the room where it happens.” Anthropic increased its Washington lobbying spending tenfold to $3 million last year, hired six lobbying firms in recent months, and brought on the Trump-connected Ballard Partners in March to support its Pentagon case. A quarter of all 13,000 federal lobbyists in Washington now work AI issues, up from 11 percent in 2023. The safety-focused lab and the commercial behemoth are both in the room where the laws get written — and spending to make sure they stay there.

The question is whether the regulation they support is the regulation that would actually constrain them, or the regulation that would constrain their competitors. The electoral dimension of that question — how the same companies are funding congressional races through super PACs and dark money vehicles to choose who writes the laws — is examined in Super PACman Part XI: “The New Maze.”

The Altman Answer

On May 12, 2026, Sam Altman sat in the witness chair and answered questions under oath for the first time in his life.

His central defence of the commercial turn was stated twice, growing firmer each time: “Every step of the way, I have done my best to maximize the value of the nonprofit. I would point out that there are not a lot of historical examples of a nonprofit at this scale. And we were kind of left for dead.”

This is the argument that the Dresser memo’s “positive message” is built on. OpenAI became commercial not to betray the mission but to fund it. The nonprofit wrapper required the for-profit engine. The only way to build a multitrillion dollar charity was to raise billions through a for-profit venture.

The argument is coherent. It is also precisely the argument that every governance structure is supposed to prevent from becoming the only argument available — the argument that the mission required abandoning the structures built to protect it. Altman’s defence of OpenAI’s commercial turn is, structurally, the same argument Anthropic’s founders were afraid of when they left. They were afraid that the mission would eventually justify the structure, rather than the structure protecting the mission.

That fear has now been stated, under oath, by the CEO of the company they left to build an alternative.

The alternative is now valued at over a trillion dollars, funded by Google and Amazon, renting compute from Musk’s data center at $1.25 billion per month, having been considered as OpenAI’s replacement during the 2023 governance crisis, and simultaneously blacklisted by and working for the Pentagon.

The mirror is complete.

The Asimov Frame

Asimov understood that the governance problem was not individual. His robots were governed by the Three Laws — not by the character of their builders. The builders could be brilliant or venal, careful or reckless. The Laws were supposed to operate independently of builder quality.

The AI industry has inverted this. The governance of both OpenAI and Anthropic depends, ultimately, on the character of their CEOs. Altman’s trustworthiness was the central question of a three-week federal trial. Amodei’s judgment is the basis on which Anthropic’s entire governance proposition rests. The safety-focused lab’s primary structural advantage over the commercial behemoth is that its CEO is the person who left the commercial behemoth because he was afraid of what it was becoming.

That is a governance structure built on a person. It is more defensible than OpenAI’s governance structure built on Altman. It is less defensible than a governance structure built on enforceable public law.

The Dresser memo said Anthropic’s story is built on fear. That is true. The fear is of what happens when you trust AI governance to individuals operating without external accountability. Three weeks of testimony in a federal courthouse confirmed, from multiple witnesses under oath, that the fear was correct.

The governance argument is not that Anthropic is good and OpenAI is bad. The governance argument is that both organizations are operating in a vacuum that neither can fill, regardless of how good their CEOs are. The Little Brother problem is not that the Little Brother is too similar to the Big Brother. It is that no brother — however safety-focused, however well-funded, however recently confirmed as the proposed solution to the Big Brother’s governance failures — is a substitute for the laws that do not yet exist.
