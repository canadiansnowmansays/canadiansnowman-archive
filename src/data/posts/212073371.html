This ledger has spent nine parts tracing how AI’s capital expenditure gets financed, obscured, and occasionally revealed as something other than what it was advertised to be. Part X is the part where that financing stops being a story about corporate balance sheets and starts showing up in the interest rate on somebody’s mortgage.

As Joe Rennison reported for the New York Times on August 20, 2026, yields on U.S. government bonds hit nearly two-decade highs during the week of August 17, 2026, prompting the Treasury Department to intervene directly in the market to try to hold borrowing costs down. Analysts increasingly attribute part of that rise to a structural shift in how the AI industry pays for itself: the largest technology companies, having spent most of the past decade self-funding their infrastructure buildout from operating cash flow, have turned to the bond market at a scale that is now large enough to move the price of money itself.

The Numbers That Mark the Shift

Lucas Baynes, a senior investment strategist at Vanguard, offered the cleanest single-sentence summary of the change: for most of the past decade, the large technology companies leading the AI build-out funded their investment from operating cash flow, and that era is ending. The data behind the claim is stark. From 2020 to 2024, the five so-called AI hyperscalers — Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, and Oracle — collectively issued an average of less than $30 billion in debt per year, according to Refinitiv figures cited in the reporting. In 2025, that figure rose above $100 billion. In the first eight months of 2026 alone, it has already surpassed $200 billion. Vanguard’s own forecast puts broader AI-related debt issuance — beyond just the five hyperscalers — on pace to exceed $1 trillion annually from 2027 through 2030.

Microsoft is, notably, the only one of the five hyperscalers that has not raised money in the bond market over the past year, a detail worth filing alongside this series’ earlier observations about which companies are managing their AI exposure conservatively and which are not. Oracle, by contrast, is a name this ledger has already spent an entire part on. Part VI covered Larry Ellison’s leveraged, roughly 500-percent debt-to-equity position in detail; this reporting adds the financing-cost consequence directly. Oracle, the lowest-rated of the five hyperscalers, paid a spread of 1.05 percentage points over Treasuries to borrow for ten years in September 2025. By February 2026, investors were demanding 1.45 percentage points for the same maturity — a forty-basis-point deterioration in borrowing terms in under six months, for the company this series already flagged as carrying the most leveraged position in the group.

Why This Isn’t Simply a Corporate Borrowing-Cost Story

The mechanism connecting AI capex to Treasury yields is more interesting, and more consequential, than “big companies are borrowing more.” Matt King, founder of the market research firm Satori Insights, frames the shift precisely: the economy has enjoyed a substantial boost from the hyperscalers spending enormous stockpiles of cash on AI infrastructure, but that dynamic has changed now that the same companies are borrowing rather than spending down reserves, which means further capital expenditure is no longer effectively “free” to the broader economy the way self-funded spending was. Real yields are rising as a result, King argues, raising costs for everyone else — not because AI companies are competing with the Treasury directly for a fixed pool of capital in any simple sense, but because AI-driven growth expectations are themselves feeding into the Federal Reserve’s own calculus.

The logic runs like this: heavier AI investment, funded increasingly through debt rather than cash reserves, has led economists and investors to raise their growth forecasts for the broader economy. Higher expected growth typically pushes the Federal Reserve toward keeping interest rates elevated, specifically to prevent that growth from generating inflation. Analysts quoted in the reporting say the yield spike in mid-to-late August 2026 partly reflects exactly this expectation — that AI-driven growth will keep the Fed’s hand on the brake longer than markets had previously priced in. This is the fiscal-policy mechanism this series’ Part VIII examined from the tax-revenue side; Part X is its interest-rate mirror image, and the two are not separate stories. A more productive, faster-growing, AI-driven economy was supposed to be the optimistic case for closing the deficit gap through higher tax receipts. This reporting shows the same growth expectation simultaneously pushing up the government’s own borrowing costs, which is exactly the offsetting mechanism Part VIII flagged as a risk without yet having a concrete 2026 data point attached to it. Now there is one: the thirty-year Treasury yield, a benchmark that feeds directly into mortgage rates, hit its highest level since 2007 during that same week.

There is a specific irony worth naming in this dynamic, one this series has not yet had occasion to spell out directly. The entire case for AI as a fiscal tailwind, examined at length in Part VIII, rests on the premise that productivity gains show up as economic growth, and economic growth shows up as tax revenue, without requiring anyone to raise a rate. That case treated the Federal Reserve’s response to AI-driven growth as a background variable, largely separate from the fiscal question. This reporting demolishes that separation. The Fed’s own reaction to AI-driven growth expectations — holding rates elevated to head off inflation — is not a side effect of the fiscal story. It is a direct, mechanical component of it, and one that raises borrowing costs on the government’s existing $40 trillion in debt at the same time it is meant to be generating the tax revenue that pays that debt down. The optimistic case and the pessimistic case are not two competing forecasts about the future. They are two halves of the same mechanism, running simultaneously, in the same fiscal year.

The Treasury’s Own Intervention, and Why It Didn’t Hold

The government’s response is itself a data point worth sitting with. On August 19, 2026, the Treasury Department stepped directly into the bond market and increased the amount of its own debt it is willing to buy back, a move explicitly designed to expand demand, push up prices, and pull yields back down. It worked, briefly. By August 20, 2026, yields were rising again. That one-day reversal is a small but telling illustration of the limits of the tools available here: the Treasury can intervene in its own market and buy itself some room, but it cannot make the underlying growth-expectation dynamic disappear, and it certainly cannot make the hyperscalers’ $200-billion-and-rising debt issuance disappear either.

The specific tool the Treasury used — expanding its own buybacks of outstanding government debt — is itself worth a moment’s scrutiny, because it is a demand-side patch applied to what this reporting shows is fundamentally a supply-and-expectations problem. Buying back debt increases demand for existing bonds, which pushes their price up and their yield down, but it does nothing to slow the pace at which new AI-related corporate debt continues entering the same broader capital markets, and it does nothing to alter the Fed’s own rate posture, which remains the deeper driver analysts are pointing to. A single day of yields falling and then resuming their climb is not evidence the intervention failed outright — one day is too short a window to draw firm conclusions either way — but it is evidence that this particular lever moves the number without touching the mechanism generating it, the fiscal-policy equivalent of opening a window in one room while the furnace in the basement keeps running unchanged.

This matters politically as well as financially. The Trump administration has explicitly promised to lower borrowing costs as part of its affordability agenda, using Treasury yields as a public gauge of its own success. An administration measuring itself against a number that is being pushed upward, at least in part, by a technology boom the same administration has spent considerable political capital promoting is not a comfortable position, and the reporting is careful to note that AI borrowing is not the only factor — the federal deficit and the ongoing Iran conflict are cited as contributing causes as well. But “not the only factor” is a different claim than “not a factor,” and the specific mechanism here — hyperscaler debt issuance feeding growth expectations feeding Fed posture — is new enough, and large enough, that it deserves to be tracked on its own terms rather than folded silently into “the deficit” as an undifferentiated backdrop.

The Spread Data, Read Company by Company

The reporting’s spread data across the hyperscalers offers a useful gauge of how investors are actually pricing AI-company risk right now, as distinct from how the companies’ equity is being priced. Alphabet, Google’s parent, still commands some of the lowest corporate borrowing costs in the group — a spread of 0.63 percentage points over ten-year Treasuries when it issued bonds in April 2026, widening to 0.85 percentage points on a second ten-year issuance the same month as this reporting. Amazon’s ten-year borrowing cost moved from a 0.55-point spread in November 2025 to 0.8 points by its July 2026 issuance. Both of these are increases, but modest ones relative to Oracle’s forty-basis-point deterioration, and both remain below the roughly one-percentage-point spread that a Bank of America investment-grade corporate bond index — indicative of an average similarly rated company’s ten-year borrowing cost — was showing at the time of the reporting.

Matt Eagan, a portfolio manager at Loomis Sayles, offers the reporting’s most important clarifying point, and one this series should take seriously rather than let get lost under the more dramatic yield-spike headline: rising corporate borrowing costs here do not reflect investor doubt about whether these companies can pay their debts back. They are, in Eagan’s words, highly rated businesses generating substantial profit, not in danger of default. The actual investor concern is volume, not solvency — the sheer amount of new debt, from governments and AI companies alike, that the market is being asked to absorb simultaneously, at a pace few investors expect to slow. Eagan’s framing of the risk — being stuck holding today’s bonds if tomorrow’s yields are higher still — is a liquidity and reinvestment concern, not a credit concern, and conflating the two would overstate the danger these companies individually pose while understating the systemic one the volume itself represents.

Where This Sits in the Ledger

Read against this series’ prior parts, Part X supplies the missing macro link between two threads that had, until now, run on separate tracks. Part I’s account of the $662 billion in off-balance-sheet lease liabilities and Michael Burry’s roughly $176 billion depreciation-accounting critique were both balance-sheet stories — questions about how AI capex was being counted, not about what it cost to finance. Part X shows that financing cost rising in real time, in the most liquid and closely watched market in the world, with a specific, named mechanism (growth expectations feeding Fed policy) rather than a vague gesture at “the deficit.” Part V’s account of Nvidia’s $105 billion Piketon commitment and Part VI’s account of Oracle’s leverage were both stories about individual companies’ financing structures; Part X shows those individual structures aggregating into a market-wide phenomenon large enough to move the thirty-year Treasury yield to an almost two-decade high.

The uncomfortable question this leaves the rest of this series’ fiscal optimism to answer is the one Part VIII raised without yet being able to quantify: if AI-driven growth is meant to help close the deficit through higher tax revenue, and the same AI-driven growth expectations are simultaneously pushing up the government’s own borrowing costs on its existing $40 trillion in debt, the two effects are not additive in the government’s favour. They are, at least partially, working against each other inside the same fiscal year, and the Treasury’s brief August 19 intervention — effective for a single day, then overwhelmed by the renewed climb on August 20 — is the first concrete evidence this ledger has that the offsetting effect is not merely theoretical. It is already visible in the bond market, and it arrived faster than the optimistic revenue case in Part VIII assumed it would.

None of this requires believing the hyperscalers themselves are in financial distress — Eagan’s point about solvency versus volume stands on its own, and this series has no basis for predicting a default among any of the five companies driving this debt issuance. What it does require acknowledging is that the AI capex boom has now graduated from a story contained within corporate balance sheets and equity valuations to one embedded in the pricing of the single most consequential interest rate in the American economy. A reader who has followed this ledger from Part I’s account of off-balance-sheet lease liabilities through Part IX’s data-center tax fights has been tracking, in effect, the slow diffusion of one industry’s financing decisions outward into ever-larger and more public arenas — from corporate filings, to local tax rolls, to state politics, and now to the Treasury market itself. Each part of that progression made the underlying story a little harder to contain and a little easier for an ordinary reader to feel directly, whether or not they have ever bought a share of Oracle or Alphabet stock. A rising thirty-year Treasury yield is not an abstraction. It is the number a bank uses to price the next thirty years of somebody’s mortgage, and as of August 2026, that number is being shaped, in part, by how badly a handful of technology companies want to build data centers faster than their own cash reserves can fund.
