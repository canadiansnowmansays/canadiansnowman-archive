The earliest draft of this series’ outline compressed all four Churchill daughters into a single part, a single sentence of description each, on the theory that a series with sixteen parts to fill could not afford much more than that for anyone who wasn’t Winston himself. I want to name that compression plainly, because it is exactly the kind of decision this series exists to interrogate rather than repeat, and because the four lives in question deserve considerably more than a clause.

Diana Spencer Churchill was born on July 11, 1909, the first of Winston and Clementine’s five children, and was known within the family by the private nickname “Puppy Kitten” or “PK” — an affectionate name that, by most family accounts, reflected a genuinely close early bond with her father. She trained briefly at the Royal Academy of Dramatic Art, though acting was never her focus, and married twice: first to the businessman John Milner Bailey in 1932, a marriage that collapsed within three years, and then, in September 1935, to the Conservative politician Duncan Sandys — a match made during one of Randolph’s own political campaigns, and one that produced three children and lasted twenty-five years before ending in divorce in 1960. Diana served in the Women’s Royal Naval Service during the war, and by most accounts spent much of her adult life managing a difficult relationship with her own mother that only began to warm considerably later, after her divorce from Sandys, when she and Clementine grew notably closer during the final years of Diana’s life. She suffered repeated nervous breakdowns from the early 1950s onward, was treated at various points with electroconvulsive therapy, and in 1962 — in a detail that reads, in retrospect, as almost unbearably poignant — began volunteering with the Samaritans, the suicide-prevention organization, apparently finding in the work a genuine sense of purpose after decades of feeling defined mainly by her relation to more famous family members. She died at her home in London on October 20, 1963, from an overdose of barbiturates, not quite two years before her father’s own death; the coroner’s inquest ruled it a suicide. She was fifty-four.

Sarah Churchill, born on October 7, 1914, took a considerably more public path than her elder sister, training and working as a professional actress and dancer against her family’s initial preference for a more conventional path, and building a genuine career on stage and screen that culminated in her best-known film role in MGM’s “Royal Wedding” in 1951, opposite Fred Astaire. She married three times — first, in 1936, to the entertainer Vic Oliver, a marriage her father strongly disapproved of and which ended in divorce in 1945; then, in 1949, to the photographer Antony Beauchamp, who died by suicide in 1957; and finally, in 1962, to Thomas, 23rd Baron Audley, who died only a year later. Sarah served during the war in the Women’s Auxiliary Air Force, working in photographic reconnaissance interpretation — painstaking analytical work studying aerial photographs for evidence of enemy troop movements, industrial targets, and, later in the war, the V-weapon launch sites in northern France, a genuinely skilled intelligence discipline rather than any ceremonial wartime posting arranged for a prime minister’s daughter — and later accompanied her father to both the Tehran and Yalta conferences as an aide, placing her, like her sister Mary at Potsdam, at some of the twentieth century’s most consequential diplomatic gatherings, in a support role history has mostly declined to record in any detail. Her later life was shadowed by a public and well-documented struggle with alcohol; she was arrested for drunken conduct on more than one occasion, including a widely reported 1958 incident in California, and her drinking is generally acknowledged to have limited a career that her talent, by most contemporary assessments, could otherwise have sustained considerably further. In her later decades she turned to painting, producing a body of work that included portraits of her father, and to writing, publishing two volumes of memoir before her death in 1982 at the age of sixty-seven.

Marigold Frances Churchill, born in November 1918, is the daughter this series’ earliest outline came closest to erasing entirely, and it is worth pausing specifically on her, because a family’s genuine tragedies deserve better than a subordinate clause. She died on August 23, 1921, at not quite three years old, of septicaemia following a streptococcal throat infection, while the family was on a seaside holiday in Kent — a death that, by every account from those who witnessed it, very nearly broke Clementine Churchill entirely. Contemporaries described Clementine’s grief as approaching a kind of temporary derangement, a mother’s mourning so total that it frightened those around her, and the marriage itself, already tested by Winston’s demanding career, absorbed a wound from Marigold’s death that never fully closed. What emerged from that grief, roughly two years later, was Mary — conceived, by the family’s own later description of the sequence, in the specific aftermath of Marigold’s death, and raised with the private designation, half-affectionate and half-clinical, of “child of consolation.” It is a difficult thing to build an entire human being’s role in a family around the function of easing someone else’s grief, and Mary herself, across a long life, seems to have carried that designation with remarkably little apparent resentment — though what she may have felt privately about being conceived, in effect, as a replacement for a dead sister is not something the historical record allows this series to know with any confidence, and I am not going to speculate about it further than the plain fact permits.

Mary Spencer-Churchill, born on September 15, 1922, the youngest of the five children, built by a considerable margin the most durable and consequential second act of any Churchill sibling this series has examined, and did so largely by declining to compete on the terrain — journalism, political office, public performance — where her brother and sisters had each, in their own ways, struggled. She served during the war in the Auxiliary Territorial Service, operating anti-aircraft gun batteries in London, Belgium, and Germany — genuinely dangerous, hands-on military service rather than a ceremonial posting — and accompanied her father to the Potsdam Conference in July 1945, present in the room for one of the century’s most consequential diplomatic gatherings at the age of twenty-two. In 1947, visiting Paris as a guest of the British ambassador Duff Cooper and his wife, she met Christopher Soames, an assistant military attaché, and married him later that year over her mother’s initial misgivings — misgivings that, by every subsequent account, proved entirely unfounded. Soames became her father’s parliamentary private secretary and one of a small handful of men who effectively ran the government during the concealed stroke Churchill suffered in 1953, and went on to serve as British Ambassador to Paris and, later, the last British Governor of Southern Rhodesia during the delicate transition to Zimbabwean independence — a genuinely significant diplomatic career that Mary navigated alongside him with what contemporaries consistently describe as unusual skill and warmth. She wrote acclaimed biographies of both her parents, edited collections of their correspondence, and remained, by the time of her death in 2014 at ninety-one, the last surviving child of Winston and Clementine Churchill and one of the most respected figures in the family’s own institutional memory.

The Rhodesia posting, arriving late in the couple’s shared career and easy to skip past in a short account, deserves a moment’s specific attention, because it placed Mary at the center of one of the most fraught moments in the dissolution of the British Empire her own father had spent much of his career defending. Christopher Soames was appointed the last Governor of Southern Rhodesia in December 1979, tasked with overseeing the territory’s turbulent transition from the white-minority rule of Ian Smith’s unilateral declaration of independence to the internationally recognized independence of Zimbabwe under Robert Mugabe — a assignment that required him to preside, in effect, over the final act of exactly the kind of imperial contraction Winston Churchill had spent the 1930s trying to prevent in India and had watched, with visible discomfort, continue across the rest of the empire in the years after his own death. Mary accompanied him through the assignment’s genuine physical danger and diplomatic delicacy, in a colonial administration under armed guard amid an ongoing guerrilla war, and by contemporary accounts she managed the social and diplomatic demands of the posting with real skill during a handover most observers expected to go considerably worse than it eventually did. It is a fitting, if largely unremarked, closing chapter to this family’s imperial story: the daughter of the man who fought hardest against Indian self-government, present and instrumental at the literal handover ceremony ending Britain’s rule over one of its last African possessions.

There is a pattern across these four lives that this series would be dishonest to ignore, because it echoes a theme that has run through every part since the Introduction: the family’s inheritance, whatever else it included, does not appear to have included any reliable mechanism for protecting its own daughters from the psychological cost of being raised inside so large a shadow. Diana’s repeated breakdowns and eventual suicide, Sarah’s public struggles with alcohol across three difficult marriages, even Marigold’s early death and the particular weight subsequently placed on Mary as her designated replacement — none of this fits comfortably into the recovery-and-triumph template this series has traced since Leonard Jerome’s Wall Street ruins. That template, built by men operating in public arenas with public stages large enough to make catastrophic setbacks recoverable, appears to have had considerably less to offer daughters whose difficulties were, by the conventions of the period, expected to be managed privately rather than narrated publicly as a comeback in progress. Mary’s own success is genuinely remarkable, and this series does not want to diminish it by folding it into a uniformly tragic account of the sisters as a group. But it is worth noting that Mary’s success came specifically from building an identity oriented around supporting other people’s public careers — her husband’s diplomatic postings, her parents’ historical legacy — rather than from claiming a public stage of her own the way her father, grandfather, and even her more troubled siblings each, in their own ways, tried and often failed to do. It may be the shrewdest response available to a family whose model of resilience was built by and for men operating in arenas that were never, in the period these four lives spanned, equally open to their sisters.

One further detail is worth naming before this part closes, because it complicates any temptation to read these four lives as a unified sisterhood weathering the same pressures together. By most family accounts, the surviving sisters were not, in fact, especially close to one another or to their mother — each built a life oriented primarily around her father, her husband, or her own individual pursuits, rather than around any collective bond as “the Churchill girls” that popular retellings sometimes impose on them in retrospect. Diana’s warmest years came only after her divorce, when she grew closer to Clementine specifically; Sarah’s difficulties played out largely in the glare of her own public career and marriages, at some remove from her sisters’ domestic lives; and Mary’s own memoirs, by the account of biographers who have worked with the family’s papers, describe a household in which the four surviving children were raised alongside one another without necessarily being raised together, in the emotional sense the phrase implies. This series has spent nine parts examining what the Churchill men inherited and did with it. It is worth recording plainly, before moving on, that whatever bond held these four women together was considerably thinner than the family mythology of a united clan tends to suggest — which may be its own kind of inheritance, quietly passed down from a mother and father who were, by their own daughter’s later account, rather better at managing a nation’s survival than at managing their own children’s.

Part XI turns to the marriage that made nearly all of this survivable in the first place — Clementine herself, whose own ledger this series has referenced in passing for ten parts now, and who has earned considerably more than a passing reference by this point.
