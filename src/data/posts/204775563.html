I have been writing about Donald Trump for a year now — across the Trump Accountability Project, the Vance Project, When Democracy Fights Back, and more essays than my husband thinks is healthy — and I have learned that the man is most revealing not when he lies, which is constant and therefore uninformative, but when he tells the truth about what he wants. On climate and energy, he has been telling the truth with unusual clarity, and the truth is stranger and more interesting than the simple “Trump denies climate change” headline suggests. He is not, mostly, denying the science. He is doing something more audacious: he is insisting, loudly and on the record, that the correct response to a warming planet is to burn more of the thing that warms it, and that doing so is not a retreat but a form of strength.

I want to take that insistence seriously, because the easy move — the move most of my American colleagues make — is to treat it as mere stupidity or mere corruption, and it is neither, or rather it is not only those things. It is a coherent worldview, and worldviews are defeated by understanding, not by sputtering. So let me lay out what he has actually done, and then ask the harder question of why he insists it is the best way, because the why is where the real essay is.

Start with the record, which is not in dispute. On his first day back in office, January 20, 2025, he signed the order withdrawing the United States — for the second time — from the Paris Agreement, the accord nearly two hundred nations had signed to hold warming below catastrophic levels. The withdrawal formally took effect this past January, making the United States the only nation on Earth to have left the agreement not once but twice. The same day, he declared a “national energy emergency” in a country producing more oil than any nation in the history of the world, which is a bit like declaring a famine in a bakery. He revoked Biden’s climate executive orders, ordered the rollback of offshore drilling restrictions, reopened the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge to the question of drilling, and — the detail I want you to hold onto — imposed a moratorium on new wind projects on federal land.

Then came the coal, and the coal is where the worldview shows its face. In April 2025 he signed a clutch of executive orders titled, without irony, to “Reinvigorate America’s Beautiful Clean Coal Industry.” He designated coal a “mineral” to fast-track its extraction, ordered agencies to identify coal deposits on federal land, lifted the moratorium on coal leasing, and directed the entire federal apparatus to rescind any policy that sought to move the country away from the dirtiest fuel humanity has ever set fire to. By the end of 2025 his Energy Department was boasting of having saved more than seventeen gigawatts of coal power and prevented the closure of five coal plants that the market had already condemned.

And here is the justification, stated openly in the text of the order itself, that ties this essay to another I have been writing for over a year. The coal is to feed the machines. Section after section of the “beautiful clean coal” order directs the government to identify where coal infrastructure can be used “to power data centers to meet the electricity needs of AI and high-performance computing operations.” The order declares, in so many words, that America’s coal resources “will be critical to meeting the rise in electricity demand due to ... the construction of artificial intelligence data processing centers.” Readers of my Asimov Gap series will recognize this immediately, because it is the place where two of my long preoccupations collide. The explosion in AI computing demand that I have been tracking as a governance problem — Goldman Sachs projects it could raise global power demand by 165 per cent in five years — has now become the official reason to keep the dirtiest power plants in America burning past their retirement dates. The abstract energy appetite of the data center, which I have written about as a question of water and chips and governance, turns out to have a smokestack attached to it, and the smokestack is in Michigan.

It is worth following that Michigan example, because it makes the abstraction concrete. The J.H. Campbell plant on the shore of Lake Michigan, six decades old, was scheduled to close in May 2025; the workers were being moved to other jobs, the community was deciding what to do with the land. Then the administration ordered it to keep running, citing an energy emergency, and the cost of operating an aging coal plant that the market had already given up on will now be spread across millions of ratepayers across the Midwest. This is happening across the country — Santee Cooper in South Carolina pushing a citation-plagued plant’s retirement from 2028 into the mid-2030s, utilities everywhere “un-retiring” coal to chase the data center boom. By one analysis there are some twenty-five gigawatts of aging, costly coal capacity that will now keep running through 2030 to feed the incremental demand from data centers.

And the punchline, the thing that turns this from policy into doctrine, is the cost. Coal is not only the dirtiest option; it is now also among the most expensive. The U.S. Energy Information Administration puts the cost of coal power at roughly $89 per megawatt hour, against $31 for onshore wind and $23 for solar. Research from Energy Innovation found that ninety-nine per cent of existing American coal plants are more expensive to run than building new solar or wind would be. So the administration is not choosing coal because it is cheap, or clean, or modern. It is choosing the most expensive and dirtiest way to power the most modern machines ever built — and asking ordinary ratepayers to cover the difference. The miners are real, and I will come to them. But the economics are not on coal’s side, and have not been for years, which means the choice is being made for reasons that have nothing to do with economics at all.

By this past spring his Energy Department was even funding the construction of new coal plants — including, I am not making this up, a 1.25-gigawatt plant in Anchorage, Alaska, a state that is watching its own permafrost liquefy in real time.

Now, here is the line that I think unlocks the whole man. At the signing ceremony, he told the assembled miners: “I told my people, never use the word coal unless you put beautiful clean before it.” And then, of coal: “You could drop a bomb on it and it’s going to be there for you to use the next day.”

Sit with that for a moment, because it is not the statement of a man who has weighed the science and concluded it is wrong. It is the statement of a man for whom coal is not a fuel but a symbol — of permanence, of indestructibility, of a masculine and unkillable America that does not bend to weather or to Europeans or to scientists. “You could drop a bomb on it.” Coal, in this telling, is the thing that survives the apocalypse, which is a remarkable quality to prize in the substance that is helping to cause one. The denial here is not of physics. It is of vulnerability. The whole appeal of coal is that it cannot be hurt, and a man who has built a politics on never being seen to be hurt finds in it a kind of mascot.

And it gets more telling, because in 2025, with American electricity prices rising at more than twice the rate of inflation — driven, the energy analysts will tell you, by surging demand from AI data centres, by aging infrastructure, and by the very extreme weather that climate change is intensifying — he did not blame any of those. He blamed wind and solar, the cheapest power available, posting that renewable energy was “THE SCAM OF THE CENTURY!” and vowing to approve no new wind or solar projects at all. This is the inversion at the heart of the doctrine: the cheap, clean, domestic, fast-to-build energy source is the “scam,” and the expensive, dirty, slow source you have to dig out of a mountain is “beautiful.” The words have been made to mean their opposites, which, as I noted in the essay on the climate TACO, is the obfuscation stage raised to a kind of dark art.

The most revealing chapter, though, is the wind story, because it exposes what the doctrine actually costs. When the courts kept striking down his wind bans — a federal judge twice lifted the stop-work orders on the nearly-finished Revolution Wind project off Rhode Island, calling the first of them “the height of arbitrary and capricious action” — the administration did not give up. It switched tactics. It began paying offshore wind developers to walk away. By this spring it had agreed to spend nearly two billion dollars in taxpayer money — including $928 million to the French company TotalEnergies alone — to reimburse companies for the leases on permitted, financed, in-some-cases partly-built wind farms, on the condition that they cancel the projects and, in several cases, redirect the money into liquefied natural gas and oil instead. TotalEnergies agreed to pour its refund into a new LNG plant on the Gulf Coast. Seven states, led by New York’s attorney general, are now suing to undo the deal, calling it a scheme to pay a foreign company hundreds of millions of taxpayer dollars to abandon American energy.

Read that again, slowly. In the middle of what his own government calls an energy emergency, with electricity demand surging and prices climbing, the United States is spending billions of dollars to pay companies not to build energy. The Revolution Wind project off Rhode Island was more than eighty per cent complete — foundations in, all but seven turbines installed, transmission cable already laid — when it was halted on a “national security” rationale that a federal judge, reviewing even the classified version, found unpersuasive. This is not energy policy as most people understand the term. It is the deliberate destruction of cheap power to protect the symbolic primacy of expensive power, and the bill is being handed to the ratepayer and the taxpayer both.

I have laid out the full machinery of this in my energy series, The Great Energy Challenge, and will not repeat all of it here — the carbon-capture funds quietly rerouted to build new coal plants, the billions paid to strand the very wind and solar workers who had stepped into the jobs the miners left. It is, when you assemble it in one place, one of the strangest energy policies any wealthy nation has ever pursued: a government spending public money simultaneously to revive the most expensive power and to demolish the cheapest, and calling the combination strength. What I want to add here, in the context of this series, is only the deeper point — that all of it, every dollar of it, is in the service of defending a house built for a winter that is over.

So: why does he insist this is the best way? Here, I think, are the honest answers, and I want to give the strongest version of each before I weigh it, because anything less is the cheap shot I promised to avoid.

The first is genuine, and deserves respect: there are real people and real communities — in West Virginia, in Wyoming, in the coal counties that two generations of politicians from both parties wrote off — for whom this fuel is not a symbol but a paycheque, a heritage, a town’s whole reason to exist. When the elites of the coasts tell those communities that their work is shameful and their future is a retraining seminar, and then a man comes along and calls their coal “beautiful,” the appeal is not hard to understand, and a Canadian who has watched his own resource towns hollow out has no business sneering at it. That is the part of the doctrine that is about dignity, and it is real.

But dignity and abandonment are not the same thing, and here the doctrine's contradictions become physical. Even as the administration invokes those miners as the moral center of its coal revival, it has been slow-walking the very rule meant to keep them alive long enough to enjoy it. As the New York Times has reported, black lung disease — coal workers' pneumoconiosis, the "old man's disease" — is now afflicting men in their thirties and forties with a severity once seen only after decades underground; nationwide, an estimated one in ten working miners has it, and in the heart of central Appalachia, one in five. The cause is not mystery but arithmetic: as the big seams run out, miners cut through more rock to get less coal, and the crystalline silica in that rock shreds lungs faster than coal dust ever did. A federal rule tightening silica exposure limits — one MSHA's own analysis under the prior administration estimated would save over a thousand miners' lives — has been delayed under enforcement pressure from the industry whose workers it was designed to protect. The same government that tells these men their coal is beautiful and unkillable has not found the urgency to keep their lungs that way.

The second is about power, in both senses. Trump genuinely believes — and he is not alone, and it is not a crazy thing to believe — that cheap and abundant energy is the foundation of national strength, that a country which can power its factories and its data centres and its military without depending on anyone else has its hand on the master switch of the modern world. “Energy dominance” is the phrase. The error is not in valuing energy independence; the error is in deciding that the energy must be the old energy, that strength can only wear the costume of 1955, when in fact the cheapest and most domestic and most rapidly deployable power available to America today is the wind and sun he is paying to destroy. He has correctly identified energy as power and then chosen, for reasons that are about symbolism rather than strategy, the weaker source.

The third reason is the one I find hardest to be charitable about, so I will simply state it: the fossil fuel industry has been, for decades, among the most generous patrons of his party and his campaigns, and “drill, baby, drill” is not only a worldview but an invoice. I will not pretend the money is the whole story — it would be lazy, and it would let the genuine cultural appeal off the hook — but I will not pretend it is no part of the story either. The next essay, on the long history of manufactured doubt, will show how old and how deep that particular river runs.

But underneath the dignity and the power and the money, I think there is something simpler, and it returns us to where this series began. Donald Trump is, in the most literal sense, the political embodiment of the house built for winter. He is selling a vision of America calibrated perfectly to a world that no longer exists — an America of coal smoke and steel and indestructible certainties, of a climate that stays put and a future that looks like the past, of a strength that is measured in how much you can burn. It is a comprehensible vision. It is, for millions, a comforting vision. And it is built, brick by beautiful clean brick, to retain a heat that is now killing the people inside the house.

The bricks are not stupid. The man who laid them is not stupid. They are simply built for a country that is leaving, and the most powerful person on the continent has decided that the correct response to the world changing is to insist, with all the authority of his office, that it is not.

Next: where the insistence came from — the long, patient, well-funded history of teaching a civilization to doubt what its own scientists had already proven.
