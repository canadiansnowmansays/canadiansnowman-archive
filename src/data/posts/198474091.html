INTRODUCTION: Why Sue Other People When You Can Sue Yourself?

For decades, I’ve been telling people: nobody knows lawsuits like me. I’ve been sued thousands of times. I’ve sued hundreds of people. I’ve settled, I’ve stalled, I’ve appealed, I’ve outlasted. I wrote the book on litigation — well, someone wrote it for me, but the concept was mine, which in publishing is basically the same thing.

But it wasn’t until my second term as president that I discovered the ultimate legal innovation.

Why sue other people — with their lawyers, their evidence, their irritating habit of fighting back — when you can sue yourself?

Think about it. You control both sides. You pick the defendant. You pick the lawyer defending the defendant. You pick the lawyer prosecuting the case. You decide when to settle. You decide the terms. And if some judge starts asking uncomfortable questions — like whether the case is real — you simply withdraw the suit and take the money through a different door.

I call it the Self-Deal.™

And today, for the first time, I’m sharing the method with you.

CHAPTER 1: Identify Something That Made You Angry

The IRS headquarters, where my own government had the audacity to audit the greatest president in history. Naturally, I sued them. Tremendously

Every great lawsuit begins with a grievance. Mine began when the IRS — my IRS, technically, since I run the government that runs the IRS — allowed a former contractor to leak my tax returns to the New York Times and ProPublica.

Was I angry? Tremendously. Was the leak a genuine violation of my privacy? Probably. Could I have addressed it through normal channels — inspector general investigations, administrative reforms, the firing of responsible officials? Of course.

But normal channels don’t generate $10 billion headlines.

KEY PRINCIPLE: The size of the lawsuit should bear no relationship to the size of the harm. The number is not a legal claim. It is a press release.

CHAPTER 2: Sue Your Own Government

This is where most people get confused. “But you are the government,” they say. “You run the IRS. You appointed the Treasury Secretary. You control the Justice Department. How can you sue yourself?”

The answer is simple: very easily, if nobody stops you.

File the suit in a federal court. Name the IRS as the defendant. Demand $10 billion — a number so large that it functions as its own anaesthetic. People hear “$10 billion” and their brains short-circuit. They cannot process it. They move on. They assume someone is handling it.

Nobody is handling it.

KEY PRINCIPLE: In a normal lawsuit, the plaintiff and defendant have opposing interests. In the Self-Deal,™ they have the same interest: yours. This is not a bug. It is the entire product.

CHAPTER 3: Install Your Personal Lawyer as Attorney General

This step is critical. The Justice Department represents the government in lawsuits. If you are suing the government, the Justice Department is technically your opponent. This is inconvenient.

Solution: make sure your personal defense lawyer — the man who represented you in the classified documents case, the man who defended you against the January 6th prosecution — is running the Justice Department.

Todd will handle the details. Todd always handles the details.

Some people will call this a conflict of interest. These people do not understand that when you are the president, the phrase “conflict of interest” is just a description of your calendar.

KEY PRINCIPLE: The best opponent in a lawsuit is your own employee.

CHAPTER 4: When the Judge Asks Questions, Leave

A typical federal courtroom. Beautiful, isn’t it? Until some judge starts asking whether the plaintiff and defendant are the exact same person. Very rude

There is one risk in the Self-Deal:™ an independent judiciary.

In my case, Judge Kathleen M. Williams — who, I should note, was appointed by Obama, which tells you everything — ordered both sides to explain whether an actual legal dispute existed, or whether the president was suing himself.

This is what we in the Self-Deal™ business call an “uncomfortable question.”

The deadline was May 20. I withdrew the suit on May 18. Judge Williams acknowledged she had been “stripped of jurisdiction” and closed the case by end of day.

The judge who was about to expose the arrangement was cut out of the process entirely.

KEY PRINCIPLE: You do not need to win the lawsuit. You need to win around the lawsuit. The suit is the distraction. The settlement is the product.

CHAPTER 5: Create the Fund

Here is where the Self-Deal™ achieves its full elegance.

You have withdrawn the lawsuit. The judge is out of the picture. Now you need to get paid. But you cannot pay yourself directly — even I know that looks bad.

Instead, you have your consigliere — excuse me, your acting attorney general — create a $1.776 billion fund. The amount is symbolic: 1776, the year of American independence, because nothing says “liberty” like a slush fund administered by the president’s former criminal defense lawyer.

The fund has:

  • No eligibility criteria

  • No evidentiary standards

  • No compensation caps

  • No court oversight

  • No congressional approval

  • A five-member commission appointed by your lawyer, whose members you can fire at will

The money comes from the Judgment Fund — a perpetual appropriation that requires no vote from Congress. It is, essentially, a chequebook that never needs a co-signer.

KEY PRINCIPLE: The fund is not for you. The fund is for your friends. You take care of your friends, and your friends take care of you. This is not corruption. This is networking.

The beautiful organizational chart of the U.S. Treasury. Notice the little box called the Judgment Fund — basically an unlimited credit card with no congressional co-signer. I love that box

CHAPTER 6: The Bonus Round

Most Self-Deal™ practitioners would stop at the fund. $1.776 billion is, after all, more than the GDP of several small nations.

I am not most practitioners.

On the day after the fund was announced, my acting attorney general quietly posted a one-page document on the Justice Department’s website — a supplemental agreement barring the IRS from pursuing tax claims against me, my family, or my businesses.

Not the fund. A separate gift. An addendum. A little something extra, tucked into the settlement like a hotel mint on a pillow.

The IRS is required by its own procedures to audit the president annually. Federal law prohibits the president from ordering audits to stop. But the law contains a carve-out for the attorney general.

My attorney general. My former defense lawyer. The man I installed at the Justice Department specifically so that moments like this would go smoothly.

A loss in the IRS audit could have cost me over $100 million. Now there will be no audit. The one-page document took care of it. Quietly. On a Monday. While everyone was focused on the $1.776 billion.

KEY PRINCIPLE: The headline is the distraction. The footnote is where the money is.

CHAPTER 7: Repeat

The Self-Deal™ is not a one-time strategy. It is a lifestyle.

Sue yourself for $10 billion. Withdraw. Create a fund. Block the audit. Move on. Find the next grievance. File the next suit. Install the next lawyer. Create the next fund.

The system has no mechanism to stop you because the system was not designed for a president who would sue his own government, settle with himself, pay his allies, and block his own audit — all in the same week, all through the same lawyer, all with money that requires no congressional vote.

They built the guardrails for a country that expected its presidents to have shame.

I don’t have shame. I have a method.
