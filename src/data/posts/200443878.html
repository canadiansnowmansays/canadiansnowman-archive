There is a legal concept called quid pro quo — something for something. A transaction in which each party gives and each party receives. It does not require explicit agreement, written terms, or even a handshake. Sometimes the terms are understood by all parties without being spoken. The lawyers call it implied.

What happened between Paramount Global and the Trump administration in the summer of 2025 has a quid pro quo shape to it, even if every party involved would dispute that characterisation. Paramount needed regulatory approval to complete its $8.4 billion merger with Skydance Media. That approval required the sign-off of the Federal Communications Commission, whose chairman, Brendan Carr, had been appointed by Donald Trump and who had made clear that the 60 Minutes interview with Kamala Harris would factor into the commission’s review. And so Paramount settled.

The lawsuit was, by any objective journalistic standard, meritless. CBS had aired two versions of Harris’s answer to a question about the Middle East — one in a preview clip on Face the Nation, one in the full 60 Minutes broadcast — a practice as routine in television news as ellipsis marks in print. Trump had sued for $20 billion. A Democratic FCC commissioner called the case “entirely meritless.” Multiple legal experts said CBS would prevail. The network’s own news president, Wendy McMahon, opposed a settlement. So did the executive producer of 60 Minutes, Bill Owens, who had said explicitly that he would not apologise to the President for journalism that required no apology.

Paramount settled anyway, for $16 million — without an apology, they were careful to note — with the money directed to Trump’s future presidential library. The FCC promptly approved the merger. Former FCC chairman Thomas Wheeler, writing in a Harvard commentary, said the time had come to “unfurl the Mission Accomplished banner.”

Scott Pelley, in his first interview after being fired, did not equivocate about what had happened. “The very last thing that the previous ownership did,” he said, “was pay a multi-million dollar bribe to the president to settle this frivolous, ridiculous lawsuit. And very shortly after that, somehow the Trump administration approved the sale.” He had initially welcomed the Ellison acquisition — had seen it as a rescue of a company in financial trouble. “Paying the bribe broke our hearts,” he said. “No lawyer thought that was necessary. But they did it to get the sale through.”

You may call it what you like. Colbert called it, on air, “a big fat bribe” — carefully noting that “big fat bribe” was “the technical name in legal circles.” He was told, three days later, that his show was being cancelled.

The Departures Begin

The settlement was the visible wound. But the institutional haemorrhage had been building since David Ellison closed his $8.4 billion acquisition of Paramount in August 2024 and installed Bari Weiss as editor-in-chief of CBS News two months later.

The announcement of Weiss’s appointment landed inside CBS News with what was described variously as dread, disbelief, and fury. Weiss is a talented writer and a genuinely influential figure in American media. She is also an opinion journalist who had spent the years since leaving the New York Times building a platform explicitly critical of the legacy media institutions she was now being handed. She had no experience in broadcast television. She would report not through the normal chain of editorial command but directly to Ellison himself — a structural arrangement that told the CBS newsroom everything it needed to know about where power now resided.

Bari Weiss, appointed editor-in-chief of CBS News in late 2024. Her arrival marked the beginning of sweeping changes, including the departure of longtime 60 Minutes leaders and correspondents.

The cruelty of it was the contrast with Ellison’s arrival. On the morning the merger closed, his first stop — as far as anyone could tell — was 60 Minutes. He walked through the offices, came into Pelley’s office, extended his hand and said, “I’m proud to meet you, sir.” He walked out into the newsroom and gave what Pelley described as a very nice speech about independence, about how important 60 Minutes was to the corporation. “We were walking on air,” Pelley said. “We liked him. He had the financial backing to back up his words. We thought a bright new era was coming.”

Then came Bari Weiss.

The first casualty was Bill Owens. He had spent thirty-seven years at CBS News, twenty-four of them at 60 Minutes, and had served as the programme’s executive producer since 2019. In April 2025, as Paramount’s settlement negotiations with Trump proceeded over his objections, he wrote a memo to his staff that was a model of professional dignity in extremis.

Bill Owens, longtime executive producer of 60 Minutes, who resigned in April 2025 rather than accept editorial constraints. “The show is too important to the country to die with me,” he wrote.Suggested insertion: In the “The Departures Begin” section, right after describing Owens’s memo.

“Over the past months, it has become clear that I would not be allowed to run the show as I have always run it,” he wrote, “to make independent decisions based on what was right for 60 Minutes, right for the audience. So, having defended this show — and what we stand for — from every angle, over time with everything I could, I am stepping aside so the show can move forward.”

He had been at CBS News for thirty-seven years. The show, he wrote, was too important to the country to die with him. It had to continue.

He did not add: just not as what it was. He did not need to.

Wendy McMahon, the president and CEO of CBS News and Stations, was more explicit. She had backed Owens throughout the Trump lawsuit, had called his leadership a “privilege” and had opposed the settlement. She lasted four more weeks after his resignation before writing her own departure memo: “It’s become clear that the company and I do not agree on the path forward. It’s time for me to move on.”

Two of the most senior people in the institution — the ones whose job was specifically to defend its editorial independence — had been replaced by the market.

The 60 Minutes logo — for decades the gold standard of American broadcast journalism. Under new ownership, the program lost its executive producer, senior correspondents, and much of its institutional autonomy in a matter of months.

The 60 Minutes Purge

Anderson Cooper had joined 60 Minutes in 2006. He spent twenty years contributing to the programme while anchoring CNN. In May 2026, he filmed his final 60 Minutes segment — not fired, technically, but unwilling to continue under conditions he described as including “abnormal” editorial interference. His farewell was characteristically understated: “Things can always evolve and change, and that’s awesome,” he said, “but I hope the core of what 60 Minutes is always remains.”

He did not sound as though he believed it would.

Within days of Cooper’s departure, Bari Weiss moved. Pelley, in his first interview after being fired, called it “Black Thursday.” The entire senior staff of 60 Minutes — wiped out in a single day, the night after they had attended the Emmy Awards together and won two of them.

Tanya Simon was the first to go. She had spent thirty years at 60 Minutes, the last year as its executive producer — the first woman ever to hold that position. She had led the programme to a nine percent audience increase in her debut season, and a hundred and ninety percent growth in its online presence. Two and a half billion views. Pelley called it “a triumphal year.” What it earned Simon was a summons to what she was told would be a planning meeting for the next season. She arrived without a lawyer, without a witness. Tom Cibrowski, the CBS News president, opened the meeting with: “The nature of this meeting has changed.” She was told she was fired and had to clear her office by five o’clock.

Her father was Bob Simon — the CBS foreign correspondent who had covered every war everywhere in the world for four decades, who had taught a young Scott Pelley how to be a war correspondent while they stood on a hotel roof in Kuwait watching missiles come in during the Gulf War. Tanya Simon had spent her entire childhood waiting for the phone call that her father was dead. She had devoted thirty years of her own career to the same institution.

Get out by five o’clock.

Weiss then fired Draggan Mihailovich, the executive editor, and Matthew Polevoy, a senior producer. And she fired correspondents Sharyn Alfonsi and Cecilia Vega — a third of the correspondent corps, gone without stated reason or explanation.

Alfonsi had been with CBS for twenty years, more than a decade of them at 60 Minutes. She had been in open conflict with Weiss since late 2025, when Weiss pulled a 60 Minutes segment she had produced — a report on conditions inside the Salvadoran prison system, where the Trump administration was deporting migrants. The segment, Alfonsi believed, had been killed for political reasons. It eventually aired, but the damage to the relationship was permanent.

“In recent months,” Alfonsi wrote in her exit statement, “my producing teams and I have experienced efforts to insert political bias into our stories.”

Cecilia Vega, who had been 60 Minutes‘ first Latina correspondent, said something similar: “I was fired today,” she wrote. “In recent months, my producing teams and I have experienced efforts to insert political bias into our stories.”

Both women were accusing the leadership of CBS News of something more serious than editorial disagreement. They were accusing it of the inversion of the institution’s founding purpose — of using the machinery of journalism to serve political ends rather than to resist them.

This is what Murrow was hired to prevent. This is what Cronkite protected, at some personal cost, for twenty years. This is what Hewitt’s editorial culture at 60 Minutes had been designed, specifically, to make difficult.

Scott Pelley’s Last Stand

Pelley had cancelled a long-planned hiking trip in the Canadian Rockies with his wife to be at Nick Bilton’s first staff meeting. He felt it was an existential moment for 60 Minutes and that someone senior needed to be in the room.

What he found when he arrived told him everything he needed to know about who was now running the institution. Bilton walked to the front of a room containing fifty heartbroken people — colleagues who had worked together for ten, twenty, thirty years, who had travelled together, dined together, gone into literal combat together — and pulled out his phone. He read his opening statement off the screen. Pelley said you could hear the groan in the room. Someone had also arranged a spread of bagels, as though pastry might soften the blow of watching your family be dismembered.

Before the meeting, Bilton had sent a staff email introducing himself that Pelley described as “insulting to the history of 60 Minutes.” It noted, helpfully, that gasoline no longer cost thirty-two cents a gallon, and suggested the staff had been “frozen in amber” since 1968 — apparently unaware that 60 Minutes had launched its first digital show in 2010, that correspondents were shooting TikTok verticals on every assignment, or that the programme had logged two and a half billion online views the previous season. The email also mentioned, as an aside, Bilton’s excitement about introducing “a new crop of correspondents.” Pelley read that and thought: they’re going to fire all of us eventually. He put it in writing for everyone to see.

Pelley stood up and said what he said. He told Bilton that Weiss was murdering 60 Minutes, that his qualifications were slender, that he would never be welcome there. The meeting ended in thunderous applause for Pelley. It was a closed, internal staff meeting — none of it was meant to be public.

The next day, Pelley was called to a meeting with Weiss and Cibrowski. He went in expecting a conversation. Before he could take his seat, Cibrowski told him it was “a firing offense.” He then accused Pelley of physically abusing Nick Bilton — a lie, Pelley said flatly, that Cibrowski retracted when pressed. Pelley turned to Weiss and asked her, directly, why the firings had been necessary. Why had Tanya Simon, after a triumphal year, been removed? Why had Alfonsi and Vega been dismissed?

“I’m not answering that question,” Weiss said.

He asked again, a different way. “I’m not answering that question.” Again. “I’m not answering that question.”

The meeting lasted approximately ten minutes. As Pelley was leaving, Cibrowski told him he would have their answer shortly. Pelley went back to his office. His team was waiting. He told them he thought he had just been fired, but that no one had confirmed it. He looked out through the plate glass window at the newsroom beyond and noticed that staff had gathered outside. An hour passed. Two hours. Three. Four.

“This is a vigil,” he realised.

He eventually went out and told them to go home to their families. Then he left the building himself. The termination email arrived shortly after.

The official letter cited Pelley’s “performative display of hostility” and his “remarkable incivility” at the Bilton meeting. It did not address the substance of anything he had said.

What Pelley had said — to Bilton, in the subsequent meeting with Weiss, and in his first interview after the firing — was that Weiss had put “a thumb on the scale for the president’s version of events.” He had a specific example. In February, his team had produced a 60 Minutes story about the ICE crackdowns in Minneapolis, including the killing of Renée Good. The team had gone out of its way to represent the protesters’ own aggression fairly — finding images of confrontations, showing Alex Prey kicking a police car taillight. The story was, in Pelley’s assessment, balanced. It was approved by the senior editors. Four hours after the noon deadline — with the broadcast nineteen minutes from potentially not making air on the night of the Grammys — Weiss sent an email through to Tanya Simon with two requests: could the protesters be made to look more violent, and could the script describe Renée Good as driving toward the officer who shot her.

The video, Pelley said, showed the wheels of Good’s car turned as far as possible away from the officer. He reviewed it frame by frame, in slow motion, and refused to make the changes. The broadcast nearly didn’t get on the air. Nobody said anything to him the next day.

“There was a thumb on the scale for the president’s version of events,” Pelley told the interviewer. “That was a level of political influence I had never seen in thirty-seven years at CBS News.”

When told that Trump had gone on a podcast and called him a “stiff” and “part of a gang of stupid, crooked people that don’t care about your country,” Pelley paused.

“Stupid. I can take that. Stiff. Yeah, probably.” Then: “I’ve been in combat in Afghanistan and Iraq, Kuwait, been shot at, spent nights in foxholes filling up with water in the desert. I’m not aware that the president of the United States has ever done any of those things for his country. Please correct me if I’m wrong.”

He said the same thing he had said the night he was fired, the thing that Alfonsi and Vega had said, the thing that Owens and McMahon had said in different language when they resigned: the institution was being used for something it was never built for.

“There is no democracy without journalism,” Pelley said. “It can’t be done. And that is why I am a journalist.”

He was asked what he hoped his departure would accomplish. He said he hoped Paramount’s leadership would look at what was happening — the near-missed broadcasts, the respected journalists alleging a thumb on the scale for one political party, the gutting of the most successful programme in the history of American television — and ask themselves why they were making these changes.

“We need adult supervision,” he said, “and at the moment we don’t have it.”

He paused.

“Right now, CBS News, in my view, is on fire.”

Stephen Colbert on the set of The Late Show. His program was cancelled shortly after he publicly called the Paramount-Trump settlement “a big fat bribe.” His final weeks featured pointed satire about the network’s direction.

The Signal at the End

Ten days before Stephen Colbert’s final broadcast, CBS News Radio went off the air.

The service had been broadcasting since 1927 — ninety-nine years. It had carried Edward R. Murrow’s voice from London during the Blitz, had told Americans about Pearl Harbor, had broadcast the news of Kennedy’s assassination, had covered September 11. The World News Roundup, the flagship programme, had been running continuously since 1938. Its final broadcast signed off, fittingly, with the sound of Murrow’s own voice: Good night, and good luck.

All twenty-six union positions were eliminated. Seven hundred affiliated stations lost their CBS News feed. Weiss and Cibrowski said it was an economic necessity.

It may have been. Media economics are genuinely brutal, and CBS News Radio had been fighting a losing battle against a fragmented digital landscape for years. But it is worth noting the sequence: the settlement that compromised CBS’s editorial independence was reached in July 2025. Weiss was installed in October. Owens resigned in April 2026. McMahon resigned in May. Cooper departed in May. Alfonsi, Vega, Simon, Mihailovich, and Polevoy were all fired in the last two weeks of May. Colbert’s final broadcast aired on May 21. CBS News Radio signed off on May 22. Pelley was fired on June 2.

In the span of thirteen months, CBS News lost its executive producer, its news president, its flagship late-night host, its ninety-nine-year-old radio service, five senior 60 Minutes journalists and producers, and its most decorated active correspondent — the man who had asked himself every day what Walter Cronkite would do.

It is possible to argue that each of these decisions was individually defensible — that media economics are hard, that editorial cultures require renewal, that new owners have the right to reshape their acquisitions. You can make that case. People are making it.

Walter Cronkite in the CBS newsroom — the face of the Tiffany Network’s most trusted era. The institution that once defined broadcast journalism was systematically dismantled in 2025–2026.

But Murrow would not have found it convincing. Cronkite would have said something on air about it. Hewitt would have called it an attack on the idea that serious journalism and popular audiences were compatible. Letterman would have dropped something off the roof of the Ed Sullivan Theater in protest — which, as it happens, is exactly what Colbert did, in his final week, in a bit he called “The Wanton Destruction of CBS Property.”

Colbert knew, as Pelley knew, as Owens knew, as McMahon knew, what they were watching. They had all grown up in an institution that had defined American broadcast journalism for seventy years. They had all inherited something that had been built at great cost and maintained with something resembling genuine civic purpose.

They watched it be dismantled, quickly and efficiently, by people who understood its commercial value and were indifferent to everything else it represented.

The Tiffany Network. It was what the industry called CBS because of what the institution stood for. Not the cost, not the ratings, not the merger valuation.

The standard.

Which raises a question worth sitting with: if the American public mobilised within days to reverse Jimmy Kimmel’s five-day suspension — cancelling 1.7 million streaming subscriptions, filling streets outside Disney’s headquarters — why has the systematic demolition of CBS News produced almost no equivalent response? The Kimmel case and the CBS case share an antagonist, the same FCC, the same political pressure. But the public reactions could not have been more different.
