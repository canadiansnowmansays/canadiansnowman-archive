An obituary usually names the surviving relatives. Rachel Maddow’s does not.

In The Atlantic on November 9, 2026, in an excerpt from her book Department of Fate, published the following day, Ms. Maddow pronounces the United States Department of Justice “gone,” or, as she puts it, “missing, presumed dead.” The department is “mostly irrelevant,” she writes. Its threats have been “(mostly) just menacing hot air.” The excerpt names Pam Bondi, Trump’s first attorney general of the second term. It mocks a former acting U.S. attorney for his self-awarded nickname. It does not mention Todd Blanche at all.

This is awkward, because Mr. Blanche has not been informed. The Senate confirmed him by a vote of 50 to 49 on August 8, according to the Associated Press, and he took the oath two days later at the White House. He has been coming in to work. Before anyone closes this particular file, it seems only polite to check what the deceased has been up to since.

Ms. Maddow is right about the incompetence, and this series has no quarrel with her contempt. Where she goes wrong is in the word “irrelevant.” The Justice Department has not vanished. It has changed sides, and a department that has changed sides can lose nearly every case and still do what it is now being asked to do.

I. What the Coroner Got Right

Start with the evidence for death, because there is plenty of it.

On October 27, 2026, a Washington grand jury refused to indict Cassidy Hutchinson over her January 6 testimony, as The Washington Post reported. Another grand jury in the same city had refused in February to indict six Democratic lawmakers. Every one of the more than two dozen courts to rule on the department’s demand for state voter rolls has rejected it, as Quinta Jurecic noted in The Atlantic on November 2.

Then there is the “grand conspiracy” inquiry, which David A. Graham dissected in The Atlantic on September 29. The department fired the FBI agents who had investigated Mr. Trump, then discovered that it needed those same agents to testify that the investigation was corrupt. They replied, through counsel, that they would be happy to describe “a properly predicated allegation.” The career prosecutor leading the team was removed. Her replacement, Joseph diGenova, a man not generally suspected of insufficient loyalty, quit and told the New York Post: “If you want indictments where there’s no evidence, you have an ethical problem.” Mr. Graham’s closing question stays with you: is the probe investigating a grand conspiracy, or is it evidence of one?

So yes, the thing is comically inept. Ms. Maddow says so herself, and then supplies the best image anyone has found for it. The department, she writes, “is dangerous now in the way a defective firework or a broken gun is. It can still kill, just not in the manner or at the time you might intend.”

That sentence is the whole of the case against her own headline. Nobody calls a broken gun irrelevant while it is pointed at them.

II. The Deceased’s Calendar

Consider how the corpse spent the final weeks before the midterms, using Ms. Jurecic’s inventory.

It sued California and Alaska over how they run their elections. It charged more than 100 people with voter fraud; a Washington Post analysis she cites counted 180 such cases since January 2025, 130 of them filed since September 2026. It arrested 28 people in California in mid-October and posted photographs of handcuffed defendants in North Carolina. It sent grand jury subpoenas to California election officials and letters warning election officials across the country of possible criminal liability.

Over the same weeks, federal agents searched the home of Representative Ilhan Omar on October 12. A Democratic state senator in Virginia was indicted on October 22. Two former FBI officials were charged the next day, in a case where the agent who signed off acknowledged in court that he had been pressed by agency leadership and that some colleagues considered it a “witch hunt.” James Comey was charged for a third time. That is not a department at rest.

Most of it may never survive contact with a judge. Ms. Jurecic’s point is that surviving was not the assignment. Analysts at the University of Washington described “a larger strategy of creating an attention-grabbing spectacle to foster a false perception of widespread fraud.” A lost lawsuit still generates a headline. An indictment later thrown out has still cost its target a fortune in legal fees, and months of a life.

Asked by CNN’s Dana Bash whether all this was laying the groundwork for overturning an election, Mr. Blanche replied, “That is crazy.” In a podcast interview with the New York Post columnist Miranda Devine, he dismissed what he called the “lie” of Justice Department independence and said: “I work for President Trump.” When Kristen Welker of NBC asked him on August 16 to pledge that the department would always act independently of the White House, he answered, as Axios reported: “No, I’m not going to pledge that. And no attorney general should ever pledge that.”

An institution cannot be gone when its head keeps telling you precisely where it went.

III. A Thousand Pairs of Eyes

For more than 60 years, Justice Department staff have gone to polling places to see that Americans could vote free of discrimination or intimidation. In 2022 the department sent 289 people, according to CBS News. For November 3, 2026, Harmeet Dhillon’s Civil Rights Division promised 1,000, in every state.

Yvonne Wingett Sanchez, Madeleine Carlisle and Marie-Rose Sheinerman went through the programme for The Atlantic on November 1. For the first time, criminal prosecutors were recruited as monitors. “We never wanted to do anything that might scare people,” a former Civil Rights Division official said of why that had never been done. The department’s spokeswoman said that monitors would not identify themselves as prosecutors, which is not obviously reassuring. Staff from the Bureau of Prisons, the DEA and the ATF were also included. Among those sent to the primaries were a lawyer who had represented the Trump campaign’s attempt to overturn Wisconsin’s 2020 result, and a man who had pleaded guilty in 2016 to hacking two election websites in Florida. Maricopa County’s Republican-led county attorney’s office felt moved to ask, in writing, that no one with a felony conviction be sent to watch its voters.

The department deserves its answer here. The spokeswoman, Kiersten Pels, pointed out that no one could name a single voter intimidated by a monitor. Nearly all the primary visits were professional, by the reporters’ own account, and experienced career lawyers helped coordinate the effort.

As it turned out, the feared disaster did not arrive. Election Day was largely smooth, and many counties saw no monitors at all, according to Governing. The exceptions were modest. In Dearborn and Flint, monitors questioned poll workers about their training, and the department’s own coordinator agreed that this was inconsistent with what they had been taught, the Michigan Advance reported. Monitors in Mecklenburg County, North Carolina, allegedly asked to see voters’ phones.

So score that for the coroner? Only partly. The fuller cost was paid earlier, and in a different currency. In Pima County, the elections director felt obliged to remind federal monitors that they could not bring a gun into a vote centre. In Boston, administrators wrote procedures for inventorying ballots in case federal agents seized them. In Wisconsin, clerks fielded hundreds of emails asking what the plan was if federal law enforcement appeared. A programme built in the civil-rights era to reassure frightened voters had become the thing that election workers were preparing for. A landslide made it moot this time. Nobody should count on a landslide every time.

And in Alaska, the matter is not closed. The department is appealing the dismissal described in Part XI. About 20 target or preservation letters have gone out to Democrats, including the lawyer Marc Elias, with roughly 100 subpoenas, and Mr. Trump said on Election Day that Mr. Elias “should go to jail,” according to The New York Times.

IV. The Division of Labour

Ms. Maddow’s strongest argument is that the real menace has moved. The people “shooting protesters,” breaking down doors and grabbing people in courthouse hallways are mostly agents of Homeland Security, she writes, an “amorphous, masked, pro-Trump secret police force with as little connection to the Article III legal system as they can get away with.”

Every word of that is fair, and readers of this series have seen the names: Renee Good and Alex Pretti in Minneapolis, Lorenzo Salgado Araujo in Houston, Joan Sebastian Guerrero in Biddeford, Maine. But ask how an armed agency gets to have so little connection to the legal system. It does not manage this alone. Someone has to decline to open the civil-rights file. Someone has to hold the evidence while state investigators go to court to get it. Someone has to argue that federal officers are immune from a state’s murder statute. That someone has an office on Pennsylvania Avenue.

The masked men are the visible half of the arrangement. The Justice Department is the half that does not turn up. Ms. Maddow’s own publicity for the book makes the point better than I can. “What DOJ chooses to pursue — and what it lets go,” she said, according to the Associated Press, can determine the boundaries of Americans’ rights. An attorney general who lets go of Minneapolis has not become irrelevant to Minneapolis. In her excerpt she writes that with the department fully on a case, “almost anything is possible,” and that without it, “almost nothing is.” That is an excellent description of what an agent with a gun gets to count on.

V. Akerman’s Department, Doar’s Division

Ms. Maddow’s history supplies the proper measure. The department was created in 1870 to make the Reconstruction Amendments real. Amos T. Akerman, a Georgian who had served the Confederacy, went after the Ku Klux Klan in South Carolina with a ferocity that cost him friends, and he held the job for about a year and a half before Grant asked for his resignation. John Doar, of the Civil Rights Division, stood between a furious crowd and the police in Jackson, Mississippi, after the funeral of Medgar Evers in 1963, and later prosecuted the men behind the Neshoba County murders.

Now look at Doar’s division. More than 70 per cent of its career staff have taken early retirement or resigned, CBS reported. What remains ran the thousand monitors. It has sued dozens of state election officials for their voter rolls. And it was the Civil Rights Division, in a break from precedent reported by Axios, that took the false-statements case against Ms. Hutchinson to the grand jury. The office that once protected witnesses from Mississippi sheriffs spent the autumn of 2026 trying to indict a witness against the president.

Ms. Maddow calls this a department “emptied out and deconsecrated.” It is an accurate phrase for the building. It is less accurate for the machinery, which was emptied of the people who would say no and left running.

She is careful to say that convictions have mostly failed “with a few notable exceptions.” John Bolton’s guilty plea, as this newsletter’s Enemies List has argued, was a real case that predated the grievance, and the department’s defenders are entitled to it. Others went the other way: on May 22, a federal court dismissed the charges against Kilmar Abrego Garcia as vindictive, as Alliance for Justice records. But a conviction was never the only product. Jack Smith, quoted in the excerpt, saw that: “This attack on public servants — it’s not a byproduct of the attack on the rule of law. It is a central component of it.” Thousands of career lawyers have been pushed out, and Ms. Maddow’s most hopeful passage records that many now defend the very people their old department pursues. That is admirable. It is also a sign of which side of the courtroom the Justice Department’s conscience now has to sit on.

VI. The Reading of the Will

Ms. Maddow ends with a command. “When Trump’s reign ends, the first order of business in the country will be to found a new Department of Justice. And properly this time. For good.”

I would like to believe it, and her opening paragraph explains why it is possible. The department, she notes, “is not a creature of the U.S. Constitution; it is a creature of Congress.” What Congress created, Congress can repair, and Democrats won control of both chambers on November 3. But the wall between the White House and Main Justice that Mr. Blanche calls a “lie” was, for half a century, mostly custom. It held for as long as attorneys general chose to observe it, and it came down the moment one chose not to. A new department built on the same honour system would last until the next president who had a personal lawyer he was fond of.

So the first question for any refounding is not who should run it. It is what, in writing, would have stopped Todd Blanche. Until someone can answer that, Ms. Maddow’s obituary is premature in the most uncomfortable way. The patient is not dead. The patient is under new management, and the next owner inherits the keys.

A Note From the Canadian Side of the Border

Canada settled a piece of this question in statute. Under the Director of Public Prosecutions Act of 2006, federal prosecutions are run by an independent director. The Attorney General of Canada can still issue instructions in a specific case, but they must be in writing and published in the Canada Gazette, where everyone can read them.

We are in no position to be smug. In 2019, the SNC-Lavalin affair showed how much pressure a prime minister’s office could bring on an attorney general without writing anything down, and the Ethics Commissioner found that Justin Trudeau had broken the conflict-of-interest law. But note how that story went. The attorney general refused, said so in public, and the scandal was that anyone had asked. In Washington the attorney general has said on a podcast that the asking is the job.

The department was created so that the promise of equal protection would reach real people on real streets. It is not gone. It is simply taking calls from someone else.
