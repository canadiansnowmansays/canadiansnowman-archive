The Docket, Closed

The Loyalty Trials of May 2026 are over.

In Indiana on May 5, eight Republican state senators faced the electoral consequences of a December vote that defied a president. Six of them fell. In Louisiana on May 16, a sitting senator discovered that five years of subsequent loyalty was not enough to survive a single act of conscience. He finished third. In Kentucky and Georgia on May 19, a libertarian congressman and a meticulously honest secretary of state found out whether their respective Republican electorates valued independence and institutional integrity. They did not. Both were defeated.

The machine spent somewhere north of $50 million to conduct these trials. The president personally recruited at least one of the challengers. His allies ran the super PACs, cut the ads, managed the campaigns, and produced AI-generated deepfake videos. The full apparatus of the Trump political operation was deployed against members of his own party.

Now we have the verdicts. Let’s read them carefully, because the numbers tell one story and the implications tell another.

Indiana: The Machine Worked

Incumbents who survived: 2 of 8. Incumbents defeated: 6 of 8.

Nine million dollars from Virginia-based consultants and national super PACs descended on Indiana state Senate races — races that normally attract five-figure ad buys, not seven-figure ones. The money was a message, and the message was received. Six of the eight Republican incumbents who had voted against Trump’s redistricting demands in December were defeated in their May 5 primaries.

The machine reached down to the lowest level of contested office — state senators, officials with the deepest local roots and the least visibility to nationalized resentment — and it still worked. That was the warning shot heard in Louisville and Baton Rouge and Atlanta. If $9 million could override a decade of constituent service in an Indiana state Senate district, what could $33 million do to a congressman or a sitting U.S. senator?

One result merits its own line. Tyler Goode survived clearly, 54-36 — a margin wide enough to call comfortable. And Spencer Deery, the young state senator who had called the December vote “conservatism,” survived by a coin flip: 50-50, a margin so narrow that it will be weeks before it is certified. Deery still has breath. He used it to win by a handful of votes. The six who lost used their breath too. In the end, $9 million was louder.

The Indiana result established the machine’s jurisdiction. It can reach this far down, and it can win.

Louisiana: One Vote, Five Years, Done

Cassidy: 24.8% | Letlow: 44.8% | Fleming: 28.3%

Bill Cassidy finished third. He was eliminated outright — he will not appear on the June 27 runoff ballot. More than 73 percent of Republican primary voters chose someone other than their sitting senator. The anti-Cassidy vote did not need to consolidate around a single challenger; there was enough of it for two. Fleming, who did not even have Trump’s endorsement, still beat the incumbent by nearly four points.

A senator with a decade of genuine legislative achievement for his state. A conservative record on nearly every issue. Billions in healthcare funding, disaster relief, and infrastructure delivered to Louisiana. Only one deviation in eleven years of service — one vote, in one trial, on one afternoon. Insufficient.

On Fox News, as the numbers came in, Cassidy offered the clinical language he had used for five years: “You may go back and flagellate yourself over decisions in the past. I do not. I move on.” But the concession speech was different. The mask dropped: “Our country is not about one individual. If someone doesn’t understand that and attempts to control others through using the levers of power, they’re about serving themselves.” It was the diagnosis he had declined to make for five years. He made it on the way out the door.

Meanwhile, the man Cassidy had confirmed as health secretary — the olive branch that was supposed to prove his loyalty — had pledged one million dollars through MAHA Action to end him. Kennedy’s advisory panel had voted to remove the recommendation for the hepatitis B vaccine at birth, the vaccine that defined Cassidy’s medical career. Two infants died of whooping cough in Louisiana; Cassidy asked Kennedy to urge parents to vaccinate; Kennedy said nothing.

He confirmed the man. The man spent a million dollars to end him. The accommodation cycle is complete.

James Carville’s fourteen words: “Bill Cassidy sold his soul to the devil, and he didn’t get anything for it.”

Derek Babcock, the chair of the Louisiana Republican Party, provided the obituary for the accommodation strategy: Cassidy had worked hard to earn the party’s forgiveness. “I just can’t say that I’m seeing it.”

The forgiveness was never available. It was never going to be.

One vote. Five years. Done.

The June 27 runoff is Letlow versus Fleming — Trump’s endorsed candidate against Trump’s unofficial understudy. It is no longer a trial of loyalty versus independence. It is an argument within MAGA about which form of loyalty deserves the seat. Cassidy is not a party to it.

Kentucky: The Debt Clock Stopped

Massie: 45% | Gallrein: 55%

Thomas Massie lost by nine points — 55-45 — in the most expensive House primary in American history. Thirty-three million dollars in total ad spending. Nineteen million of it deployed against him, including $9 million from pro-Israel groups and a super PAC created by Trump’s own aides. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth campaigned for Gallrein in the final days. On the morning of the vote, Trump called Massie “the worst congressman the Republican Party’s ever had.”

Massie won Lewis County, where he grew up, by nearly two-thirds. He lost virtually everywhere else. His Kentucky, like Cassidy’s Louisiana, shrank to the place where people actually knew him. The same night, Andy Barr won the Senate primary to replace McConnell — Kentucky replacing both its independent federal voices with compliant allies in a single evening.

Weeks before the vote, Massie had framed the stakes plainly: “For my colleagues, this election is about, can we deviate occasionally from the plan and still survive our primaries.” The answer is in. The answer is no.

Georgia: The Ghost Is Laid to Rest

Jones: 37.9% | Jackson: 34.1% | Raffensperger: 14.5%

Brad Raffensperger — the man who told the President of the United States that the 11,780 votes he wanted did not exist — finished third with 14.5 percent. He outperformed his polling average by three and a half points. It changed nothing. Jones and Jackson advance to a June 16 runoff. Raffensperger is eliminated.

The Georgia verdict is the cleanest in the series. Raffensperger’s crime was committed on a recorded phone call, in full public view, five and a half years ago. He did his job. He certified the truth. He asked Georgia Republicans to reward the refusal. They gave him 14.5 percent and a runoff between two men competing to prove who would have found the votes.

The runoff itself carries its own lesson. Rick Jackson, a billionaire with no political experience who entered the race three months ago and spent $83 million of his own money, came within four points of Trump’s endorsed candidate. The loyalty brand has partially detached from the man. It can be purchased at scale.

Reading the Verdicts Together

The Loyalty Trials of May 2026 tested the machine against four different types of Republican resistance:

Indiana tested institutional resistance — the collective no of a legislative body. The machine won. Six of eight targeted incumbents fell.

Louisiana tested the durability of electoral punishment — whether a five-year-old vote could still end a career. The machine won. Cassidy finished third. There is no statute of limitations.

Kentucky tested individual resistance — whether one man’s credibility could outlast a super PAC. The machine won. The most expensive House primary in history produced a nine-point defeat.

Georgia tested integrity as a political asset — whether saying no to a president who asked you to lie was survivable. The machine won. Raffensperger got 14.5 percent. The truth was worth less than fifteen cents on the dollar.

Every test had the same answer. The machine won them all.

The Loyalty Trials established their jurisdiction. The message to every Republican officeholder in America is now calibrated with precision: the cost of deviation is quantifiable, the timeline for punishment is indefinite, and there is no local credibility sufficient to neutralise the machinery when it is properly funded and fully engaged. The investment schedule is now public: one million for a state senate seat, eight million for a batch of them, thirty-three million for a congressman, and an open chequebook for a senator or a governor. The Republican Party has become, in functional terms, a compliance operation.

The next Republican who considers saying no to this president has a complete set of data points. They are not encouraging.

Spencer Deery survived by a coin flip in Indiana. That is the entire list of defendants who made it through. One man, one state senate seat, a margin of a handful of votes. Everyone else — the senator, the congressman, the secretary of state, and four of Deery’s six colleagues — fell.

This is not a mixed verdict. This is a sweep.

The Escalation

One development from primary weekend deserves its own section, because it reveals where the loyalty test is heading next.

On the same day Cassidy was eliminated in Louisiana, Trump posted a warning to Lauren Boebert of Colorado: “Even though I long ago endorsed Boebert, if the right person came along, it would be my Honor to withdraw that Endorsement, and endorse a good and proper alternative.”

Boebert’s offence: she had appeared alongside Massie in Kentucky, supporting his primary campaign. She had not opposed Trump on any policy. She had not criticised the president. She had simply been photographed next to someone who had.

Boebert’s response — posting that she was still “MAGA” and was simply “standing with a friend” — captures the psychology perfectly. She is not defending Massie’s positions. She is not challenging the president’s authority to enforce loyalty. She is simply asking not to be punished for an act of personal friendship.

The escalation sequence is now visible:

In 2021, the test was whether you voted against Trump on impeachment. Censure, primary challenge, career over.

In 2025, the test was whether you voted against Trump’s redistricting demands. Eight million dollars in attack ads, five of seven incumbents defeated.

In 2025 and 2026, the test was whether you criticised Trump on Epstein, on Iran, on policy. Thirty-three million dollars in the most expensive House primary in American history, personal attacks on family, AI-generated deepfake ads seen 49 million times.

In May 2026, the test is whether you were seen in public with someone who criticised Trump. Endorsement threat.

Each step lowers the threshold. The final step — guilt by association — means there is no safe distance from which to observe dissent. Watching is participation. Proximity is complicity. The only safe position is active denunciation of the dissident.

This is how loyalty regimes work. The Loyalty Trials did not invent the mechanism. They calibrated it.

And then, on the morning of the Kentucky primary, the calibration was extended one step further. As reported in the New York Times Midterms Live Updates, Trump endorsed Ken Paxton, the Texas attorney general, in the Republican Senate runoff against John Cornyn — a four-term incumbent who had voted with the president 99 percent of the time. Cornyn had hired Trump’s own pollster. One of Trump’s 2024 campaign managers ran the pro-Cornyn super PAC. In the final stretch, Cornyn introduced legislation to rename a highway Interstate 47 in honour of the president — “1,800 miles of open road to forever be known as the Trump Interstate,” he wrote, with an American flag emoji.

It was not enough. Trump specifically cited “loyalty” in his endorsement of Paxton — the man who filed a lawsuit to invalidate swing state results in 2020, the man who has been indicted, impeached, and accused of adultery. Cornyn’s offence: he had been “not supportive of me when times were tough” and had been “very late in backing me” in 2024. The senator had expressed doubts about Trump’s viability during his third presidential run. That was sufficient grounds to endorse a scandal-plagued attorney general over a sitting senator with a 99 percent loyalty score.

Lisa Murkowski — one of the two surviving impeachment-conviction senators — said she was “supremely disappointed” and asked the question that no one in the party’s leadership wanted to answer: “How does that help strengthen the president’s hand when we lose a state like Texas?”

It doesn’t. But the Loyalty Trials are not about strengthening the president’s hand. They are about demonstrating that the hand cannot be questioned. A 99 percent voting record and a highway named after the president is not sufficient if, at any point, in any way, you hesitated. Cornyn hesitated in 2024. The hesitation was remembered. The highway was not.

The Paxton endorsement is not one of the four trials in this series. But it is their logical extension — and it landed on the same day as the Kentucky verdict, ensuring that every Republican in America absorbed both data points simultaneously: Massie’s result and Cornyn’s humiliation. The machine operates on multiple frequencies at once.

All of this is happening while the president’s approval rating has sunk to a second-term low of 37 percent in the latest New York Times/Siena poll. Sixty-four percent of voters disapprove of his handling of the economy. Sixty-nine percent disapprove of the war in Iran. The machine is purging dissent inside the party while the party’s standing with the general electorate collapses. The Loyalty Trials are producing a more compliant Republican Party. They are also producing a more vulnerable one.

The Defendant Who Still Matters Most

Thomas Massie has not been mentioned in this closing essay’s analysis yet. That is intentional.

His case is the one that carries the longest implications, because his primary was about something different from the others. Indiana was about redistricting. Louisiana was about a single vote five years ago. Georgia was about 2020. Massie’s trial was about something harder to define: the right of an elected official to think.

Ninety percent alignment was insufficient. The debt clock was a prop of obstruction. The Epstein files legislation was bipartisan treachery. His new wife was suspicious. His independent thought was peacocking.

His colleagues got their answer. The price of independent thought in the Republican Party of 2026 is your career, your wife’s reputation, and thirty-three million dollars in attack ads. Plan accordingly.

But he owned his words. That remains the most dangerous thing a Republican can do.

And the most necessary.

What We’ve Learned

The Loyalty Trials of May 2026 will be studied not as a curiosity but as a case study. Here is what they established, regardless of the individual verdicts:

The primary system has been successfully weaponised as a compliance mechanism at every level of American government — federal, state, down to individual congressional districts and state Senate seats. The investment required to run a Loyalty Trial is now known: approximately one million dollars for a state Senate seat, eight million for a batch of them, thirty-three million for a single congressman the president wants gone.

The defendants who fared best were those with the deepest local roots, the longest incumbency, and the most specific legislative records — things that nationalised ad spending struggles to erase. The defendants who fared worst were those whose transgression was most recently visible or most symbolically potent to the base.

The Voting Rights Act, in its meaningful form, died in the same month as these trials. That is not a coincidence of timing. It is a convergence of strategy. The machine is being built at every level simultaneously — in the super PAC operations, in the primary challengers, in the federal judiciary, in the Supreme Court. The Loyalty Trials are one courtroom in a much larger courthouse.

And from Toronto — from the other side of the longest undefended border in the world, watching the country that invented modern democracy conduct a loyalty purge of its own party in the name of the man that party nominates — I find myself returning to the same thought I had watching Massie in his barn.

He owned his words.

He lost his seat for it. So did Cassidy. So did Raffensperger. So did five state senators in Indiana. The machine swept every courtroom it entered. The verdicts are unanimous. The price list is public. The compliance operation is fully functional.

But the thing the machine is trying to eliminate — the thing it spent $50 million to destroy across four states in three weeks — is the thing Massie still has, even without his seat. The thing Cassidy said in his concession speech, finally, after five years of not saying it. The thing Raffensperger has had since January 2, 2021, when he told the president the votes did not exist.

The ability to say: this is what I think, and I said it, and it’s mine.

The Loyalty Trials will continue past May. There will be more defendants, more cases, more verdicts. The docket in a compliance operation never empties. The Paxton endorsement over Cornyn — a 99 percent loyalty score still insufficient — ensures that the next round of trials has already begun.

But the resistance also continues. Not in office. Not from positions of power. Not with the institutional authority the machine has stripped from them. But in the words they owned, on the record, in the transcript, and in the voting booth where they cast the votes that ended their careers.

One stubborn man at a time. Even when the stubborn men lose.
