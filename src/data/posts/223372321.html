Every institution has a founding myth. FIFA’s official one involves seven European associations meeting in Paris in May 1904, united by a love of the game and a shared distaste for the English, who declined to join. The more useful founding date for the organisation we now know is seventy years later, in a congress hall in Frankfurt in June 1974, when a Brazilian swimmer turned businessman persuaded the football nations of Africa and Asia that an elderly English schoolmaster did not have their interests at heart.

He was right about the schoolmaster. Whether he had their interests at heart is the question this series sets out to answer, and it takes ten essays because the answer keeps getting more expensive.

The man who beat Sir Stanley

Sir Stanley Rous had been FIFA president since 1961. He was a former referee and a former secretary of the Football Association, a decent and rather starchy administrator who regarded FIFA as a kind of gentlemen’s club with a ball. His FIFA was small, European-run and nearly broke. It was also, by the standards of the time, culpably slow on apartheid South Africa, which Rous had tried to keep inside the fold long after much of the world had decided otherwise. That was not a small thing to the African associations, and the man from Rio noticed.

João Havelange had swum for Brazil at the 1936 Berlin Olympics and played water polo for Brazil at Helsinki in 1952. By 1974 he had run the Brazilian sports confederation for sixteen years, a period that included three World Cup triumphs, and had sat on the International Olympic Committee since 1963. He campaigned for the FIFA presidency as no one had campaigned before, visiting dozens of countries, often with Pelé at his elbow. His pitch was simple: more World Cup places for Africa and Asia, more money for development, more tournaments, more of everything.

At the Frankfurt congress the first round went 62 votes to 56 in his favour, short of the two-thirds majority FIFA’s rules then required for a first-round win. In the second round he won 68 to 52. The Associated Press, in a history of FIFA elections republished by Sports Illustrated on May 21, 2015, records both counts. It was the first time a non-European had led world football, and the European associations never quite recovered from the shock.

Promises have to be paid for

The problem with promising everyone more is that someone has to pay. Rous’s FIFA could not have funded a youth tournament, let alone a global development programme. Havelange’s solution, and it was genuinely innovative, was to sell football to corporations as a global advertising platform rather than treat it as a sport that happened to attract sponsors.

His essential partner was Horst Dassler, son of the founder of Adidas and the man who ran its French operation. Dassler understood earlier than almost anyone that the people who controlled the votes in international sport controlled its commercial rights, and that an equipment company which befriended the voters could become something much larger than an equipment company. He is widely reported to have worked the delegates on Havelange’s behalf in 1974. Afterwards Coca-Cola and Adidas became FIFA’s anchor partners, and money began to flow in a direction it had never flowed before.

In 1975 FIFA hired a young Swiss marketing man named Joseph S. Blatter as its technical director, with a remit to build the development programmes that Havelange had promised. In 1981 Blatter became general secretary. He is the subject of Part II, and of a good deal of what follows, but it is worth noting here that he was not an outsider who inherited Havelange’s FIFA. He was one of its architects.

The promises were kept, and that matters. A world youth championship (now the Under-20 World Cup) began in 1977, and an under-16 tournament (now the Under-17 World Cup) followed in 1985. The World Cup grew from 16 teams to 24 for Spain in 1982, and from 24 to 32 for France in 1998. African and Asian teams that had once been left to scrap over a single place between them began to qualify in numbers. It would be dishonest to write the history of the Havelange era as if nothing good came of it. Football genuinely became a world game in those twenty-four years, and Havelange deserves a portion of the credit.

He also deserves a portion of the invoice.

ISL: the company in the middle

In 1982 Dassler founded International Sport and Leisure, a sports marketing company based in Switzerland. ISL’s business was, in essence, to buy the television and marketing rights to the big sporting properties (the World Cup above all, but also Olympic and athletics events) and sell them on to broadcasters and sponsors at a profit. It was bound closely to FIFA for nearly two decades. Dassler died in 1987; the company outlived him by fourteen years.

The model contained an obvious structural problem. The officials who decided which company received the rights were the same officials whom that company had every incentive to keep happy. A marketing agency that pays the decision-makers is not, in my view, a marketing agency so much as a toll booth with a logo.

ISL collapsed into bankruptcy in May 2001 with debts reported at around $300 million. Its liquidation opened its books, and what was in them took more than a decade to reach the public, because powerful people spent a great deal of money keeping it there.

What a court decided, and what it did not

Precision matters here, so let me be exact about which bodies found what.

The first proceedings were against ISL’s own executives. Six former executives were tried in the Swiss canton of Zug in 2008 on charges that included fraud, embezzlement and falsifying documents, a prosecution set in motion by a criminal complaint from FIFA. As Kirsten Sparre reported for Play the Game on July 9, 2008, all six were cleared of most of the charges. Jean-Marie Weber, ISL’s long-time fixer, was convicted of embezzling 90,000 Swiss francs he had moved into his own account; two other executives were convicted over false documents used to set up sham companies. The court heard that ISL had paid sports officials some €87.5 million between 1989 and 2001 to secure rights. Paying such commissions to officials of private sports bodies was not a crime in Switzerland at the time, and FIFA was ordered to pay €72,000 in costs for having lodged the complaint.

So the first court to examine ISL’s books convicted no one for paying, or receiving, the money. That fact is less exculpatory than it sounds. It meant that, in Switzerland at the time, there was no law to break.

The second set of proceedings concerned the recipients. The Zug prosecutor, Thomas Hildbrand, investigated Havelange and his former son-in-law Ricardo Teixeira, president of the Brazilian football confederation and a member of FIFA’s executive committee, for embezzlement. That investigation never reached a court. In May 2010 it was closed after repayments totalling 5.5 million Swiss francs, on condition that the identities of those involved stay confidential. As the Associated Press reported in a story carried by Al Jazeera on July 12, 2012, the repayments came from FIFA, Havelange and Teixeira, with Teixeira’s share put at 2.5 million and Havelange’s at 500,000, and FIFA helped broker the anonymity that kept the arrangement secret for two years.

Neither man was convicted of anything in Switzerland. Neither admitted a crime. The case was closed by agreement, which is a distinct thing from an acquittal and a distinct thing from a conviction, and readers should hold all three categories separately in their heads for the rest of this series.

The document that would not stay buried

A coalition of media organisations, led by the BBC, fought through the Swiss courts for the release of the Zug closing order. Havelange and Teixeira appealed to stop it. On July 11, 2012, hours after Switzerland’s Federal Supreme Court rejected that appeal, FIFA published the 41-page document itself. As Swissinfo reported on July 12, 2012, the order said the two men may have received some 22 million Swiss francs between 1992 and 2000, with Teixeira receiving at least 12.74 million between 1992 and 1997 and Havelange at least 1.5 million. Hildbrand’s report also described pre-collapse FIFA as a deficient organisation.

Sepp Blatter, by then FIFA president, supplied what is in my view the most revealing line in the whole affair. He acknowledged that he was the senior FIFA figure identified in the order as “P1”, who would have known about an ISL payment of one million francs mistakenly sent to a FIFA account; he was not accused of any offence. He then explained that in the 1990s such payments could even be deducted from tax as a business expense, adding that today they would be punishable. That observation was offered, it seems, as a defence.

The Olympian exit

By then Havelange had already left one of his two great institutions. A BBC Panorama programme by the investigative reporter Andrew Jennings, broadcast on November 29, 2010, three days before FIFA voted on the 2018 and 2022 World Cups, alleged that Havelange had received a $1 million payment from ISL. The International Olympic Committee’s ethics commission opened an inquiry before Christmas. On December 4, 2011, the IOC announced that Havelange had resigned his membership after 48 years, citing his health. As Duncan Mackay reported for Inside World Football that day, the IOC executive board had been due to consider a suspension or expulsion on December 8. Resignation ended the IOC’s jurisdiction. That same week the IOC reprimanded Issa Hayatou and warned Lamine Diack over ISL money; Havelange, by leaving, avoided a ruling altogether.

Teixeira followed a similar path. On March 12, 2012, he resigned as president of the Brazilian confederation after twenty-three years, citing ill health, and soon gave up his posts on FIFA’s executive committee and the organising committee for the 2014 World Cup in Brazil.

The Eckert report

FIFA, having published the Zug document, referred the matter to its own ethics committee. The adjudicatory chamber was chaired by the German judge Hans-Joachim Eckert. His report, released on April 30, 2013, did what no court had done: it used the word bribes.

Eckert found that Havelange, Teixeira and Nicolás Leoz, the long-time president of the South American confederation, had accepted payments from ISL. As Andrew Warshaw reported for Inside World Football on April 30, 2013, Eckert wrote that the men should not have accepted any bribe money, that taking it had not been an offence under Swiss law at the time, and that their conduct was morally and ethically reproachable.

Here the categories matter again. This was an ethics finding by FIFA’s own internal body, not a criminal judgment. It came with no sanction against Havelange or Teixeira because both had already gone. Havelange stepped down as FIFA’s honorary president with effect from April 18, 2013, twelve days before the report was published, citing health and personal reasons. Leoz resigned from FIFA’s executive committee and the presidency of his confederation the week before publication, citing his health. Eckert declared the ISL matter closed.

On Blatter, Eckert found no misconduct under any ethics rule. He questioned whether Blatter knew or should have known about the payments before ISL’s bankruptcy, and described his conduct as possibly “clumsy”. Blatter welcomed the report and credited FIFA’s reforms. Havelange, who never accepted the characterisation of the payments as wrongdoing, died in Rio de Janeiro on August 16, 2016, aged 100, during the Olympic Games he had helped bring to his city.

Teixeira’s story did not end there. On December 3, 2015, the United States Justice Department unsealed a superseding indictment in the Eastern District of New York naming him among sixteen new defendants charged with racketeering, wire fraud and money laundering conspiracies, among other offences, in connection with football marketing contracts. He has denied wrongdoing and, living in Brazil, which does not extradite its citizens, has never stood trial in the United States; the charges remain allegations. On November 29, 2019, FIFA’s ethics committee found him guilty of bribery under its code and banned him from football for life, an ethics-code sanction rather than a criminal conviction.

What was actually inherited

It is tempting to read the ISL affair as a story about two greedy men and one corrupt agency, now safely dead or banned. That reading lets the institution off too lightly.

Consider what the Havelange era actually built. It built a structure in which a tiny executive committee, two dozen men at most, controlled the most valuable rights in world sport, and in which the companies that bought those rights had every reason to cultivate the men who sold them. It built a voting system in which every national association, whether Brazil or Montserrat, had one vote for the presidency, which made development money a powerful instrument of patronage. It built a culture in which the president was also the chief salesman, and in which the line between growing the game and growing one’s support was never clearly drawn. And it built, through the Zug settlement, a precedent: when the bill arrived, it could be settled quietly, with confidentiality as a condition.

It is hard not to conclude that the business model was the point. Havelange did not corrupt a pure institution; he invented a commercial institution in which the opportunities for corruption were part of the architecture. Some of what that architecture produced was wonderful. The rest is the subject of the next nine essays.

Sepp Blatter, the general secretary who had sat beside Havelange since 1981, inherited the whole of it in Paris in June 1998, on the eve of the first 32-team World Cup. He would keep it for seventeen years. Part II follows how he held it.
