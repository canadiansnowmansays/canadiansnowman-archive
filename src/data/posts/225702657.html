On election night, as the results turned against his party, Rep. Robert Garcia of California posted two words on X: “Lawyer up.” President Trump posted one word on Truth Social: “VETO.”

Between them, those three words describe the next two years of American accountability politics better than any think-tank report. Garcia, the top Democrat on the House Oversight Committee, was promising investigations. Trump was reminding everyone that investigations are not laws, and that laws still need his signature. This series has spent sixteen parts documenting how the Trump family turned the presidency into a business. Part 17 asks the obvious follow-up question: now that the voters have handed Congress to the Democrats, what can Congress actually do about it?

The honest answer has three parts, because the Democrats are working on three clocks at once. One starts in January. One runs into the veto. And one is set for a president who has not yet been elected.

The First Clock: Investigation

The 120th Congress convenes on January 3, 2027. As of 2:53 p.m. Eastern on November 19, the Associated Press count gave Democrats 56 Senate seats, including two who caucus with them, to the Republicans’ 44. As of 2:54 p.m., its House count stood at 257 seats won by Democrats and 172 by Republicans, with six seats not yet counted as won by either party. Seven of the nine Senate seats the Democrats flipped were in states Trump carried by double digits in 2024, as The New York Times noted in its results coverage. Committees organise in January, and according to a post-election analysis by the law firm Morrison Foerster, the first subpoenas are possible early in the new year.

The cast is already assembled. As the Associated Press reported on November 5, 2026, Hakeem Jeffries, in line to become Speaker, has handed the investigations to Garcia at Oversight and Jamie Raskin at Judiciary. Jared Huffman, the senior Democrat on Natural Resources, wants to examine the White House ballroom and the Trump family’s role in mineral deals. In the Senate, Richard Blumenthal is expected to chair the Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations and Ron Wyden is in line to chair Finance. The target list includes the family’s business dealings, its growing wealth since January 2025, and the crypto ventures it shares with the president’s special envoy, Steve Witkoff.

The investigators’ most promising targets are not, however, the president himself. As Axios observed in July, the ripest subjects are the people around Trump — family, appointees and allies — who, unlike him, can be compelled to testify under oath. Part 16 of this series covered the first name on that list, Jared Kushner, whose undisclosed defence stake surfaced two days after the election. He will not be the last.

The case that best shows what investigators are after is World Liberty Financial, the crypto venture founded by the Trump and Witkoff families. In February 2026, the Wall Street Journal reported that an Abu Dhabi vehicle backed by Sheikh Tahnoon bin Zayed — the UAE’s national security adviser and the brother of its president — had bought 49 percent of the company for $500 million, four days before Trump’s second inauguration. The deal was signed by Eric Trump. Of the first $250 million, about $187 million went to Trump family entities and at least $31 million to entities tied to Witkoff’s family, as The Block summarised the Journal’s findings. Within months, the administration had pledged to sell the UAE hundreds of thousands of advanced AI chips a year, including to Tahnoon’s company G42.

The president has a settled answer for all of this. In a 60 Minutes interview broadcast on November 8, Ross Douthat asked why he had not divested his stake in World Liberty. “Because I know nothing about it,” Trump replied, adding that he could not tell his sons to stop running it while he was president, as the International Business Times reported. That is an answer designed for a press conference. It is not one designed to survive the sons themselves being asked, under oath, who knew what and when.

The Problem With Subpoenas

Here the essay has to be blunter than the election-night celebrations were. A subpoena is a demand, not a guarantee.

When a witness refuses to comply, Congress has three options: refer the case to the Justice Department for criminal contempt, sue in federal court, or invoke its own long-dormant power of inherent contempt, unused for the better part of a century. Each is weaker than it sounds. Morrison Foerster counts nearly 600 congressional subpoenas issued since 2021 and only three civil enforcement suits in that time. Criminal contempt requires a prosecution by the Justice Department, which rarely follows when the other party controls Congress. And the White House has staffed up its legal office and may claim executive privilege even over its communications with private parties, citing a recent opinion from the Justice Department’s own Office of Legal Counsel.

Imagine the sequence. A committee subpoenas a Trump family business. The business refuses. The committee votes contempt. The House votes contempt. The file is then sent for prosecution to a Justice Department run by the president’s appointees. Even in the rare case where a court imposes fines, MS NOW noted on November 9, the federal government could simply pay them.

The Democrats know this, and their workaround is the most interesting thing in their plan. According to MS NOW, they intend to use their subpoena power on private companies and individuals rather than relying on the administration to answer. The reasoning is sound: much of the evidence that matters sits outside government, in the files of law firms, banks, corporations and former officials. A crypto exchange, an investment fund or an accounting firm has no executive privilege to claim, no friendly Justice Department to lean on, and a board of directors that does not care to be held in contempt of Congress. If the White House is a locked door, the Democrats mean to go round to the windows.

The Second Clock: Legislation

Investigation exposes. Legislation prevents. The Democrats have both kinds of tool ready, and the legislative ones are more ambitious than the coverage has suggested.

The centrepiece is a bill Chuck Schumer introduced on July 30, 2026, which he called the biggest, boldest and broadest anti-corruption proposal ever put forward. The Anti-Corruption Bureau Creation Act would build a new, independent seven-member agency with subpoena and enforcement powers, folding the Federal Election Commission, the Office of Government Ethics and the Office of Special Counsel under one roof, as The Block reported. Its jurisdiction would reach back to January 20, 2025, with a ten-year statute of limitations. It would ban presidents, vice presidents, their spouses, adult children and senior officials from accepting gifts worth more than $50,000. And, in the provision that matters most, it would let ordinary Americans and state attorneys general sue to recover money obtained through presidential corruption, as Senate Democrats set out in announcing it. When it was introduced, the bill had four Democratic sponsors and no Republicans.

Outside Congress, the policy shops are filling in the gaps. A report this month from the Center for American Progress urges the incoming Congress to write the Constitution’s emoluments clauses into statute for the first time, defining an emolument to include cryptocurrency and digital assets, and giving anyone who can show concrete economic injury — Congress and state attorneys general among them — the standing to sue. It also proposes using Congress’s taxing power to claw back presidential self-enrichment, an idea with the elegance of making the grift pay for its own audit.

The third front is crypto, where the grift and the policy collide most directly. The industry badly wants the Clarity Act, a market-structure bill that would settle its regulatory status, and Democrats have spent the year making their votes conditional on ethics rules. Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand has said work continues on a bipartisan ethics package that would ban presidents, vice presidents and members of Congress from profiting personally from crypto, according to a separate report in The Block. After its August report, Public Citizen went further, urging Congress to write presidential divestment rules into the bill itself. With the majority now changing hands, the industry’s most wanted legislation has become the Democrats’ best bargaining chip.

The Veto Wall

Which brings us back to the one-word post.

Every one of these bills must eventually land on the president’s desk, and the president has already announced what he intends to do with them. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of both chambers, so any override will turn on how many Republicans are prepared to vote to restrain a president of their own party on the subject of his own family’s money. That is not impossible — Republican voters dislike the conduct too, as Part 15 noted — but nobody should plan a two-year agenda around it.

There are partial routes around the wall. Ethics rules can ride on bills the president wants, which is why the Clarity Act matters so much. Spending bills can carry conditions; a president who vetoes a government funding bill to protect his family’s crypto business would be making the Democrats’ argument for them. And some reforms need no presidential signature at all: the House and Senate can ban their own members from trading stocks, as Jeffries has promised, and can bar former members from lobbying, without asking anyone’s permission. That will not touch the Trump family. It will, however, take away the Republicans’ favourite reply to every charge of presidential self-dealing, which is that Congress is no better.

The Third Clock: The Next President

The most important thing about Schumer’s bill is not what it would do in 2027. It is what it would do in 2029 and beyond.

Look again at its design. Jurisdiction backdated to the first day of this presidency. A ten-year limitation period, long enough to outlast any second term. Civil recovery suits brought by state attorneys general and private citizens. Each feature answers a problem the Atlantic’s readers kept raising in the comments under the Kushner story: what happens when the president simply pardons everyone? A presidential pardon reaches federal crimes. It does not reach a state prosecution, and it does not reach a civil suit to recover money. The Democrats who drafted this bill were plainly thinking about the pardon power, and they built around it.

There is a historical template here, and it is not a comforting one. The Office of Government Ethics, which Schumer’s bureau would absorb, was itself a child of Watergate, created by the Ethics in Government Act of 1978 — four years after Richard Nixon left office. The great post-Watergate reforms were not passed to remove Nixon; they were passed to prevent the next one. The Democrats are, in effect, attempting the same thing on a shorter timetable, writing the rules while the conduct they are meant to stop is still under way, in the knowledge that the man who inspired them can veto them until January 2029.

That makes the investigations more important, not less. A bill with no chance of becoming law in 2027 becomes a serious bill in 2029 if, in the meantime, the hearings have built the factual record that makes it necessary. Exposure now is the groundwork for prevention later.

What Could Go Wrong

Two things, mainly, and the Democrats’ own senators have named one of them. “We can’t turn this into just a vengeance tour,” Sen. Peter Welch warned after the election. Jeffries has not ruled out impeaching Trump a third time. Part 15 of this series argued that the watchdogs were losing to exhaustion — too many scandals, too fast, for any one to hold the public’s attention. A Congress that responds by holding forty hearings a week will reproduce that problem with gavels. The investigations will work only if they are chosen with the discipline of a prosecutor rather than the appetite of a cable-news booker.

The second risk is forgetting who the victims are. Public Citizen estimated in August 2026 that investors in Trump-linked crypto ventures were at least $4.7 billion underwater, including $3.2 billion on the $TRUMP memecoin alone, while the president himself made at least $1.4 billion from crypto in 2025. The report’s author, Zach Everson, cautioned critics against mocking the people who bought in. He is right. Most of the marks in this casino are not sheikhs or sovereign funds. They are ordinary people who believed the businessman myth that Part 14 described and put their savings where the president’s name was. If the hearings treat them as fools rather than as the defrauded, Democrats will hand the White House its favourite story: that the investigations are an attack on Trump voters, not on the people who took their money.

This series has always run on a casino metaphor: the house always wins, unless the marks stop playing. On November 3, enough of them stopped. What follows is the slower part of the story, the part where someone has to count the chips, reconstruct the books, and rewrite the house rules so the next owner cannot run the same game. None of that will happen as fast as the grift did. It rarely does. But for the first time since this series began in June 2025, there is someone in Washington with both the power and the motive to start.
