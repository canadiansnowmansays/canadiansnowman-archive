A ratchet has a pawl, a small hinged catch that drops into the teeth of the wheel and stops it turning back. Most of the reversals in the first three tiers of the Reversal Ledger have a pawl that can be lifted, by a president, an agency or a Congress, if enough of them agree. The fourth tier is where the pawl has been welded in place.

Fourteen reversals sit there. Three, struck down under the Congressional Review Act, were the subject of Part III. The rest fall into four kinds of lock: rulings of the Supreme Court, the president’s power to pardon, contracts already signed, and treaties that the other parties have left or let die. Each is a different kind of permanence, and each says something about how the American system was built.

The constitutional locks

On 29 June 2026, the last week of its term, the Supreme Court decided Trump v. Slaughter by six votes to three. Rebecca Kelly Slaughter, a Democratic member of the Federal Trade Commission, had been fired in March 2025 without the cause the FTC’s founding statute required. The Court held that Congress cannot protect the members of such commissions from dismissal by the president, and in doing so it overruled Humphrey’s Executor v. United States, the 1935 decision that had upheld exactly that protection.

Humphrey’s Executor was the legal foundation for the independent regulatory commission: bodies like the Federal Trade Commission, the National Labor Relations Board and the Merit Systems Protection Board, whose members serve fixed terms and could be removed only for misconduct, so that they could make decisions the president disliked. After Slaughter, any president may fire their members at will. The Court carved out the Federal Reserve, and on the same day, in a separate case, it refused to let the president remove Fed Governor Lisa Cook. But the rest of the independent agencies are now, in practice, departments of the executive branch.

Nothing a future president or Congress does can undo this. A statute restoring removal protection would be unconstitutional under the Court’s reading. A future president could promise not to fire commissioners, and might keep the promise, but the promise would bind no successor. Only the Supreme Court can restore what the Supreme Court has held the Constitution forbids.

The inspectors general show what follows. Seventeen of them, the internal watchdogs of federal departments under a law passed in 1978 with support from both parties, were fired on a single night in January 2025. A federal judge found in September 2025 that the firings were probably unlawful, because the statute requires thirty days’ notice to Congress and a reason, but declined to reinstate anyone. The case was put on hold to wait for Slaughter. After Slaughter, it is hard to see how the notice requirement constrains a president who is now held to have the power to remove at will. Watchdogs who serve at the pleasure of the officials they watch are not independent, whatever the statute says.

Two more constitutional locks concern transgender Americans, and they show that not every lock in the ledger runs against public opinion. In June 2025, in United States v. Skrmetti, the Court upheld Tennessee’s ban on gender-affirming medical care for minors. In June 2026 it upheld two state laws barring transgender girls and women from girls’ and women’s sports. Polling has consistently shown majority support for restrictions on transgender athletes, and this series said in its introduction that it would not pretend otherwise. The federal policies built on those rulings could be changed by a future administration. The state laws now carry constitutional approval, and no federal policy can override that. A Medicaid rule cutting federal funding for gender care for minors takes effect on 13 October 2026, and it rests on that foundation, though twenty-one states and the District of Columbia have challenged it.

The pardon

Article II of the Constitution gives the president the power “to grant reprieves and pardons for offences against the United States, except in cases of impeachment.” It is one of the few powers in the Constitution with almost no stated limit, and it has one property that no other presidential act shares. It cannot be taken back.

On his first afternoon in office, Trump pardoned about 1,500 people charged in connection with the attack on the Capitol on 6 January 2021, and commuted the sentences of fourteen more. Some had been convicted of assaulting police officers. Some were serving long sentences for seditious conspiracy. All of them were released or cleared.

There is no mechanism to revoke a pardon once it has been accepted. A future president cannot do it. Congress cannot do it. The courts have never recognised a way to do it. The prosecutions built over four years, the evidence gathered and the convictions obtained remain on the record as history, but their legal consequences are gone for good. Of all the reversals in the ledger, this is the most completely final.

The contract

Some locks are not legal doctrines but ordinary commercial law.

In March 2026 the Interior Department held a lease sale in the National Petroleum Reserve in Alaska that drew a record $163.7 million in winning bids. In June it held one on the coastal plain of the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge, which drew $3.7 million; no major oil company bid. Both sales reversed Biden administration decisions to cancel leases and protect most of the reserve.

A federal oil lease is a contract between the government and the leaseholder. A future administration that wanted to protect the land again could not simply tear the leases up. It would have to buy them back, negotiate their surrender, or cancel them and face claims for damages. The Biden administration did cancel seven leases in the Arctic Refuge in 2023, but those were leases it argued had been issued unlawfully, and the cancellations were themselves litigated. A lease issued properly is property. The same principle protects mining claims staked on land removed from a national monument, as Part I described for Bears Ears.

The treaties

The last kind of lock is the one this series finds most instructive, because it involves not the American system at all but the rest of the world, which did not stand still.

The Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty, signed by Ronald Reagan and Mikhail Gorbachev in 1987, eliminated an entire class of nuclear missiles from Europe and established inspections to verify it. The United States withdrew in August 2019, citing Russian violations. The treaty is dead. There is nothing to rejoin, and negotiating a new one would mean persuading Russia to destroy weapons it has since built.

The Open Skies Treaty, negotiated under George H.W. Bush and signed in 1992, let its members fly unarmed surveillance aircraft over one another’s territory to build confidence. The United States left in November 2020. Russia left the following year. The two American aircraft that flew the missions were retired to storage in 2021. Rejoining would mean rejoining a treaty without its most important counterpart, with no aircraft to fly.

The Trans-Pacific Partnership, which the Obama administration negotiated with eleven Pacific countries, was abandoned by the United States in the first week of Trump’s first term, in January 2017. The other eleven went ahead without it. Their Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership took effect at the end of 2018, and the United Kingdom joined in December 2024. If the United States wanted in now, it would apply to join an agreement whose terms were written without it.

The Iran nuclear agreement of 2015 is the starkest case. The United States withdrew in May 2018. Iran then expanded its nuclear programme. In September 2025 the European parties triggered the return of United Nations sanctions. After the hostilities of 2026, the International Atomic Energy Agency has said it can no longer verify what Iran is doing. The inspection regime that was the agreement’s core no longer exists. There is nothing to restore; there would only be something new to build, from a much worse starting point.

Locks made by the other side

The locks are not all of one party’s making, and one recent example shows the restorers reaching for the same tool.

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act of 1953 lets a president withdraw offshore areas from oil and gas leasing. It says nothing about a later president reversing such a withdrawal. Obama used it in 2016 to protect large parts of the Arctic and Atlantic. When Trump tried to revoke those withdrawals in 2017, a federal judge in Alaska ruled in 2019 that the Act gives presidents the power to withdraw but not to undo a withdrawal; that ruling was later set aside on appeal after Biden reinstated the protections, leaving the question unresolved. On 6 January 2025, two weeks before leaving office, Biden used the same power to withdraw about 625 million acres of coastal waters, explicitly to put them beyond his successor’s reach. Trump moved to revoke that withdrawal on his first day in office. Whether he can is a question the courts have still to settle.

That is a ratchet built deliberately, at the last moment, by the side this series has mostly been describing as its victim. It may be a legitimate use of the law Congress wrote. It is also a reminder that the mechanism, not the direction, is the subject here: each side has learned to look for the pawl.

The irony in the lock

There is one consolation in this tier, and it lies in the very ruling that fastened its strongest lock.

Slaughter overruled a precedent that had stood for ninety-one years. Loper Bright, discussed in Part II, overruled one that had stood for forty. Dobbs in 2022 overruled one that had stood for nearly fifty. The current Court has shown that no precedent is permanent, which means its own rulings are not permanent either. A future Court with a different majority could restore Humphrey’s Executor as readily as this one discarded it.

But the Court changes slowly, and only through vacancies, confirmations and time. Its majority was assembled over decades by a legal movement that planned for it carefully, a story Part VI will tell. Undoing a constitutional lock is not a policy decision. It is a generational project, and it depends on the timing of deaths and retirements no one controls. Proposals to change the Court itself, by adding seats or setting term limits, have been debated for years and would require either a constitutional amendment or a political consensus that does not exist.

The bottom of the arc

In Poe’s story, there comes a moment when the blade is so close that the prisoner can smell the steel. The fourth tier of the ledger is that moment. Here the question is no longer how long a restoration would take, but whether restoration is even the right word.

Pardons are permanent. Leases are property. Treaties whose other parties have moved on cannot be rejoined as they were. Constitutional rulings last until the Court changes its mind, which may take a generation. What a future government can do in this tier is not restore. It is replace: write new laws within the new constitutional limits, negotiate new agreements from the position the country is now in, and accept that some things that existed in 2016 will not exist again in the same form.

Not everything held, then. But not everything gave way. The next part of this series looks at the ten reversals the courts stopped outright, and asks why those held when so much else did not.
