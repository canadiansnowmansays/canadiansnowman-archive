Postmark: A tissue-culture lab, University of Tokyo. A small robotic hand twitches. The twitch is not a motor. It is a strip of living human muscle, contracting on command, exactly the way the muscle in your own hand does.

Part III of this series, Nature’s Patent Office, was about engineers who studied biology and then built machines that copied what they found — the gecko’s foot reproduced in synthetic adhesive, the cat’s gait reproduced in a robot’s control software. This postcard is about a stranger and more recent branch of the same impulse, one that has stopped copying biology and started simply using it. Biohybrid robots do not imitate a muscle. They contain one — living tissue, grown or harvested, wired directly into a mechanical structure, doing the actual biological job it evolved to do, inside a machine built by an engineer rather than an organism.

The hand that grows its own fingers

The most striking demonstration of this field comes from Shoji Takeuchi’s laboratory at the University of Tokyo, one of the field’s most productive research groups. As one 2026 survey of biohybrid robots put it, quoting Takeuchi directly, organisms have spent millions of years evolving to perform certain tasks, which allows their bodies to do things that remain difficult or even impossible for purely mechanical systems — and by combining biology with engineering, a researcher can build robots with capabilities beyond what either discipline manages alone. His laboratory has demonstrated a biohybrid hand actuated by multiple human muscle tissue samples working antagonistically against each other, the same way a real hand’s flexor and extensor muscles pull against one another to bend and straighten a finger. Electrical pulses, rather than nerve signals, trigger the contraction — but the contraction itself is genuine muscle tissue doing genuine muscle work, not a motor simulating it.

The broader field this single hand belongs to has grown considerably in scope. As a comprehensive 2025 review in the journal npj Robotics documents, researchers have built biohybrid actuators from cardiac muscle cells, skeletal muscle tissue, nerve cells, and even the dorsal vessel tissue of insects, achieving movements that include crawling, swimming, gripping, and pumping — a catalogue of biological actions borrowed wholesale rather than reverse-engineered. One particularly striking recent result, reported in the journal Matter, describes a bipedal biohybrid robot powered entirely by rings of muscle tissue engineered specifically to deliver enhanced contractile force through tetanic stimulation — muscle held in continuous, sustained contraction the way a weightlifter’s muscle locks under a heavy hold, applied here to make a small machine walk on two legs using tissue rather than servos.

The insect that never needs recharging

The second branch of this field takes a different approach entirely: rather than growing tissue in a laboratory dish, it recruits a whole living organism and turns its own body into the robot’s chassis, power supply, and locomotion system simultaneously. As one detailed technical review of the field explains, cyborg insects — built from cockroaches, beetles, and locusts, among other species — are controlled through targeted electrical stimulation of sensory organs, neural pathways, or muscle groups, inducing walking, jumping, or climbing on command while the insect’s own legs, wings, and nervous system do all the actual mechanical work. A comprehensive 2026 review in Advanced Intelligent Systems traces the field back to its first appearance in 1997 and catalogues seven distinct insect platforms now in active research use, each exploiting the specific locomotion strengths — walking, jumping, climbing — that millions of years of evolution already optimized for that particular species.

The most elegant detail in this branch of the field, and the one this series finds hardest to improve on, is the power source. As the same technical review describes, researchers have developed biofuel cells that generate electricity by consuming an insect’s own hemolymph — its equivalent of blood, rich in the sugars trehalose and glucose — oxidizing that sugar to produce usable current, mounted directly onto or implanted within the insect’s body. A cyborg locust, wired this way, never needs its battery changed, because it is, in the most literal engineering sense, running on its own metabolism. The same review notes that using the living animal to navigate around obstacles saves the onboard computation and battery capacity a fully artificial robot would otherwise need to spend solving the same problem — the insect’s own evolved reflexes handling the hard part, for free, that a purely mechanical insect-scale robot would need considerable engineering to replicate.

What this is actually for

None of this is being pursued for its own curious sake, and the applications researchers describe are the same ones this series has traced through its more conventional robotics postcards, reached by a stranger route. Cyborg insects, small enough to fit through rubble a wheeled robot could never navigate, are being explored specifically for the same environmental-monitoring and search-and-rescue applications this series covered in Part XX — a tiny, insect-piloted platform that can climb into a collapsed building’s voids using the insect’s own evolved climbing ability, rather than an engineer’s imperfect attempt to reproduce it in gears and motors. Biohybrid muscle actuators, meanwhile, are being explored for soft robotic grippers gentle enough to handle delicate objects — the same soft-robotics problem this series found solved with pneumatics in the Octobot of Part III, here solved instead with tissue that contracts and relaxes the way biological muscle always has.

The honest limits of a living machine

No postcard in this series has earned the right to report only the wonder, and biohybrid robotics carries a specific, unglamorous limitation that every other robot in this series’ pages has been spared: the machine dies. A living-muscle-limited biological bot documented in one of the field’s founding papers achieved a functional lifespan of roughly one week before the tissue itself degraded past the point of useful contraction — a hard ceiling no battery replacement or firmware update can extend, because the actuator is not a component that wears out gradually. It is a piece of biology that was never going to live forever outside a body in the first place. Every biohybrid robot inherits this constraint in some form: tissue requires a nutrient supply, a temperature range, and a waste-removal pathway that a purely mechanical actuator simply does not need, and researchers spend as much engineering effort keeping the living component alive as they spend making it move.

This is worth setting against the field’s genuine advantages, because the honest trade is precise rather than one-sided. A biohybrid actuator can achieve force-to-weight ratios and self-healing properties no synthetic material yet matches, and a cyborg insect’s obstacle-navigation ability, borrowed wholesale from evolution, would take a purely mechanical robot’s control software years to approximate at the same size and power budget. What the field trades for those advantages is durability, shelf life, and the entire apparatus of tissue culture — incubators, nutrient media, sterile technique — that a robotics lab building purely mechanical systems never has to maintain. Every biohybrid robot in this postcard is, in the most literal sense, a machine with a shelf life measured in the same units as a living organism’s, rather than a warranty period.

The self-healing property deserves its own line, because it is the one advantage no synthetic material has yet come close to matching at any price. A torn synthetic actuator stays torn until an engineer replaces it. Living muscle tissue, within limits, repairs its own microscopic damage the same way the muscle in a human arm recovers after a hard workout — a capability evolution spent hundreds of millions of years perfecting, and one that remains, for now, entirely beyond what any synthetic polymer or metal alloy can reproduce. Researchers building biohybrid actuators are not merely borrowing a muscle’s contraction. They are borrowing its entire maintenance department along with it.

The ethics that arrived before the headlines

A field this genuinely strange has, to its credit, generated serious ethical scrutiny from within its own ranks rather than waiting for outside critics to raise the alarm — and this series has learned to take that kind of internal candour seriously wherever it finds it. As a peer-reviewed paper on responsibility in biohybrid robotics research lays out, the field’s own practitioners have begun asking, in print, questions this series has circled since its very first postcard about what we owe the living systems we recruit into our machines: what welfare considerations apply to an insect wired for remote control, whether tissue grown specifically to be used as an actuator carries any different moral weight than tissue grown for any other laboratory purpose, and how researchers should think about systems that blur, more than any purely mechanical robot ever could, the line between tool and organism.

The most philosophically unsettling frontier within this same research space involves neurons rather than muscle. Researchers have demonstrated that living neurons, grown in a dish and connected to electrodes, can learn to play a simple video game when embodied in a simulated environment — a result reported in the journal Neuron under the provocative framing that the neurons exhibited a measurable form of sentience once given a body, however simulated, to act through. This series does not have the standing to resolve what that finding means, and neither, on the honest evidence, does the field that produced it. What it can report is that the question is being asked from inside the laboratories doing the work, in peer-reviewed journals, years before this technology reaches anything like the public visibility this series’ other postcards have covered — which is, if nothing else, a healthier starting position than most new technologies manage.

What the pulse actually proves

From the balcony, biohybrid robotics is the strangest and most quietly radical territory this series has yet visited, because it does not ask whether a machine can be made to resemble a living thing — the question Part VIII spent an entire postcard on — but whether the distinction between machine and living thing was ever as solid as engineering convention assumed. A robotic hand moved by human muscle tissue is not a humanoid in the sense Part VIII or Part IX examined; it makes no attempt to look like a person, dance like one, or fold laundry in a living room. It simply borrows, directly and without translation, one specific biological capability that evolution already perfected, and asks an engineer to build only the parts biology did not already provide.

A Canadian, heir to a research tradition that has produced its own share of biomedical engineering — from neuroArm’s brain surgery inside an MRI scanner, which this series traced back to the Canadarm lineage in Part II, to the university labs this country has quietly staffed for decades — should recognize the family resemblance in this postcard immediately. The instinct that took a robotic arm built for orbital assembly and repurposed it for neurosurgery is the same instinct, wearing a different lab coat, that takes a strip of cultured human muscle and asks it to close a robotic hand’s fingers. Biology, this series keeps discovering, was never merely a design reference to be copied. In enough laboratories now, it has simply been hired.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one, unusually, has a pulse.
