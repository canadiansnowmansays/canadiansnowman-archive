Postmark: A Vancouver lab. A robotic hand, twenty degrees of freedom, touches an unfamiliar object for the first time and, without being told how, simply picks it up.

This series’ own Introduction, published in June, named Sanctuary AI directly as part of the extraordinary and largely untold Canadian robotics story it promised to keep returning to — “from Canadarm to Waterloo’s RoboHub to Sanctuary AI to a fifteen-year-old in Burlington.” Eight rotations later, this is that promise finally kept. Sanctuary AI is Vancouver’s entry in the Mirror territory, the company this series has referenced in passing since its first postcard and never yet visited properly. It belongs here for a reason beyond geography: it has spent seven years betting against nearly every assumption the rest of the humanoid industry has built itself around.

The bet against legs

As the Global Recognition Awards noted in recognising the company in February 2026, Sanctuary AI’s argument is disarmingly simple: a general-purpose humanoid that can be moved from one job to the next is worth more than a machine built to do only one thing perfectly, because the work itself keeps changing — a warehouse worker who can also stock shelves, a factory hand who can also inspect parts. That argument is not unique to Sanctuary; Part XI’s five-thousand-dollar humanoid and Part XV’s surgical robots share the same underlying premise. What distinguishes Sanctuary’s approach is where it chose to spend its engineering effort first: the hand, built for a dexterity considerably closer to a human hand than competing platforms have generally prioritised, in a field that has spent the last several years chasing the more visually dramatic capabilities this series has tracked closely: the marathon-winning gait of Part XVIII, the kung-fu choreography of Part IX, the backflips and balance recoveries of Part VIII.

The company’s own founding announcement made this priority explicit rather than incidental. As Sanctuary AI stated when it unveiled Phoenix on May 16, 2023, the sixth-generation robot was built to be, in co-founder Geordie Rose’s words, the most sensor-rich and physically capable humanoid ever built — engineered specifically to let its AI control system, called Carbon, perform the broadest possible set of real work tasks rather than the narrowest set of impressive demonstrations. As the specification sheet at Humanoid.guide records, that Phoenix stands 170 centimetres tall, weighs 70 kilograms, carries a 25-kilogram payload, and its signature feature is its hands: twenty degrees of freedom per hand, fitted with proprietary haptic sensors that let the robot feel what it is touching rather than gripping blindly by pre-programmed force. The hand has kept improving since. As The Robot Report reported on April 2, 2026, Sanctuary’s newer hydraulic hand, trained entirely in simulation and never on the real object, turned a lettered cube to a target position ten times in a row without dropping it — what the field calls zero-shot manipulation, a skill learned in software and carried into the physical world on the first attempt.

The patent portfolio nobody talks about

Sanctuary’s public profile has never matched the scale of its underlying intellectual property, and this series has learned, across every territory it has visited, that the quieter Canadian story is often the more consequential one. As John Koetsier reported for Forbes on May 16, 2026, following an interview at Web Summit Vancouver, CEO James Wells described Sanctuary as Canada’s only homegrown humanoid robotics company, and stated the company holds what he characterised as the third-largest intellectual property portfolio in the global humanoid space — a claim that Morgan Stanley’s own research has lent independent weight to, ranking Sanctuary third for humanoid-related patent filings in its “Humanoid 100” report mapping the entire humanoid robot value chain, and that patent-analytics firm PatentVest has separately flagged for a striking disconnect: Sanctuary’s IP strength runs well ahead of the venture capital funding the company has attracted relative to better-capitalized American rivals like Figure, which has raised more than half a billion dollars, or Tesla’s Optimus programme, backed by the resources of one of the world’s largest automakers.

This is a familiar shape to any reader who has followed this series’ Canadian thread from the start. Canadarm3 was built for a lunar station that got cancelled and found new work on the lunar surface instead. Open Ocean Robotics grew from a Vancouver Island garage into a NATO-affiliated ocean-monitoring fleet without ever leaving Vancouver Island. Sanctuary AI has assembled what independent analysts consider one of the deepest patent moats in an industry chasing tens of billions of dollars in Chinese and American capital, from a lab in Vancouver, with a fraction of its rivals’ funding — quiet, patient, underfunded relative to the hype, and, on the evidence of the IP filings, not obviously behind.

The industrial deployment already underway

Unlike several humanoid companies this series has examined whose demonstrations remain closer to spectacle than production, Sanctuary’s current generation is already working inside real manufacturing facilities. As the same Global Recognition Awards profile records, Phoenix has been deployed in actual manufacturing plants and has demonstrated the ability to learn a new job in twenty-four hours — a tenfold improvement over the company’s own previous generation of robots, and a capability the same profile frames against a stark economic backdrop: a global labour shortage the same account values at more than ten trillion dollars, driven by the reality that ninety per cent of manufacturing worldwide still involves human labour despite fifty years of industrial robotics, precisely because conventional industrial robots remain what the profile calls idiot savants — extraordinary at one narrow task, but requiring an entire facility to be re-engineered around their limitations, with cages, precise positioning, and structured lighting standing between a robot and the messy, variable reality of an ordinary workplace.

The production numbers are starting to arrive, too. As Sanctuary AI announced through Business Wire on June 17, 2026, its AI software, running on an existing commercial robot rather than a humanoid body, achieved a 99.5 per cent success rate performing fast wire assembly at a global Tier 1 automotive supplier — the kind of unglamorous, repetitive, precision task this series has learned to treat as the true test of a robot’s readiness, rather than a viral demonstration filmed once under ideal conditions.

The contrarian who doesn’t want your laundry done

The most striking thing about Sanctuary AI’s public position in 2026, and the detail that earns this postcard its title, is CEO James Wells’s own stated view on where humanoid robots will actually end up working — a view that runs directly against nearly every consumer-facing humanoid company this series has covered, including 1X’s NEO in Part XVII and the laundry-folding robots of Part XXXI. In the same Forbes interview, Wells ranked the home dead last among the places a humanoid robot will find real, sustained work, at least three to five years behind the factory floor — not the first, as 1X, Figure, and a wave of well-funded competitors are currently betting. He went further, dismissing the home-robot pitch for 1X’s NEO as, in his phrase, a marketing initiative.

This is worth sitting with, because it inverts the entire narrative arc Part VII through Part XXXI of this series has traced — the slow, halting, genuinely encouraging march from a bankrupt Roomba company toward a laundry robot that finally, mostly, works. Wells’s argument, distilled from years running a company that chose factory floors and warehouses over living rooms from its very first generation, is that the home is simply the hardest possible environment for a general-purpose robot to prove itself economically viable in: infinitely variable, lightly supervised, low-margin, and populated by exactly the kind of unpredictable clutter — the crumpled sock behind the couch, this series’ own recurring example — that a robot trained on structured industrial tasks has the least experience handling. Industrial customers, by contrast, will pay a premium for measurable, repeatable value today, in an environment where imperfect performance costs a company money rather than merely disappointing a homeowner.

Where Sanctuary actually came from

The company’s own history deserves a fuller telling than its current product line alone provides. Sanctuary AI was founded in 2018 by a team that had previously built a company called Kindred AI, a Vancouver robotics venture focused on teleoperated warehouse robots and reinforcement learning — meaning Sanctuary’s founders arrived at the humanoid problem already having spent years wrestling with the harder, less glamorous question of how a machine actually learns to manipulate the physical world reliably, rather than starting from a blank slate chasing a viral demo. That lineage shows up directly in Carbon, the proprietary AI control system underpinning every Phoenix generation since 2023 — described by the company, in a line worth taking at face value, as the real star of the show, with the physical robot itself framed as merely the tool that lets Carbon’s intelligence act in the world. Most humanoid companies build a body first and hunt for the software to animate it. Sanctuary built the control system first, out of a prior company’s hard-won lessons about robotic manipulation, and grew the body around it.

What the balcony sees

From the balcony, Sanctuary AI closes a loop this series opened in its very first Introduction and has now, five rotations and forty postcards later, finally paid off properly. The company’s own strategic instincts — dexterity over spectacle, patents over publicity, factories before homes — read as a distinctly Canadian sensibility applied to an industry that has, in nearly every other country this series has examined, optimised hard for the opposite: the viral demo, the marathon record, the Spring Festival Gala performance watched by a billion people. Sanctuary bet, from a lab in Vancouver with a fraction of its rivals’ funding, that the hand — not the leg, not the face, not the gait — was the part of the human body actually worth replicating first, because the hand is what does the world’s unglamorous, repetitive, economically essential work.

Whether Wells is right that the home will be humanoid robotics’ very last frontier rather than its first is a question this series cannot yet answer, and the honest postcard says so plainly: 1X, Figure, and Tesla are all betting against him, with considerably more capital than Sanctuary has ever raised. But a company that has spent seven years quietly building what independent analysts consider one of the deepest patent portfolios in its entire industry, from Canada, without the noise, has earned the right to a hearing — and this series, true to the promise made in its very first paragraph, has finally given it one.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one came from the one address this series owed a visit from the start.
