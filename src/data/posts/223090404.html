For more than sixty years, the United States Department of Justice has sent its people to watch Americans vote. The programme was born of the civil rights era, and it had a narrow, honourable job. Monitors watched to see whether Black voters were being turned away, whether a voter in a wheelchair could get through the door, and whether someone who could not read the ballot, because of blindness, new English or simply never having been taught, got the help the law promised. They went where there had been trouble before. Local officials in places like Tucson considered them partners.

On Tuesday, November 3, 2026, the Justice Department will send about a thousand of them to polling places in every state. As Yvonne Wingett Sanchez, Madeleine Carlisle and Marie-Rose Sheinerman reported for The Atlantic on November 1, 2026, that is far more than most administrations of either party have deployed, and many of the people being sent are not the people who used to go.

This series has spent sixty-two instalments tracking the ways the administration has tried to put its hand on the 2026 election: the mail-ballot rule the courts struck down, the citizenship database, the ballot seizure in Fulton County, the FBI director who would not say plainly whether his agents would be at the polls. Most of those efforts needed a court’s permission, and most of them lost. The monitor programme needs nobody’s permission. It already exists. It has a sixty-year record. That is precisely what makes it useful.

The same programme, different people

Start with the case for calm, because it is real and deserves a fair hearing.

The Justice Department’s spokesperson, Kiersten V. Pels, told the reporters that monitors observe, do not interfere, and follow each state’s rules on access. She pointed out that none of the dozen-plus election officials the reporters interviewed could name a single voter who had been intimidated by a monitor. The magazine’s own review of roughly 200 public-records requests found that nearly all of the monitors’ visits during the summer primaries were professional. The department coordinated with local officials ahead of time. A department lawyer assured Utah’s lieutenant governor in writing that monitors would wear business casual, with no uniforms and no DOJ pins. At least two long-serving career attorneys in the Voting Section are helping run the operation, and former officials say their presence reassures them.

There is arithmetic on the side of calm, too. The country has close to 100,000 polling places. A thousand monitors works out to one for every hundred or so, and millions of ballots have already gone in by mail. Anna Baldwin, a former monitor now at the Campaign Legal Center, told the magazine she doubts the deployment will change many voters’ experience at all.

All of that is true. None of it answers the question the clerks are actually asking, which is not what the monitors will do on Tuesday, but what the department will do with what they saw on Wednesday.

Who is holding the clipboard

Here is what has changed.

For decades the department kept criminal prosecutors out of the monitor corps, for a reason a former Civil Rights Division official stated with admirable plainness: “we never wanted to do anything that might scare people.” This year the prosecutors are in. In Pima County, Arizona, Elections Director Constance Hargrove learned before the July primary that most of the six representatives coming her way were criminal prosecutors. She called the department’s coordinator and asked that they be reminded guns and weapons are not allowed in vote centres. It is a small detail. It is also not a request anyone needed to make in 2012.

Employees of the Bureau of Prisons, the ATF and the Drug Enforcement Administration are on the roster as well, according to a sworn declaration from Eric Neff, the acting chief of the Voting Section. The FBI and ICE are not. The department says these people are not being sent as law enforcement and that their inclusion is not new policy. Three former DOJ attorneys told the magazine it is a departure from long-held norms. The department also says monitors will not identify themselves as prosecutors, which is meant to reassure and does the opposite: a voter has no way of knowing who is standing by the door.

Then there are the names. Through public records and research by the legal nonprofit Democracy Forward, the reporters identified some of the more than eighty monitors sent out during the primaries. Among them were a lawyer who represented the Fulton County Republican Party in 2024 when it backed an election-board member who refused to certify a primary; a lawyer who represented the Trump campaign in its attempt to overturn Wisconsin’s 2020 result; and a senior adviser to Harmeet Dhillon, the assistant attorney general who runs the programme, whose professional profile listed him as a “Trump 47 Election Integrity Captain” for a Pennsylvania county in 2024. In Maricopa County, the primary roster included a violent-crime section chief, a public-corruption prosecutor, and a man who pleaded guilty in 2016 to unauthorized computer intrusions after hacking two Florida election websites.

That last one was too much even for a county attorney’s office led by a Republican. In the last week of October, Maricopa told the department it would not admit him, and asked for a written commitment that no monitor with a felony conviction, or any election-related conviction in any state, would be sent. By Saturday, October 31, the department said he no longer worked there and would not be a monitor on Election Day.

Dhillon herself has described the programme as a way to raise voter confidence and troubleshoot problems. In a July post on X, she also said it would help make sure no American’s vote is cancelled out by someone who should not be voting. Her division has sued dozens of states for voter rolls containing partial Social Security numbers, so far without success, in pursuit of noncitizen voting that study after study finds to be vanishingly rare. The department says its monitors will not question voters about citizenship at the polls. It also says that if monitors come across evidence of such crimes, it will use it.

Put those sentences side by side and the shift comes into focus. The old programme watched for people being kept from voting. The new one also watches for people who, in the department’s view, should not have voted at all. Same name on the door, same business casual. Different job.

A witness is not a monitor

The most revealing moment in the reporting came not at a polling place but in a recorded meeting in late October between DOJ attorneys and Arizona Secretary of State Adrian Fontes, a Democrat.

Fontes asked the obvious question. Why should he believe the monitors are nonpartisan, when the same department has filed suit to decertify Alaska’s Senate result if the Democrat wins? A lawsuit triggered by an outcome, he told them, puts the department in a compromised position. The attorneys said the monitors were, and would remain, nonpartisan.

Then Fontes asked the better question. Could monitors be called to testify in court, as witnesses, about what they saw? Neff called that speculative and said it would be assessed case by case. The department’s spokesperson later told the reporters this was nothing new, and that this is simply how the law works.

She is right. That is how the law works. And that is the problem in a sentence.

A monitor who watches a polling place to make sure a voter in a wheelchair gets in is producing something whose value ends when the polls close. A monitor who might later be a witness is producing evidence, and evidence is only useful after the fact: in a proceeding, before a judge, about a result someone wants changed. Put a prosecutor in the chair. Choose some of the people for their 2020 résumés. File a decertification suit in Alaska before a single vote is counted. Remind every state election official, in the closing days of October, to preserve its election materials. The parts assemble themselves into something nobody ever has to announce.

Part LI of this series argued that an investigation, unlike a rule, needs no court’s blessing to begin and no resolution to keep running. Part LXI described the pretext machinery being readied for after the vote. This is the piece that sits between them: people in the room on Tuesday who could supply the affidavits on Wednesday. The department need not intend that. It only needs to have built the capacity, and to have declined, when asked directly, to rule it out.

Cheyenne

What does it look like when the new programme meets an actual polling place? In most states, during the primaries, it looked like nothing much. Wyoming was the exception, and Wyoming is about as red as American states come.

On primary day in August, two monitors at a Cheyenne polling place asked poll workers questions a local official described as highly unusual and outside their purview: how the electronic poll books had been acquired, whether they could see the tabulators. The department disputes the procurement question and calls the rest routine. Workers described the pair as very assertive, walking around as though they owned the place. The official, who spoke anonymously because of safety concerns, observed that big men nobody was expecting could rattle a volunteer.

Wyoming’s Republican governor, Mark Gordon, criticized the visit at a public meeting. He no more liked the idea of Washington taking Wyoming’s votes, he said, than of it taking Wyoming’s guns. The department disagreed with that framing, and noted that Dhillon later had a cordial meeting with the governor, the state attorney general and a county clerk.

Nobody was arrested in Cheyenne. Nobody was turned away. By the department’s standard, nothing happened. But the questions asked were not about whether voters could get in. They were about the machinery, which is exactly what the 2020 claims were about. And a Republican governor, in a state Trump carried by more than forty points in 2024, noticed.

How the clerks are fighting back

The title of this series has always been a description, not a hope. Here is what it looks like in the first days of November.

Maricopa County refused a convicted election hacker and put its terms in writing. Hennepin County, Minnesota, required monitors to stay 100 feet from one voting location in August and will do so again on Tuesday; a video from the primary shows an election judge holding that line against two men in polo shirts. States are entitled to keep monitors outside, and some red states, Missouri and Florida among them, did exactly that in 2022. Fontes recorded his meeting and told the department he was doing so, which is the only reason anyone knows what Neff said. Boston has assigned workers to inventory ballots in case federal agents try to seize them, a procedure that would have sounded paranoid in 2022 and sounds merely prudent after Fulton County. And in Pima County, Hargrove has reminded her poll workers what any of them can do if a monitor, or anyone else, becomes disruptive: “You can always call 911.”

There is a cost to all of this, and it is the cost this series keeps returning to. A Boston official told the reporters that hours normally spent on the nuts and bolts of running an election have gone instead to answering volunteers who want to know what happens if the federal government intervenes. Wisconsin clerks have received hundreds of emails asking the same thing. In Charleston County, South Carolina, elections director Isaac Cramer said that when monitors visited a primary runoff this summer, false conspiracy theories sprang up online almost at once, and that the mere news that the DOJ is coming can bring fear.

That is friction, and friction is the point. It does not require a single voter to be intimidated. It requires clerks to be distracted, volunteers to be nervous, and the whole public conversation about Election Day to include the federal government as a possible adversary. The department can truthfully say no one was harmed. The harm is in the hours, the emails, and the doubt.

What to watch for

On Tuesday, November 3, the monitors will mostly stand quietly in business casual in places where nothing happens, and the department will point to that quiet as vindication. Fine. Let it.

The test comes afterward. Watch whether monitors’ observations turn up in court filings, in Alaska or anywhere else. Watch whether “evidence uncovered while monitoring” becomes a press release. Watch whether any of the people with 2020 résumés appear as declarants. And watch whether the courts, which have been this series’ steadiest guardrail, treat a monitor’s affidavit as observation or as advocacy.

The reporters ended with a sentence that should trouble anyone who remembers why the programme was created: a tool meant to protect the right to vote is now seen, by the people who actually run elections, as a way to undermine it. That perception is not paranoia. It is a reasonable reading of who was hired, what they said they were looking for, and what the department answered when asked straight out. The clerks are not waiting to find out. They are measuring off the hundred feet.
