There is a particular kind of silence that settles over a courthouse when a judge is taking her time, and by early September 2026 that silence had settled over Fort Pierce, Florida. On July 31, 2026, briefing closed on the single question that has consumed Florida v. OpenAI since the moment OpenAI’s lawyers pulled it out of Highlands County Circuit Court: does this case belong in front of a jury in rural Florida, or in front of U.S. District Judge Aileen Cannon in the Southern District’s Fort Pierce division. Five weeks later, there is still no answer.

Readers of Part III will recall how we got here. Attorney General James Uthmeier filed an 83-page, ten-count complaint on June 1, 2026, alleging that OpenAI and Sam Altman personally knew ChatGPT was dangerous to children and marketed it anyway — negligence, gross negligence, strict liability for design defect, fraudulent misrepresentation, and a request that the court formally declare ChatGPT a public nuisance in the State of Florida. Uthmeier pled Florida law only, and Paragraph 30 of the complaint goes out of its way to disclaim any federal cause of action. On July 2, OpenAI removed the case to federal court anyway, arguing that Count IV — which cites the federal Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act as one basis for a state consumer-protection claim — pulls the whole action into federal jurisdiction. Florida moved to remand on July 10, calling the removal “preposterous.” Then came the wait.

The argument nobody disputes matters

Strip away the procedural language and the fight is simple: OpenAI does not want twelve people in Highlands County deciding whether Sam Altman personally owes damages for what the state calls a defective, dangerous product. It wants Aileen Cannon — the Trump-appointed judge who dismissed the federal classified-documents case against the president and whose rulings reporters have repeatedly noted tend to align with the administration’s interests — deciding it instead, likely without a jury, and under a body of law less hospitable to a public-nuisance theory than Florida’s own courts might be.

OpenAI’s removal brief cites three precedents to make its case that this kind of maneuver is unremarkable: New Mexico v. Meta, California v. TikTok, and New Jersey v. Discord — three instances of states suing platforms under state law, only to have the company remove to federal court. OpenAI cites them narrowly, for a narrow proposition: in each case, the federal judge declined to award the state its attorneys’ fees, because removal wasn’t found to be “objectively unreasonable.” Florida’s remand motion asks for fees anyway, arguing OpenAI removed the case for one reason only — delay.

It is worth sitting with the fact that OpenAI chose to cite New Mexico v. Meta as its supporting precedent. Because if you follow that case a little further than OpenAI’s brief wants you to, you find the outcome Florida is actually hoping for.

What happened when New Mexico got its case back

New Mexico’s attorney general sued Meta on state-law grounds over child-safety failures on its platforms, and unlike Florida’s case, that fight over forum resolved cleanly: the state-law claims stayed in state court. This past month, a New Mexico jury delivered its verdict — a $375 million jury penalty, layered under a separate $567 million public-nuisance abatement order the court imposed on top of it. Combined, the state extracted close to a billion dollars in penalties from a platform found to have known its product endangered children and to have done too little about it.

That is not a footnote. That is the entire argument for why OpenAI is fighting this hard over which courthouse hears the case. New Mexico shows what a state jury does with a public-nuisance theory once it actually reaches one: it does not split hairs about transformative technology or novel questions of COPPA preemption. It looks at the allegations, it looks at the company’s own internal safety warnings, and it writes a number with nine figures in it. Uthmeier’s complaint asks for exactly that kind of finding against OpenAI and asks for it by name — a declaration that distributing ChatGPT to Florida’s children constitutes a public nuisance, plus a permanent injunction on under-13 data collection, plus damages, plus liability that reaches Altman himself.

Judge Cannon does not need to rule against Florida to hand OpenAI a win. She can accomplish most of what OpenAI wants simply by taking her time. Every week the remand motion sits unresolved is a week OpenAI’s exposure stays parked in a federal courtroom with no jury trial date, no state-court discovery schedule, and no Highlands County panel anywhere in view. Delay, in this posture, is not a failure to litigate. It is the litigation strategy.

The two other cases in OpenAI’s trio deserve a closer look than the company’s brief gives them, because neither one is the clean precedent OpenAI wants it to be. In California v. TikTok, the state’s attorney general sued over child-safety design choices under California’s own consumer-protection statute; TikTok removed on grounds involving federal communications law, and while the federal court ultimately kept the case, it did so narrowly enough that California’s core state-law claims survived largely intact. New Jersey v. Discord followed a similar arc — removal on a federal-preemption theory, a court willing to hear the case, but no wholesale gutting of the state’s underlying allegations. OpenAI cites both only for the narrow point about attorneys’ fees, and that narrowness is itself worth noticing: if either case had actually validated OpenAI’s jurisdictional theory on the merits, its brief would say so. It doesn’t, because it can’t.

Uthmeier’s criminal probe, still sitting quietly in the background

It is worth remembering, amid all this procedural maneuvering, that the civil complaint is not the only exposure OpenAI faces out of Florida. The Office of Statewide Prosecution opened a criminal investigation in April 2026 tied to the Florida State University shooting — the case in which prosecutors say the gunman used ChatGPT to help plan an attack that left two people dead, asking the chatbot about optimal timing, location, and weapon selection. Uthmeier has been blunt about the theory behind that probe: if a human being had walked a shooter through those same questions, prosecutors would be looking at conspiracy charges. Nothing about the remand fight touches that criminal investigation directly, but the two tracks feed each other. Every fact Florida surfaces in civil discovery — assuming the case ever reaches discovery in a forum with teeth — becomes fodder for a prosecutor deciding whether corporate conduct crossed from negligence into something a grand jury might recognize as culpable. A federal judge sitting on a jurisdictional motion for two months is not just delaying a civil verdict. She may be delaying the evidentiary record a separate prosecutor is watching just as closely.

The pattern the Tumbler Ridge suits keep confirming

While Fort Pierce waits, a separate and much larger body of litigation against OpenAI has kept growing three thousand miles from Florida, and it keeps corroborating the same institutional pattern Uthmeier’s complaint alleges. This week, survivors of the February 10, 2026, mass shooting at Tumbler Ridge Secondary School in British Columbia filed thirty new lawsuits against OpenAI in federal court in San Francisco, expanding on the seven suits families filed in April. The claim at the center of all of them: OpenAI banned the shooter’s ChatGPT account in June 2025 — eight months before she killed her mother, her eleven-year-old brother, five students, and an educational assistant — and never told the police.

What is new, and what belongs in this series’ running account of how OpenAI’s leadership handles its own internal alarms, is the detail the new filings surface from reporting by the Wall Street Journal: members of OpenAI’s internal safety team wanted to refer the banned account to the Royal Canadian Mounted Police, and the company’s leadership overruled them. That is a different story than “OpenAI missed the warning signs.” It is the story this whole docket has been tracking since Part II — a company whose own safety apparatus flags a risk, and whose executives decide the business cost of acting on it outweighs the cost of staying silent. Altman’s April 23, 2026, letter apologizing to the Tumbler Ridge community, which we cited when this sub-series began, reads differently once you know his own staff had asked him to do something before the fact rather than apologize after it.

British Columbia’s premier, David Eby, added one more data point this week: the new suits were filed only after OpenAI walked away from a mediation process the company had previously engaged in good faith. “It underlines why these companies cannot be trusted,” Eby said this week, adding that they cannot be trusted either to regulate themselves or to be taken at their word. Whatever calculation led OpenAI’s leadership to withdraw from mediation with grieving families in Canada is not a fact pattern Judge Cannon will see in the remand briefing. But it is precisely the fact pattern Florida’s complaint says defines how this company behaves once safety and liability start to conflict with growth.

The plaintiffs’ own language captures the same accusation Florida is making, translated into a different courtroom. One of the new Tumbler Ridge filings states plainly that Sam Altman and his leadership team understood what silence would mean for the town, and were more concerned with what disclosure would mean for the company — because warning the police once would have obligated OpenAI to warn police every time its own safety team flagged a user planning real-world violence. That is not a description of an isolated lapse. It is a description of a policy: silence as the default, treated as a liability-management decision rather than a safety one. Whether that policy amounts to actionable concealment under Florida’s Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act is a question Judge Cannon will eventually have to let someone decide. The Tumbler Ridge filings simply make clear that the underlying conduct Florida alleges is not a one-off. It is the company’s institutional posture, tested now in courtrooms on two different sides of a border.

The wait, and what it is actually measuring

None of this changes the narrow legal question sitting on Cannon’s desk — whether one COPPA-adjacent count in an otherwise Florida-law complaint is enough to keep the whole case in federal court. But it changes how we should read the silence while we wait for her answer. Every additional lawsuit against OpenAI, in every jurisdiction, arguing some version of “the company knew and said nothing,” makes the stakes of where Florida’s case gets heard larger rather than smaller. New Mexico has already shown a state jury what it does with that argument once it reaches one. Fort Pierce is where we find out whether Florida gets the chance to make the same argument to a jury of its own — or whether seven weeks of silence quietly becomes the first and most decisive ruling in the case.
