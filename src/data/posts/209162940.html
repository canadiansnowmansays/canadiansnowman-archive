There is a particular kind of silence that only a lawyer can engineer, and on Wednesday, July 29, 2026, Anthony Fauci sat inside it for nearly three hours. Twenty-one words, repeated by one count more than a hundred times: on the advice of counsel, I respectfully decline to answer based upon my rights under the Fifth Amendment. Around him, the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee performed the loudest kind of theatre — Senator Josh Hawley calling him a megalomaniac and a liar, Senator Rick Scott calling him a villain who forced people to die alone, Senator Rand Paul, the hearing’s chairman and its author, promising the room that the committee would vote next week on whether to hold the witness in contempt. The man at the center of it all said nothing, over and over, until the room ran out of things to accuse him of.

I want to be honest about where I sit in this. I was, during the worst of it, a Fauci admirer — one of the many who found something steadying in his Tuesday-briefing cadence, in the particular way he could say we don’t know yet without making it sound like weakness. I have not changed my mind about that. But I have also come to believe that admiration, if it is going to be worth anything, has to survive contact with the parts of a man that are less flattering — his vanity, his taste for the spotlight, the diary entries in which the death toll and the Barbra Streisand phone calls sit on the same page, sometimes in the same paragraph. A hagiography is not a biography. This series is an attempt at the latter.

So let me say plainly what the next several essays intend to do, because the temptation with Fauci — on both sides of the room he now finds himself testifying to — is to flatten him into a symbol. To the MAHA right, he is the sun god of science, punished at last for four decades of unaccountable empire-building. To much of the institutional left, he is the last honest man in a government that stopped listening to honest men. Both of those are usable myths. Neither is a person. The actual man — Jesuit-schooled, workaholic, prone to a temper his own former deputies still describe carefully, capable of both PEPFAR and a diary entry marveling at bobbleheads made in his likeness — is more interesting than either myth, and more instructive.

Here is the shape of what happened this week, briefly, because it is the reason this series exists now rather than at some other moment. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who has spent years accusing Fauci of colluding with pharmaceutical companies and Bill Gates, discovered a more than 1,100-page diary Fauci had kept on a government-linked computer from December 2019 through his retirement at the end of 2022. Kennedy handed it to Senator Paul, who has pursued Fauci for the better part of a decade over the theory that gain-of-function research funded by Fauci’s institute leaked from a laboratory in Wuhan and caused the pandemic. Paul released the diary publicly last weekend, and the press — this newspaper included — spent days combing through it for the parts that would either indict or embarrass its author. There was plenty of both. Fauci logged conversations with Joan Baez, Julia Roberts, and Barbra Streisand alongside running death tolls. He noted, more soberly, his own uncertainty in January 2020 about whether the virus had jumped from an animal at a Wuhan market or something stranger, and his convening of a call with eleven virologists, most of whom were not sure themselves. On Wednesday, subpoenaed and unprotected by the pre-emptive pardon Joe Biden had given him on his last day in office — a pardon that covers everything before January 20, 2025, and nothing said under oath since — Fauci took the only defense his lawyers thought he had left, and stopped talking.

It is worth sitting for a moment on just how strange that exchange over the tie actually was. Hawley did not ask the question because he wanted to know the answer. He asked it because he knew, with total certainty, that Fauci’s lawyers had instructed him to give the identical Fifth Amendment response to every question in the room, regardless of subject, and that the resulting loop — a serious constitutional protection deployed against a question about neckwear — would look absurd on camera. It did look absurd. That was the point. The hearing was not designed to extract information; the diary release the weekend before had already made clear that no new information was forthcoming. It was designed to produce a clip, and it succeeded at that, several times over, regardless of what it accomplished as oversight.

It would be easy to write a single outraged essay about the indignity of an 85-year-old man being asked what color tie he was wearing, and move on. I don’t think that does justice to either the moment or the man. What deserves the longer treatment is the fact that almost everything being relitigated this week — the masking reversal, the school closures, the funding of research in Wuhan, the fame that swallowed him — has a real history behind it, a history that started decades before Covid and that most of his current interrogators were not paying attention to when it happened. Fauci’s career runs from a Jesuit high school in Manhattan through nearly forty years running the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases, through a genuinely adversarial and genuinely transformative fight with AIDS activists — the playwright Larry Kramer once called him, in an open letter, a murderer, and in 1990 the group ACT UP marched onto the NIH campus carrying a casket addressed to him — that Fauci did not simply survive but eventually turned into a working alliance, inviting the same activists who had vilified him to help shape the direction of AIDS research. It runs through PEPFAR, a program credited with preventing something on the order of 25 million deaths, through service to seven presidents of both parties who somehow never learned how he voted, up to and including a Presidential Medal of Freedom from George W. Bush and praise from Barack Obama as a once-in-a-century public health leader. That is the record his critics are asking the public to forget in favor of three hours about a diary. I don’t intend to let them.

It is also worth remembering that Fauci’s current fall from official favor was not gradual. Within days of taking office in January 2025, the Trump administration stripped the government-funded security detail he had relied on since 2020, after menacing threats against him and his family became frequent enough that his staff began flagging the worst of his mail for a security team. His image was removed from a mural on the NIH campus that spring. Officials went on to substantially dismantle PEPFAR itself — the program he is proudest of — and to force out much of the leadership at his old institute, including the woman who succeeded him as its director, Jeanne Marrazzo, whom Kennedy personally fired after she filed a whistleblower complaint. None of that happened because new evidence emerged. It happened because the administration had decided, as one of Fauci’s former colleagues put it to The Atlantic, that anything with his name on it was worth destroying.

Nor, in fairness, do I intend to write past his flaws to get there. As Katherine J. Wu reported for The Atlantic this week, seven separate diary entries mention a prolonged standing ovation, and Fauci's institute, by the account of the woman who eventually succeeded him, functioned as something close to a personal empire that has not survived his departure from it particularly well His former colleagues describe a boss with what one called a very Italian temper, capable of shredding a subordinate behind a closed door. He wanted, by his own emailed request after leaving office, to know what would happen to an unhung portrait of himself. None of that erases the AIDS work or PEPFAR. It also isn’t nothing. A fair accounting holds both.

What this series will not do is pretend that the hearing itself was a legitimate forum for adjudicating any of it. A three-hour spectacle in which one side asks a man what day of the week it is, knowing he cannot answer, is not oversight. It is a show trial with better lighting, and Senator Andy Kim was the only person in the room who seemed to notice that the format itself — not just its target — was corroding something in how the country conducts its arguments with itself. I happen to agree with him. I also happen to think the Trump administration’s broader campaign against Fauci’s legacy — the removal of his image from a mural at the NIH, the stripping of his security detail within days of the pardon, the dismantling of much of PEPFAR, the ouster of his successor and his former adviser’s indictment — reads less like accountability than like vengeance dressed in the language of it. A man who spent decades trying to stay above politics has ended up, entirely without his consent, as one of its most fought-over symbols. That is worth examining on its own terms, separate from whatever the diary does or doesn’t prove about January 2020.

Part of what makes this hard to write about fairly is that Fauci’s most extreme critics and his most devoted admirers were, for a while, arguing about the same set of facts and arriving at opposite religions. On votive candles sold online during the pandemic’s worst months, artists ringed his gray hair with a golden halo under the words In Fauci We Trust. Joe Rogan, on his podcast, called him a monster; Elon Musk called him a freaking demon; Robert Kennedy Jr.’s 2021 book on him ran to five hundred pages and accused him of engineering something close to a coup against Western democracy. Fauci did not always resist the deification on the other side — he told MSNBC in 2021 that attacks on him amounted to attacks on science itself, a line that, however understandable in the moment, was also the moment a great many otherwise persuadable Americans decided he had confused himself with the institution he represented. Senator Hawley’s line this week, calling him a man who wanted to be “the sun god of science,” was cruel, but it was not invented from nothing. It borrowed a real vulnerability and sharpened it into an accusation. Distinguishing between the parts of that caricature that are fair and the parts that are propaganda is, I think, the actual work of a series like this one — not defending a saint, and not conceding a villain, but describing an actual man who spent four decades acquiring exactly the kind of institutional trust that makes a person very useful to have on your side during a pandemic, and very easy to burn in effigy once the pandemic no longer needs a face.

So: nine parts and an epilogue, roughly. We will start where the record actually starts — a Jesuit education in Manhattan and the unglamorous, pre-fame decades at the NIH — and move forward from there, through the AIDS years, through PEPFAR, through the seven-presidents run of quiet bipartisan trust, into the pandemic itself and the fame that both made and unmade him, and finally back to this week's hearing and what it means that a government can subpoena a man, strip him of every legal protection he thought he had, and then be surprised when his lawyers tell him to stop talking. The contempt vote came a week later, along predictable party lines, and is unlikely to go anywhere given the sixty votes it would need in a Senate that Republicans control by a much smaller margin — a fitting first verdict on a process built more for cameras than for consequence. I do know that the man being investigated built, over half a century, one of the more consequential careers in American public health, and that the sum of that career deserves better than a diary and three hours of Senate theatre as its final word. This series is my attempt to give it something closer to that.
