Good evening, and welcome to the forecast.

We begin, as we increasingly do, with a closure. The state fair — I will not say which one, because by the time you read this it could be any of them — was shut down yesterday on account of heat. Not rain. Not a threat. Heat. The mercury climbed past the number at which a reasonable jurisdiction decides that funnel cake, prize hogs, and a Ferris wheel are no longer worth a human life, and so they turned off the Tilt-A-Whirl, sent everyone home, and locked the gate on the whole enterprise until the air agrees to behave.

I want to be clear that this was the correct decision. When it is too hot to safely operate a deep fryer next to a livestock barn, you close the fair. This is what a functioning society does: it reads the thermometer, it does the arithmetic, and when the number says stop, it stops. There is something almost moving about it — a whole institution, all that noise and grease and joy, possessing the humility to admit that today the sky has won, and there is no shame in going home.

Which brings me to the one place in America that has never, in living memory, closed for heat.

Let us go to the national map.

Highs across the Midwest were in the range of 104, 105, with a heat index — that lovely modern number, the temperature it feels like, which is really just the honesty setting on the thermostat — pushing toward the deadly. The Plains are baking. The South is the South. The Pacific Northwest, which was designed by God and the last ice age to be cool and drizzly, is now recording temperatures it was never issued the infrastructure to survive, its residents Googling “what is air conditioning” in a region that historically considered it a Californian affectation.

And then we come to the District of Columbia, where the forecast is, as ever, the same.

Hot. Hot with a chance of hotter. A stationary front that never moves through, because the front is not weather — the front is the place itself. No relief expected. None ever is. Other cities cool off at night; the capital generates its own climate, a self-sustaining thermal event, a swamp that every incoming administration promises to drain and every outgoing one leaves a little deeper, because you cannot drain a thing by campaigning against it. The readings out of Washington do not follow the season. The readings out of Washington follow the outrage cycle, which runs hot year-round and hottest of all in the small hours, when the feeds come alive and the temperature ticks up two degrees — not because of any one account, though there are famous ones, but because the whole machine is built to run warm. Cooling off does not raise money. Equilibrium does not win primaries. The fever is the business model, and the business is bipartisan.

Now, some of you have written in to ask about the algae, and I will address it briefly, because the algae is a weather story whether we like it or not.

I have written before about the bloom — the green scum that turned a reflecting pool into a science experiment — and I will not relitigate it here, except to note, as your forecaster, the meteorological principle involved: heat does not create the bloom. Heat finishes it. The nutrients have to be there first, the runoff, the years of loosened rules and unenforced protections, the quiet accumulation of everything nobody wanted to pay to prevent. The pollution loads the water. The heat is merely the match. And so when the green arrives, the heat steps forward to take the credit, the way heat does — it was me, I’m very powerful, biggest bloom anyone’s ever seen — and the patient observer has to point out that the heat only lit what a decade of ineptitude had carefully arranged into kindling. Mother Nature struck the match. But somebody built the woodpile, and left the matches out, and fired the man whose job was to keep them apart.

Which brings me to the second heat source, the one the satellites miss.

We are, this summer, in a midterm cycle — an off-year, technically, a fallow season, the part of the calendar when the machinery of American politics is supposed to idle. It does not idle. It has forgotten how. The fundraising apparatus runs at full throttle in July of a year with no presidential race, the super PACs feeding, the appeals arriving at all hours — URGENT, FINAL NOTICE, the deadline is TONIGHT — deadlines that renew themselves the way a seventy-two-hour ceasefire renews itself, forever, on a rolling basis. All that money moving, all that friction, all those servers running hot to tell you the end is nigh unless you chip in $15 before midnight: it generates heat of its own. A thermal signature you could track from orbit if you knew to look for it.

Waka waka waka. Bipartisan fever, since forever. The fair closed for heat. This never does

Picture them working in tandem, if you can bear it. Mother Nature above, cranking the mercury. And below her, gobbling through the neighborhood dot by dot, the campaign-finance machine — Super PACman, I have taken to calling him, that relentless yellow mouth, waka-waka-waka across the electoral map, consuming everything in his path and turning it into pure radiant heat, powered by small-dollar dread and never, ever pausing for the season. And here is the thing about his appetite: it is perfectly nonpartisan. He does not care which side you are terrified of. The red dots and the blue dots taste exactly the same to him, and he eats them on the same night — your inbox this evening holds a FINAL NOTICE from the left and a DEADLINE TONIGHT from the right, both engineered by the same species of consultant, both running the same play, both warming the same overheated room. The machine is not loyal to a party. It is loyal to the fever, because the fever is what pays. Between the two of them — the sky and the SuperPAC, the front that won’t move and the fund that won’t rest — they have generated, this summer, a combined thermal load that a humble state fair simply could not survive.

Too hot to handle. The fair read the number and went home. The machinery read the same number and asked if you could give monthly.

So here is your extended forecast.

For most of the country: dangerous heat, easing eventually, because that is what heat does everywhere except one place — it breaks, it passes, the front moves through, the evening comes. Close the fair, drink water, check on your neighbors, and wait for the relief that, for you, will actually arrive.

For the District: no change. Continued hot, indefinitely, with the self-generated warmth of a permanent campaign and a permanent grievance keeping the overnight lows well above anything the rest of the map will see. The swamp remains undrained, by this administration and the last one and the one before. The servers remain warm. The deadline remains tonight, and tonight, and tonight, in both colors, forever.

And from up here, on the cool side of the border, where I file this forecast in a Toronto that has its own problems but has not yet learned to run a fever year-round — a small, neighborly observation. Everywhere in America now has a temperature at which it admits the sky has won and sends everyone home to lie down. The fairground has one. The ballpark has one. The school has one. Every good and grease-scented institution in the republic knows the number at which it stops.

Except the one that sets the thermostat for all the others. That one has no such number. That one runs hot on purpose, generates its own weather, and calls the heat a sign of strength.

Stay cool out there. Somebody has to.
