The previous essay in this series left the League of Nations orphaned at birth — built by an American president whose own Senate refused to let the United States join it, and structured around a Council whose most basic decisions required the unanimous agreement of every member state represented, not merely the great powers among them. An institution with those two features had, by the early 1930s, an obvious weakness waiting to be discovered by whichever government decided to test it first. That government was Japan, the year was 1931, and the location was a stretch of railway track outside a city in northeastern China called Mukden.

Manchuria: the first great power says no

On 18 September 1931, a junior Japanese officer detonated a small charge of dynamite near a section of track belonging to Japan’s South Manchuria Railway. The explosion barely damaged the line — a train passed over it minutes later — but the Japanese Army, which had planned the incident itself as a pretext, used it to justify a full military occupation of Manchuria, the resource-rich Chinese region bordering Japan’s existing colonial holdings in Korea. Within months, Japan had installed a puppet state, Manchukuo, with the deposed last emperor of China, Puyi, as its nominal ruler.

China appealed to the League, and the League did roughly what its Covenant obliged it to do: in December 1931 it appointed a Commission of Enquiry, chaired by the British peer Victor Bulwer-Lytton, to investigate on the ground and recommend a resolution consistent with the Covenant’s principles. The Lytton Commission arrived in early 1932 and delivered its report that October — a carefully hedged document that acknowledged Japan’s security concerns in the region while concluding, unambiguously, that Japan’s military action had exceeded any reasonable claim of self-defence and that Manchukuo could not be recognised as a legitimate, independent state. Notably, the report was not a simple demand for unconditional Japanese withdrawal; it proposed a compromise — autonomy for Manchuria under nominal Chinese sovereignty, with special provisions safeguarding Japan’s existing economic interests in the region — precisely the kind of split-the-difference outcome a body built around consensus tends to produce even when investigating a fairly unambiguous act of aggression. Japan rejected even this softened settlement outright. When the League Assembly voted in February 1933 to adopt the report’s findings anyway, the vote was 42 to 1 — every member state present except Japan itself. It was, on paper, as close to unanimous international condemnation as the interwar world ever produced.

It did not matter. The Japanese delegation, led by Yōsuke Matsuoka, walked out of the Assembly chamber as the vote was being taken. On 27 March 1933, Japan gave formal notice of its withdrawal from the League altogether, and continued occupying Manchuria for the next twelve years, until its defeat in 1945. The League’s response to a member state simply leaving in response to a resolution it disliked was: nothing. There was no mechanism to compel Japan to stay, no economic sanction regime imposed with any real teeth, and no military response contemplated by any of the powers capable of mounting one. The lesson available to any government paying attention — and several were — was that the League could investigate an act of aggression thoroughly, condemn it almost unanimously, and still be powerless to reverse a single metre of the ground that had been seized.

The one power that might, in a different world, have applied real pressure sat outside the League entirely — for reasons the first essay in this series covered in some detail. The United States, non-member since 1920, responded to Manchuria with what became known as the Stimson Doctrine, announced by Secretary of State Henry Stimson in January 1932: a declaration that Washington would not recognise any territorial changes brought about by force, including Manchukuo. It was a moral position with no enforcement mechanism attached to it whatsoever, articulated by a government that had specifically declined the one collective-security structure that might have given non-recognition some actual weight. The absence flagged at the end of the previous essay was not an abstraction; it was, in the Manchurian crisis, a government issuing high-minded statements from entirely outside the institution built to act on them.

Abyssinia: sanctions with a hole in the middle

If Manchuria demonstrated that the League could not act against a great power on the other side of the world, Abyssinia demonstrated something more damning still: that it could not act decisively even against a middling European power, in a crisis its own leading members had months of advance warning to prepare for. Tensions between Italy and Abyssinia (modern Ethiopia) had been building since a border skirmish at Walwal in December 1934, and when Benito Mussolini launched a full invasion on 3 October 1935, the League moved with genuine speed by its own standards: on 18 November 1935, fifty member states voted to impose economic sanctions on Italy, with only Italy itself opposed and three small states abstaining.

The sanctions had a hole in the middle large enough to drive an army through it. Oil was deliberately excluded from the sanctioned goods list, out of fear among British and French policymakers that cutting off Italy’s fuel supply might provoke Mussolini into a wider war they were not prepared to fight — precisely the kind of collective-security commitment the League existed, on paper, to make credible. The exclusion mattered enormously in practice, because the one major producer capable of filling any gap the League’s members did create sat, once again, entirely outside the institution: American oil exports to Italy roughly tripled over the course of the sanctions period, as US firms, unconstrained by an embargo Washington had no obligation to observe, sold Mussolini’s war machine the fuel Geneva’s own members had declined to withhold. A body attempting collective punishment of an aggressor state cannot function as intended when the world’s largest oil producer is not a party to the collective in the first place, and Rome’s war planners knew it well enough to factor American supply into their calculations from the outset. Worse followed in December 1935, when it emerged that the British and French foreign ministers, Samuel Hoare and Pierre Laval, had secretly negotiated a plan — the Hoare-Laval Pact — that would have ceded roughly two-thirds of Abyssinian territory to Italy in exchange for ending the war. The plan leaked to the press before it could be implemented, producing a public outcry severe enough to force Hoare’s resignation, but the damage to the League’s credibility was already done: the two powers most capable of enforcing its own sanctions regime had been caught, in private, trying to hand the aggressor most of what he wanted anyway.

Italy completed its conquest by May 1936, formally annexing Ethiopia that month. On 30 June 1936, the exiled Emperor Haile Selassie addressed the League Assembly in Geneva in person — the first head of state ever to do so — describing, in detail, the Italian army’s use of chemical weapons against Ethiopian soldiers and civilians in violation of a prohibition the international community had agreed to after the First World War. He closed with a warning that has outlived almost everything else said in Geneva that decade: “It is us today. It will be you tomorrow.” Four days later, on 4 July 1936, the League voted to lift the sanctions on Italy altogether, formally acknowledging that the effort to reverse the annexation had been abandoned.

The Rhineland: nobody even votes

The remilitarisation of the Rhineland, eight months earlier, had already shown the pattern that Abyssinia would only confirm. On 7 March 1936, German troops crossed the Rhine into the demilitarised zone that both the Treaty of Versailles and the Locarno Treaties of 1925 — an agreement Germany itself had signed voluntarily, alongside Britain, France, and Italy as guarantors — guaranteed would remain free of German military forces. It was a calculated gamble on Hitler’s part; he later admitted privately that it was one of the most nerve-wracking moments of his political career, and had ordered his troops to withdraw immediately if France mobilised in response. France, in the middle of an election campaign and governed by a caretaker cabinet reluctant to act unilaterally without British backing, did not mobilise. Britain, whose foreign secretary Anthony Eden argued in a private memorandum the day after the crossing that the matter should be referred to the League Council rather than answered with force — writing that Britain “must discourage any military action by France against Germany,” since demanding withdrawal without being prepared to enforce that demand militarily would only invite humiliation — declined as well. The matter was, formally, passed along to Geneva — and then quietly allowed to lapse, since neither major European power was willing to be the one to act on whatever the Council might recommend.

What is notable about the Rhineland case, set against Manchuria and Abyssinia, is that the League’s unanimity problem barely had to surface at all. There was no serious attempt to build a coalition for sanctions, no commission of enquiry, no Assembly vote comparable to the 42-to-1 condemnation of Japan. The powers with the standing and the military capacity to enforce the Locarno guarantees they had personally signed simply chose not to, and the machinery of collective security that existed on paper was never meaningfully engaged. If Manchuria showed that unanimous condemnation changes nothing without enforcement behind it, and Abyssinia showed that even a sanctions regime with fifty votes behind it can be hollowed out by the exclusion of the one commodity that mattered, the Rhineland showed something more basic still: that the whole apparatus depends, at every stage, on the voluntary willingness of powerful states to use it, and that willingness cannot be assumed.

The membership rolls themselves tell the same story in miniature. Germany, which had joined the League in 1926 as part of the post-Locarno rapprochement, withdrew the same year Hitler took power, in October 1933 — six months after Japan. Italy, having weathered the Abyssinia sanctions largely intact, gave notice of its own withdrawal in December 1937. The Soviet Union, admitted only in 1934 in part as a Western hedge against exactly the kind of German rearmament the Rhineland crisis then confirmed, was expelled from the League in December 1939 — the organisation’s only formal expulsion in its entire history — for invading Finland, a decision that at least demonstrated the Council could still act decisively when the aggressor in question happened to be a state nobody was afraid of provoking. By the time the Second World War actually began, the League’s roster of great powers willing to lend the institution any real weight had thinned to almost nothing, and the war it had been built twenty years earlier to prevent arrived anyway.

What the League’s architects got right, in reverse

There is a temptation to read these three episodes as proof that international institutions are simply powerless against determined aggressors, full stop, and to carry that conclusion forward into every essay in this series that follows. That would be too easy, and it would also be wrong, because the specific mechanism of failure in each of these three cases was not vague or mysterious — it was the unanimity rule this series’ introduction flagged at the outset, operating exactly as its critics warned it would. A Council that needed everyone in the room to agree could be blocked by the one government whose actions were actually in question, whether by that government simply voting no, walking out, or — as with Rhineland — never being seriously asked to answer for anything in the first place. The League’s failure was not a failure of good intentions, of which Geneva had a considerable supply throughout the 1930s. It was a failure of design, and it is the specific design flaw the UN’s founders would spend considerable energy trying to fix a decade later, when they sat down at Dumbarton Oaks and Yalta to decide how a new organisation’s Council should actually reach a decision.

It is also worth naming the connective tissue between the three crises directly, because contemporaries at the time did not always see it, even though it now looks obvious in hindsight. Each failure taught the next aggressor something specific and transferable. Japan learned in 1931–33 that a member state could occupy territory, withdraw from the League when condemned for it, and suffer no meaningful consequence. Italy, watching closely, drew the reasonable inference in 1935 that a sanctions regime assembled after the fact, missing its most important commodity, backed by powers more interested in a quiet diplomatic settlement than a costly confrontation, was not a serious deterrent. And Germany, watching both, concluded by March 1936 that the two European powers most invested in the postwar settlement would not risk war to defend even an agreement they had personally negotiated and Germany itself had signed a decade earlier. None of these three governments needed to coordinate with one another to learn the same lesson from the same institution in the space of five years. The lesson was simply there to be read, in Geneva’s own record, by anyone paying attention — and by 1936, everyone who mattered was paying very close attention indeed.

Their fix, as the next essay in this series covers, was to stop pretending every member state carried equal weight and instead build the inequality into the Charter openly: five great powers, an explicit veto each, and a frank acknowledgement that an institution honest about who actually held power might outlast one that was not. Whether that produced an organisation any more capable of restraining a great power that had simply decided not to be restrained — or merely moved the same underlying problem from an unspoken unanimity requirement to an explicit one — is the question the rest of this series exists to answer.
