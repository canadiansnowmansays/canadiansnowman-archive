Every mechanism this series has examined so far for building and defending a throne has involved some combination of administration, theology, and force — the granary ledger, the pharaoh’s divinity, the Mandate’s omens, the legion’s loyalty. Medieval Europe developed a mechanism that required none of these in the short term and produced, over several centuries, one of the largest and most improbable concentrations of territory ever assembled by a single family: marriage. Where Rome expanded through conquest and administration, and China’s dynasties rose and fell through battlefield victories certified after the fact by Heaven’s supposed verdict, the Habsburgs came to rule over Austria, Spain, the Netherlands, parts of Italy, and vast stretches of the Americas largely by managing, across roughly two centuries, to marry correctly, generation after generation, until half a continent belonged to a single bloodline almost by accident.

The conditions that made this possible were specific to medieval Europe’s political fragmentation, and it’s worth understanding why marriage became such a powerful tool there in a way it never quite did in Egypt or China. After Rome’s western empire collapsed in the fifth century, Europe did not consolidate into a small number of large, centrally administered states the way China repeatedly did after its own periods of fragmentation. It splintered instead into a genuinely bewildering patchwork of kingdoms, duchies, counties, prince-bishoprics, and free cities, layered with overlapping and often contradictory feudal obligations, in which a single noble family might owe loyalty to several different overlords simultaneously depending on which specific piece of land was in question. The Holy Roman Empire, nominally the successor to Rome’s western authority and formally established under Otto the Great in 962, never functioned as a unified state in any sense Augustus or a Han emperor would have recognized. It was instead a loose and constantly shifting confederation of hundreds of essentially autonomous territories, whose emperor was elected by a small college of powerful princes rather than inheriting the throne automatically, and whose actual authority over any given territory depended far more on personal negotiation, inherited landholding, and marriage alliance than on any centralized bureaucracy comparable to what Uruk or Han China had built millennia and centuries earlier.

In a landscape this fragmented, war was expensive, uncertain, and frequently counterproductive — a noble family that conquered a neighboring territory by force often inherited a population actively hostile to its new rulers, along with the ongoing cost of garrisoning and administering land its soldiers had just finished devastating. Marriage offered something conquest rarely could: the acquisition of territory, alliance, or succession rights without a single battle being fought, frequently with the annexed population’s cooperation rather than its resentment, since the transfer occurred through inheritance law and dynastic legitimacy rather than force. A well-arranged marriage could settle a border dispute, end a generations-long war, secure a trading partnership, or, most consequentially, position a family’s children to inherit an entirely separate kingdom’s throne if that kingdom’s own line happened to fail — which, given the medical realities of the period, happened with grim regularity. This is the specific structural gap the Habsburgs exploited more completely and more patiently than any other European family in this era, and understanding how they did it means tracing several generations of what amounts to extraordinarily successful dynastic real estate planning.

The Habsburgs did not invent marriage diplomacy; they simply executed it more patiently and more completely than anyone before them. The practice was already well established by the twelfth century, and one case in particular shows both its power and its volatility clearly enough to be worth a brief detour before the Habsburgs enter the story properly. Eleanor of Aquitaine, heiress to the vast and wealthy Duchy of Aquitaine in southwestern France, married King Louis VII of France in 1137, a match that briefly brought her enormous inherited territory under the French crown’s effective control. The marriage was annulled in 1152, reportedly over the couple’s failure to produce a male heir and a personal incompatibility contemporary chroniclers described in some detail, and within only weeks of the annulment, Eleanor married Henry, Duke of Normandy and soon to be Henry II of England — a decision that instantly transferred Aquitaine’s allegiance from the French crown to the English one, and helped create the sprawling cross-Channel territorial holding historians call the Angevin Empire. A single woman’s marital choices, made within weeks of each other, had shifted control of a third of France from one crown to a rival crown, and set up more than three centuries of intermittent warfare between England and France over exactly the territories her two marriages had first joined and then divided. The lesson medieval Europe’s ruling families drew from cases like this was not that marriage diplomacy was too unpredictable to rely on. It was that the family who managed the process most patiently, across the most generations, with the least reliance on any single unpredictable personality, would eventually win the largest and most durable prize. That is precisely the lesson the Habsburgs took to its logical extreme three centuries later.

The family’s rise from a comparatively modest base in what is now Switzerland and Austria to genuine continental dominance really begins with Maximilian I, who became Holy Roman Emperor in 1508 but whose most consequential achievement came earlier, in 1477, when his marriage to Mary of Burgundy brought the extraordinarily wealthy Burgundian territories — including most of the modern Low Countries, one of the most commercially and industrially developed regions in Europe — into Habsburg hands. Maximilian did not stop at securing this inheritance for himself, and he understood, better than most rulers of his era, that a marriage arranged now could pay off two generations later in ways a military campaign never could. He arranged the marriages of his own children with a strategic precision that later generations of the family would enshrine in a Latin motto still cited today: Bella gerant alii, tu felix Austria nube — let others wage war; you, happy Austria, marry. His son Philip the Handsome was married to Joanna, heir to the united crowns of Castile and Aragon — the Spanish monarchy, freshly consolidated by Ferdinand and Isabella and, crucially, already in possession of the enormous and rapidly expanding wealth flowing back from Spain’s new American territories following Columbus’s voyages. Philip and Joanna’s marriage was, at the time it was arranged, a solid but not extraordinary alliance between two royal houses. It became something else entirely because of what happened next: a sequence of deaths among heirs who, under ordinary circumstances, should have inherited Spain and Burgundy separately, left the Habsburgs’ children as the last individuals standing in both lines simultaneously.

The beneficiary of this convergence was Charles V, born in 1500, who inherited an assemblage of territory so vast and so geographically scattered that it is genuinely difficult to describe it as a single coherent state at all rather than a personal holding company of crowns. From his father’s line, Charles inherited the Habsburg lands in Austria and the Burgundian territories in the Netherlands. From his mother’s line, he inherited Castile, Aragon, Naples, Sicily, Sardinia, and Spain’s rapidly growing American empire, freshly enriched by silver extracted from Mexican and Peruvian mines on a scale that was, by the standards of any previous economy examined in this series, genuinely unprecedented. In 1519, he was elected Holy Roman Emperor as well, adding at least nominal authority over the patchwork of German territories to everything else already in his possession. By the early 1520s, Charles V ruled, in some combination of direct sovereignty and looser imperial authority, over territory stretching from the Andes to the Danube, encompassing millions of subjects speaking Spanish, German, Dutch, Italian, and dozens of indigenous American languages, none of whom had ever voted, fought, or organized to join this particular political arrangement — an empire on which, as contemporaries and later historians alike observed, the sun literally never set, achieved almost entirely without Charles personally conquering a single one of its major components. He inherited nearly all of it, the accumulated product of roughly two generations of carefully arranged marriages that had, individually, each looked like ordinary dynastic housekeeping at the time they were made.

Charles V’s reign also shows, in real time, the strain this strategy placed on actually governing what marriage had assembled. An empire stitched together from Spanish, Austrian, Burgundian, and Italian territories, with no shared language, legal system, or administrative tradition connecting them, proved extraordinarily difficult to rule as anything resembling a coherent state. Charles spent much of his reign in near-constant travel between his territories, fighting wars on multiple fronts simultaneously — against the Ottoman Empire pressing from the southeast, against a resurgent France that found itself geographically encircled by Habsburg territory on nearly every border and reacted with predictable hostility, and against the Protestant Reformation, which fractured the religious unity of the Holy Roman Empire’s German territories specifically at the moment Charles most needed those territories’ cooperation and taxation to fund his other wars. In 1556, worn down by decades of this juggling act, Charles did something almost no monarch examined so far in this series had done voluntarily: he abdicated, splitting his inheritance in two rather than attempting to pass the entire unwieldy mass to a single heir. His son Philip II received Spain, the Netherlands, and the Italian and American territories. His brother Ferdinand received the Austrian lands and, shortly after, the Holy Roman imperial title itself, formally splitting the Habsburg dynasty into Spanish and Austrian branches that would follow substantially different trajectories over the following centuries while still frequently intermarrying with each other, a decision made explicitly to keep the family’s combined holdings from ever fully reuniting under a single ruler again, since even Charles himself seems to have concluded that no one person could actually govern what marriage alone had assembled.

That final detail — the continued intermarriage between the Spanish and Austrian branches even after the formal split — is where this family’s story turns from a triumph of dynastic strategy into a cautionary tale about the biological cost of treating marriage purely as statecraft with no regard for its other consequences. Because the Habsburgs so consistently married relatives to keep territory and alliances concentrated within the family rather than dispersing them to outside houses, the Spanish line in particular became progressively and severely inbred over successive generations, uncle marrying niece, first cousins marrying first cousins, in a pattern that geneticists studying the family’s surviving portraits and medical records have since traced with considerable precision. The endpoint of this pattern was Charles II of Spain, born in 1661, whose parents were uncle and niece and whose family tree, when reconstructed, shows an inbreeding coefficient higher than would result from a direct sibling pairing. Charles II was severely disabled from birth, unable to speak intelligibly until the age of four, afflicted throughout his life with a severe jaw deformity so pronounced he reportedly had difficulty chewing food, intellectually disabled by the standards of his own era’s physicians, and infertile — the last Habsburg king of Spain, dying in 1700 without an heir, having spent much of his reign as a genuinely pitiable figure subjected to exorcisms his own court believed might cure afflictions that were, in fact, straightforwardly genetic. His death without a direct heir triggered the War of the Spanish Succession, a continent-wide conflict fought specifically over which other royal house would inherit the vacancy the Habsburg marriage strategy had, quite literally, bred itself out of existence to create.

The Austrian branch of the family, meanwhile, kept the marriage strategy alive for another century and a half, and its own most celebrated practitioner shows the tactic being deployed with an ambition that briefly rivaled Maximilian’s original design. Maria Theresa, who inherited the Habsburg Austrian lands in 1740 despite fierce resistance from other European powers unwilling to accept a woman’s claim under the Pragmatic Sanction her father had spent years negotiating specifically to secure it, went on to arrange the marriages of eleven surviving children with a strategic sweep that touched nearly every major court in Europe. Her son Joseph became Holy Roman Emperor; her daughter Maria Carolina married into the Neapolitan royal family and came to dominate that kingdom’s politics; and most consequentially of all, her youngest daughter, Maria Antonia, was married in 1770 to the future Louis XVI of France, becoming the queen the French Revolution would eventually know as Marie Antoinette. That marriage, intended by Maria Theresa as the capstone alliance binding the Habsburgs’ oldest continental rival into the family’s orbit, instead placed an Austrian-born queen at the center of a French monarchy already sliding toward the very collapse this series will examine directly in Part XIV — a reminder that a marriage arranged for one dynasty’s advantage could, within a single generation, become a fatal liability for the crown it married into, if the underlying legitimacy of that crown had already begun to erode for reasons no marriage could fix.

Charles II’s fate, on the Spanish side of the family, is worth sitting with as this series’ first clear demonstration of a pattern it will see again in different form: every mechanism a monarchy develops to secure and perpetuate its own legitimacy carries a cost, often deferred for generations, that eventually comes due. Egypt’s theology bought three thousand years of continuity but could not argue its way past a failed Nile flood. China’s Mandate bought a vocabulary for surviving collapse but not immunity from it. And the Habsburgs’ marriage strategy, the single most successful territorial acquisition method examined anywhere in this series, bought Charles V an empire on which the sun never set — and, two centuries later, bought his Spanish descendants a genetic collapse so severe it ended the line entirely, a debt that had been quietly accumulating with every strategically advantageous marriage to a close relative, paid off all at once by a single unfortunate king who never had any say in the marriages that produced him.

Part VI turns from a family that expanded its power almost entirely without armies to a king who tried, and spectacularly failed, to hold onto power entirely through force and legal maneuvering — and who ended up, in the process of that failure, handing every English-speaking country that followed him the first written document in Western history to put a formal, permanent leash on what a king could take from his own people.
