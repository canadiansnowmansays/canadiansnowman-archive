On the evening of October 13, 2026, President Trump stood before a rally crowd in Wasilla, Alaska, and complained about a word. “Every time they want to try and get me, they say affordability,” he told them, before assuring the room that American families had seen substantial improvement on his watch.

At 8:30 the next morning, the Bureau of Labor Statistics released the last Consumer Price Index report Americans will see before they vote on November 3. It is rare for a government’s own statisticians to rebut a president quite so promptly, and they did not mean to. The calendar did it for them. By the time most of Wasilla was awake, the word the president was tired of hearing had acquired a number, and the number had gone up.

The Number

Consumer prices rose 0.6 percent in September, the largest monthly increase in five months, and stood 3.7 percent higher than a year earlier, up from 3.4 percent in August. The measure economists use to judge momentum — the annualized pace over the most recent three months — jumped to 3.4 percent, as Talmon Joseph Smith noted for The New York Times on October 14, 2026, up from a mild 2 percent the month before. “Core” inflation, which strips out food and energy, rose 0.3 percent for the month and 2.6 percent for the year. Part X of this series argued that voters judge prices by memory rather than by rate. September removed the need for the distinction. The rate itself is now heading the wrong way.

As Colby Smith reported for The New York Times the same morning, the bulk of the increase came straight from energy, and energy came straight from the war with Iran. Gasoline rose 9.1 percent in September alone, enough to account for more than half of the entire monthly increase in prices, and is up 34.2 percent on the year. In late September the national average neared $4.50 a gallon; on October 14, AAA put it at $4.33, more than a dollar above where it stood a year earlier. Diesel sat at $6.30, up 72 percent.

Then there is the line item that arrives with the first frost. Fuel oil, which heats many homes in the American Northeast, rose 15.3 percent in September and is up 74.2 percent on the year. As Sydney Ember reported for The New York Times, the Energy Information Administration’s national average for heating oil reached $6.04 a gallon, against $4.10 the week before the war began. Airfares, riding the price of jet fuel, are up 22.4 percent from a year ago. Energy as a whole rose 5.2 percent in a single month and 20.4 percent over twelve.

The war that produced these numbers is nearing the end of its eighth month. The president once promised it would be over in weeks.

This series has never hidden the parts of a report that cut the other way. Tariffs are still visible at the margins: toys rose 4.2 percent in September. But apparel rose only 0.3 percent, and household furnishings actually fell 0.2 percent. Auto insurance is down 5 percent on the year, genuine relief after years of punishing increases. Ground beef fell 1 percent from August to $6.79 a pound, though it remains 5.4 percent dearer than a year ago, and the steak of Part X has not become a bargain. The artificial-intelligence boom, meanwhile, has produced what some economists have dubbed “chipflation”: computers and related equipment up 9.7 percent on the year, software up 28.4 percent, smartphones down nearly 10.

The picture, in short, is not uniformly grim. It is merely grim in the places a household cannot avoid — the tank, the furnace, the flight home for the holidays — and mild in the places it can.

The job market offers the same mixed verdict. Unemployment stood at 4.2 percent in September and has held at or below 4.5 percent for five years, a record, and retail sales rose at a healthy clip. But economists describe a “low-hire, low-fire” economy: companies are not laying people off, and they are not taking many on either. The average unemployed American has now been looking for work for nearly six months. Most people have a job. Few can find a better one.

Back Where We Started

The single most important number released on October 14 was not technically an inflation figure at all.

As Ben Casselman reported for The New York Times, average hourly earnings adjusted for inflation fell 0.5 percent in September and were down 0.7 percent from a year earlier — the sixth straight month in which prices outran pay on a year-over-year basis. Before adjusting for inflation, hourly earnings have risen 5.5 percent since January 2025. Prices have risen 5.44 percent over the same period. Subtract one from the other and the result is very nearly zero. Nor is the paycheque side of the race speeding up. Wage growth slowed to 3 percent in September, from nearly 4 percent a year earlier.

Put plainly: the average American’s hour of work now buys almost exactly what it bought on January 20, 2025. Twenty-one months of raises, promotions, overtime and job changes, and the household has run in place.

From north of the border, where we keep our own quarrel with the price of a litre, it is tempting to call this stagnation. It is not quite that. A great deal happened; it simply all cancelled out, in the way a man rowing hard against a current may work up a tremendous sweat and remain, at the end of the afternoon, opposite the same boathouse.

The president did not campaign on the boathouse. He campaigned, as Representative Brendan Boyle of Pennsylvania reminded reporters within minutes of the release, on ending inflation and lowering costs on day one. “More than 600 days into his second term, he has done the exact opposite,” Boyle said. A partisan statement, certainly. It is also, as of October 14, a description of the government’s own wage data. It is not only Democrats saying so. Michael Strain, an economist at the conservative American Enterprise Institute, told Ben Casselman of the Times that voters blamed Mr. Biden’s policies for higher prices and now blame Mr. Trump’s. “In both cases, I think voters made the right judgment.”

Tuesday’s Script, Wednesday’s Script

The White House had a version of this story ready. It simply had the misfortune of delivering it a day early.

On October 13, as Tony Romm reported for The New York Times, Kevin Hassett, director of the National Economic Council, went on Fox Business to insist that inflation had “decelerated sharply over the last three months,” and to lay the blame for high price levels at the feet of former President Biden, who left office nearly two years ago. On October 14, Hassett returned to the same network, called the report “disappointing,” and attributed most of it to the war — the war his own administration has been prosecuting since February.

Twenty-four hours is not a long shelf life for an economic claim. “Decelerated sharply” was retired on contact with the data, which showed the three-month pace had not decelerated at all but had risen by more than half. The president, for his part, has diagnosed the country’s frustration as essentially a problem of public relations. A public relations problem, it should be said, is what one calls a fact one would prefer the public not have.

The Seventy-Nine Dollars

The same morning delivered a second announcement, and for millions of retirees it may matter more than the first.

As Mark Miller reported for The New York Times, the Social Security Administration set the cost-of-living adjustment for 2027 at 3.6 percent, the largest since 2023 — worth about $75 a month to the average beneficiary. The size of the raise is not good news. It is the inflation, arriving by mail.

But Medicare’s trustees project the monthly Part B premium will rise by $6.60 in 2027, to $209.50, and that premium is usually deducted straight from the Social Security cheque. Some Part D drug plans will rise by $50 a month or more.

Into this the administration has dropped a one-time $90 payment, sent this month to the 21 million people in traditional Medicare and billed by the White House as “historic action to help senior citizens.” The arithmetic deserves a moment. Twelve months of the projected Part B increase comes to $79.20. The historic action, in other words, refunds next year’s premium increase in advance, with $10.80 left over — roughly the price of a modest lunch, provided one does not travel to it by air. Medicare Advantage enrollees are ineligible, which means fewer than half of all beneficiaries will see even that. The cheque lands days before the election. The premium increase arrives in January.

The poverty rate for Americans 65 and older rose to 15.4 percent in 2025, the highest of any age group, up from 12 percent in 2019. And behind all of it sits the trustees’ June projection that the retirement trust fund will be exhausted in 2032, triggering an automatic cut of roughly 22 percent absent congressional action — a subject large enough to need its own series once the votes are counted. These are the voters most likely to show up on November 3.

Half a Country Doing Arithmetic

As Sydney Ember reported for The New York Times on October 14, a Pew Research Center study found that close to half of Americans are having trouble paying for basic needs. Among lower-income adults, nearly three-quarters said they had struggled to pay for food, housing, medical care or transportation in the past year, compared with 13 percent of upper-income adults.

The numbers have faces. As Yvonne Wingett Sanchez reported for The Atlantic on October 14, 2026, a line of cars nearly a mile long recently stretched along a high-desert highway in Yavapai County, Arizona, every driver waiting for food. Trump carried the county with 66 percent of the vote in 2024. Among those Sanchez met was Kandy Soto, 66, a retired courtroom clerk who once donated to her local food bank and now drives 20 miles to collect from it. She voted for Trump expecting him to make America affordable again. “It takes an older person a lot of giving up to do something like this,” she said.

Arizona shows what happens when rising prices meet a shrinking safety net. About half a million residents have lost their food stamps since the summer of 2025 under the president’s signature tax law, and the state’s food banks now serve roughly 786,000 people a month, up from about 627,000 in 2024. Ron Brannon, a 68-year-old disabled Army veteran whose monthly benefit was cut to $60, told Sanchez that once the balance reaches zero, he simply goes without. Nearly everyone she interviewed, Trump voters included, said they were worse off than before the 2024 election. Even the loyal are wavering: Dawn Zimmerman, a salon owner who voted for Trump in 2024, told Sanchez she has not decided whether to back Republicans this fall, citing his habit of blaming others while seeming disconnected from her pain. That is not a hunger a $90 cheque addresses.

Twenty-Five Cents and a Cello

Some of the most telling prices in America are the ones that have not moved yet.

As Ana Swanson reported for The New York Times on October 12, the average price of bananas — about 65 cents a pound in August — has barely changed in a decade. Grocers sell them as a loss leader to get shoppers through the door. Behind that unchanging price, the diesel that powers the refrigerated trucks is up roughly 70 percent since the war began, fertilizer made from Middle Eastern natural gas has climbed, and so have shipping, cardboard and the chemicals that keep the fruit free of disease. Del Monte’s margin on bananas in the second quarter was 2.3 percent. Many supply contracts expire in December.

“What else can you buy in a grocery store for 25 cents?” asked Tom Stenzel of the Banana Association of North America, warning that the industry’s ability to absorb costs is at a breaking point. The answer, after December, may be: not a banana. The cost has not been cancelled. It has been queued, and it is scheduled, like the heating bill, to arrive after the vote. The data released in the week after the CPI report suggest the queue is getting longer. Producer prices and import prices both came in hot, and diesel, the fuel that moves nearly everything a grocery store sells, has reached record prices.

The tariffs, meanwhile, have found a quieter victim. As Sydney Ember and Maddie McGarvey reported for The New York Times on October 2, Jeff Lipscomb, an orchestra director in Kanawha County, West Virginia, has twelve cellists, ten cellos, an annual budget of $500 to $600, and a single new cello costing more than $1,000. A freshman viola player dropped her class this fall because her family could not afford the rental. In August, the administration proposed a further 25 percent tariff on brass instruments, citing national security.

There is no line in the Consumer Price Index for a fourteen-year-old who stops playing the viola. A tuba is not, by any reasonable definition, a threat to national security. A country whose public schools can no longer field a band has lost something, though no economist will ever chart it.

Running the Report Through TACO

The September report runs through all four stages of this series’ TACO framework in a single week. Threaten: the tariffs and the war with Iran, each sold in its moment as strength, each arriving months later as a line item. Accommodate: a $90 rebate timed to land before the vote, a $5,000 cheque pledged if Republicans hold Congress, and a flurry of executive orders on the price of beef. Capitulate: the National Economic Council director conceding on live television that the numbers were “disappointing.” Obfuscate: the same official, one day earlier, declaring that inflation had “decelerated sharply,” the blame assigned to a predecessor nearly two years gone, and a president who calls the result a public relations problem. What is new is the speed. The Obfuscate stage used to survive for weeks. This one survived a single news cycle.

The Meeting on October 28

Until October 14, the Federal Reserve’s last meeting before the midterms looked like a formality. Having raised rates in September, officials had signalled they would wait until December rather than act days before an election.

The September report changed the arithmetic. As Joe Rennison reported for The New York Times, investors raised the odds of an October increase from roughly 20 percent to 44 percent within hours of the release, and the two-year Treasury yield jumped a tenth of a point to 4.88 percent. As Colby Smith noted, Kevin Warsh faced pressure to explain why the Fed was moving gradually at the very moment price pressures were picking up.

In the end the committee held rates steady, by a vote of 10 to 2, as Colby Smith reported for The New York Times on October 29. It was a poor outcome for the president either way. A hike would have landed on every credit card balance and variable-rate loan in the country six days before the vote. The hold invites the charge that the Fed blinked for political reasons, with inflation rising and the White House watching. Part XII takes up what the pause may cost.

Report Card

A report card is supposed to tell a family something it does not already know. This one did not. Households had graded the economy long before the Bureau of Labor Statistics issued its verdict — at the pump in late September, at the heating-oil truck in early October, at the kitchen table where the choice between the furnace and the grocery bill gets made. The October 14 report merely confirmed, with the government’s own seal, the conclusion they had already reached. Elizabeth Pancotti of the Groundwork Collaborative, a progressive research group, told the Times that what people want most is not growth or even opportunity but stability. Twenty-one months of running in place has offered neither.

The president promised Americans he would take them somewhere. Twenty-one months later, by his own government’s measure, the average worker’s hour of labour is back where it started. In fairness, that is a destination. It is simply not one anybody voted for — and on November 3, they will have the chance to say so.
