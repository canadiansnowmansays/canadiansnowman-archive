It took the Catholic Church a century to respond to the Industrial Revolution. Pope Leo XIII published Rerum Novarum, his encyclical on the rights of workers and the obligations of capital, in 1891 — more than a hundred years after the spinning jenny, decades after children had been pulled from mines, long after the damage was done and the organizing had begun without the Vatican’s blessing.

Pope Leo XIV published Magnifica Humanitas — “on safeguarding the human person in the time of artificial intelligence” — on May 25, 2026, thirteen months after his election. ChatGPT is three and a half years old. The first comprehensive federal AI legislation in the United States has not been introduced. The Pope moved faster than Congress.

This is not a small thing. The Vatican, as one aphorism has it, thinks in centuries. This Pope is thinking in months. And the document he produced is, in substance if not in enforceability, the closest thing to a comprehensive AI governance framework that any global institution has issued.

It is also, in ways the Pope may or may not have intended, the most complete statement of the Asimov Gap thesis that anyone outside this series has written.

What the Encyclical Says

Magnifica Humanitas runs to more than 42,000 words. Francis X. Rocca, writing in the Atlantic, noted that Leo “seeks to counterbalance alarm with hope” but “lands firmly on one side” — his account of what could go wrong is detailed and expansive, while his account of what could go right is perfunctory and brief. This is not, as Rocca gently implies, a failure of balance. It is a recognition that the dangers are specific and documented while the benefits remain, for the most part, speculative and conditional.

The encyclical addresses, in sequence, the arguments this series has been making for forty parts.

On concentration: “When control over platforms, data, and computing power is concentrated in the hands of a few, it tends to become opaque and evade public oversight, increasing the risk of distorted forms of development.” Leo warns that “small but highly influential groups can shape information and consumption patterns, influence democratic processes and steer economic dynamics to their own advantage, undermining social justice and solidarity among peoples.”

On autonomous weapons: “Moral judgment cannot be reduced to calculation, for it involves conscience, personal responsibility and the recognition of the other as a person. Therefore, it is not permissible to entrust lethal or otherwise irreversible decisions to artificial systems.” This is the Anthropic v. DOD case stated as moral theology. Anthropic refused to remove its red lines against autonomous weapons and was blacklisted by the Pentagon. The Pope has now declared, from the authority of the oldest continuous institution in the Western world, that Anthropic was right — not as a business decision but as a moral one.

On governance: “Robust legal frameworks, independent oversight, informed users and a political system that does not abdicate its responsibility are required.” This sentence could have appeared in any of the forty parts of this series. It did not. It appeared in a papal encyclical, because the Pope published his governance framework before any legislature did.

On work: “Every introduction of automation and AI should be accompanied by verifiable measures to protect the employment, retraining and participation of workers.” Leo decries AI-driven unemployment, especially among young people, and condemns the exploitation of data labellers, content moderators, and the workers who extract the resources for the devices the technology depends on.

On transparency: “When data and algorithms influence credit distribution, personnel selection or access to services and opportunities, it is necessary that decisions be understandable, contestable and subject to oversight, so that individuals are not reduced to mere profiles.”

On the cognitive cost: AI can “weaken personal creativity and judgment” and promote the “illusion of a relationship with a real personal subject” that can lead users to “lose the very desire to form genuine human connections.” The Carnegie Mellon study documented in Part 39 found that ten minutes of AI use measurably degraded problem-solving ability. The Pope is not citing the study. He is describing the same phenomenon from a different tradition — one that has been thinking about what diminishes human capacity for considerably longer than the AI safety community has existed.

On the human person: Leo rejects transhumanism and posthumanism — the Silicon Valley philosophies that see technology as a means to augment or perfect people. Such conceptions of perfectibility, he writes, make it “easier to accept that some lives are less useful, less desirable or less worthy.” And then the line that may be the encyclical’s most quietly radical: “Humanity flourishes not despite limitations, but often through them.”

The Pope is answering a specific person, whether he knows it or not. Elizabeth Bruenig, writing in the Atlantic, noted that Peter Thiel — the billionaire venture capitalist who spent $15 million electing the vice president of the United States — hesitated in a New York Times interview to answer the question of whether humanity ought to survive. Thiel and others in the tech-investor class have suggested that humans might be better off outsourcing their imperfect moral decision-making to superintelligent AI. The Pope’s encyclical is a direct response to that hesitation — a 42,000-word argument that the imperfection Thiel finds dispensable is the thing that makes us human.

The encyclical’s most sustained passage on this point deserves the record, because it is the argument no legislature, no courtroom, and no regulatory framework has yet made:

“To eliminate suffering entirely would mean, in the end, extinguishing love and desire as well. Those who love and desire cannot avoid passing through trial and suffering; and over the years, we carry within us lessons that leave their mark like scars, the memories of a journey shaped by freedom and failure, dreams and disappointments. It is only thanks to the interplay of these elements that the wonders of the soul occur within us, allowing us to sense the richness of our humanity. To renounce this adventure, both tragic and splendid, in the name of a presumed transcendence of all limits, could mean many things, but it would no longer be human.”

Gal Beckerman, also in the Atlantic, responded to this passage with a sentence that matters for this series: “I am not a Christian, and really not much of a believer at all, but this articulates so well what I feel AI is taking from us.” The secular reader’s permission to take the encyclical seriously is, for the governance argument, as important as the encyclical itself. The Pope is not speaking only to Catholics. He is speaking to anyone who has felt The Bad Feeling and could not name what was being lost.

And then the line that compresses the entire argument into fourteen words: “For an algorithm, an error is a flaw to be corrected; for a person, however, an error can be a catalyst for profound change.”

The Olah Statement

Co-founder of US artificial intelligence (AI) company Anthropic, Christopher Olah.Alberto Pizzoli/AFP/Getty Images

At the Vatican’s presentation of the encyclical, Christopher Olah — a co-founder of Anthropic — appeared on a panel alongside the Pope. His statement deserves the full record because it is the most remarkable thing an AI company executive has said in public since the founding of the industry.

“Every frontier AI lab — including Anthropic — operates inside a set of incentives and constraints that can sometimes conflict with doing the right thing,” Olah said. “That is why, if we want this technology to go well, it is enormously important that there be people outside those incentives — people who care about things going well, who are paying close attention, who are willing to say hard things and insist on safety, who are willing to be our earnest, thoughtful critics.”

Read that again. A co-founder of one of the two most important AI companies on earth stood beside the Pope and said, in public, that his own company operates inside incentives that can conflict with doing the right thing. He said that the solution is not better incentives inside the company but people outside the company who are willing to be earnest, thoughtful critics. He said this at the Vatican, in front of cameras, on the record.

This is the Little Brother argument from Part 34 stated by the Little Brother himself — not as a marketing position but as a confession. Anthropic’s governance proposition has always been that it is the safety-focused alternative. Olah is saying, in the most public forum available, that being the safety-focused alternative is not sufficient. The incentives inside the lab — any lab, including his — can conflict with doing the right thing. The solution is external accountability. The solution is the referee being separate from the players.

The Asimov Gap is the distance between what AI can do and what democratic institutions are empowered to govern. Olah is saying the gap exists inside his own company. The Pope is saying it exists inside the entire industry. Both are saying the same thing: the builders cannot govern themselves. The laws must come from outside.

What Leo XIII Did

The parallel between the two Leos is not decorative. It is structural, and it illuminates what this encyclical might actually accomplish.

Leo XIII’s Rerum Novarum did not create labour law. It did not establish minimum wages, limit working hours, or prohibit child labour. It had no enforcement mechanism. No government was bound by it. The encyclical was, in the most literal sense, unenforceable — a moral argument issued by a religious authority with no legislative power.

What it did was provide a moral framework that energised a movement. American Catholics — who were, in 1891, overwhelmingly working-class immigrants — read Rerum Novarum and found in it a theological justification for the labour organising they were already doing. The encyclical did not start the labour movement. It legitimised it. It gave it a language that transcended the secular arguments about wages and hours and connected the cause to something larger — the dignity of the human person, the obligations of capital, the common good.

The American Catholic labour movement that followed — the movement that produced the Congress of Industrial Organizations, that fought for the eight-hour day and the weekend, that built the middle class that the post-war economy depended on — drew explicitly on Rerum Novarum for its moral authority. The encyclical did not pass a law. It created the conditions under which laws could be passed, by giving millions of people permission to believe that their instinct — that the system was wrong and needed to change — was not merely an economic complaint but a moral truth.

Magnifica Humanitas may do the same thing.

The Bad Feeling documented in Part 39 — the anxiety, the alienation, the somatic response to a technology that feels like it is happening to you — is, in Leo XIV’s framework, not irrational and not merely emotional. It is a recognition of a moral reality. The technology, as currently governed, diminishes the human person. The concentration of power in the hands of a few undermines democratic accountability. The delegation of lethal decisions to machines violates conscience. The replacement of human judgment with algorithmic calculation weakens the capacities that make us human.

Fifty-seven percent of American voters say AI’s risks outweigh its benefits. Seventy percent oppose data centre construction in their communities. The Pope is telling them their instinct is correct. Not as a political calculation. As a moral judgment.

Whether that moral judgment energises a movement — the way Rerum Novarum energised the labour movement — depends on whether the people who feel The Bad Feeling can find, in the encyclical’s framework, the same permission to organise that Catholic workers found in 1891. The 142 data centre activist groups in 24 states are already organising. The Working Families Party has a platform. Progressives are betting AI anxiety will win them elections. The movement exists. What it has lacked is a moral framework that transcends the policy arguments.

The Pope just provided one.

The Asimov Frame

Asimov’s Three Laws were prior, public, and enforceable. They were built into the robots before the robots were activated. They did not depend on the character of the builders. They did not require the robots to volunteer for accountability. They were structural constraints, not moral aspirations.

Magnifica Humanitas is prior and public. It is not enforceable. No government is bound by it. No company is required to comply. It is, like Rerum Novarum before it, a moral argument issued by an institution with no legislative authority — an institution that thinks in centuries but, this time, moved in months.

The gap between the encyclical and the Three Laws is the gap between moral authority and legal authority. The Pope can say that lethal decisions must not be entrusted to machines. The Pentagon can ignore him. The Pope can say that robust legal frameworks are required. Congress can continue not to pass them. The Pope can say that humanity flourishes through limitations. Silicon Valley can continue to treat every limitation as a defect to be optimised away.

But the encyclical exists. It is 42,000 words of sustained moral argument, issued by the leader of 1.4 billion Catholics, endorsed at its presentation by a co-founder of one of the two most important AI companies on earth, published faster than any legislature has managed to act, on the same day a jury in Oakland was deliberating a $150 billion lawsuit that it would resolve in ninety minutes without reaching the merits.

The jury answered a question about a calendar. The Pope answered the question the jury never reached.

The laws that should govern AI do not yet exist. The moral framework does. Whether the framework produces the laws — the way Rerum Novarum eventually produced the labour laws that it could not itself enforce — is the question that the next decade will answer.

The gap between the encyclical and the enforceable law is the Asimov Gap, stated in its most ancient and most modern form simultaneously. The oldest continuous institution in the Western world has spoken. The newest industry on earth has not yet been governed.

The distance between the two is the work that remains.
