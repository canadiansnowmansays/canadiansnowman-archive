A Ministry of Memory has two functions, and I have spent nine essays on only one of them. The first is erasure — the deletion of the inconvenient past, which is what a memorial hollowed, a dataset deleted, and a portrait removed all have in common. But Orwell understood that erasure alone is only half the machine. Winston Smith did not merely drop Comrade Withers down the memory hole; he replaced him with the invented hero Comrade Ogilvy. The Ministry does not leave a blank where the unpersoned official stood. It fills the blank with someone the Party would rather the record celebrated. Forgetting and canonizing are the same operation, run in opposite directions: you erase whom the nation is told to forget, and you elevate whom the nation is told to admire.

This essay is about the second direction — the honours. A nation’s honours are memory in its most deliberate form. They are the official answer to the question a culture asks of itself: whom do we choose to remember, and to celebrate, and to hold up as the best of us? To control that answer is to control the canon — the roster of the admired, which shapes what a country believes it is. And in 2025, one of the nation’s premier honours passed almost entirely into the hands of a single man, who told us, precisely, how much of it he now controlled: about ninety-eight percent.

The Number

The Kennedy Center Honors have been given since 1978, to artists judged to have shaped American culture over a lifetime — the roster reads as a kind of running canon of the American performing arts. For nearly half a century, the honourees were chosen the way such things are meant to be chosen in a republic: by a bipartisan selection committee working with arts advisors and staff, a process deliberately insulated from whoever happened to be president. Presidents attended; presidents did not pick. The honourees were announced by press release, and the medals were presented at a State Department dinner — an event of the culture rather than of the White House.

In early 2025, as I described in the very first essay of this series, the president fired the Kennedy Center’s leadership, replaced most of its board with loyalists, installed Richard Grenell as interim president, and had the new board elect him chairman of the institution. What followed the takeover of the building was the takeover of its honour. On August 13, 2025, he announced the year’s honourees himself, in person, at the Kennedy Center — and, asked how involved he had been in choosing them, gave a number that no president in the history of the award had ever had occasion to give. He was, as CBS News reported, “about 98% involved.”

Ninety-eight percent. Sit with the specificity of it. It is not a boast of general influence; it is a claim of near-total authorship. The bipartisan committee that had chosen forty-seven classes of honourees was, by the chairman’s own arithmetic, reduced to the remaining two percent. And the two percent did not include a veto. CNN reported that the president said he had personally rejected some candidates he considered too “woke.” The canon of American arts — the official annual answer to whom do we celebrate? — was now being written, by his own account, almost entirely by one hand, with a strike-through pen held in the other.

Then the medals moved. On December 6, 2025, they were placed around the honourees’ necks not at the State Department but in the Oval Office. On December 7, the president hosted the ceremony itself — the first sitting president to take the stage at the Honors rather than watch from a box, and he did it after remaking the institution that grants them.

Why This Is Erasure, Told Forward

A reader might reasonably ask why a series about erasure is spending an essay on an award — on something added to the culture rather than taken from it. The honourees of 2025 — Sylvester Stallone, George Strait, Gloria Gaynor, Michael Crawford and KISS — whatever one thinks of the process, were real artists with real careers. Nothing was deleted. Where is the erasure?

The erasure is in what an honour displaces. A canon is a finite thing. Each year’s honourees are chosen against the field of everyone who was not chosen, and the roster, accumulated over decades, becomes an argument about what American culture is and whom it belongs to. When the selection passes from a bipartisan committee to a single partisan hand, the argument changes — not by striking names already on the list, but by controlling every name added from now on, and thereby slowly redefining, going forward, the whole meaning of the honour. The past honourees are not erased. The future is pre-written. This is Comrade Ogilvy’s function exactly: you do not need to delete the old heroes if you control the manufacture of the new ones, because in time the new ones outnumber and reframe the old.

And the capture of an honour carries a second, quieter erasure, which this series has already documented at the same address. When the president took over the Kennedy Center, artists cancelled performances and staff resigned in protest; audiences fell away; the institution hollowed, as Part 1 described, until an eighty-seven-year-old tour guide called it a black hole. The people who withdrew — the performers who would not appear, the artists who in another year might have been chosen and now never will be — are erased from the record precisely by their absence. We do at least know they exist, because the chairman told us so: some names were struck for being too “woke.” We will never learn which. You will not see them on the roster. You will not know who declined, who was passed over for the wrong politics, who quietly fell off the list of the celebrated because the list acquired a single author with known tastes. The canon that results looks complete. It is missing everyone who does not fit, and their absence leaves no blank you can point to. It simply looks like the honourees are who the honourees are.

The Redesigned Medal

There is a detail here that belongs in the same glass case as “restored to 2008” and “not immediately available,” because it is the physical artifact of the capture, and it is almost too apt.

For forty-seven years, the Honors medallion was made by a single D.C.-area family, the Baturins — gold-plated discs on the familiar rainbow ribbon, hung around the necks of everyone from Johnny Cash to Oprah Winfrey to the Grateful Dead. In August 2025, as the Washington Post reported, the Kennedy Center thanked the family for their craftsmanship and dropped them, announcing it was moving to “a new partnership.” The new partner was Tiffany & Co., which donated a redesigned medallion — the first significant redesign since 1978. Salon described the change: the long-standing rainbow ribbon gave way to a solid deep blue one, with a weightier gold disc, the building etched on one face, and on the reverse the honourees’ names above the date of the medal ceremony.

I do not want to overread a piece of jewellery, but I also do not want to miss it, because objects like this are where a change in control becomes tangible. Count the small unpersonings in a single ribbon. The family that made the medal for nearly half a century, written out with a polite letter. The rainbow — the ribbon’s whole visual signature — reduced to a hint of colour at the edges of an etching. And the date on the back is not the date of the gala, the culture’s night, but the date of the Oval Office presentation, the chairman’s afternoon. The honour now looks different. It is minted under new management, presented in a new venue, chosen by a new and singular authority, and it hangs a redesigned medal around the neck of a roster a new chairman has, by his own count, ninety-eight-percent chosen. Everything about the award now says: this is his to give.

Which is, of course, the point of capturing an honour rather than merely attending it. An honour you present is a favour you bestow, and a favour creates a debt, and a debt creates loyalty. The canon becomes not a mirror the culture holds up to itself but a court the sovereign convenes — the admired assembled at his pleasure, wearing his medal, in his Oval Office, grateful to him. That is a different thing from a national honour. It is patronage wearing the costume of a national honour, and the costume is the redesigned medal.

Whom We Are Told to Remember

Let me draw the thread back to the argument of the whole series, because the honours close a loop the earlier essays opened.

I have written, across nine parts, about a machinery for controlling the past: what the museum may display, what the sign may say, what the dataset may record, whose portrait may hang, whose name a base may carry, which books a reader may reach. Every one of those was a mechanism for governing memory by subtraction — deciding what the nation is not permitted to remember. The honours are the same machinery running by addition — deciding whom the nation is instructed to admire. And the two directions serve one end. A country’s sense of itself is built as much from its heroes as from its history; tell it whom to celebrate and you have shaped its self-image as surely as by telling it what to forget. Orwell put the celebration and the erasure in the same building, under the same Ministry, performed by the same clerk at the same desk, because they are one job. Winston deleted Withers and wrote Ogilvy in a single sitting.

The president’s ninety-eight percent is the addition half of that job, stated with unusual candour. He did not claim merely to admire this year’s honourees; he claimed to have chosen them, nearly all of them, personally. At the Oval Office presentation he called them “perhaps the most accomplished and renowned class” ever assembled, and then added, revealingly, “I know most of them.” That last phrase is the tell. A national honour is not supposed to be given by a man to people he knows. It is supposed to be given by a country to people it wishes to remember. When the giver is a single person and the qualification includes his personal acquaintance, the honour has stopped being a national memory and become a personal one — a record not of whom America chooses to celebrate, but of whom the chairman chooses to have around him, wearing his medal, in his room.

The Country Changed the Channel

Here is where the essay, written as the medals were being hung, has to account for what happened next — because the country was given a vote of sorts, and it cast it with the remote.

The chairman predicted the broadcast would be the highest-rated in the programme’s history. When CBS aired it on December 23, 2025, TheWrap reported, it drew 3.01 million viewers — the smallest audience the Honors had ever recorded. A canon is only a canon if people attend to it. You can author the roster, mint the medal and host the show, but you cannot compel the nation to watch itself being told whom to admire. This is the same pattern Part 12 of this series will examine at the Smithsonian: the erasure that works on paper and fails in public.

The rest of the year’s story belongs mostly to other essays — Part 1 for the hollowed building, The Name Game for the inscription — so I will only sketch it. In December 2025, days after the gala, the chairman’s board voted to rename the institution after him, and his name went up on the facade the next day. On May 29, 2026, a federal judge ruled the renaming illegal and ordered the signage removed; by mid-June it was gone, a tarp hanging where the letters had been. On September 15, 2026, Judge Christopher Cooper rejected a second attempt to put the name back. Stephen Dinan of the Washington Times reported that the judge held the board could not install memorials to the president, or to anyone else, without Congress’s blessing — and that minutes after his ruling, the board voted to close the building.

Which brings the honour back into the story. On October 2, 2026, the Kennedy Center announced that the 49th Honors will be held on December 7, 2026 — not at the Kennedy Center, but at Capital One Arena, the twenty-thousand-seat home of the Wizards and the Capitals. It is the first time in the award’s history that the gala has left the building that gives it its name. CBS, which had broadcast the Honors every year since 1978, is gone too; its contract lapsed after last year’s record-low telecast, and the new broadcast partner is to be named “at a later date.” So are the honourees.

But one decision has already been made, and it tells you everything. BroadwayWorld, citing the New York Times, noted that the save-the-date for the 2026 Honors features a photograph not of an honouree, not of an artist, not of the building, but of the chairman himself, onstage at last year’s ceremony. The honourees have not been chosen. The face of the honour has.

The Canon Is a Kind of Memory Too

The honours will not make headlines the way a deleted dataset or a banned book does, and that is exactly why they belong in this series. The erasures are alarming and visible; the capture of an honour is genial and celebratory, conducted in tuxedos, set to the honourees’ own greatest hits. Nobody protests a medal ceremony the way they protest a book ban. And so the quietest and most durable form of memory-control proceeds under cover of a party: year by year, a single hand writes the roster of the officially admired, and the canon of American culture is slowly re-authored, not by striking the past but by owning the future of who gets remembered.

Now the party is moving to a hockey arena, because the memorial it was named for cannot be made to carry the chairman’s name, and so has been shut. There is something almost Leacockian in it — the man who could not get his name onto the building taking the honour to a venue named for a credit card. But the joke should not distract from the mechanism, which travels intact. The medal is his design. The roster is his ninety-eight percent. The invitation is his face. Whatever stage it stands on, the honour is now his to give.

They did not erase anyone at the Kennedy Center Honors. They simply took over the list of whom we are told to celebrate, and told us — ninety-eight percent — who would be on it. That is the other half of the Ministry of Memory, the half that smiles. It does not drop the inconvenient down a memory hole. It hangs a redesigned medal on the convenient, in the Oval Office, and calls it perhaps the best class ever assembled. Winston Smith would have recognized the ceremony. He wrote the citations for Comrade Ogilvy himself.

This is Part 10 of The Ministry of Memory, a series on the erasure of shared public memory — and the first to examine erasure’s twin, the manufacture of who gets remembered. When the president took near-total control of the Kennedy Center Honors — “about 98% involved,” by his own account, “woke” candidates vetoed, medals moved to the Oval Office, the medallion and its makers replaced — he was performing the addition half of the same job the rest of this series documents by subtraction: not deleting whom the nation must forget, but choosing whom it will be told to admire. A year on, the audience has dwindled, the building is shut, and the Honors are bound for a hockey arena with his photograph on the invitation. The canon is a kind of memory too, and it now has a single author.
