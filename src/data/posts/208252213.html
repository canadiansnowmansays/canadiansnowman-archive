Bottom Line Up Front: While this series’ companion, The Great Canadian Trade War, has spent the week on Canada’s new 50 percent tariff, the Trump administration has quietly kept the rest of the whack-a-mole game running in parallel. The Section 122 global tariff — the stopgap the administration built after the Supreme Court struck down IEEPA in February — expired at 12:01 a.m. Friday, precisely on the schedule this series flagged back in June. Its replacement is ready: Section 301 tariffs of 10 to 12.5 percent on 59 countries and the European Union, covering more than 99 percent of U.S. imports, timed to land the moment the old authority lapses. A second Section 301 wave, targeting 15 more countries over manufacturing practices, is queued behind it. And Canada’s 50 percent tariff, this series now confirms, was built on a fourth statutory vehicle entirely — Section 338 of the same 1930 Tariff Act that gave the world Smoot-Hawley, used to impose tariffs for the first time in its 96-year history. Four laws in five months. The mallet hasn’t slowed down. It’s just gotten a bigger toolbox.

The Second Wall, Global Edition

This series called it in June: Section 301 was always the planned successor to the expiring Section 122 bridge, timed to land before the clock ran out. That prediction has now fully played out. The 59-country, 10-to-12.5-percent tariff proposed in Greer’s forced-labor report is set to take effect essentially the moment Section 122 lapses — recreating, almost to the percentage point, the global tariff the courts are in the process of unwinding. Georgetown’s Peter Harrell put the mechanism plainly: the administration is using a statute Congress designed for discrete, country-specific trade disputes to instead impose what he called something closer to blanket, indefinite tariffs across nearly the entire import base — a use the statute’s authors never intended and courts have never tested at this scale.

A second batch, aimed at 15 more countries plus the EU over manufacturing practices, is queued to follow. If the pattern from Part III holds, expect the rates on this second wave to closely mirror whatever bilateral deals the administration already prefers — the tariff number arrived at first, the legal justification built to fit it after.

Canada’s Own Statute

Here is the detail this series’ Canada-focused companion piece hasn’t fully unpacked yet: the 50 percent tariff that landed on Canadian autos, dairy and alcohol this week did not come from Section 301 at all. It came from Section 338 — a provision of the 1930 Tariff Act that has sat unused for the entirety of its near-century existence until this week. Where Section 301 at least has a decade of China-tariff litigation behind it, Section 338 has none. Nobody, including the courts, knows yet how it holds up.

That makes four statutory vehicles in five months: IEEPA, struck down by the Supreme Court in February; Section 122, expiring on its own clock this week after also losing at the Court of International Trade; Section 301, now doing double duty as both the global 59-country replacement wall and the tool this series has tracked since Part III; and Section 338, debuting exclusively on America’s second-largest trading partner. Three different legal theories, deployed against three different problems, arriving at remarkably similar destinations.

The Admission

Testifying before the Senate Finance Committee this week, Jamieson Greer said the quiet part in about as many words as a U.S. Trade Representative is likely to ever say it: “The specific authorities this administration is using have changed, but the trade strategy has not.” That is, more or less, the thesis this series has been building since October. The instrument is negotiable. The outcome was never up for discussion.

Brazil, Actually Finalized

While Canada and the global 59-country wave dominate this week’s headlines, one thread from Part III quietly closed the loop: Brazil’s 25 percent Section 301 tariff — the one this series flagged as the clearest case study in the administration’s pattern, since it began as a punitive tariff over the Bolsonaro prosecution that the Supreme Court struck down, then got rebuilt on Section 301 grounds — was finalized on July 15 and took effect July 22, one day before this piece was published. A year-long investigation, a two-day public hearing in early July, and exemptions carved out for beef, orange juice, aircraft parts, coffee, and a few other politically sensitive categories. The instrument changed, as this series predicted in June. The target didn’t. The rate didn’t. Only the legal paperwork did.

The Refund War, Actually Moving

This series owes its readers a correction, not just an update. Part III reported the refund pace at roughly 13 cents on the dollar as of late May, and framed the administration’s posture as pure obstruction. That was accurate at the time. It is no longer the full picture. As of a June 29 filing, CBP had authorized $104.29 billion in refunds — nearly two-thirds of the $166 billion owed — and actually paid out $71.06 billion, including interest. That’s real money moving, not just a paper authorization sitting in a queue. Judge Eaton also got his Phase 3 order for finally-liquidated entries — the $11.4 billion category the government had resisted hardest — issued July 17, with CBP due to report progress by August 4.

That’s the honest half of the update. The other half is that friction is real and ongoing. The government did successfully shield Commissioner Rodney Scott from personally testifying — an appeals court stayed the order, and Judge Eaton ultimately let a subordinate, executive assistant commissioner Susan Thomas, testify in his place on June 9. The government is still appealing the scope of Eaton’s universal injunction, arguing it shouldn’t cover importers who never filed their own suit. And more than 8,000 approved refund declarations remain unpaid simply because CBP says it lacks banking information to send the money — a friction point that is either bureaucratic incompetence or a very convenient excuse, depending on how charitable a reader wants to be. Congress has noticed the gap too: the Tariff Refund Act of 2026 would force full refunds within 180 days of enactment, with interest, prioritizing small businesses — a sign that lawmakers don’t fully trust the executive branch to finish the job on its own.

None of this pauses the newer fight. The government is simultaneously building tariff wall number four while still working through the mechanics of returning the proceeds from wall number one — slower than it should be, faster than this series gave it credit for in June.

The Accountability Ledger

Updated from Part III:

  • Statutory vehicles attempted: Four — IEEPA, Section 122, Section 301, Section 338

  • Struck down or expired: IEEPA (SCOTUS, February 20); Section 122 (Court of International Trade ruling, plus its own 150-day clock, expiring July 24)

  • Section 301, three separate actions running at once: the 59-country global forced-labor wave (replacing Section 122); a second, 15-country manufacturing-practices wave, still queued; and Brazil’s own dedicated action, already finalized and in effect

  • Section 338: debuting exclusively on Canada, 50 percent — the highest rate of any vehicle in this sequence, and the only one never before used to impose a tariff

  • Illegal tariff revenue collected under IEEPA: $166 billion

  • Refund progress as of June 29: $104.29 billion authorized, $71.06 billion actually paid — up sharply from the roughly 13 cents on the dollar this series reported in Part III

  • Still stuck: more than 8,000 approved refunds unpaid for lack of banking information; the government’s appeal of the refund order’s scope remains pending

The mole has found a fourth hole, and Brazil’s tariffs are already in effect. The mallet hasn’t changed hands. But for once, some of the money is actually moving too.
