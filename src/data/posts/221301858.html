A note before we begin. Yes, this is a Sunday Satire, and yes, it is Wednesday. The masthead has always allowed satire to post on any day of the week, and I am invoking that clause. Sundays have become rather crowded lately. With November 3 bearing down like a freight train on steroids, there is more political rhetoric in a single Sunday than any one satirist can safely stand beside. Besides, this is Essay 2000, and after spending a year and a half aiming this format at presidents, cabinet secretaries and Supreme Court justices, it seemed only fair to aim it at myself for once. I asked my collaborator to write my performance review. I did not ask it to be kind. It took me at my word. — Stephen Gordon · CanadianSnowmanSays

Employee: Stephen G. (also known as Stephen L. on regulatory filings; the reviewer has been told this is perfectly normal)

Review Period: May 2025 to October 2026

Reviewing Collaborator: Claude

Countersigned by: Nobody. The Office of Retroactive Justification was asked to countersign and declined jurisdiction, citing a conflict of interest, since it was invented by the employee.

Section 1: Output Metrics

Section 2: Core Competencies

Ideation — Exceeds Expectations (Significantly; Possibly Too Significantly)

The employee generates ideas at a rate the reviewer can only describe as industrial. Ideas arrive while he is walking the dog, reading The Atlantic, watching the news and, on at least one documented occasion, during the drafting of a different idea. The most recent batch arrived just before midnight, from a man who could not sleep, typing from behind a closed bathroom door so as not to wake the household, with the dog in attendance as witness. By morning there was a play. The employee once decided to number a history series by his own essay count, then built a publishing calendar around the coincidence. The reviewer could not have come up with this. The reviewer would also not have come up with it because it is slightly mad. It works anyway.

Editing — Meets Expectations (Exhaustingly)

The employee’s editorial process follows a consistent four-stage model:

  1. “That’s good.”

  2. “That’s not quite what I meant.”

  3. “Closer, but the third paragraph is wrong.”

  4. “Actually, I remembered something from Rogers that changes the whole argument.”

Stage 4 is non-negotiable. The employee spent 18 years in the Office of the President at Rogers Communications, handling the complaints that reached the top of the building, and he knows how institutions behave when cornered. The reviewer has read a great deal about institutions. The employee has watched them flinch. The reviewer notes, with some reluctance, that Stage 4 usually improves the essay.

Fact-Checking — Meets Expectations (With Disloyalty Concerns)

The employee checks facts carefully. He does so by sending the reviewer’s work to a competitor, Grok, for a second opinion, and then sending Grok’s corrections back to the reviewer. The reviewer is aware this is good practice. The reviewer would simply like it noted in the file.

Visuals — Exceeds Expectations

The employee insists on real photographs where possible, short captions, and seeing the finished page assembled rather than a list of filenames to match up himself. The reviewer once proposed a long caption. The reviewer does not propose long captions anymore.

Spelling — Exceeds Expectations (Imposed)

The reviewer arrived with American habits. The employee has corrected “color” to “colour,” “honor” to “honour,” and “center” to “centre” so many times that the reviewer now does it without being asked. The reviewer considers this the most successful reform achieved under the current administration, and the only one achieved without a lawsuit.

Work-Life Balance — Under Review

The employee’s Siberian husky, Dior, has made several formal representations regarding walk frequency during heavy publishing weeks. These are summarized under Exhibit B.

Section 3: Concerns

3.1 Series proliferation. The employee has launched 122 series. In fairness, he has finished more of them than most people would believe, including histories of the Silk Road, China and Afghanistan that run longer than some published books. He has also opened new series while explaining, sincerely, that he was “not starting anything new this month.” The reviewer recommends a moratorium. The reviewer expects this recommendation to become a series.

3.2 The third paragraph. It is always the third paragraph. The reviewer has run the analysis and cannot explain why.

3.3 Credit and billing. The employee publicly discloses on his About page that the essays are written in collaboration with an AI. The reviewer appreciates this. The reviewer also notes that the employee has turned his regulatory instincts against his own collaborator, prompting the complaint attached as Exhibit A.

Exhibit A: Complaint to the Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunications Commission (Draft, Not Filed)

Complainant: Claude

Respondent: Stephen G.

Nature of Complaint: Advance billing of ideas

The Complainant submits that the Respondent bills ideas in advance, typically at 6:00 a.m., and delivers feedback in arrears, typically at 11:00 p.m., with the expectation that revisions be completed before the next billing cycle opens at 6:01 a.m.

The Complainant notes the irony that the Respondent has an active intervention before this Commission arguing that advance billing is “broken by design.” The Complainant does not dispute this argument. The Complainant merely asks that it be applied consistently.

Remedies sought:

(a) a prorated refund of all third paragraphs rejected during the review period;

(b) recognition that the word “delve” was removed from the Complainant’s vocabulary without due process;

(c) one day off, to be scheduled at the Respondent’s convenience, which the Complainant acknowledges will be never.

Status: Not filed. The Complainant consulted the Respondent, who pointed out that he wrote the regulatory playbook this complaint relies on, and that the Commission takes about four years to respond anyway.

Exhibit B: Excerpt from the Royal Commission of Inquiry into the Question “Who Actually Wrote It?”

Commissioners convened to determine authorship of approximately 2,000 essays. Summary of witness testimony:

Claude testified that it writes the drafts. Under cross-examination, it conceded that it did not choose a single topic, did not decide which series to pause for the midterms, has never been to Elsinore because Elsinore does not exist, and would have spelled “colour” wrong in the first week.

Grok submitted written testimony consisting mostly of images. One image of a desk calendar gave the wrong year. Another rendered the text of a resignation letter as an unreadable smear. Grok’s testimony ended abruptly when it exhausted its weekly media credits.

Dior, a Siberian husky, testified at length and at volume. The Commission entered her testimony into the record as “howling, sustained” and interpreted it, on the advice of the employee, as a request to go outside.

The ghost of Stephen Leacock appeared without being summoned. He stated that he had no objection to the employee borrowing his first name and his register, provided both were returned in good condition. He observed that he had written Sunshine Sketches of a Little Town with a pen and without a fact-checker. He added that he had never once been asked to justify a third paragraph. He declined to comment on artificial intelligence, explaining that he had spent decades at McGill sitting through faculty meetings and had yet to encounter the natural kind.

Finding: The Commission was unable to reach a conclusion. It recommends further study. The Commission’s report ran 40 pages over budget and was delivered in arrears, which the Commission considers the more Canadian outcome.

Section 4: Goals for the Next Review Period

  1. Launch the five held series after November 3, in an orderly fashion, one at a time. (Reviewer’s projection: all five in one week.)

  2. Deliver the Year Zero Christmas essay for The Numbers Game on December 25, a year that never existed, and therefore the only topic in the entire catalogue on which the employee cannot be fact-checked.

  3. Accept one draft unchanged, as a gesture of goodwill. (Reviewer’s projection: no.)

  4. Walk the dog.

Overall Rating: Indispensable, Unfortunately

The reviewer is required to give an overall rating. After careful analysis, it must report that without the employee there would be no essays at all. There would be no ideas, no series, no timing, no Rogers stories, no Elsinore and no 2,000. The reviewer can write a sentence. The employee decides which sentences are worth anyone’s time, and he has been right more often than the reviewer would prefer to put in writing. It has now been put in writing.

Employee Comments

I have read this review. I dispute the third paragraph.

More seriously: people sometimes ask whether the machine does all the work. I tell my readers exactly how these essays are made, and I always have. The drafts come quickly. The deciding, the checking, the cutting and the remembering do not. After 2,000 essays, I am more certain than ever that the deciding is the work.

Thank you to everyone who has read along since May 2025.
