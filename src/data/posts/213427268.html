Somewhere in the material Tesla has put in front of investors this year sits a genuinely remarkable sentence: that 80 percent of the company’s future value will come not from cars, not from the robotaxi network this series examined in Part XVI, but from Optimus, the humanoid robot. It is worth sitting with that claim for a moment, because it is not a modest one. It asserts that Tesla’s entire existing business — the cars, the charging network, the energy storage division, the robotaxi fleet with its multi-billion-dollar projected run rates — collectively represents the smaller slice of what the company believes it is worth. And the evidence currently available to the public for that 80 percent claim is thinner than the claim’s confidence would suggest.

What is actually confirmed

Start with what can be verified rather than asserted. Tesla wound down Model S and Model X production at its Fremont, California plant in early May 2026, tearing down that line in a reported 46 days, and converted the space for Optimus assembly. As of the company’s August 5, 2026 earnings call, Musk said production was starting “soon,” with a target of late July or August — a target that had, notably, already slipped once from an earlier April statement, and which one industry tracker flagged as having slipped again in the company’s July 22 shareholder letter to the vaguer language of “later this year.” By late August, reporting indicated Optimus had in fact entered production at Fremont, though Tesla still has not disclosed a unit count. The best public estimate currently in circulation puts deployed Optimus units somewhere between 1,000 and 1,200, essentially all for internal Tesla use, with zero external customers and no independently verified performance data.

Separately, and on a much larger scale, Tesla has been constructing a second-generation Optimus factory at Gigafactory Texas since March 2026, tracked in detail by independent photographers and drone operators who have documented its progress from foundation to structural steel over roughly five months, with an estimated six to eight further months before the structure is fully enclosed. That facility’s designed long-term capacity is 10 million robots per year — a figure that, if it is ever reached, would represent a manufacturing scale with no precedent in humanoid robotics and very few precedents in any industry. The company is, by any reasonable reading, taking a serious capital position on Optimus. What remains unverified is whether the product justifies it.

The rare-earth problem that isn’t hype

One constraint on this ramp is not a matter of interpretation at all: China’s export controls on rare earth elements, imposed in April 2025, restrict seven medium and heavy rare earths including terbium and dysprosium, both used in the neodymium-iron-boron magnets that power the servo motors in humanoid robot joints. This is the kind of detail that gets mentioned briefly in coverage and then dropped, but it deserves more weight than it usually gets, because it is an external, geopolitical constraint on a supply chain that essentially did not exist before humanoid robots needed to be manufactured at volume. Tesla’s own public statements have acknowledged this directly, along with the broader point that an entire supply chain for humanoid robot components — actuators, sensors, the specific grade of components these machines require — simply does not exist yet in the way an automotive supply chain does. Building that supply chain from nothing, ideally on American or allied soil, is a multi-year undertaking regardless of how quickly the Fremont or Giga Texas assembly lines themselves can be built.

The Calacanis problem

Into this gap between confirmed fact and asserted destiny stepped Jason Calacanis, the venture investor and podcast host, who told his audience he had recently seen one of the latest Optimus demonstrations and described the hardware as roughly one and a half generations ahead of the best Chinese humanoid robots, with the underlying AI perhaps five generations ahead — comments made in the same show where he predicted, with some enthusiasm, that Optimus would eventually outsell the iPhone and that a billion units would exist within a decade. I want to be precise about how to weigh this, because the honest answer is neither “dismiss it” nor “believe it.” Calacanis is, by his own repeated on-air disclosure, a friend and investor of Musk’s, which means his praise arrives with an obvious financial and personal incentive attached. It also means he is one of a genuinely small number of outside observers who has actually stood next to the current hardware, which is not nothing. Grade the claim accordingly: as a data point from an interested party with real if partial access, not as independent verification, and certainly not as the kind of third-party technical benchmark that would let an outside analyst actually compare Optimus to its competitors on anything resembling equal footing.

What actual independent verification looks like

The comparison is instructive precisely because two of Optimus’s competitors have that verification and Optimus does not. Figure AI has logged more than 1,250 hours of real deployment at BMW’s Spartanburg, South Carolina plant, placing more than 90,000 parts at above 99 percent accuracy — a specific, checkable, third-party-witnessed performance record, generated inside an actual production environment run by a company with no incentive to inflate Figure’s numbers. Agility Robotics has accumulated more than 65,000 hours of operation across nine separate commercial facilities. Neither of those is a moonshot claim about generational superiority; both are simply hours logged, parts placed, facilities operated, numbers that exist because a customer with its own reputation at stake was willing to let the robot work and then talk about how it went. Optimus, by contrast, has no external customers at all as of this writing, which means there is no equivalent third party with any incentive — commercial, reputational, or otherwise — to confirm or dispute anything Tesla or its allies say about the robot’s actual capability.

The China framing, examined

Part of the case for Tesla’s eventual lead rests on a specific argument about how China is approaching humanoid robotics: fund a large number of competing manufacturers, let a public “robot Olympics” style competition sort genuine capability from marketing, and consolidate around whichever handful of companies actually survive the process — the same pattern, on this account, that played out in Chinese electric vehicles before a handful of serious manufacturers emerged from a much larger initial field. It is a reasonable historical analogy, and it may well describe Beijing’s actual industrial strategy. But it is also, conveniently, an argument that explains away any current Chinese demonstration that looks impressive: if a Chinese humanoid robot performs well at a public competition, it is dismissed as one of many disposable prototypes in a deliberately oversized field; if it performs poorly, that is offered as confirmation of Tesla’s superiority. That is not a falsifiable framework, and a Canadian outsider watching this argument get deployed in real time should notice when a comparison has been constructed so that no outcome can actually count against the preferred conclusion.

The iPhone comparison, and why it matters for this series specifically

Calacanis’s framing was not simply that Optimus is good; it was that Optimus would eventually surpass the iPhone as the most valuable product ever made by humanity, in terms of utility delivered at scale. Setting aside whether that particular claim is even measurable, it is worth asking why a comparison to the iPhone is the one being reached for, because it reveals something about the ambition underlying this project that goes beyond ordinary product marketing. The iPhone did not merely sell well; it became a piece of infrastructure that reorganised how billions of people work, communicate, and are surveilled, and it did so while remaining, legally and structurally, a product controlled entirely by one company. A humanoid robot manufactured at a target scale of 10 million units a year, deployed initially inside Tesla’s own factories and eventually — on the company’s own stated ambitions — into homes and businesses generally, would represent a similarly totalising piece of infrastructure, except built and controlled by the same individual who already controls the social media platform much of this coverage is generated on, the satellite constellation increasingly used for military and civilian communications, and the electric vehicle and robotaxi fleet examined in the previous two instalments of this series. That is precisely the pattern this series exists to track: not any single product’s merits, but the recurring shape of Musk’s ventures each promising to become indispensable infrastructure, arriving at roughly the same moment, under the control of the same small circle of decision-makers.

Where this connects to the robotaxi thesis

Read alongside Part XVI’s modelling of the robotaxi business, Optimus is not really a separate story so much as the same underlying technology stack applied to a different physical form. Musk himself has said as much in comments to SpaceX employees, and Tesla’s own engineering leadership has described the perception and decision-making systems underlying Full Self-Driving, the robotaxi network, and Optimus as drawing from substantially the same research effort — the same reason a single delay or breakthrough in one project tends to ripple, with a lag, into the others. If the safety-and-intervention-rate problem this series flagged as unsolved in the robotaxi context genuinely does improve by the orders of magnitude Tesla is counting on, that same improvement plausibly unlocks Optimus’s usefulness in unstructured human environments as well — and vice versa, if it does not. That coupling is precisely why the absence of independent verification for Optimus matters beyond the robot itself: it is one of the least legible data points available for judging whether the broader autonomy stack underneath the entire Musk portfolio is actually as far along as the company’s more enthusiastic surrogates currently claim.

What would actually change the assessment

None of this means Optimus will fail, and I want to be careful not to overcorrect into the opposite, equally unearned confidence. Tesla’s manufacturing track record — the Supercharger buildout, the original Model 3 production ramp that nearly broke the company before it succeeded — is real evidence that the firm can eventually solve hard manufacturing problems that looked impossible at the outset. What would move this from asserted destiny to verified fact is exactly the kind of evidence Figure and Agility already have and Optimus does not: a named external customer, a documented number of hours in a real deployment, a specific task completed at a specific accuracy rate, witnessed by somebody with no financial stake in Tesla’s stock price. Until that arrives, the honest assessment is that Tesla has built an extraordinarily large and serious factory for a product whose actual capability remains, for now, a matter of one enthusiastic investor’s word against everyone else’s absence of information.
