In February 2026, a United States senator asked a simple question.

The senator was Chris Murphy of Connecticut. The man he was questioning was Jeremy Carl, a 53-year-old senior fellow at the Claremont Institute whom the Trump administration had nominated for a senior State Department position. Carl had built his career on a specific argument: that white Americans were under threat, that anti-white racism was “tearing America apart,” and that the country’s historically European-centered identity was being deliberately eroded by immigration and diversity policies.

Murphy’s question was not a trick. It was, given Carl’s career, the most obvious question in the room.

“Tell me,” Murphy said, “the white values that you believe are being erased.”

Carl could not answer.

He tried. He offered “Scotch-Irish military culture.” He suggested that the white church differed from the Black church “in terms of its tone and style.” He mentioned foodways. He mentioned music. Murphy, to his credit, did not pretend these were adequate answers. “So our ability to access white churches or white food or white music is being erased?” he asked. The nomination failed.

But the failure of Jeremy Carl to define what he claims to be defending should not be mistaken for the failure of the movement he represents. As Thomas Chatterton Williams documents in The Atlantic, the white identitarian project is more politically and culturally influential today than at any point in the past half century. It has a Vice President. It has executive orders. It has a refugee program that prioritizes Afrikaners. It has a theory of history that is, at its core, the most explicit rejection of the creedal nation model since the Civil War.

This part of the series examines that theory, where it comes from, and why it is not merely wrong but specifically, structurally incompatible with what America has always claimed to be.

I. The Vacuum and What Filled It

To understand the white identitarian moment, you have to understand the vacuum it filled.

For most of the post-civil-rights era, white Americans occupied an unusual position in the American racial conversation: they were, culturally, the default. Whiteness was, as Chatterton Williams observes, the water white Americans swam in — so ambient as to be nearly invisible. Race was something other people had. Identity was something other people emphasized. The consensus historians’ America was, in this sense, a white America that did not quite know it was a white America — that presented its particular cultural inheritance as the universal American inheritance, available to all who subscribed to it.

The civil rights movement, the subsequent explosion of multicultural scholarship, and the progressive cultural politics of the 1990s and 2000s began to name this default. They pointed out that what had been presented as universal was, in fact, particular — that the consensus America was a white, male, Protestant America that had presented its specific experience as the human norm. This critique was, in its essential argument, correct. It was also, in its political execution, often delivered in ways that produced exactly the reaction its proponents did not intend.

By the 2010s, whiteness had been so thoroughly named as a category of structural power that some corners of progressive culture had begun to treat it primarily as a source of shame rather than a fact requiring analysis. “White” became, in some institutional contexts, a term of mild disparagement — associated reflexively with privilege, fragility, and complicity. At the same moment, diversity programs in universities, corporations, and public institutions explicitly deprioritized white applicants in ways that were, at minimum, experienced as discriminatory by white Americans who did not feel powerful.

The political backlash was predictable. What was not fully predicted — or not predicted seriously enough, by the institutions that should have been paying attention — was the speed and ambition with which the backlash was organized, theorized, and weaponized.

The Charlottesville march of August 2017, with its tiki torches and its chant of “You will not replace us,” was the first mass public demonstration of the new white identity politics. It was easy, in retrospect, to misread Charlottesville as a fringe event — the last convulsion of an old racism unable to compete in the modern world. It was not. It was a preview.

II. The Vance Doctrine

The most important statement of the white identitarian position has not come from Jeremy Carl or from the marchers at Charlottesville. It has come from the Vice President of the United States.

Last July, J.D. Vance joined a debate about the then-New York City mayoral candidate Zohran Mamdani, who had said that he was “proud of our country even as we constantly strive to make it better.” This is, verbatim, the mainstream school’s definition of patriotism — the love-as-process formulation that Gal Beckerman identifies as perhaps the most intellectually defensible version of American patriotism available. It is also what Obama said at Selma. It is what King said his whole career. It is the tradition of the promissory note.

Vance attacked it. He suggested that Mamdani’s conditional pride — the qualifier “even as we constantly strive to make it better” — represented insufficient gratitude and deference to America’s past and past Americans. “Who the hell do these people think they are?” he asked. Then he said the thing that matters most: “America is not just an idea. We’re a particular place, with a particular people.”

That sentence is the clearest statement of the anti-creedal position that any senior American official has made in the post-civil-rights era. It is worth examining with some care.

“America is not just an idea” is, in part, a defensible observation. No country is purely an idea. Countries have territories, traditions, institutions, cultures, histories — all of which shape and constrain the ideas they claim to embody. The creedal nation model does not require pretending that America is a disembodied proposition floating in space. It requires treating the proposition as the organizing principle of membership and identity — the thing that determines who belongs and what belonging entails.

“We’re a particular place, with a particular people” is not a defensible observation. It is a political position — specifically, the position that “people” in the American sense is not defined by subscription to the creed but by something prior to it. By ancestry. By heritage. By being the kind of people who were already here. By being, as the white identitarian movement puts it with varying degrees of explicitness, European.

This is the blood-and-soil model. It is what the creedal nation was invented to supersede. And it is being advanced, openly and without apology, by the Vice President of the United States in the 250th anniversary year of a republic founded on the opposite principle.

III. The Historical Irony

The deepest irony of the white identitarian moment is historical, and it deserves to be named.

The “heritage Americans” whose inheritance the white identitarian movement claims to protect are, virtually without exception, the descendants of people who were not considered adequately American when they arrived.

The Irish, who came in the millions fleeing famine in the 1840s, were met with signs reading “No Irish Need Apply” and with a sustained campaign to portray them as racially unfit for American democracy — too Catholic, too clannish, too different from the Anglo-Protestant mainstream to ever become real Americans. They became real Americans. Their descendants are now among the most enthusiastic proponents of the heritage American framework.

The Italians, the Poles, the Eastern Europeans who arrived in the great waves of immigration between 1880 and 1924 were characterized by the immigration restrictionists of their day in terms almost identical to those used by today’s restrictionists about today’s immigrants. They were unassimilable. They were racially inferior. They would dilute the Anglo-Saxon stock. Their presence would undermine American institutions. They became Americans. Their grandchildren and great-grandchildren are, disproportionately, among the voters who support the very immigration restrictions their ancestors faced.

As Chatterton Williams documents, the white identitarian project seeks to close the door behind the last group that walked through it — a pattern so consistent across American history that it has its own name: nativism. Every generation of nativists has been wrong about the group it sought to exclude. The Irish did not destroy American institutions. The Italians did not undermine American culture. The Eastern European Jews who arrived between 1880 and 1924 — including the grandfathers of the historian whose commission-room scene opens this series — did not dilute the American character. They became it, in ways that the exclusionists could not predict and would not have chosen.

There is no reason to believe that the pattern has reversed. The evidence of 250 years is that diversity has been, for a creedal nation, a source of extraordinary strength — that the creedal model, precisely because it does not require ancestry, has been able to draw talent, ambition, and commitment from every corner of the world in ways that no ethnic nation can match. This is the argument of The American Contradiction, and the evidence for it is extensive.

IV. The Strange Convergence, Revisited

Part V of this series identified Johann Neem’s observation of a strange convergence: the far right and the far left have arrived, by entirely different routes, at the same conclusion — that American history is fundamentally a story about whiteness.

The white identitarian moment makes this convergence concrete and consequential.

The post-American left argues that America is defined by white supremacy and that the creedal narrative is a myth deployed to obscure this reality. The hyper-American right argues that America is defined by its European heritage and that the creedal narrative is a liberal construct designed to dilute this heritage. Both positions treat whiteness as the operative category of American identity — as the thing that the creedal story is either concealing (in the left’s version) or defending (in the right’s version).

What neither position can accommodate is the woman who wrote to Jill Lepore after reading These Truths — the immigrant who studied for her citizenship exam and felt, for the first time, that she belonged, because she understood the whole journey of the story. She is the creedal nation’s vindication: someone from outside the founding ethnic group who subscribed to the proposition and found that the subscription was genuine, that the welcome was real, that the promise — however imperfectly kept, however burdened with contradiction — was available to her.

The white identitarian movement would tell her the welcome was contingent. The post-American left would tell her the welcome was a lie. The mainstream school would tell her something more demanding and more honest: that the welcome was real, that it was also incomplete, and that the ongoing work of making it fully real is part of the story she has now joined.

She chose the mainstream school. Most naturalized citizens do. They tend to be, as the data consistently shows, more patriotic than native-born Americans, more trusting of institutions, more committed to the ongoing argument about the creed. This makes sense. They chose the country. They know what the alternative looks like. They are, in the most direct way possible, believers in the creedal bargain.

The white identitarian movement is at war with them. Not primarily because of anything specific about their beliefs or their politics or their behavior — but because their very presence, and the legitimacy of their belonging, is evidence against the “particular people” thesis. A creedal nation that actually works — that takes in people from every corner of the world and makes them genuinely American — is a standing refutation of the claim that America is a particular place with a particular people. And so the particular people argument requires, at some level, that the creedal nation not work.

That is the political logic. It is also, for a nation that has drawn its greatest strength from the creedal model, the most dangerous bet it has ever made.

V. What the Identitarians Cannot Explain

Jeremy Carl could not define white culture when Chris Murphy asked him. I want to suggest that this was not a failure of preparation. It was a structural feature of the argument he was making.

White culture, as a category, is not coherent — not because whiteness does not exist as a social and political fact, but because what has historically been called “white culture” in America is, on inspection, a collection of other things: English Protestantism, Scottish-Irish frontier culture, German immigrant traditions, Italian neighborhood culture, Jewish intellectual life, and dozens of other distinct inheritances that have been folded, over time, into a generic “whiteness” that obscures their specificity. The things that are genuinely distinctive about these cultures — their music, their foodways, their religious traditions, their literary inheritances — are not under threat. They are, in most cases, thriving, precisely because they have continued to absorb and be absorbed by other cultures in the long process of American cultural formation.

What the white identitarian movement is actually defending is not a culture. It is a position — the position of default, of unearned centrality, of not having to think about your identity because your identity is the norm. That position is eroding. And the erosion of an unfair advantage is not, in any meaningful sense, discrimination.

The creedal nation has always required that its particular inheritances — all of them, including the European ones — be available as contributions to a common project rather than as exclusive claims. The Irish contributed their labor and their political organization and their literature. The Italians contributed their food and their craftsmanship and their music. The Jews contributed their intellectual tradition and their civil rights activism and their comedy. The Black Americans contributed their music and their theology and their political courage and, most fundamentally, their insistence that the creed mean what it says — which is the single most important contribution to the ongoing project of American democracy.

None of these contributions required their authors to be the particular people. All of them required their authors to subscribe.

That is the creedal bargain. That is what the identitarians, on both the right and the left, cannot accommodate. And that is why the birthday of the creedal nation, at 250, finds the creedal model under more serious attack than at any point since the question was settled, at the cost of 620,000 lives, between 1861 and 1865.
