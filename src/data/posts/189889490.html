February 28, 2026, early morning. The United States and Israel launch Operation Epic Fury against Iran, killing Supreme Leader Ayatollah Khamenei and triggering what President Donald Trump calls a “massive and ongoing” war.

March 3, 2026, late evening. While American bombs still fall on Iran, U.S. Southern Command announces via social media that American and Ecuadorian forces have launched joint military operations against “designated terrorist organizations” in Ecuador.

In both cases—and in five other countries where Trump ordered military strikes in 2025-2026—Congress never voted to authorize war.

Congress was not consulted in advance.

In some cases, Congress wasn’t even notified until after the bombs started falling.

This is not an accident. This is not a legal gray area. This is not the fog of war or the exigencies of national security or the complexities of modern warfare.

This is a system.

Donald Trump has engineered every military operation of his second presidency to evade the constitutional requirement for congressional authorization. He has done this through a combination of legal fictions, procedural manipulations, and strategic timing so systematic that it amounts to a doctrine: wage war however you want, wherever you want, against whomever you want, and dare Congress to stop you.

They won’t.

III. THE FOURTEEN EVASION TECHNIQUES

Over the course of 2025-2026, Trump and his legal advisors perfected a systematic approach to evading congressional war powers. Here are the fourteen techniques, documented across multiple operations:

A. LEGAL AND DEFINITIONAL EVASION

1. Call it “law enforcement” instead of war

The most brazen example: Venezuela.

On January 3, 2026, Trump ordered Delta Force commandos to raid President Nicolás Maduro’s residence in Caracas, killing approximately 75 Cuban and Venezuelan guards, kidnapping Maduro and his wife Cilia Flores, and transporting them to New York to face federal narcoterrorism charges.

This was not called an invasion. It was not called regime change. It was not called war.

It was called “executing a federal arrest warrant.”

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth characterized the six-month campaign of boat strikes leading up to the Maduro extraction as “counternarcotics law enforcement.” Because it was “law enforcement” rather than “military action,” the administration argued, it did not require congressional authorization under the War Powers Act.

Never mind that 151 people had been killed in these “law enforcement” operations. Never mind that the “arrest” involved a military assault on a sovereign nation’s capital, the killing of its security forces, and the forcible removal of its head of state. Because the Justice Department had issued an indictment, Trump claimed this was a domestic law enforcement operation that happened to occur in another country.

The Brennan Center for Justice called this argument “absurd,” noting that “it’s absurd to suggest that the indictments against Maduro for cocaine trafficking and firearm possession, first handed down years ago, create a basis for war and regime change.”

But absurd or not, it worked. Congress held no vote. The operation continued.

2. Invoke the 2001 AUMF as blanket authorization

After the September 11, 2001 attacks, Congress passed the Authorization for Use of Military Force (AUMF) against “those nations, organizations, or persons [the President] determines planned, authorized, committed, or aided the terrorist attacks.”

Twenty-five years later, Trump is still using it.

Syria, Iraq, Somalia, Nigeria—all strikes justified as “continuation” of existing counterterrorism operations under the 2001 AUMF. Never mind that many of the groups being targeted didn’t exist in 2001. Never mind that the operations have nothing to do with 9/11 or al-Qaeda. Any strike against any group labeled “terrorist” gets categorized as a continuation of the original authorization.

The 2001 AUMF has become what legal scholars call a “blank check for global war.” Trump is simply cashing it more aggressively than his predecessors.

3. Claim “defensive action” or “collective self-defense”

Iran presented a different legal challenge. Unlike Venezuela or the boat strikes, there was no pretense of law enforcement. Unlike Syria or Somalia, there was no 2001 AUMF justification.

So Trump’s lawyers invoked “collective self-defense with Israel.”

The argument: Israel faces threats from Iran’s nuclear program. The United States has a defense relationship with Israel. Therefore, American strikes on Iran constitute “collective self-defense” rather than offensive war.

Rachel VanLandingham, former chief of international law at U.S. Central Command, told The Intercept this was legally indefensible: “Not only does this violate international law in numerous respects, it clearly violates the U.S. Constitution and the War Powers Resolution.”

The Constitution’s self-defense exception applies when the United States faces an imminent attack. There was no imminent Iranian attack on the United States. There wasn’t even credible intelligence of Iranian attack planning. But by stretching “self-defense” to include potential future threats to an ally, Trump created legal cover for preemptive war.

4. Expand the definition of “imminent threat”

Related to the self-defense claim: redefining what counts as “imminent.”

Traditionally, an imminent threat means an attack is about to occur—hours or days away. Trump’s lawyers have expanded this to mean any potential future threat, no matter how distant or speculative.

Iran’s nuclear facilities, struck in June 2025, were justified on the grounds that Iran might someday develop nuclear weapons that could threaten the United States. This despite no evidence Iran was close to a weapon, and despite the fact that Iran had been complying with international monitoring.

If “imminent” means “could happen eventually,” then any country developing any military capability becomes an imminent threat justifying American attack. The concept loses all meaning.

5. Designate targets as Foreign Terrorist Organizations

Here’s how the FTO designation works in practice:

Step 1: Designate a group as a Foreign Terrorist Organization Step 2: Claim operations against FTOs fall under existing counterterrorism authorities Step 3: No new congressional authorization required

Ecuador demonstrates this perfectly. In September 2025, the State Department designated two Ecuadorian criminal gangs—Los Lobos and Los Choneros—as FTOs. Five months later, in March 2026, Trump announced joint U.S.-Ecuadorian military operations against these “designated terrorist organizations.”

Because the groups were FTOs, the administration argued, operations against them were covered by counterterrorism authorities. Never mind that these were domestic Ecuadorian criminal organizations with no connection to international terrorism. The designation made them fair game.

The same logic applied to Venezuela: Trump designated Cartel of the Suns and Tren de Aragua as FTOs, then used that designation to justify military operations as counterterrorism.

The FTO designation has become a magic wand: designate any group you want to attack, claim counterterrorism authority, skip congressional authorization.

B. PROCEDURAL EVASION

6. Brief only the “Gang of Eight”

Historical image of War Powers Resolution documents or signing.

The War Powers Resolution requires the president to notify Congress when introducing U.S. forces into hostilities. Trump’s interpretation: brief eight congressional leaders and call it compliance.

The “Gang of Eight” consists of:

  • House Speaker and Minority Leader

  • Senate Majority and Minority Leaders

  • Chairs and ranking members of House and Senate Intelligence Committees

These eight people get briefed on sensitive intelligence operations. They do not represent congressional authorization. They are not empowered to approve military action. They are simply informed—often after the fact.

But Trump treats Gang of Eight briefings as satisfying the congressional notification requirement. The War Powers Resolution contemplates reporting to Congress as an institution. Trump reports to eight people and calls it good enough.

7. Brief only Republicans when required

When Trump struck Iranian nuclear facilities in June 2025, he took the partisan approach even further: he briefed the Republican members of the Gang of Eight but excluded the Democrats.

Congressional Democrats discovered they’d been cut out of the notification only after the strikes occurred. This wasn’t just a violation of the War Powers Resolution—it was a violation of basic intelligence community protocols that require bipartisan briefings.

But there was no consequence. The strikes continued. And when Trump needed to brief Congress on subsequent Iran operations, he went back to the full Gang of Eight—not because Democrats threatened consequences for the exclusion, but because there was no benefit to excluding them the second time.

8. Notify AFTER operations begin

Venezuela, January 3, 2026: Zero advance congressional notification.

The first members of Congress learned about Operation Absolute Resolve when Trump announced it at a press conference. As PBS reported, “The Trump administration informed congressional leaders about the strikes only after the Venezuela operation began.”

Senator Mark Warner, vice chair of the Senate Intelligence Committee, confirmed he was called by Secretary of State Marco Rubio the night of the operation—but only after the helicopters were already approaching Caracas.

Ecuador, March 3, 2026: Announced via U.S. Southern Command social media post after operations had launched.

The War Powers Resolution requires notification within 48 hours of introducing forces into hostilities. Trump’s practice: notify during or after, claim the 48-hour deadline technically met, and continue operations.

9. Truth Social posts = congressional reporting

For Iran, Trump posted an eight-minute video on Truth Social announcing the strikes and declaring it a “massive and ongoing operation.”

Administration officials later pointed to this social media post as fulfilling the War Powers Resolution’s reporting requirement.

Legal experts were incredulous. The War Powers Resolution envisions a formal written report to Congress detailing the legal basis, objectives, and expected scope of military action. An eight-minute Truth Social video does not satisfy this requirement.

But again: no consequence. The strikes continued.

10. Claim “mission security” prevents notification

When challenged on the lack of advance congressional notification for Venezuela, Trump was direct: “Congress will leak, and we don’t want leakers.”

Secretary of State Marco Rubio elaborated: “This was not the kind of mission that you can do congressional notification on. It was a trigger-based mission.”

The argument: operational security requires keeping Congress in the dark. Advance notification would compromise the mission. Therefore, the constitutional requirement for congressional consultation can be ignored on national security grounds.

This is circular reasoning at its finest: the president decides Congress can’t be trusted with notification, uses that distrust to justify skipping notification, and claims national security as the reason Congress can’t object.

C. STRATEGIC EVASION

11. Keep operations under the 60-day threshold

The War Powers Resolution allows the president to commit forces for 60 days without congressional authorization, with a possible 30-day extension. After 90 days, authorization is required.

Trump’s approach: start operations, pause before the deadline, restart as a “new” operation.

The boat strikes demonstrate this perfectly. The campaign began September 2, 2025. By late October, the 60-day mark was approaching. Rather than seek authorization, operations “paused” briefly, then resumed under the expanded rubric of “Operation Southern Spear.”

Each pause-and-restart resets the clock. The campaign continues indefinitely, but because it’s technically a series of operations rather than one continuous engagement, the 90-day limit never applies.

12. Use partner forces for ground operations

Ecuador reveals this technique clearly.

According to U.S. officials who spoke to the New York Times and ABC News, American special forces in Ecuador provide “planning, intelligence, and operational support” to Ecuadorian commandos. The Ecuadorians conduct the actual raids. Americans advise but don’t participate in combat.

This allows the administration to claim U.S. forces aren’t “engaged in hostilities”—they’re merely supporting partner forces. If they’re not in hostilities, the War Powers Act doesn’t apply.

Never mind that the Americans are directing the operations, providing the intelligence, planning the missions, and would defend themselves if fired upon. Because Ecuadorians pull the triggers, Trump claims no congressional authorization is needed.

13. Declare the War Powers Act unconstitutional

On January 8, 2026, five days after the Venezuela operation, Trump posted on Truth Social:

“The War Powers Act is Unconstitutional, totally violating Article II of the Constitution, as all Presidents, and their Departments of Justice, have determined before me.”

This is factually wrong—courts have never ruled the War Powers Resolution unconstitutional, though they’ve declined to rule on its constitutionality in the eight cases that have reached them.

But accuracy doesn’t matter. What matters is the declaration itself: Trump is asserting that he has unlimited war-making authority under Article II, and that Congress has no power to constrain him.

Previous presidents violated the War Powers Resolution while maintaining the legal fiction that it was constitutional. Trump rejects the fiction. He claims the resolution is invalid and he’s not bound by it.

14. Expand existing missions to avoid new authorization

Syria shows this technique at work.

The United States has been conducting counterterrorism operations in Syria since 2014. When two U.S. soldiers and a civilian interpreter were killed in a December 2025 attack on a base in Palmyra, Trump responded by striking 35 ISIS targets in January 2026.

This wasn’t characterized as a new operation requiring authorization. It was an “expansion” of the existing Syria mission—a proportional response to an attack on American forces.

Never mind that the scale and intensity exceeded anything conducted under previous administrations. Because the legal predicate for the Syria presence already existed, Trump claimed no new authorization was needed.

Every expansion, every escalation, gets characterized as a continuation of existing operations. New authorizations are never required because everything is framed as old.

VI. THE CONSTITUTIONAL CRISIS

What the Constitution says:

Article I, Section 8: Congress shall have power to “declare War.”

Article II: The President is “Commander in Chief” of the armed forces.

The Framers’ intent was clear: Congress decides whether to go to war. The President commands the forces once war is declared. Defensive action against imminent attack doesn’t require prior authorization, but offensive military operations do.

What Trump does:

Offensive military operations across seven countries. More than 1,000 people killed. A Supreme Leader assassinated. A head of state kidnapped. Zero congressional authorization. Zero advance notification in multiple cases.

And when Congress tries to assert its authority—twice in the Senate on Venezuela—Trump ignores the resolution and continues operations.

Legal scholars’ assessment:

David Janovsky (Acting Director, Constitution Project, Project on Government Oversight): “There is no track record for a president being held to account in a legal process for an unconstitutional war. The main remedies run through Congress—votes to end or restrict the military action, aggressive oversight, or even impeachment.”

Brennan Center for Justice: “Unaccountable war-making was one of the chief dangers the Constitution’s framers sought to prevent. That’s why they took care to give the power to declare war to Congress, not a president modeled on the British monarch.”

As historian Robert Kagan has worried, Trump could use a foreign war as a pretext to consolidate control domestically.”
