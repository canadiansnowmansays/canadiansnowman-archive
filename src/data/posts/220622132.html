Part IV ended on Wednesday night, October 14, 2026, with Ukraine still holding its refinery drones on the ground, three days into a ceasefire that bound nobody else. It had lost its early warnings twice, had its drones throttled, and watched all three of Kyiv’s heat-and-power plants burn.

This essay is about what Ukraine did next. It is, for a change, a story about the cards Ukraine holds rather than the ones it has been told to fold.

“The block is out”

On Thursday morning, October 15, Ukraine stopped waiting.

In an interview with the Financial Times, given in London the day before and reported by Francesca Regalado for the New York Times, Volodymyr Zelensky was asked how much longer Ukraine would keep its refinery strikes on hold. “That’s it,” he said. “The block is out.” Within hours, the governor of Russia’s Ryazan region reported a fire at an oil refinery after an overnight drone strike. On Sunday, October 18, Ukrainian drones set a large oil terminal in St. Petersburg ablaze, as Constant Méheut reported for the New York Times. The terminal exports about 120,000 barrels a day of refined products, mostly fuel oil, according to Kpler data cited by CNBC.

Zelensky also said, for the first time on the record, what his officials had only hinted at. American military officials, he said, had told their Ukrainian counterparts: “If you will not stop, we will block intelligence.” American officials deny any threat. On the missed missile warnings, he admitted he did not know the cause: “Block or technical problem or will, I don’t know.” On Starshield, he had no doubt. “It was not a technical problem,” he said. “It was the will.”

He was angry and let it show. “Business is interesting between the United States and Russia,” he said. “I think that they want to have energy projects.” And of Putin: “He’s the real evil of today. How can Trump be friends with him? I think it’s a mistake.”

The pause had never been Kyiv’s idea. For months, as Anton Troianovski and Luke Broadwater reported for the New York Times on October 15, Trump’s envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, and Vice President JD Vance, had each asked Zelensky not to strike Russian refineries. On September 13, Trump said it in public: “Mr. Zelensky has to do one thing: He has to stop knocking out diesel fuel in Russia.” Kyiv offered to leave Russia’s refineries alone if Russia left Ukraine’s power stations alone. Putin refused, and Trump lifted the diesel sanctions anyway. Most of the world’s diesel shortage, the Times added, comes from the war on Iran, not from Ukraine’s drones.

Not every friend of Ukraine in Washington welcomed the defiance. Meaghan Mobbs, a conservative activist whose father, Keith Kellogg, was Trump’s envoy to Ukraine last year, said Putin had embarrassed the President by breaking the energy ceasefire almost at once: “I don’t know of a bigger middle finger than that.” But she feared what Zelensky’s words would do in the Oval Office. “It’s not going to end well,” she said, “based on everything that we know about the president’s psychology.”

Taught by the Americans

There is an irony here that Washington would rather not dwell on. The refinery campaign Trump wants stopped was sharpened with American help.

Julian E. Barnes, John Ismay and Edward Wong of the New York Times reported on October 15 that from June 2025, U.S. intelligence officials helped Ukraine improve the accuracy of its drone strikes on Russia’s energy infrastructure, particularly its oil refineries. The administration never gave blanket approval for strikes inside Russia, they wrote, “but the United States has covertly helped — with Mr. Trump’s permission.”

The rest of the ledger runs the other way. Under Biden, the United States sent Ukraine about $67 billion in weapons and funds to buy them. Pete Hegseth gave up America’s leadership of the Ukraine Defense Contact Group soon after taking office, and Trump cut off direct supplies of Patriot missiles; only a small number still arrive each month, bought with allies’ help.

What is left of the partnership is mostly intelligence, the thread Ukraine fears will be cut next. But a skill, once taught, cannot be recalled. Ukraine’s engineers learned where a Russian refinery is weakest. They still know.

Plenty of other targets

Ukraine was never as disarmed as the word “pause” suggests.

When Trump first told Kyiv to leave Russia’s refineries alone, he tempered the demand. “There are plenty of other targets,” he said. Ukraine, as Simon Shuster reported for The Atlantic on October 15, 2026, took him at his word.

Since October 8, Ukraine has struck at least four Russian data centres, most belonging to Yandex, Russia’s answer to Google. Banking, delivery, map and taxi apps went down for millions of Russians. Zelensky noted a side effect: Russian bot activity across the internet fell sharply. “There was less hate, less division, and fewer provocations,” he said, “because there were fewer bots.”

Then there are the airports. Since early September, Ukrainian drones have interfered with air traffic over Moscow almost every day, under an operation code-named M&Ms. By the tally of the Russian outlet Echo of Moscow, 345 flights were cancelled at the capital’s airports in a single week.

And on Friday, October 9, the very day Putin spent 90 minutes on the phone with Trump arranging the diesel deal, all four of Moscow’s airports shut while the Russian president was flying home from Turkmenistan. His plane, the so-called Flying Kremlin, was left circling over his own capital with nowhere to land. “It was purely coincidental,” a Ukrainian officer told Shuster. “Just a matter of chance.”

I will confess that this is the first image from this war that has made me laugh. Stephen Leacock would have built a whole sketch around it: the autocrat who had just talked the leader of the free world into lifting his sanctions, unable to get his own aeroplane onto his own runway.

What matters more than the comedy is this. The two architects of the airport campaign told Shuster that it relies on Ukrainian-built, AI-guided drones and uses no American technology or intelligence. Whatever Washington withholds, this card stays in Ukraine’s hand.

The card Zelensky has not played

The full version of M&Ms has not yet been used, and the reason is to Ukraine’s credit.

When officials first brought Zelensky the plan at the start of 2026, it called for as many as 1,000 drones a day over Moscow’s airports, enough to make civilian flights impossible. He judged it too dangerous. Even now, his forces clog the airspace near Russian airports rather than strike them. One adviser explained why the president will not target civilian aircraft: “That would be piracy. The president doesn’t want to allow that.”

“It’s not fair for one side to break all the rules of war while the other side holds back,” a senior European official told Shuster. Perhaps not. But a country under bombardment that still refuses to aim at airliners is drawing a line its enemy erased long ago.

Russia was losing

The other card is on the ground, and it is a strong one.

By the count of the Institute for the Study of War, cited by David French in the New York Times on October 15, 2026, Russia has made a net gain of only about 100 square kilometres in all of 2026, while Ukrainian counteroffensives have liberated at least 1,046. “Russia’s summer offensive has failed,” the institute reported, “and Russia’s fall offensive is likely to fail as well.”

Gen. David Petraeus told French that Ukraine’s long-range campaign has sunk roughly 300 Russian vessels in the Black Sea and nearby waters, and has isolated Crimea so well that “they’re essentially starving Crimea of fuel.” Russia is one of the world’s great oil producers, “and yet increasingly, that doesn’t mean that Russians can find gasoline.”

That is the context for everything Washington did this month. As French’s headline put it: Russia was losing. Enter President Trump.

Russia’s answer

None of this comes free. Russia is now trying something history says rarely works: bombing a country into surrender. “Punishing the adversaries’ civilian population and trying to break its will has a very poor historical track record,” Seth G. Jones of the Center for Strategic and International Studies told Constant Méheut of the New York Times. Harry Nedelcu of Rasmussen Global put it more simply: “The only card they have to pressure the Ukrainians are these air attacks. They don’t have anything else.”

Since Ukraine began its airport campaign, Russia has stepped up daytime strikes on apartment blocks and schools. Trains have become a favourite target. Oleksandr Pertsovskyi, who runs Ukraine’s state railway, told Shuster that 37 locomotives were attacked in August and 47 in September. “This is how my mornings start now,” he said, scrolling through photographs of gutted sleeper carriages, “with these reports and images, every day.”

The war at sea goes on too. On Tuesday, October 13, drones struck two ships off Bulgaria’s coast, as Lara Jakes and Boryana Dzhambazova reported for the New York Times. The HS Dignity sank quickly; only five of its 16 crew were rescued. When Moscow’s ambassador suggested a third party, Bulgaria’s prime minister, Rumen Radev, replied that one ship was registered in Odesa: “There is no logic for Ukraine to destroy its own ships.”

The other side’s dead

A series that claims to tell the truth has to tell this part too. Ukraine’s strikes deep inside Russia have also killed civilians, among them foreign workers drawn by Russia’s labour shortage.

Valerie Hopkins, Showkat Nanda and Paul Sonne reported for the New York Times on Ramaya Anjarana, a 29-year-old steel fitter from India, killed in May at the Moscow refinery where he worked, and on Delnoz Yusupova and Umedjon Yusupov, a Tajik couple killed outside Moscow on September 20, their 22nd wedding anniversary. “My brother and his wife were born on the same day, in the same year,” his brother said. “And they died on the same day, together.”

Russian strikes in Ukraine have killed far more civilians; the United Nations counted at least 200 dead and 1,234 injured in Ukraine between September 1 and 24 alone. That does not make these deaths smaller, and I will not pretend they did not happen.

“Russia will not collapse”

It would be easy to end on the Flying Kremlin and call it a good few days. Ukraine’s own people will not let me.

One of the officials behind the airport campaign told Shuster that if the United States sides with Russia, Ukraine may have little choice but to step off the ladder of escalation and look for peace. By throwing a lifeline to Russia’s energy industry, Washington has undercut the strategy of wearing Russia down. “That means we need to change our strategy,” he said. “Russia will not collapse, at least not now.”

It is not defeatism. It is a country thinking clearly about what it can do on its own and what it can do only with friends. It is also, precisely, the outcome Putin’s strategy is built to produce, and the one Washington has made more likely.

Losing America

By the end of the week, Zelensky had said it out loud. Asked by Piers Morgan whether Ukraine was “losing” the United States, he replied: “Yes.” Trump answered on Newsmax: “Zelensky is not a good guy for us to deal with.” On Friday, October 16, in Belgium, Zelensky was asked whether he was being made a scapegoat for Trump’s problems. The former comedian, as Kim Barker reported for the New York Times, gave a sideways smile and let the pause run until the room laughed. Then he leaned into the microphone: “This is my answer.” Two days later, Trump gave his. Zelensky, he wrote on Sunday, “doesn’t want to end this war, and probably never did.” Zelensky has called the President’s decisions “weak,” a word he knows will sting, as David E. Sanger reported for the New York Times.

The Treasury Secretary added a warning. On NBC’s Meet the Press on Sunday, Scott Bessent called it “very reckless” of Zelensky to blame the United States for the Starshield restrictions. “President Zelenskyy should be very careful about making statements like this that aren’t true,” he said, as Garrett Downs reported for CNBC. The President has said: “It wasn’t me, and I don’t think it was Elon.” Elon Musk has written that “any limits on speed or usage are decided by the US military and ultimately POTUS.” Those two statements cannot both be true. On Tuesday, the Wall Street Journal reported that Musk himself had ordered the slowdown, as Jonathan Lemire and Simon Shuster noted in The Atlantic. If that holds, the statement that was not true was his. Then, on Wednesday, Secretary of State Marco Rubio said it was SpaceX that decides. Brig. Gen. Andriy Biletsky, who commands Ukraine’s Center Grouping of Forces, offered a way they could all be telling a version of the truth: a 100-mile-an-hour limit had always been on the books, he said, but had never been enforced until last week, as Siobhán O’Grady, Carlotta Gall and Anton Troianovski reported for the New York Times. For the drones that chase Russia’s jets, that is the difference between defending a city and watching it burn.

How much would losing America cost? Less than it once would have, argues Nico Lange, a former German defence official now at the Munich Security Conference. Ukraine has built up its own arms industry. “There is still a public impression, and also the impression maybe with Trump, that Ukraine will lose the war if the U.S. can’t help anymore,” he told Barker. “But this is not true.”

There are two things Ukraine cannot yet make for itself: American early warning of ballistic and ship-launched missiles, and the Patriot interceptor, the only weapon it can get that brings down Russian ballistic missiles. Without both, Lange said, Russia could strike with impunity. Solomiia Bobrovska, of the Ukrainian parliament’s defence committee, wants allies to start finding alternatives now. She was clear about what is not on offer: “If you want to hear from me the words ‘peace’ and ‘capitulation,’ it’s not an option at all.”

By Sunday, Zelensky was saying aloud what Bobrovska feared. “I believe steps have been taken to convince Ukraine to capitulate,” he told Bloomberg News, as Constant Méheut reported for the New York Times. He did not want to believe Washington was seeking a quicker end to the war at Ukraine’s expense, he said. “But that’s how it looks.” Asked on Meet the Press whether Ukraine could survive without American help, he did not pretend: “We will have a low chance — low chance to survive without support of the United States.

Richard Haass, who served presidents of both parties, could think of nothing like it. “The reason it’s hard to think of a precedent of America switching sides in a conflict like this is that there really isn’t one,” he told David E. Sanger of the New York Times. “What’s unprecedented here is the decision to reverse course and penalize the victim of the invasion — Ukraine — while rewarding the aggressor.

The scorecard

At the close of Part III, I said this series would keep track of four things. I am adding a fifth.

Is Russia respecting any ceasefire? No. It struck Ukrainian energy within hours of Trump’s announcement and fired the largest ballistic salvo of the war at Kyiv’s power plants. Ukraine held its refinery strikes for three days. On Thursday, it said it was done. On Sunday night, Russia fired 16 more ballistic missiles and nearly 270 drones at Kyiv and the region around it, again with no early warning for the first salvo. Kyiv no longer publishes how many ballistic missiles it shoots down; it is short of interceptors. On Monday, a Russian drone strike on a public park killed six people, two of them children, one a 1-year-old girl in her stroller. Hours later, the President gave his verdict: “Russia, frankly, has been fine on Ukraine. They haven’t been doing much. By the United Nations’ count, civilian casualties from Russian attacks in Ukraine last month were 85 per cent higher than a year before, as Maria Varenikova reported for the New York Times.

Are Washington’s deals with Moscow advancing? Yes, faster than ever. The diesel licence stands, and on Friday, October 16, Trump exempted Russia from the sanctions law he had signed. Next may be gas. Witkoff and Kushner have urged Kyiv and Europe to consider restarting the pipelines that once carried Russian gas west, as Anton Troianovski and Jim Tankersley reported for the New York Times on October 17. Germany has pushed back hard. “Maybe it will be gas,” Zelensky told the FT. “Definitely it will be gas.” Kirill Dmitriev, Putin’s economic envoy, was delighted: “The ice has finally broken.” The Kremlin has offered nothing in return.

Are Europe and Canada filling the gaps? Britain, partly, with air-defence missiles. NATO and the EU, in words. Canada has been quiet in the reporting I have seen, which is itself worth noting.

What is being arranged for Doral? Nothing visible yet, but a new venue has appeared. On Monday, the Kremlin said Putin, Trump and Xi Jinping could meet next month in Shenzhen, China. In Kyiv, that sounds like a table being set for a settlement Ukraine will be handed rather than asked to sign. Putin’s invitation to the December G20 at Doral still stands.

New: can Russians buy fuel? This is the simplest public measure of whether Ukraine’s refinery campaign is biting. On Friday, October 2, 2026, only 46 per cent of Russia’s petrol stations had fuel to sell, by the count of the crowdsourced tracker Gdebenz, as Ivan Nechepurenko reported for the New York Times. That is the baseline. If the number climbs, the pressure Washington asked Kyiv to lift is lifting. If it falls, Ukraine’s drones are back at work.

Ukraine spent three days doing what it was told and was punished for it. Then it reached for the cards that are entirely its own: its drones, its engineers, its soldiers’ hard-won ground, and a moral line it has not crossed even when its enemy has. It may not be able to win this war alone. It has shown it will not be traded out of it.
