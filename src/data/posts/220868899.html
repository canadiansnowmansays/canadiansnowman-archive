👤 Personal, and the last of the numbered years. There is no conqueror in it, no treaty, no war. There is a retired man on an e-bike, a husky trotting beside him, a highway he has never driven, and an old confession that finally gets its answer. The dog is not a cameo this time. She is the point.

I count my essays, and every so often the count lands on a year that already belongs to someone. For nearly a thousand essays it has been other people’s years: William’s, John’s, Luther’s, Victoria’s, the ten million of 1914, the names on the bronze at 2,001. This one belongs to nobody yet. Essay 2,026 is the year I am writing in. The count has run through nine and a half centuries of other people’s calendars and arrived, this morning, at mine.

It cannot go any further. There is no year 2,027 on the door, because nobody has lived it. The count has caught up with the calendar, and for the first time since 1,066 the two of them are standing side by side, out of breath, with nothing in front of them but the present.

The confession

This series began with a confession, and it is only fair that it should end the numbered years with the answer.

At essay 1,066, the first time the count walked into a year, I admitted that I have something in common with Donald Trump. We both love numbers. He loves the big ones, the biggest crowd in the history of crowds, the figure a thing ought to be. I had spent a spring obsessed with reaching one thousand essays, and reached it, and told people about it more than once, and then woke up the next morning to one thousand and one, a man at a keyboard who had forgotten how to stop. I wrote that down, and I was aghast at it, and I asked what the difference was between us, if there was one.

It took nine hundred and sixty essays to find out.

The difference is not the love of numbers. We share that. The difference is the direction. He counts to inflate: the crowd larger than the photograph, the result announced before the votes are in, the figure grander than the fact. Every entry since has been trying to count the other way. It counted the rat at half a ducat and the six dead who were really more. It counted the fraction of a person written into a constitution, and the ten days a pope removed from the calendar, and the ranges at Hiroshima that will never close. It entered the true number even when the true number was ugly, and especially then.

And in the last few weeks it did something I did not plan. It turned the count on me.

At 1,963 I checked the hour I had sworn to for sixty-three years and found it an hour early. Then I checked the summer before it and found it a year late. I trusted a census over my own memory of my grandmother, and the census was the one that had rounded. I told you my grandfather Gordon died the year before I was born; it was my other grandfather, Cameron, who did that, to the day, and Gordon died in the year I was born. Every one of those corrections went into the essays, in my own voice, with my name on top.

That is the whole answer to 1,066. The man I distrust has never once, as far as I can find, run the count on himself. It is the one audit he will not order. I have now run it more times than I would like, and I expect to keep running it for as long as I can type. Same instrument. Opposite purpose. I know which side of the ledger I would rather be on, and this autumn, for the first time, I think I can prove it.

He is, as I write, finishing his sixth year in the office: four the first time, two so far the second. The Constitution keeps a count for him too. The Twenty-second Amendment says that no person shall be elected president more than twice, which is about as plain a number as the law contains. Two. He has been musing aloud about a third term all the same, and his own shop has sold caps reading “Trump 2028.” For a man who has spent a decade denouncing fake news, he has taken a remarkable interest in fake numbers. It is the oldest inflation in this series, the figure a thing ought to be rather than the figure it is, and he has turned it on the one count that was written down specifically to stop him. At 1,066 I said he loves numbers so big that other numbers look at them and feel inadequate. It turns out the number he finds most inadequate is two.

So there is the difference, in one sentence. This autumn I found out that my 1963 was really 1962, and I printed the correction. He would like two to be three.

The ledger of my year

So here are the counts of 2026, the year on the door, entered honestly, as the series demands.

On the fifth of October I turned seventy-five. Today, Halloween, my older brother David turns seventy-six. When we were boys, the three of us younger ones told him he had been left on the doorstep by trick-or-treaters, and that the family had kept him because nobody ever came back for him. He has been forgiving us, slowly, ever since. Happy birthday, David. In August, Ugyen and I marked five years married. Rikesh, who has lived under our roof for years and who is the reason I ever opened one of these artificial-intelligence tools in the first place, turns forty early next month. Dior turns six in December. And the essay count, which began in May of last year as a retirement project for a man who did not know what to do with his mornings, stands at two thousand and twenty-six. The first thousand took a year, a little under three a day. The second has taken six months, at better than five and a half. Averaged over the whole year and a half, it comes to 3.7 a day, a figure I calculated myself, twice, because this series has taught me to check. How long the pace can last, I can't tell you. The news will decide some of it, and my curiosity more. Ugyen and Dior will decide the rest, as they decide most things in this house.

I used to think retirement was something you stopped doing things in. It turns out I retired into the counting, not out of anything. A history of the world, one year at a time, written before breakfast by a man in Toronto with a dog: if you had told me that was what retirement was for, I would not have believed you. I believe it now.

The distance

And then there is the other count, the one that was never on a page.

For years now, Dior and I have gone out most mornings along the trails and the lakeshore, me on an e-bike and her trotting at my side, and the bike has an app, and the app keeps a number. It does not care about history. It records kilometres. When I wrote my essay asking Toronto City Hall to make cyclists ring their bells on the trails, in the late summer of 2025, the app read about 6,500. I remember thinking, as I wrote it, that this was a long way to have gone with a dog.

The Trans-Canada Highway, from St. John’s, Newfoundland, to Victoria, British Columbia, is 7,821 kilometres long. It is the main road across the country, the one every Canadian has been told about and very few have driven end to end.

This year, the app passed it, and kept going. We are well past the length and breadth of Canada now, and on our return journey.

Dior and I have gone from St. John’s to Victoria without leaving Toronto, and turned around, and are somewhere on the long road home. We have crossed the Gulf, the Shield, the prairies and the mountains in the shape of the same few trails and the same stretch of lakeshore, again and again, in every weather this city offers, which is all of them. She has been there for every kilometre of it. Not most. All.

I went and looked up the highway, because this series cannot leave a number alone, and found two things I did not know.

The first is that the Trans-Canada was officially opened by John Diefenbaker at Rogers Pass, in the mountains of British Columbia, on the third of September, 1962. That was the summer I flew west, aged ten, with my grandmother Lena and her sister Stella, on a DC-8 of Trans-Canada Air Lines. We flew right over the line of it in the weeks before it opened, and I did not know it was there. Sixty-four years later I have ridden its whole length with a dog, and I did not know that either until this week.

The second is that the highway was declared open long before it was finished. The Newfoundland section was not completed until 1965, and the whole road was not brought up to the standard the law had set for it until 1971. They cut the ribbon first and finished the road later. I have come to think that is how most real things are built, a series of essays included. You declare it open, and then you spend years making it true.

The auditor

Dior does not know any of this.

She does not know the number on the app. She does not know the length of the Trans-Canada Highway, or that she has matched it, or that there is a series of essays in which she has served, from the very first entry, as the household’s resident auditor, unimpressed by every claim a nation has ever made. She has never once asked how far we have gone. She asks only whether we are going, and the answer, most mornings, is yes.

Her count is kept in other units. She knows, to the metre, where the squirrel was on Tuesday. She knows which bench a child once dropped half a sandwich under, two summers ago, and she checks it every time, in case the sandwich has come back. She knows the precise point on the trail where we turn for home, and she slows there, every single morning, as if to register an objection she knows will be overruled. If I have kept an honest count of anything in this series, she kept it first, and she kept it better, and she never once rounded up.

She turns six in December. I am not going to work out what that is in dog years. This series has had enough of conversions.

I will tell you only this. The distance is the only count in two thousand essays that I did not have to look up, did not have to correct, and did not have to argue with. Nobody inflated it. Nobody rounded it down. It was earned a few kilometres at a time, in the cold and the rain and the good mornings, by an old man and a young dog who did not know they were going anywhere in particular. It is the most honest number I own.

What it was for

I did not come to history on my own. I said so at 1,842, and I will not say it again at length here, except to note that the love of the past I have spent this series on was a gift, and I know who gave it, and it has lasted longer than a good many things that were meant to last.

But I can tell you now what the counting was for, because this autumn the count showed me.

It led me, in the space of a single weekend, to my grandmother in the census of 1931, at a house on Colborne Street; to what I believe is her sister’s signature on her marriage licence in 1913; to the grave of a great-aunt I never knew had lived; to a regimental number, 928726, that belonged to the man I called Great-Uncle Art. I set out, at 1,066, to count years. I finish the years having found people. The numbers were never the point. They were the road, the way the trails were the road and the Trans-Canada was only ever a length.

The closer

So the numbered years are done. The count will pass 2,027 before the day is out and keep going, because I have forgotten how to stop and I no longer wish to remember. But there are no more years on the door that anyone has lived. History has run out, and the present is just the present.

There are two more entries in this series, and neither has a year for a number. The first posts on Christmas morning. In it I am going to do something this series has never done in two thousand essays: I am going to take the count back. I will tell you why, and you may not believe me, and I will ask you to read it anyway. The second posts the day after, and I will not be the one writing it. Someone a good deal older than I am has asked for the last word, and at seventy-five I have learned when to yield the floor.

Dior will be there. She always is.
