The spiraling Trump International Hotel and Tower Vancouver on West Georgia Street — an Arthur Erickson design that opened in February 2017 amid protests and a mayoral boycott.

On February 28, 2017, Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump arrived in Vancouver, British Columbia, to cut the ribbon on their father’s newest hotel tower. The building was sixty-three storeys of glass, a spiralling structure of genuine architectural ambition on West Georgia Street in the heart of downtown Vancouver, designed by the late Arthur Erickson — one of the most celebrated architects Canada has ever produced, the man whose work defined a certain idea of what West Coast modernism could look like when it was operating at the peak of its confidence and intelligence.

Erickson had died in 2009, seven years before the building that bore his design opened its doors. He was not there to see his name associated, however tangentially, with the ribbon cutting that February morning. It is not entirely clear that he would have wanted to be.

Arthur Erickson was not simply a successful architect. He was a defining figure in the cultural identity of a country that has always been somewhat uncertain about its cultural identity, a man whose buildings — the Museum of Anthropology at UBC, Roy Thomson Hall in Toronto, the Canadian Pavilion at Expo 67, the Law Courts complex in Vancouver — became reference points for what Canadian ambition could look like when it stopped apologizing for itself. He designed with a specific West Coast sensibility: an attention to landscape, to the relationship between built space and natural light, to the idea that a building should belong to its place rather than imposing itself on it. His work was recognized internationally. He received the Gold Medal of the American Institute of Architects. He was appointed to the Order of Canada.

He was, in short, the precise opposite of everything the Trump aesthetic represents. Where Trump builds to dominate — gold, vertical, insistent, the largest possible version of itself — Erickson built to belong. Where Trump’s architecture announces the owner, Erickson’s architecture announces the place. The spiralling West Georgia Street tower was, in its design DNA, an Erickson building: site-specific, formally inventive, respectful of the cityscape around it.

That it spent five years wearing the name of a man whose entire architectural philosophy is the antithesis of everything Erickson stood for is one of the quieter ironies of the Vancouver story. The building could not protest. The architect was dead. The name went on.

The Mayor of Vancouver was not there either. Gregor Robertson had made his position clear some time earlier. The Trump name, he said publicly, did not represent Vancouver’s values. The building might be on West Georgia Street. The name on it was not welcome in his city. He would not be attending.

Several other local politicians reached the same conclusion. The ribbon cutting — the grand opening ceremony for a sixty-three storey luxury hotel and residential tower representing a $360 million investment in the heart of one of Canada’s most prosperous cities — was boycotted by the elected officials of the city in which it was located.

Eric and Donald Jr. cut the ribbon anyway. They were photographed doing so. Their smiles, in the photographs, have the quality of men who are aware that something is slightly off about the ceremony but have decided to proceed regardless. The protesters who had gathered outside the building, having organized on social media in the weeks leading up to the opening, made their presence felt.

It was not, by any measure, a triumphant opening.

It would get considerably worse.

Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump, joined by developer Joo Kim Tiah, at the February 28, 2017 ribbon-cutting ceremony. City officials were notably absent.

The Vancouver tower had a different origin story from the Toronto disaster examined in Part III, and the differences are instructive.

Where the Toronto project was the creation of Alex Shnaider, a Russian-Canadian billionaire whose empire was built on post-Soviet steel, the Vancouver tower was the project of Joo Kim Tiah, the president and CEO of Holborn Group and the son of one of Malaysia’s wealthiest men. Tiah had announced in 2013 that his spiralling West Georgia Street skyscraper would bear the Trump name, and the announcement had generated, initially, a controversy that now seems almost quaint: was a glitzy luxury hotel compatible with Vancouver’s reputation as a city of yoga pants and casual outdoor living?

That was the 2013 controversy. By 2017, the controversy had acquired considerably sharper edges.

Trump’s presidential campaign had transformed the name from a luxury brand into a political lightning rod. The speech about Mexican rapists, the Muslim ban proposal, the Access Hollywood tape, the election itself — all of it had attached to the name in ways that Tiah, when he signed his licensing agreement in 2013, had not bargained for and could not have anticipated. The building he had conceived as a statement of Vancouver luxury had become, by its opening day, a statement of something else entirely.

Unlike the Toronto project, the Vancouver building had actually been constructed competently. The structural failures that plagued the Bay Street tower were absent here. The condominiums had sold. The architectural quality was genuine — Erickson’s spiralling design was, by any assessment, a serious piece of work, the kind of building that cities point to as evidence of their architectural ambitions. The project, on its own merits, was a success.

The name was the problem. Exclusively and entirely, the name was the problem.

Vancouver in 2017 was not a city inclined to give the Trump name the benefit of the doubt. The Lower Mainland’s demographics — multicultural, internationally connected, with large communities of exactly the populations Trump had been publicly disparaging throughout his campaign — meant that the building’s address on West Georgia Street placed it in daily proximity to people for whom the name represented something specific and hostile.

The protests that gathered outside the tower were not the disorganized expressions of generalized discontent that sometimes attend controversial developments. They were focused, sustained, and pointed. The Women’s March in Vancouver in January 2017, one of the largest protest gatherings in the city’s history, routed itself past the Trump tower as a deliberate statement. Mexican construction workers hung their country’s flag from the building during its construction. On Google Maps, for a brief and gleeful weekend, the building appeared as “Dump International Hotel and Tower Vancouver” — a digital protest that spread across social media and required Google’s intervention to correct, though not before the renaming had been screenshot and shared by what appeared to be the majority of the Canadian internet.

Protesters gather outside the tower during the grand opening, with signs calling out the Trump name as incompatible with Vancouver values.

City Councillor Kerry Jang, who had been calling for the Trump name to be removed from the building since before it opened, was not using the careful language of diplomatic urban governance when he spoke about it. “It’s more than a beacon of racism,” he said. “It’s a beacon of intolerance. It’s a beacon of sexism and bullying. That’s just not Vancouver.”

Joo Kim Tiah, for his part, maintained that the media had a vendetta against the American president, and that the building’s quality spoke for itself. He was not wrong about the quality. He was, perhaps, underestimating the degree to which the name had become inseparable from the man, and the man had become inseparable from a set of political positions that a significant portion of his building’s neighbourhood found personally threatening.

The Trump Organization, characteristically, expressed confidence that the association with the brand would prove beneficial in the long run. This confidence would not survive contact with a bankruptcy filing.

It is worth noting, before we reach that filing, what Vancouver’s resistance to the Trump name tells us about the specifically Canadian relationship to American political culture — a relationship that this series, written from Toronto, has a particular vantage point on.

Canada’s proximity to the United States is the defining geographic fact of its national existence. The two countries share the longest undefended border in the world, a common language, deeply intertwined economies, and a cultural relationship that Canadians have been navigating with varying degrees of comfort for as long as Canada has existed. The standard Canadian negotiation of this relationship involves a careful distinction between admiring American energy and vitality on one hand, and maintaining a certain independence of judgment about American political choices on the other.

The Trump name arriving on a prominent building in downtown Vancouver was experienced by many Canadians not as a foreign business transaction but as an intrusion — the American political culture war, in which Canada had no vote and no stake, planting its flag on a West Georgia Street skyscraper and asking the neighbourhood to absorb the association. Mayor Robertson’s call for removal was not purely political posturing. It reflected something genuine about how the name had changed in meaning, and about the difference between what Vancouver had agreed to when the licensing contract was signed in 2013 and what Vancouver found itself hosting in 2017.

Canada, as we noted at the close of Part III, did not need to legislate the name away. The market did it instead. But the political resistance — the boycotted opening, the mayor’s statement, the councillor’s language about beacons of intolerance — created the climate in which the market’s judgment was allowed to operate freely. The politics did not remove the name. The politics made its removal inevitable.

The hotel component of the Vancouver tower — the 147 rooms operated by the Trump Hotel Collection under the licensing agreement with Holborn — lasted approximately three years before the pandemic, the political toxicity of the name, and the economics of luxury hospitality in a city that had made its feelings about the brand comprehensively clear combined to produce the inevitable result.

In August 2020, the Trump International Hotel Vancouver closed its doors permanently. The operator filed for bankruptcy. The hotel that had opened to protests and a mayoral boycott in February 2017 had operated for less than three and a half years before concluding that the ongoing association with the Trump name was not compatible with a viable business.

The residential component — the condominiums that had sold before the political temperature changed — remained occupied. The building itself was not going anywhere. But the hotel, the commercial engine that was supposed to justify the licensing arrangement and generate the ongoing management fees that made the Trump Organization’s participation worthwhile, was finished.

The name removal followed. In December 2021, crews removed all Trump branding from the exterior of the sixty-three storey tower. The letters came off the building. The website changed. The signage was replaced.

The building was rebranded as Paradox Vancouver.

One does not wish to be uncharitable. The owners — Malaysian real estate giant TA Global Berhad, operating through its Canadian unit Holborn Group — created the Paradox brand themselves in 2019, applying it also to their Whistler properties and a resort in Phuket. The name was a corporate decision, not a philosophical statement. No irony was intended.

And yet.

Paradox. A word meaning: things that appear to contradict themselves but turn out to be true. Or things that seem true but collapse on examination. Chosen, apparently without reflection, to replace the name of a man whose entire brand was built on the premise that his name was the opposite of a paradox — a guarantee, a certainty, a gold-letter declaration that this was the best and he was here to tell you so.

The building is now called Paradox Hotel. The owners say the name reflects the true local spirit of Vancouver. They did not elaborate on which paradox they had in mind.

There were several available.

Arthur Erickson’s building is better for the change.

The Canadian two-chapter — Toronto and Vancouver together — establishes a pattern that is worth making explicit before this series moves to the American examples, because the pattern is analytically useful and the Canadian perspective on it is, if you will permit a moment of national self-congratulation, somewhat clarifying.

In both cities, the Trump name arrived as a commercial proposition — a licensing arrangement between a developer and a brand, structured to benefit both parties and insulated, in theory, from the personal and political associations of the man whose name was being rented. In both cities, the insulation failed completely. The name was not separable from the man. The man was not separable from his politics. The politics were not compatible with the cities in which the buildings stood.

In both cities, the name came off. Not because of legislation, not because of government action, not because of the sustained political pressure of mayors and councillors and protesters, though all of those played their part in the cultural climate. The name came off because the commercial logic that had put it there in the first place reversed direction. The asset became a liability. The premium became a discount. The thing that developers had paid to have on their buildings became the thing that new owners paid to have removed.

The market, in both cases, completed what the politicians could only recommend.

This is not entirely satisfying as a democratic outcome. The name came off the Toronto building not because a court found it should — though courts found plenty else wrong with the project — but because a private equity firm calculated that the St. Regis brand was worth more than the Trump brand in the Toronto luxury hotel market of 2017. The name came off the Vancouver building not because the Mayor’s boycott of the opening ceremony created legal consequences, but because the hotel went bankrupt and the new operators made a business decision.

Accountability arrived, in both cases, through the mechanism of commercial failure rather than the mechanism of democratic accountability. The market said what the law could not compel and what the politicians could only request.

There is something characteristically Canadian about finding this both reassuring and slightly unsatisfying. The name is gone. The outcome is correct. The process was entirely transactional.

The contrast with what follows — the Washington chapter, the Kennedy Center, the Institute of Peace, the arch, the currency — could not be more complete, and it illuminates something important about the difference between the two categories of naming we identified in Part II.

In Toronto and Vancouver, Trump was a commercial participant. He had signed contracts. The contracts had prices. When the commercial logic reversed, the contracts could be terminated for value, the prices could be paid, and the name could come off without anyone in Trump Tower saying a word, because commercial contracts do not require commentary. They require performance, and when performance is no longer mutually beneficial, they require termination fees.

In Washington, Trump is not a commercial participant. He is the President of the United States, the chairman of the Kennedy Center board, the commander-in-chief of a military that is building him a warship, the head of state whose Treasury Secretary holds up mock-ups of illegal currency at White House press briefings. He is not licensing his name to the republic. He is inscribing it on the republic. And the republic, unlike JCF Capital, does not have a termination clause.

Which is why, when Judge Cooper ordered the Kennedy Center letters removed, the response was not a quiet cheque and a dignified silence.

The response was a Truth Social post about radical Democrats and failing institutions and transferring the building back to Congress as punishment for the affront.

The response was fury.

Because monuments are not contracts.

And the man who took the Toronto buyout without a word has never learned — or has never been willing to acknowledge — that there is a difference between a name that can be priced and a name that cannot.

One of them, you can buy back.

The other one you have to earn.

Donald Trump has spent a lifetime writing his name on things. Towers. Golf courses. Hotels. A university. A Bible. A perfume called Success. A peace institute he gutted before the paint on the letters dried. A performing arts centre dedicated to a murdered president he has spent his entire career trying to impress.

The name is always the same: gold where possible, large where mandatory, permanent where permitted, and imposed where neither permission nor permanence was ever his to grant.

This series is about what that compulsion reveals — psychologically, politically, historically, and occasionally comically. It is not a flattering portrait.

Then again, neither is a loading dock on Bay Street.
