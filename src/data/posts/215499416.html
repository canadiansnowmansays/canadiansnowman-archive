Stephen Gordon Sept 17, 2026

Last September, Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. stood before cameras and released “Make Our Children Healthy Again,” a document he said contained reforms that would have been “unthinkable two years ago,” delivered, he promised, “very, very quickly.” It ran to more than one hundred goals across four pillars — poor diet, chemical exposure, lack of physical activity and chronic stress, and overmedicalization — and medical experts who read it split cleanly down the middle, praising some proposals as overdue and dismissing others as unscientific theatre dressed up as policy.

One year is enough time to check the receipts. As Dani Blum and Alice Callahan reported for the New York Times on Sept. 8, 2026, the paper went through all four pillars, promise by promise, and found a pattern this series has been documenting piecemeal for months, now visible all at once: a small number of genuine, defensible reforms, running alongside a much larger set of actions that point in the opposite direction of the report’s own stated goals. A health department spokeswoman, Grace Davis Jamison, told the Times that Kennedy and Trump have “delivered historic reforms” and that the administration’s “record speaks for itself.” It does. That is rather the problem.

Start with diet, the report’s first and most publicly popular pillar. The administration can point to real things: new dietary guidelines that for the first time recommend avoiding highly processed foods, a soda-and-candy purchase restriction now approved in twenty-three states for SNAP recipients (blocked so far in five, but showing early signs of reducing sugary drink purchases by roughly thirteen percent where it operates), and an FDA rule aimed at closing the so-called GRAS loophole that lets manufacturers add chemicals to food without telling anyone. The dietary guidelines were also produced through research reviews written in secret by scientists with financial ties to the meat and dairy industry, which is an odd way to keep a promise about reducing conflicts of interest in policymaking. And the same month the guidelines celebrated real food, the Agriculture Department cancelled more than a billion dollars earmarked for schools and food banks to buy fresh produce from local farms, cut SNAP enrollment by an estimated five million people — at least 1.2 million of them children — and eliminated the $536 million SNAP-Ed program that funded cooking classes, community gardens and farmers’ markets for low-income families. You cannot solve “poor diet” by simultaneously making it harder for poor families to afford food. The report also promised front-of-package nutrition labels and limits on junk-food marketing to children. A year on, neither has moved an inch.

Chemical exposure follows the identical template. The administration announced real money — $130 million for research into chemical exposure and pesticide alternatives, $100 million toward reducing pesticide use, $144 million for microplastics research — while President Trump separately issued an executive order to spur production of glyphosate, an herbicide with an unsettled but genuinely concerning carcinogenicity literature, before a public backlash forced a partially walked-back version. In the same period, the EPA proposed scaling back air pollution guardrails in ways that would let trucks emit more smog-forming chemicals — a change that lands hardest on children’s still-developing lungs — and the administration moved to repeal limits on PFAS “forever chemicals” in drinking water, chemicals with a documented link to developmental delays and elevated cancer risk in children. The report’s promise and the administration’s own regulatory agenda are, on this pillar, working against each other in real time.

Physical activity and chronic stress produced the thinnest returns and the starkest reversal. President Trump reinstated the Presidential Fitness Test by executive order, but it isn’t mandatory, and a CDC study found most states aren’t equipped to run it. The Surgeon General issued a non-binding warning urging parents to delay children’s screen time “as long as possible” — a statement, not a policy. Meanwhile the administration made sweeping Medicaid cuts that strip pediatric mental health coverage from families who rely on it, and moved to halt a billion dollars in youth mental-health funding that Congress approved after the 2022 Uvalde shooting — funding that paid for school counsellors, until a judge ordered some of it restored and the fight continued in court. The same administration that issued a Surgeon General’s warning about the mental health cost of screen time also paved the way for flavoured e-cigarettes, over the objections of experts who note the report itself links vaping to anxiety and depression in adolescents. If there is a coherent theory connecting “warn about children’s chronic stress” and “make vaping more appealing to children,” nobody at HHS has been asked to explain it on the record.

Which brings us, as it always does in this series, to overmedicalization — and to the vaccine story this column left off a month ago.

As covered in this series’ Aug. 13, 2026 installment, President Trump signed an executive order on Aug. 10 narrowing the recommended childhood immunization schedule from eighteen diseases to eleven and directing HHS to pursue splitting the combined measles-mumps-rubella shot into three separate single-disease injections. That essay focused on the CDC’s newly confirmed director, Erica Schwartz, and the striking fact that the order required none of her involvement. The month since has supplied two further data points, and neither is flattering.

The first is what Kennedy said about the order after the fact. As CNN’s interview aired on Aug. 15, 2026, Kennedy told the network that “a recent study just came out” providing a “meta review” synthesis that “very strongly supports” splitting the MMR shot, and that the CDC’s own website — which still states there is no published evidence for splitting the vaccine — is “probably outdated.” As FactCheck.org subsequently documented, no such study exists, then or since. The World Health Organization, for its part, issued a statement two days after the signing warning that splitting vaccines and adding doctor visits tends to produce missed doses and lower population immunity, not better safety — the opposite of the order’s stated rationale. This is the same “dispute the framing without specifying which fact is wrong” pattern this series flagged from HHS’s press shop in July, now applied to a claim Kennedy made about his own department’s website.

The second data point is more damning, because it doesn’t require debating anyone’s motives at all — it’s a manufacturing schedule. As one pharmaceutical-industry newsletter laid out in late August 2026, single-antigen measles, mumps and rubella vaccines have not been produced or sold in the United States for close to two decades; only the combined shot is manufactured domestically now. Bringing standalone versions back into production — clinical trials, manufacturing lines, regulatory approval — would take, by the vaccine maker’s own estimate, up to a decade. The executive order does not merely ask HHS to recommend something contested. It orders a product that does not currently exist, cannot be summoned by presidential signature, and would not be ready before most of the children it claims to protect had already aged out of the schedule it revises. Then, on top of that, Kennedy’s department issued a request for public feedback in late August asking stakeholders — clinicians, researchers, manufacturers, and, specifically, “faith communities” — whether the current three-tier vaccine recommendation system (routine, shared clinical decision-making, and so on) needs new categories. It is process, aimed at a substance that a follow-through would need a manufacturing sector this administration has not funded and years this administration does not have.

Set against that backdrop, the actual disease this pillar exists to prevent is not cooperating with the narrative of overmedicalization as the country’s central health threat. By Aug. 20, 2026, the United States had recorded 2,777 confirmed measles cases — already past the entirety of 2025’s total of 2,289, which had itself been the worst year since 1991. Ninety-three percent of this year’s cases are in people who are unvaccinated or whose vaccination status is unknown. The report’s “overmedicalization” pillar promised to examine whether American children receive too many shots. What it has actually delivered, one year in, is a country on pace to lose the measles elimination status it earned in 2000 — an achievement, worth noting, that a properly functioning immunization schedule is what produced in the first place.

The one clean win on this pillar belongs, fittingly, to the FDA rather than to Kennedy’s messaging operation: a genuine crackdown on deceptive prescription drug advertising, including warning letters sent to a company marketing ADHD medication to children. Kennedy has said such ads have “hooked” Americans on pharmaceuticals, and on this narrow point the agency’s action matches the rhetoric. It is one real item on a scorecard otherwise dominated by promises unmet, promises reversed, and — in the vaccine story specifically — a policy built around a product HHS cannot manufacture, defended with a study that does not exist, in response to a disease outbreak the policy itself will make worse before it makes it better, if it ever does either.

Reading this through the TACO framework this series keeps returning to, the pattern holds across all four pillars, not just the vaccine fight. Kennedy and the administration threaten the appearance of decisive reform — a hundred-point report, an executive order, a Fitness Test revival, a Surgeon General’s warning. Congress and the public are meant to accommodate that appearance, taking the report’s headline promises at face value. When the substance underneath those promises is examined — SNAP cuts alongside SNAP restrictions, PFAS rollbacks alongside microplastics grants, e-cigarette flavour approvals alongside chronic-stress warnings, a vaccine that cannot be manufactured alongside a schedule that claims to reduce overmedication — the department’s standard move is obfuscation: dispute the framing, don’t specify the fact, let the news cycle move on. And when a court, a WHO statement, or a fact-checking organization forces the issue anyway, what follows isn’t correction. It’s the fourth move, capitulation, dressed up as continued forward motion — another RFI, another executive order, another tour stop, another year’s worth of goals quietly rolled into next year’s progress report.

Dr. Erica Schwartz, one month into the CDC directorship this series covered on Aug. 13, has still not been reported taking a public position on any of this. The silence that was notable five days into her tenure is, a month later, no longer news. It is simply the baseline the department now operates from — measured, this time, not by an executive order she wasn’t in the room for, but by a report card nobody at HHS seems eager to grade themselves.
