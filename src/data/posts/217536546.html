by Stephen Gordon

On Friday, September 25, 2026, the director of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention stood before a high-level meeting of the United Nations General Assembly in New York and declined, on behalf of the United States, to endorse a declaration pledging that countries would work together to prepare for the next pandemic.

The declaration was not a treaty. It carried no legal force and imposed no rules. It was, in the plainest sense, a statement that the nations of the world intend to cooperate when the next novel pathogen crosses a border — which it will, because pathogens do not carry passports. Most member states spoke in its favour. The United States, Italy and the European Union did not.

The next morning, the New York Times published the other half of the story. Apoorva Mandavilli, the Times’ global health reporter — who had also covered Friday’s U.N. session — laid out, with personnel data never before made public, what has become of the agency Dr. Erica Schwartz was representing. The short version: it has lost nearly a third of its people, almost all of its senior leadership, and most of its independence. Dr. Demetre Daskalakis, who ran its respiratory disease centre until he resigned in August 2025, described what remains as a “zombified C.D.C.” — the body still upright, the organs still in place, and nothing moving the way it should.

Put the two stories side by side and you have the clearest picture yet of what this series has been arguing from the start: that Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s tenure at Health and Human Services is not a domestic policy quarrel about vaccine schedules. It is the deliberate disarmament of the country’s first line of defence against biological threats, carried out in plain sight, while the United States simultaneously walks away from every international arrangement that might have compensated for it.

The Director Finds Her Voice

Readers of this series met Dr. Schwartz as a nominee in June, in “The Secretary Wars,” and again on August 13, in “The Director Who Wasn’t in the Room” — confirmed on August 5 and conspicuously absent five days later when President Trump signed the executive order narrowing the childhood vaccine schedule with Kennedy at his elbow. Back in April, in “Seven Hearings, Zero Accountability,” Kennedy told Congress he would not commit to accepting her vaccine decisions. It turns out he needn’t have worried.

“The Report Card Nobody Ordered” noted on September 17 that she had still not been reported taking a public position on anything. Mandavilli’s reporting now confirms the silence is not an accident of coverage: Schwartz has made no public statement about the importance of childhood vaccination and has not corrected a single piece of misinformation from her boss. And she has not merely been silent. In one of her first significant decisions, she directed CDC staff to dispute Pennsylvania’s announcement of two measles deaths — departing from the agency’s normal practice of deferring to state health departments on their own case counts. That is the same Pennsylvania outbreak this series covered on September 18 in “Friends in All the Wrong Places,” when Kennedy was telling an anti-vaccine conference in Washington that he had a friend in the White House, and HHS’s official account was calling Governor Josh Shapiro’s request for acknowledgement “political blackmail.” We now know the pushback was not merely a social-media spat. It came from the director’s office.

Then came New York. At the U.N. session on Friday, Schwartz told the assembly that although the text had been meaningfully revised, it still contained “divisive ideologies that lack definitional consensus,” and that the United States could not support it in full. She did not say which ideologies. The Times, which reviewed an early draft, filled in the blanks: the American delegation had asked to strike references to equity, to solidarity, and to universal access to sexual and reproductive health services.

Consider what that means in practice. The word “solidarity” — the basic proposition that when one nation is hit, the others help — was, in the view of the United States government, too ideologically loaded to sign. Former New Zealand prime minister Helen Clark, who co-chairs the Independent Panel for Pandemic Preparedness and Response, observed that agreeing to cooperate against pandemics ought to be the least contentious thing a room full of governments could do. Public Citizen’s Peter Maybarduk called the decision a self-inflicted wound, pointing out that an administration fond of demanding that other countries pay their fair share had just walked away from a mechanism designed to make that happen.

It is worth being precise about who else said no, because the company is instructive. The European Union’s objection was commercial: it believed the text went too far on intellectual property and technology transfer, which is a genuine, long-running dispute about who owns vaccines and diagnostics developed in a crisis. One can disagree with Brussels, but it is an argument about patents. The American objection was about words. Italy’s objection echoed Washington’s — including the same sovereignty anxieties Kennedy has voiced about the World Health Organization.

This is now a pattern, not an incident. Last year, Kennedy personally told a U.N. meeting that the United States would not sign a declaration on chronic disease and mental health — chronic disease being, one recalls, the signature cause of the Make America Healthy Again movement — because the U.N. was allegedly overreaching and promoting abortion and gender ideology. Earlier this year, the United States cast the lone vote against a global commitment to reduce traffic deaths, on the theory that road safety was a Trojan horse for climate agreements. And in 2025 the administration left the WHO entirely and rejected the International Health Regulations, with Kennedy claiming the regulations would empower the WHO to impose global lockdowns. They would not, and WHO Director-General Dr. Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus took the unusual step on Friday of saying so directly: the agreements were written by sovereign states, exercising their sovereignty, precisely because outbreaks ignore borders.

Those same International Health Regulations, it bears noting, are what prompted the disclosure of the hantavirus outbreak in May. The system Kennedy dismisses as a threat to American sovereignty is the system that told Americans what was coming.

Counting the Bodies That Are Still Standing

The layoffs of early 2025 cut about 18 per cent of the CDC’s workforce, which was alarming enough at the time. What the new data — compiled by a group of former federal scientists at the Public Data Observatory from Office of Personnel Management, HHS and union records — shows is that the layoffs were only the first wave. The second was quieter and larger: people who retired, resigned, or simply gave up.

In March 2025, the CDC employed 9,279 people. By May 2026, it employed 6,683. More than 1,200 people retired over that stretch, close to four times the number in the comparable period two years earlier. Over the same fourteen months, the agency hired 108 people. In the equivalent period of 2023–24, it hired more than 1,500. Roughly 270 more are still on administrative leave — paid, not working — while litigation over the layoffs grinds on.

The losses are not evenly spread, and where they fall is the most damning part. The National Center for Chronic Disease Prevention and Health Promotion — the division most directly aligned with Kennedy’s stated obsession with chronic illness in American children — went from 968 staff to 501, a loss of nearly half. The birth defects centre, the natural institutional home for any serious inquiry into autism, is likewise at about half strength. Within the chronic disease centre, the Division of Population Health lost 88 per cent of its staff, to the point where officials were soliciting volunteers from other offices simply to write the funding notices for work on lupus and epilepsy that Congress had already paid for. Entire teams — on smoking, sexual violence, child abuse, and falls among older adults — no longer exist.

A Health Secretary who genuinely believed that chronic disease was the great crisis of American health would be building these centres up. Kennedy has cut them in half and then held press conferences about the crisis.

The infectious disease centres were spared the formal layoffs, but not the attrition. The centre for emerging and zoonotic diseases, which handles Ebola and hantavirus among others, has about 300 fewer people than in March 2025. The respiratory disease centre is down about 200. Each is roughly 17 per cent smaller, heading into respiratory season, during a record measles year, with Ebola active in the Democratic Republic of Congo.

And the leadership is simply gone. In June, in “’My Dysfunctional Self,’” this series counted eleven acting directors at the CDC. The new data puts a harder number on it: of the eleven centre directors in place in March 2025, one remains. The rest have been replaced by acting directors, some promoted from one or two rungs below. The agency’s new chief operating officer, Sean Slovenski, told staff in June that he had never seen so many acting roles in his career — which is the sort of thing a chief operating officer usually says about a problem he intends to fix, rather than one his department created.

Twenty Minders and a Cod Liver Oil Memo

Headcount alone would be survivable if the people who remained were free to do their jobs. They are not.

In previous administrations, two or three political appointees oversaw the CDC. There are now close to twenty from HHS in regular contact with agency staff, according to the Times’ interviews with more than thirty current and former employees. Some of those appointees have directed scientists toward projects that range from the scientifically discredited to the institutionally bizarre.

Last year, an HHS official directed the CDC to hand a no-bid contract to Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute to study whether vaccinating pregnant women causes autism in their children — a hypothesis Kennedy has promoted and that numerous studies have already put to rest. The $362,000 to pay for it came out of the Vaccines for Children programme, which supplies free vaccines to roughly half the children in the country. Money to vaccinate children was redirected to study whether vaccines harm them. It is difficult to write a satire sharper than the procurement record.

A political appointee named Stuart Burns asked staff to compile every measles case since 2010, complete with patients’ underlying conditions and diagnostic codes — information the CDC does not routinely collect, as staff told him. The purpose seems plain enough: Kennedy has falsely claimed that measles kills only children who were already sick. Dr. William “Reyn” Archer III, another appointee who previously oversaw the agency, repeatedly pressed staff to work on screwworm, a parasite that primarily afflicts cattle, at an agency whose mandate is human health. And Dr. Ben Edwards — the Lubbock physician who treated children while himself infected with measles, and whom Kennedy has praised as an extraordinary healer — was able to email senior CDC officials asking them to investigate cod liver oil as a measles treatment, on the strength of references from the eighteenth century.

Every one of those requests consumed the time of scientists who, by the numbers above, have none to spare.

The interference extends to the science itself. As previously reported, Kennedy overrode CDC scientists to impose strict quarantine rules on people exposed to hantavirus. Dr. Jay Bhattacharya, during his six months as acting director, blocked publication of a study on Covid vaccine effectiveness — and, when that became a controversy, demoted the acting head of the respiratory disease centre, Dr. Brandi Limbago, replacing her with Rebecca Greco Koné, an operations manager who is not a scientist. The person now running the centre responsible for flu, Covid and RSV guidance heading into winter was chosen, as best one can tell, for her distance from the evidence.

A Fire Hall With a Third of the Crew Gone

Dr. Jonathan Mermin, who ran the CDC’s HIV and sexually transmitted infection centre until April 2025 and is now dean of Columbia’s Mailman School of Public Health, offered the Times an analogy that any municipal budget committee would understand: cut a third of a city’s firefighters, and then act surprised when downtown catches fire. The fires are already visible.

This series warned in June that there was no functioning vaccine advisory committee to guide the autumn flu and Covid campaigns. Autumn has arrived, and the warning has come true. By this point in 2024, the CDC had published detailed respiratory vaccine recommendations. This year there is nothing: the advisers have not met since December, and no updated guidance has been issued. Dr. Daniel Jernigan, who led the emerging disease centre before resigning in August 2025, told the Times that some remaining staff wanted to post clear flu vaccine recommendations anyway but were unwilling to pick a fight with political appointees. That is what institutional capture looks like from the inside: not an order to stay silent, but a well-founded fear of what happens if you don’t.

State and local health departments — the people who actually run the clinics — are feeling it. The grant programme that helps them train staff and build systems once had twenty project officers. It now has three. Response times have gone from under a day to more than ten, and some requests are never answered at all: one county asked for help on August 7 and was still waiting on September 21. Travel approvals take months. Job offers for eighteen critical global health positions expired while HHS failed to complete background checks, forcing the agency to start hiring from scratch. Laid-off employees who by law have priority for rehiring have been told they are ineligible for the jobs they held for years.

The measles picture is the sharpest illustration of how headcount understates the damage. The branch working on vaccine-preventable diseases lost only seven of its fifty staff — but those seven included the four most experienced scientists on the measles laboratory team, the people analysing the genetic data that will determine whether the United States can still claim to have eliminated measles at all. The country may be about to lose that status in part because it lost the four people best equipped to tell it so.

Abroad, the contrast is starker still. “The Price of the Meme” set out in May what the 2014 West African Ebola epidemic cost and what the country built afterward to avoid a repeat. Here is one measure of what is left of it: in 2014, roughly 2,000 CDC staff deployed to the region; for the current outbreak in Congo, the number is about 100. The budget of the branch that fights Ebola and similar pathogens has fallen by 36 per cent. Some staff are declining to volunteer because they object to the administration’s emphasis on keeping infected people out of the United States rather than containing the outbreak at its source; others are simply too stretched. At a recent meeting of international influenza experts, Jernigan reported, the consensus was that the world is now less prepared for a coordinated response than it was before Covid.

HHS, asked about all of this, declined to answer the Times’ specific questions and issued a statement insisting that the CDC continues to protect Americans and to communicate clearly about vaccination. It added that Schwartz and the agency’s new leadership are reviewing its structure to ensure it can advance the priorities of President Trump and Secretary Kennedy. That second sentence is the honest one.

Reading the Week Through TACO

The framework this series keeps returning to fits the last seventy-two hours almost too neatly.

Threaten. The sovereignty argument does the threatening. International health cooperation is cast as a foreign plot — WHO lockdowns, road-safety climate pacts, “divisive ideologies” left conveniently undefined. The threat is never specified because specifying it would expose it; Tedros had only to read the actual text aloud to dispose of it.

Accommodate. The Times’ source describes American negotiators proposing edits throughout the process to make the language more comfortable. At home, the accommodation was Schwartz herself: a credentialed physician who promised to follow the science, confirmed partly on the strength of that promise, installed to reassure senators that an adult was in the building.

Capitulate. One month later, that adult has said nothing in defence of childhood vaccines, disputed a state’s measles death count on the department’s behalf, and delivered the administration’s rejection at the U.N. The capitulation is not dramatic. It is simply total.

Obfuscate. HHS’s assurance that the CDC is still protecting Americans, from a department that will not answer questions about how many Americans are left at the CDC to do the protecting. The unnamed ideologies. The acting directors whose titles suggest an agency temporarily between leaders rather than permanently without them.

The National Security Ledger

This series began from a premise that some readers found overheated at the time: that Kennedy’s dismantling of American public health is a national security matter, not merely a health policy one. Friday and Saturday should settle it.

A country can choose, as a matter of policy, to go it alone against pandemics. It can leave the WHO, reject the International Health Regulations, and decline to sign declarations promising cooperation. That would be a gamble, but at least a coherent one — if it were accompanied by a domestic public health agency strong enough to carry the load alone. What the United States has done instead is withdraw from every international mechanism and hollow out the domestic one at the same time. It is the strategic equivalent of cancelling your alliances and disbanding your army in the same fiscal year, then announcing that you intend to be very firm about your borders.

Meanwhile, the world is negotiating the unresolved heart of the pandemic treaty — the pathogen access and benefit-sharing arrangement that would require countries to share samples and genetic sequences of new pathogens quickly, so that tests and vaccines can be developed before an outbreak becomes a pandemic. The United States is not at that table. When the next novel virus emerges somewhere far from Atlanta, the sequence will be shared among the countries that stayed, and the American agency that would once have led the analysis will be, in Daskalakis’s word, zombified: a body with all its parts, answering email from political appointees about cod liver oil and screwworm, while a county health department somewhere waits six weeks for a reply.

From Toronto, one watches this with something more complicated than schadenfreude. Canada’s own public health agency has always leaned on the CDC — its labs, its surveillance, its people. When the neighbour’s fire hall loses a third of its crew, the houses on our side of the street are not as safe as they were either.

The body is still there. That is what makes it so dangerous. From the outside, it still looks like the CDC.
