A professor at Texas Tech University opened a report this spring listing the lessons she needed to stop teaching. One of the courses flagged did not exist. She had never taught it. The report, generated partly by artificial intelligence as part of a system-wide curriculum review, had hallucinated a class in order to censor it.

That single detail is worth sitting with longer than the news cycle allowed it to sit, because it captures something this series has been circling for eighty-four parts: the gap is not only between what AI systems can do and what oversight requires of them before deployment. Sometimes the gap is between what an AI system actually knows and what an institution is willing to act on anyway, simply because the tool said so with enough confidence and enough apparent efficiency to save someone the trouble of reading the syllabus themselves.

The Chancellor and the Portal

As Vimal Patel reported for the New York Times, the Texas Tech University System spent the months before that report using AI-assisted review to identify course material touching on sexual orientation and gender identity, under directives issued by Chancellor Brandon Creighton, a former state lawmaker who has led the five-university system since November 2025. Creighton authored the state legislation that abolished DEI offices at Texas public universities, which took effect in 2024 and, more recently, a bill that curtailed faculty governance and tightened state control over curriculum. He is, in other words, not an outside contractor brought in to modernise a review process. He is the architect of the underlying policy, now also the administrator implementing it, using a tool he did not personally build to enforce restrictions he personally wrote into law.

The mechanics matter here as much as the politics. Faculty must submit course materials through a new portal for review. Memos issued in December 2025 and April 2026 banned any core-curriculum course from including material related to sexual orientation or gender identity, required recognition of only two sexes, and prohibited graduate dissertations on gender identity topics. The core curriculum is not an elective corner of the university — it is the mandatory menu every undergraduate must select from to graduate, which means a restriction on what the core may contain is a restriction on what every student at Texas Tech encounters, not merely what a specialist minority opts into.

Creighton’s public defence is one of scale and restraint: of more than 14,000 courses taught across the system, he says only around sixty were recommended for modification, with another 300 “proactively” altered by faculty pre-emptively. He describes the process as deliberative, layered through a Board of Regents committee, with AI serving only as an efficiency tool and most of the substantive work done by people. Read generously, this is a chancellor arguing that the headline-grabbing cases are a rounding error against a much larger, careful, mostly human-run apparatus. Read less generously, it is a chancellor citing a low error rate for a system that has already been shown, by his own faculty’s testimony, to invent errors that were not there at all.

The Phantom Course and What “Slop” Actually Costs

The professor whose report hallucinated a course she does not teach was Lucy Schiller, a creative-writing instructor. Her report did correctly flag a real book she assigns — Voice of the Fish, by Lars Horn, who is transmasculine, and whose publisher describes the book as exploring the trans experience through water, fish, and mythology. Schiller was instructed to switch it out. She declined. She left Texas Tech for Grinnell College instead, saying she had loved her job there until this year.

At the faculty meeting where these reports were distributed, one professor’s response was blunt: “It’s A.I. slop.”

That phrase deserves to anchor this essay’s central claim, because “slop” is not simply an aesthetic complaint about clumsy prose. In this context, slop is a hallucinated allegation used as the evidentiary basis for a personnel and curricular action against a tenured academic. When a large language model invents a course a professor never taught and that invention becomes part of an official compliance report circulated at a faculty meeting, the harm is not that the writing was bad. The harm is that an institution treated a probabilistic text generator’s output as though it were a factual audit, and only caught the error because the professor herself was in the room to say “I don’t even teach that.”

Ronald Hendrick, the system’s top academic officer, told Patel he did not know AI was being used in the review process at all. Sit with that admission for a moment. The chancellor’s office deployed a generative tool to produce compliance findings against faculty, and the person responsible for academic oversight was not informed it was happening. This is not a story about AI being misused by a rogue actor outside proper channels. It is a story about AI being used inside proper channels so quietly that even the official whose job is to safeguard academic standards did not know the tool existed until faculty started asking questions in a meeting room.

The Line-Reading Problem

The chilling effect here operates independently of how many courses are formally flagged, because the memos’ central terms — “sexual orientation,” “gender identity” — are left undefined. Schiller’s phrase for what this produces is exact: faculty are made to do “the work of reading between the very obvious lines,” overcomplying out of fear of being reported or disciplined rather than risk a wrong guess about where the boundary sits. A faculty senate survey in May found that roughly half of respondents had already altered their course content without being formally asked to. As Jessica Priest reported for the Texas Tribune on May 29, 2026, the survey drew 367 responses from a faculty of about 2,157, recorded changes or change requests touching roughly 277 courses, and found that more than half of respondents were looking for work elsewhere. In about half of the changes faculty had acted on their own; in roughly a quarter, administrators had asked. The chancellor’s office dismissed it as a self-selected sample. Alan Barenberg, who chaired the committee behind it, said he was “ethically bound by my discipline to teach history to the best of my ability.” That self-editing is the actual mechanism of censorship at work here — not the sixty courses Creighton cites as officially modified, but the unknown and uncounted number of self-edits made by professors trying to guess the preferences of an undefined policy enforced by a hallucinating tool.

Consider the two cases Patel’s reporting surfaces in detail. Sonja Stojanovic, who teaches a core course called Holocaust in Literature, was told the course should move to upper-level elective status because it mentions that gay and bisexual men were also targeted by the Nazis — a plainly established historical fact now functioning, under the new rules, as grounds for demotion out of required curriculum. Jacob Bell, a medievalist, removed two weeks of material on Viking mythology because the poems he taught include gender-changing gods, and he no longer felt safe including them, even though the chancellor’s own gender memo technically exempts “purely incidental” biographical references to historically significant figures — an exemption vague enough that Bell, like Schiller, chose subtraction over risk.

He has since accepted a position at a public university on the East Coast, telling Patel that he believes Texas Tech’s students are being made “unprepared to deal with normal people in the world.”

Applying TACO, Loosely

This series’ TACO framework — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — was built for tracking how institutions respond to external pressure. Here the roles are inverted in an instructive way: Creighton is not responding to pressure, he is generating it, using AI as the instrument of a Threaten posture dressed in the vocabulary of Accommodate. The public framing is administrative housekeeping — “garbage in curriculum,” efficiency, a “national model.” The lived experience, by faculty’s own account, is a Threaten campaign: comply pre-emptively, or risk being reported, disciplined, or separated from your work, in the words of one anonymous survey respondent who compared the whole effort to “using a chain saw to remove a splinter.”

The Obfuscate column belongs to the AI itself, structurally rather than intentionally. A hallucinated course report obscures the actual chain of accountability — was this professor targeted by a human reviewer with an agenda, or by a statistical error in a language model with no agenda at all? Faculty cannot easily tell, and that uncertainty is itself a control mechanism, because it makes appeal and pushback harder to aim. You can argue with a biased administrator. It is much harder to argue with “the system flagged it,” especially when the system’s own operator, Hendrick, says he did not know it was running.

The Trend Nobody Is Calling a Trend

Texas Tech is not operating in isolation, and treating it as an isolated overreach understates the pattern this series exists to track. Patel’s reporting notes that Texas Tech fits a broader movement of former Republican lawmakers installed as heads of state university systems — at least seven college presidents in Florida alone share that profile, alongside the leaders of both the Texas A&M and University of Texas systems. Jonathan Friedman of PEN America, a free-expression advocacy group, told Patel that while other Texas universities are conducting their own curriculum reviews, Texas Tech has gone furthest, describing the trajectory as one of steadily increasing ideological control. The neighbouring system is in court too. On August 3, 2026, the AAUP announced that it and four professors, with the ACLU of Texas, had sued the Texas A&M University System in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Texas over a November 2025 policy barring faculty from discussing certain topics and perspectives on race, sexual orientation and gender. The AAUP says the policy has led to course cancellations and removed material across the system’s twelve universities, including Plato’s writing on gender in the Symposium. “A university that censors Plato abandons its obligation to truth, free inquiry, and the public trust,” said Todd Wolfson, the AAUP’s president. The AI-assisted review is not the origin of that trajectory. It is an accelerant applied to a policy direction that predates the technology by years — Creighton wrote the DEI-abolition bill in 2023, well before any university system had reason to hand curriculum review to a language model.

This matters for how the story should be read. It would be a mistake to treat the hallucinated course as the scandal and the underlying policy as a neutral backdrop the AI merely executed poorly. The policy itself — banning any mention of sexual orientation or gender identity from mandatory coursework, exempting historical figures only when references are “purely incidental,” relying on faculty to self-censor around undefined terms — was designed to produce exactly the chilling effect the survey data shows it produced, with or without a hallucinating tool doing the flagging. What the AI adds is deniability and scale: a chancellor can point to a low official modification count while an unmeasured, much larger wave of self-censorship happens off the books, and when an error surfaces, it can be attributed to the tool’s limitations rather than to the policy’s intent. Creighton’s own framing supports this reading — when confronted with Jacob Bell’s decision to strip Viking mythology from his medieval history course, Creighton’s response was not to clarify the “purely incidental” standard. It was to tell professors not to make pre-emptive decisions and instead submit materials for review, which is to say: keep guessing, and let the portal decide.

Not every voice in Patel’s reporting reads this as dystopian. Preston Parsons, the local Turning Point USA chapter president, argues that Creighton is bringing ideological balance to a sector he considers overwhelmingly liberal, and asks whether it is necessarily a bad thing for universities to differ from one another on contested issues. Sherry Sylvester of the Texas Public Policy Foundation calls the effort “fearless and meticulous” and frames Creighton’s legislative record as returning universities to open debate after years of faculty-driven ideological capture, adding that faculty members are, in her words, state employees and should remember it. These are not fringe positions within the current political landscape, and this series has tried consistently not to caricature the case for reform as pure bad faith. The genuine question of whether university curricula skew in ways that exclude conservative perspectives is a legitimate one. But “state employees should remember their place” is a strange answer to a complaint about a machine inventing a phantom class, because it addresses the politics of who gets to set curriculum while saying nothing at all about whether the tool doing the enforcing can be trusted to get basic facts right. Ideological balance and factual accuracy are different problems, and Texas Tech’s defenders have, so far, only answered the first one.

The Contradiction Underneath

Readers of The American Contradiction will recognise the shape of what is happening to Stojanovic’s Holocaust course and Bell’s Viking poems: historically accurate material about a persecuted or non-conforming identity is treated as ideological contamination rather than as history, on the theory that acknowledging a fact about who was targeted or who existed somehow constitutes advocacy for a present-day political position. This is the same “unassimilable” logic that series has traced through Irish Catholics, Chinese railroad workers, and Jewish refugees, now applied retroactively to erase mention of gay and bisexual Holocaust victims and gender-fluid Norse gods from a public university’s mandatory coursework. The target has moved from living people to historical memory itself, which is a distinct and in some ways more totalising ambition — you cannot self-deport a fact about the past, so instead the fact is simply removed from the syllabus.

Where this essay stays in Asimov Gap’s lane rather than crossing fully into American Contradiction territory is the specific mechanism: an AI tool, procured and deployed without the knowledge of the system’s own chief academic officer, doing the work of ideological enforcement badly enough to invent a course out of nothing, at an institution whose chancellor simultaneously touts an Nvidia infrastructure partnership as evidence of his AI-forward vision for the university’s future.

Texas Tech wants to be positioned, in Creighton’s words, to take advantage of the AI paradigm shift. Its own faculty’s experience of that paradigm shift, so far, is a compliance system that cannot reliably tell fact from fabrication, deployed against the very people whose academic freedom the university claims to be protecting from ideological capture.

The American Association of University Professors has already sued, arguing the review constitutes viewpoint discrimination in violation of the First Amendment. The suit, filed on July 8, 2026 by the AAUP and its state affiliate, Texas AAUP-AFT, names Creighton and the regents and sits in the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Texas in El Paso. As Jessica Priest reported for the Texas Tribune, it challenges the December 1, 2025 and April 9, 2026 memos on First Amendment grounds, argues under the Fourteenth Amendment that they are too vague for faculty to know what is allowed, and alleges that restrictions on teaching Black history and racial inequality discriminate against Black faculty. The complaint’s examples include a Lubbock medical professor told that students could not observe the care of transgender patients and an instructor restricted in using the word “disparity” while teaching about health disparities. “Faculty at Texas Tech are being barred from teaching that gay and bisexual men were persecuted during the Holocaust,” Nicholas Hite of Lambda Legal, one of the plaintiffs’ lawyers, said in a statement reported by Women in Academia Report, “and an entire Women’s and Gender Studies program shuttered by administrative decree. That is viewpoint discrimination, and it’s unconstitutional.” The plaintiffs asked for a preliminary injunction on July 17. In a July 31 response, posted on CourtListener, the system’s lawyers argued that faculty self-censorship is not a concrete injury, that the memos set up a review process rather than final enforceable decisions, and that classroom teaching is the employer’s speech under Garcetti v. Ceballos and so falls outside the First Amendment’s protection for public employees. A system spokeswoman, Erin Wilson, said its commitment to academic integrity “will not be distracted by lawsuits.” As of early October 2026, no ruling on the injunction had been reported. That case will turn on the human policy — the memos, the bans, the undefined terms. But somewhere inside the discovery record will sit the artefact that makes this essay’s point better than any legal brief could: a report, generated by a machine, accusing a professor of teaching a class that never existed, treated as evidence anyway, at a public university that did not think to tell its own top academic officer the machine was running.
