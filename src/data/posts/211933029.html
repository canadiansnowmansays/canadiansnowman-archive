by Stephen Gordon

I count my essays. Readers of this series know that by now, and know that every so often the count lands on a number that was already a year before I got to it — a list, a log, a demotion — and I stop whatever I was doing and write about it.

This is essay 1,582, and it is about the only time in Western history that the books of time themselves were formally corrected — the year an institution looked at the calendar, found it running ten days in arrears, and, rather than carrying the error forward as institutions prefer to do, simply wrote the days off. In October of 1582, in the lands that obeyed the decree, Thursday the fourth was followed directly by Friday the fifteenth. Ten dates — October 5th through the 14th — were deleted from existence. No one born on those days that year; no rent, in principle, owed on them; no sins, presumably, committed. A hole in the ledger, cut on purpose, with a pope’s seal on the cut.

I have been looking forward to this one since the count left the 1400s. Let me start, as the reform itself did, with the error.

The Julian calendar — Julius Caesar’s, installed in 45 BC on the advice of an Alexandrian astronomer — reckoned the year at 365 days and one quarter, patched with a leap day every fourth year. It was a magnificent piece of rounding and it was wrong by about eleven minutes a year. Eleven minutes! I want to dwell on the smallness of it. No living person could feel eleven minutes a year; no harvest failed for it; a man could be born, work, and die inside a calendar eleven minutes fast and never file a complaint. But this series has a standing theorem, proven now across five centuries of entries, that a small error carried honestly is more dangerous than a large one, because nobody organizes a committee against it. Eleven minutes a year is a day every 128 years or so. By the sixteenth century, the drift had compounded to roughly ten days: the calendar said the spring equinox fell on March 21, and the actual sun, which does not read decrees, was arriving on the 11th.

The Church cared for a specific reason: Easter. The whole liturgical year hangs from Easter, and Easter hangs from the equinox, and the equinox had slipped its mooring. The books said one thing; the sky said another; and unlike the merchants and princes who could live with drift indefinitely, the Church had staked its authority on getting a date right.

And now the detail that converts this from a story about a fix into a story about institutions, which is the only kind this series tells: the error had been known — precisely, publicly, in writing — for more than three hundred years before anyone fixed it. Around 1267, the English friar Roger Bacon wrote to the pope himself describing the calendar’s drift and calling its persistence a scandal that any competent computist could verify. Nothing was done. Astronomers raised it again in the 1300s; councils took it up in the 1400s; learned men submitted schemes, and the schemes were received, and filed, and the eleven minutes went on compounding at their patient eleven minutes a year. Even the final push moved at the speed of committee: the Council of Trent authorized a reform in 1563, and the corrected calendar arrived nineteen years later, which any veteran of institutional life will recognize as prompt. Three centuries, then, between the audit and the adjustment. I keep a file of such intervals, drawn from the centuries this series has visited and from the ones I cover under other datelines, and the finding is depressingly stable: institutions do not act when an error is found. They act when the error finally threatens the one thing the institution cannot function without — in this case Easter, the date on which the whole edifice of authority rested. Until the error costs the crown jewel, it is a memo. Roger Bacon wrote the memo. The memo was three hundred and fifteen years old when it was actioned, which I believe stands as the record, though I write about a government currently making a run at it.

So Pope Gregory XIII convened the mathematicians, and here the story acquires the two men I actually want you to remember, because the pope got the calendar’s name and did none of the arithmetic — a distribution of credit this series documented at essay 1,522 and will document again, credit being the one quantity that always flows uphill.

The scheme belonged to Aloysius Lilius, a physician from Calabria, who worked out the correction and the elegant rule that would keep it corrected — and who died six years before the reform was enacted, so that the greatest fix in the history of timekeeping was submitted to the committee, as it were, posthumously, by his brother. The verification belonged to Christopher Clavius, the Jesuit mathematician who spent years checking, defending, and explaining the scheme against all comers. Lilius’s rule is the one you live under and have possibly never heard stated: a year divisible by four is a leap year, except century years, unless the century is divisible by 400. That triple hedge — the exception to the exception — trims the Julian overage almost exactly. It is why 1900 was not a leap year and 2000 was, a distinction that passed through your life unnoticed, which is the highest compliment an accounting rule can be paid.

So: the rule going forward, and the write-off going back. Ten days, cancelled by papal bull — the bull Inter gravissimas, issued in February 1582, effective that October. And the night the correction ran, the night of October 4th becoming the 15th, the Church happened to lose one of its greatest figures inside the fold of the ledger: Teresa of Ávila died that very night, so that her death sits precisely on the seam — on one side of midnight it was the 4th, and by morning it was the 15th, and the days between simply are not. I make no theological claim. I only note that the Numbers Game could not have staged it better: the century’s great mystic, departing through the one door in history that opens directly from a Thursday onto a week and a half later.

Now, the reception — because the reception is the essay.

You would think, and the mathematicians thought, that a correction this clean would be adopted the way one adopts any repair: gratefully. What happened instead is the purest demonstration this series owns of its bleakest finding, which is that people do not evaluate a number; they evaluate its sender. The Catholic states — Spain, Portugal, most of Italy, Poland — switched on schedule. Protestant Europe looked at ten days of impeccable arithmetic and saw the signature at the bottom. This was, remember, sixty-five years after essay 1,517; the wound was open; and the heirs of the man who demanded the Church answer his items line by line now declined to check the Church’s arithmetic at all, on the grounds of who had done it. Learned Protestant astronomers conceded privately that the mathematics was sound. It did not matter. For well over a century, Europe ran on two calendars ten days apart, so that a traveller could leave a city in one week and arrive, a day’s ride later, in the previous one; treaties carried double dates; and the continent’s merchants kept two sets of books not out of fraud, for once, but out of theology. A true number, rejected because of its publisher. I intend to say nothing further about the present day in this paragraph. The paragraph is doing it for me.

Where the calendar did change, ordinary people had questions, and the questions were about money, because the questions are always about money. If October has lost ten days, does a servant hired by the month receive a full month’s wage? Does the landlord collect a full month’s rent for a month he has partially deleted? Do interest and taxes accrue on days that did not occur? The authorities issued rulings — generally that wages, rents, and terms should be prorated or shifted rather than shortened — and I find the whole genre of these rulings quietly beautiful: solemn officials, in the autumn of 1582, drafting guidance on the billing implications of non-existent time. Somewhere in that paperwork is the true spirit of civilization, which is not the cathedral or the sonnet but the adjustment memo.

And here I must plant a flag for the future, because the best joke in this whole affair has a fuse a hundred and seventy years long. England — Protestant, proud, and in no mood for popish arithmetic — refused the reform. Refused it in 1582, refused it after the mathematics was beyond dispute, refused it while her own calendar drifted an eleventh day out of true, and went on refusing until 1752, when the empire finally submitted to the arithmetic it could no longer trade around, and eleven days were deleted from a September, and the grumbling, by tradition, was magnificent. That is a story for another essay — the count will arrive at 1,752 in due season, and when it does, I will be waiting with the fuse lit. This series has run a matched pair across a century before. It has never run one across a hundred and seventy years. The Numbers Game plays long.

Dior was informed this morning that ten days once vanished by decree, and her indifference was total, and for once I concede she has the stronger position. Dior’s calendar contains one day, renewed nightly. It cannot drift, because it does not accumulate; it cannot be corrected, because it is never wrong; every morning the equinox of breakfast arrives exactly on schedule because she is the one who defines the schedule. Gregory XIII needed Lilius, Clavius, a papal bull, and the deletion of a week and a half to get the sun and the ledger to agree. Dior does it daily by getting up. There are two kinds of timekeeping in the world, and I have spent fifteen hundred essays practising the wrong one.

The count moves on, as it does. The next landmark is essay 1,588, and it concerns the largest fleet the world had seen fitted out under a name that did its counting in advance — a hundred and thirty ships styled Most Fortunate before a single sail was raised. The sixteenth century, having corrected its calendar, was about to be reminded that the weather keeps books of its own.

Ten days went missing and the world ran better for it. Keep that on file. It is the only deletion this series has ever endorsed.

See you at 1,588, when the adjective sails before the fleet.
