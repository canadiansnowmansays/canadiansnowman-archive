By the time silk had become a fixture of elite Roman fashion, sometime in the last decades of the first century BCE, the Roman Republic — soon to become an empire — had never had, and would never have, direct contact with the Chinese producers of the fabric its citizens were increasingly obsessed with. This is the detail that makes Rome’s entire relationship with silk so peculiar, and so useful as a case study in how thoroughly a trade good can reshape a society’s economy and self-image without that society ever once controlling, understanding, or even correctly identifying its source. Rome fought no wars over silk, sent no Zhang Qian-style expeditions to find it, and never established a single trading post anywhere near the Chinese heartland where it was produced. It simply bought the fabric, at extraordinary and escalating markup, from a chain of Central Asian and Parthian middlemen who had every commercial incentive to keep Rome exactly as ignorant of the supply chain as it remained for the better part of four centuries.

The Parthian Empire’s role in this arrangement cannot be overstated, and it explains a great deal about why Roman writers complained so bitterly about the price of silk without ever proposing the obvious solution of simply trading with China directly. Parthia — a rival empire that controlled Mesopotamia and the Iranian plateau, and that fought Rome to a bloody standstill on multiple occasions, most famously at the Battle of Carrhae in 53 BCE, where the Parthians destroyed a Roman army and reportedly captured its legionary standards — sat squarely across every practical overland route between Rome and the silk-producing regions of the Far East. This was not an accident of geography that Parthia merely benefited from; it was a position Parthian merchants and administrators actively defended, since their entire commercial advantage depended on remaining the indispensable middleman between two civilizations that were never permitted to discover they could, in principle, trade with each other without Parthian markup at every stage. Silk moving west from China passed through Sogdian traders, then through Parthian hands, changing ownership and price multiple times before it ever reached a Roman port or overland caravan terminus in Syria — and every transaction along that chain added a margin that Roman buyers had no way to see around, since they had no independent knowledge of what the fabric had cost, or been worth, at its point of origin.

The Roman reaction to this arrangement split, roughly, into two registers: economic alarm and moral panic, and Roman writers frequently indulged both within the same paragraph. Pliny the Elder, writing his Natural History in the first century CE, offered what has become the era’s most quoted complaint on the subject, estimating that India, China, and Arabia combined drained the Roman Empire of a hundred million sesterces annually in exchange for luxuries that, in his view, amounted to nothing more than adornment for wives and mistresses. Pliny’s number is almost certainly an exaggeration, or at minimum an estimate built on incomplete information — Roman customs records from the period are fragmentary, and no ancient writer had anything resembling a modern balance-of-trade calculation available to them — but the fact that a serious Roman naturalist felt the drain was severe enough to include prominently in an encyclopedic work on the natural world tells you something about how genuinely unsettling the outflow of precious metal felt to at least some segment of the Roman elite. Rome, notably, had very little that Chinese or Central Asian buyers wanted in return beyond gold and silver coinage itself, which meant the silk trade functioned, from a purely fiscal standpoint, as a one-directional siphon of Roman specie flowing east with no comparable flow of goods coming back to balance it.

The moral objections ran alongside the fiscal ones and were, if anything, delivered with even more theatrical disapproval. Seneca the Younger, in one of his more quotable passages of social criticism, denounced silk garments as a betrayal of clothing’s basic purpose, arguing that fabric so sheer and revealing left a woman unable to swear honestly, before witnesses or before her own conscience, that she was not naked — an objection that says a great deal about Roman anxieties surrounding female visibility and sexual display, and rather less about the fabric itself, which had, after all, clothed emperors and empresses in China for centuries without occasioning quite the same panic about moral collapse. The Senate took these complaints seriously enough to act on them at least once: under Tiberius, in the early first century CE, the Senate passed a decree, recorded by the historian Tacitus, forbidding men from wearing silk garments — an effort to preserve some notional line between appropriately masculine Roman dress and the supposedly effeminizing softness of eastern fabric. The measure did essentially nothing to slow silk’s spread. Sumptuary laws restricting fashion have an almost perfect historical track record of failure, across every culture that has tried them, and Rome’s attempt to legislate silk off of male bodies joined a long list of such laws that were passed with great moral seriousness and then quietly ignored by precisely the elite audience they were meant to restrain.

It’s worth asking what, specifically, Roman buyers were purchasing, because “silk” in the Roman marketplace was not a single uniform product, and understanding its varieties helps explain both its price and its social meaning. Raw, unwoven Chinese silk thread commanded one price; silk that had been partially processed or rewoven in workshops in the eastern Mediterranean — often in the Phoenician cities, and later in Egyptian Alexandria — commanded another, since these intermediary workshops would unravel Chinese silk fabric and reweave it into thinner, more transparent textiles suited to Roman and Greek fashion sensibilities, effectively adding a Mediterranean manufacturing step onto a Chinese raw material before it reached its final wealthy buyer. This meant that much of the silk worn in Rome had never been produced, in its final woven form, anywhere near China at all — the Chinese contribution was the thread, and the fabric a Roman aristocrat actually wore might have been rewoven in Tyre or Berytus by craftsmen who had no more direct knowledge of Chinese sericulture than the Roman buyer did. The entire arrangement, in other words, involved multiple layers of transformation and markup across at least three or four distinct civilizations, none of whom had reliable information about what was happening at the other end of the chain — an early and instructive example of a genuinely globalized supply chain functioning acceptably well commercially while remaining almost totally opaque to everyone participating in it.

Roman knowledge of where silk actually came from remained remarkably poor throughout this entire period, and the theories offered by supposedly authoritative Roman sources reveal just how effectively the Parthians and other middlemen had managed to obscure the supply chain. Pliny himself repeated a widely circulated belief that silk was combed, rather than woven, from the leaves or bark of trees growing in a distant eastern forest — a theory presumably encouraged, whether deliberately or simply through the accumulated distortion of information passing through many hands, by intermediaries who had every commercial interest in Roman buyers remaining vague about silk’s true origin and true cost of production. The Chinese, for their part, showed comparably limited curiosity about Rome specifically, though Chinese sources from the Han and later periods do describe a distant western land called Daqin, generally understood to refer to the Roman Empire or its eastern provinces, in terms that suggest secondhand and heavily filtered knowledge rather than direct contact. A Roman embassy allegedly reached the Han court in 166 CE, during the reign of Emperor Huan, according to Chinese court records — though the gifts it presented were unimpressive enough that Chinese chroniclers suspected the visitors were merchants exaggerating their diplomatic credentials rather than genuine representatives of the Roman state. Four centuries into a trade relationship that had reshaped fashion, fiscal policy, and moral rhetoric on one end of the continent, the two civilizations at either terminus of the silk trade had still barely spoken to each other directly, and when they finally did, neither side entirely believed the other was who they claimed to be.

Not all of this trade moved overland through Parthian territory, and the maritime alternative deserves attention because it reveals just how motivated Roman merchants were to route around Parthian markup wherever geography allowed it. From roughly the first century CE onward, Roman and Greek-Egyptian traders operating out of Red Sea ports made increasing use of the monsoon wind patterns to sail directly to India, bypassing the overland Parthian corridor entirely for goods originating in South Asia, even if Chinese silk itself still had to pass through intermediary hands somewhere along the way. The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, a merchant’s guide written by an anonymous Greek-Egyptian trader probably in the first century CE, describes this maritime network in remarkable commercial detail — port by port, listing which goods could be bought and sold at each stop from the Egyptian Red Sea coast down to India’s Malabar coast — and it reveals a Roman trading world considerably more geographically ambitious, and considerably more willing to bypass Parthia when possible, than the popular image of a single overland caravan route might suggest. Archaeological finds bear this out: Roman gold coins turn up in significant quantities at sites in southern India, and Tamil poetry from the period describes Roman ships arriving loaded with wine and gold and departing loaded with pepper, evidence of a maritime spice and luxury trade that ran parallel to, and partially independent of, the silk trade’s overland Parthian bottleneck.

Palmyra, the wealthy caravan city in the Syrian desert, occupies a similarly important place in this story as a kind of pressure valve for Roman merchants seeking terms slightly more favorable than a straight Parthian monopoly could impose. Positioned at the edge of Roman territory but maintaining its own semi-independent trading relationships eastward, Palmyra grew extraordinarily wealthy during the first three centuries CE by serving as a negotiated middle ground — a city whose merchant families, some of whose funerary inscriptions and portrait busts survive in remarkable numbers today, built fortunes precisely by managing the diplomatic and commercial friction between Rome and Parthia rather than being crushed by it. The city’s spectacular ruins, including its colonnaded main street and monumental tower tombs, stand as physical evidence of just how much wealth a well-positioned middleman city could accumulate from a trade relationship in which the two ultimate producing and consuming civilizations, Rome and China, never dealt with each other directly at all. Palmyra’s fortunes would eventually collapse in the third century CE, after its ambitious queen Zenobia briefly carved out an independent empire and was crushed by Roman forces under Aurelian in 272 CE — a reminder that being an indispensable middleman was a genuinely lucrative position right up until the moment a larger power decided the middleman had grown too independent to tolerate.

It is worth spending a moment on what the Romans were actually failing to understand, because the production process they could not imagine was genuinely elaborate and helps explain why China held the monopoly as long as it did. Silk comes from the cocoon of the domesticated moth Bombyx mori, an insect so thoroughly bred for the purpose over several thousand years that it can no longer fly and cannot survive without human care. The larvae eat mulberry leaves — only mulberry leaves, in quantities that require dedicated orchards — and after roughly a month spin a cocoon from a single continuous filament that can run to hundreds of metres. To harvest an unbroken filament, the pupa must be killed before it emerges, typically by heat, and the cocoons are then softened in hot water and the filaments reeled off and twisted together, since a single strand is far too fine to weave. Every stage requires specific knowledge: which mulberry variety, what temperature and humidity for the rearing rooms, when precisely to harvest, how many filaments to combine. This is not a secret that can be stolen by examining the finished product, which is why Rome, holding the fabric in its hands for four centuries, got no closer to the answer than a theory about tree bark. Breaking the monopoly required someone to physically remove both the insects and the knowledge from China at the same time — which, as Part V of this series will describe, is eventually exactly what happened.

Chinese knowledge of Rome was correspondingly filtered, though rather less fanciful. The Hou Hanshu, the history of the Later Han compiled in the fifth century from earlier records, describes Daqin — the Roman east — in terms that are recognisably derived from real information passed through many hands: a land of walled cities and honest merchants who set fixed prices, ruled by a king who could be removed if disasters occurred, producing gold, silver, glass, and remarkable textiles. Some of this is accurate, some is idealised, and the detail about deposing kings for natural disasters looks suspiciously like a Chinese political theory of the mandate of heaven projected onto a foreign country. The same source notes, with evident interest, that Daqin had long wished to send embassies to China but that Parthia had obstructed them in order to protect its trade in Chinese silk — a Chinese observation, arrived at independently, of exactly the middleman dynamic Roman writers were complaining about from the other end. Two empires, four thousand miles apart, had each separately worked out that they were being managed by the party in the middle, and neither could do anything about it.

What this leaves us with, at the Mediterranean end of the story, is a civilization that spent roughly four centuries importing a good it could not produce, did not understand, could not source directly, and periodically tried and failed to suppress through legislation — all while its own moralists used that same good as a running metaphor for the empire’s supposed decline into decadence and softness. It’s a pattern worth remembering as this series moves further along the route, because Rome would not be the last civilization to build genuine economic and cultural anxiety around a Central Asian trade good it never fully controlled, and the silk road’s next chapters — moving through Kushan middlemen, Sassanid-Byzantine monopoly wars, and eventually the Islamic caliphates — will show variations on the same basic arrangement recurring for centuries after Rome itself had fallen: enormous wealth and status attached to a fabric, moving through hands that added value and cost at every stage, purchased by buyers who rarely had any real leverage over the source.
