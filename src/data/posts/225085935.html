A year ago this month, the Pan American Health Organization told Canada it had failed. On November 10, 2025, PAHO’s director, Jarbas Barbosa, announced that an outbreak that had begun in October 2024 had run for more than twelve months without a break, and that measles was therefore endemic in Canada again for the first time since 1998. Because one country had lost its status, the whole of the Americas had too, as Healio reported at the time. We took it badly up here. Losing an elimination certificate is the public-health equivalent of having your licence pulled: everyone knows you were careless, and everyone knows it was preventable.

So, from the Canadian Snowman, a word to our neighbours: welcome to the club.

On November 17, 2026, PAHO made it official. At a media briefing in Washington, Jarbas Barbosa announced that the independent commission PAHO had convened on November 10–13 had found endemic measles transmission re-established in the United States, as well as in Mexico and Bolivia, and had recommended that all three lose the elimination status (in America’s case, the one it earned in 2000), as PAHO announced. It was, Barbosa said, “a recommendation which I have accepted,” as CNBC reported. Tom Bartlett had reported the commission’s verdict in The Atlantic on November 14, 2026. The technical test is simple. If the same strain of the virus causes continuous infections for twelve months straight, the disease is endemic. PAHO’s review began with the West Texas outbreak of January 2025. The chain that settled it, according to KFF Health News, ran through Utah, where an outbreak of the same strain passed the one-year mark in June and is still going. The CDC’s answer was that “the measles elimination designation is not what protects Americans,” as ABC News reported, and HHS said it would keep working to close vaccination gaps.

The decision is technical. The failure is not.

The numbers

The United States has confirmed 5,412 measles cases this year, as of November 12, according to Contagion Live‘s tally of CDC figures. That is more than double the total for all of 2025 (itself the worst year since 1991, with 2,289 cases), and it was reached in just over ten months. KFF Health News counts more than 7,600 confirmed cases since the start of 2025. Paul Offit, the vaccine scientist who has spent his career explaining the obvious to people who would rather not hear it, called the coming verdict a slap in the face, an admission that the country had failed its own population.

The deaths are where it stops being a statistic. Pennsylvania began reporting measles deaths in August and has now reported seven. New York has reported one. Wisconsin reported its second on November 13, as WPR reported. New York’s death and Wisconsin’s first were the first measles deaths in those states in more than thirty years. Both were infants.

That is at least ten Americans. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s dashboard lists six.

The missing four

The gap between ten and six is not a rounding error. It is a policy.

After Pennsylvania reported its first two measles deaths in August, both of them unvaccinated, Robert F. Kennedy Jr. asked CDC Director Erica Schwartz to take them off the agency’s online tally, as Yasmeen Abutaleb and Dan Levine reported for Reuters on September 4. Agency staff had already accepted Pennsylvania’s finding; Schwartz carried out the request without objection. That broke with the CDC’s long-standing practice of taking state health departments’ counts at face value, as Lena H. Sun has reported for The Washington Post. The agency then changed its method, switching to federal death records. Those records take weeks to arrive and were never designed for tracking an outbreak while it is still spreading. Kennedy and Governor Josh Shapiro spent the last week of August arguing in public over the deaths, with Kennedy suggesting, as 6abc reported, that they might have been “altogether fabricated.”

This series has a name for the move. In the TACO framework (Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate) it is the fourth letter. When the number is bad, change the way you count it. A death that does not appear on the federal dashboard does not appear in the federal talking points, and a health secretary who would rather talk about something else is spared four of the hardest conversations of his tenure.

“It began before I came into office”

On April 22, Kennedy told the Senate health committee, as the Washington Times reported, that “the measles epidemic began before I came into office.” It is true, as far as it goes. The West Texas outbreak was spreading by January 2025, weeks before his confirmation. Childhood vaccination rates, MMR included, had been falling since the pandemic emptied doctors’ waiting rooms. The Department of Health and Human Services made the same point to Reuters on November 11, as CTV News carried it: rates were dropping before Kennedy arrived. And the United States is not alone. Canada lost its status a year ago. Austria, Spain and the United Kingdom were among six countries that lost theirs in January, as Reuters reported. Mexico and Bolivia went before the same PAHO commission as the United States.

But “it began before me” is an alibi for the first month, not the twenty-second. Kennedy has been the most senior public-health official in the United States for nearly two years. The fire was burning when he walked in. Since then measles has reached 45 states and the District of Columbia, and the question is no longer who lit it but what the man holding the hose has done with it.

Canada is a useful comparison precisely because its failure was so ordinary. Pandemic-era gaps in immunisation, under-vaccinated communities, an outbreak that got ahead of public-health departments: no Canadian health minister went on television to muse about whether the vaccine was safe, or promoted cod-liver oil from the podium. Canada’s outbreak was declared over in mid-October 2026, as CTV News reported, and the country can now begin the long climb back. The American outbreak, run by a department whose secretary built his public career on vaccine doubt, is still growing.

The vitamin A that nobody ordered

The pattern was set in the first weeks. In March 2025, after Texas recorded America’s first measles death in a decade, Kennedy told Fox that the federal government was delivering vitamin A to the stricken communities of West Texas right then. As Nicholas Florko reported for The Atlantic on March 28, 2025, none had arrived at the state health department, and the reason was not a broken promise. Texas had never asked for any, because its doctors had never asked the state for any. Over the same period Texas had received 1,760 additional doses of MMR vaccine from Washington, which is to say the part of the federal response that worked was the part that predated Kennedy.

Vitamin A has a real but narrow role in measles care: it can lower the risk of death in very young children who are already infected, particularly those who are deficient. It does not prevent the disease. Kennedy’s endorsement sent families in Gaines County to clear the drugstore shelves of it, and of cod-liver oil, and some children were soon taking doses large enough to show signs of liver damage. Within days of Kennedy’s first public comments on the outbreak, Thomas Corry, HHS’s new assistant secretary for public affairs, had resigned after two weeks in the job, frustrated, as CBS News reported, with how the secretary was handling it.

A condolence call in Seminole

Then came the funerals. On April 6, 2025, Kennedy flew to Seminole, Texas, the county seat of Gaines County, for the service of Daisy Hildebrand, eight years old and the second child to die in the outbreak. A thousand mourners filled an unmarked Mennonite church. Afterward, at a steak dinner, Kennedy sat with the Hildebrands and the family of six-year-old Kayley Fehr, who had died in February.

In public, on X that afternoon, he called the MMR vaccine the most effective way to stop measles spreading. In private, according to Bartlett’s April 10, 2025 report in The Atlantic, he told Daisy’s father something else. Peter Hildebrand said the secretary told him, “You don’t know what’s in the vaccine anymore.” Hildebrand also said Kennedy never described the vaccine as helpful. He took the remark as confirmation that his doubts about the shot had been right. The two West Texas doctors who had advised the family, and whom Kennedy had praised as extraordinary healers, were in the photographs he posted from the dinner.

A health secretary’s job, at a child’s funeral in the middle of an outbreak, is to console the family and protect the next child. Kennedy did the first, by every account warmly. He undermined the second in the same sentence.

Asked, not offered

Nineteen months on, the pattern holds. In August, as the outbreak in Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, was growing fast, Kennedy told CNN that parents should get their children vaccinated against measles. He said it because Dana Bash asked him to. For any previous health secretary the sentence would have been a routine part of the job, said without prompting and often. From Kennedy it was news. When he did say it again, in Tampa on August 26, it came in the same breath as an accusation that Pennsylvania was withholding details about its dead, as WUSF reported. Since then he has said little about measles and a good deal about Shapiro, and about his cooking show. A recent episode was devoted to chickpea salad.

HHS says Kennedy and Schwartz continue to recommend the MMR vaccine as the most effective protection, and points to staff sent to Pennsylvania, New York and Wisconsin for contact tracing and surveillance, and to $100 million recently sent to the states, as evidence of an aggressive response. The money and the epidemiologists are real, and the people doing the work deserve credit. But a CDC official, speaking anonymously to Bartlett, described a response without a clear strategy or support from the top, and the record bears that out.

The alert that didn’t go out

The clearest example is one most Americans never heard about, because it was never sent.

Measles in pregnancy has been so rare since 2000 that most American doctors have never seen a case. It can cause pneumonia and death in the mother, and miscarriage, premature birth and low birth weight in the baby. The virus can cross to the fetus and cause congenital measles, which can mean brain inflammation and death. And the MMR vaccine cannot be given during pregnancy. Pennsylvania’s health department has said that 77 pregnant women in the state have been diagnosed with measles since its outbreak began in late April. State law bars it from saying how those pregnancies ended, and there is no national database to say how many there have been elsewhere.

Around the end of September, according to Sun’s reporting, CDC scientists tried to warn clinicians through the Health Alert Network, the agency’s system for urgent notices to doctors, laboratories and health departments. The alert usually needs sign-off from the director’s office. It did not get it, and the people Sun spoke to did not know why. The scientists turned instead to the agency’s weekly journal, the Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, where clearance is slower, and then began discussing an outside journal, because other vaccine-related reports that had already cleared review were being held back. Two reports on whooping cough had their publication pushed to November. One found that most of the babies who died had mothers who had not received the Tdap vaccine in pregnancy.

Jeanne Marrazzo, chief executive of the Infectious Diseases Society of America, called the situation a profound vacuum of guidance. Michelle Barton-Forbes, who heads paediatric infectious diseases at the Children’s Hospital at London Health Sciences Centre in London, Ontario (the city where I was born), managed pregnant patients through Canada’s outbreak and told Sun how thin the data are. Canada’s doctors had to learn this on the job. America’s doctors are learning it the same way, while the warning their own government’s scientists wrote waits for a signature.

Friends, and a Kraken

None of this happens in isolation, though Kennedy would like it to seem so. In September, Children’s Health Defense, the anti-vaccine nonprofit he founded, published an article telling parents they need not fear measles and repeating the false claim that the infection makes children stronger. A week later, with four Pennsylvanians dead, Kennedy told a Children’s Health Defense conference that its members had a strong and steadfast friend at the White House. He has said nothing about the article. As this series noted in “Friends in All the Wrong Places,” the friendship is not in doubt. The question is who else he is a friend to.

The political ground has moved under him since. On November 3, the movement’s flagship candidates lost badly. Ken Paxton, who wrapped himself in Make America Healthy Again, lost the Texas Senate race, and Zach Lahn, who had appeared with Kennedy the week before, lost the race for governor of Iowa. Annie Andrews, a paediatrician, won a Senate seat in South Carolina partly by attacking Kennedy on vaccines. Calley Means, one of Kennedy’s closest allies, quit his post as a senior adviser in late October and went on Joe Rogan’s podcast to blame Susie Wiles, then the White House chief of staff, for stalling the agenda, as Sinclair’s National Desk reported. Within days Wiles was gone.

But Kennedy’s position has, if anything, been fortified at the top. President Trump backs his vaccine agenda. The new White House chief of staff, Stephen Miller, has echoed Trump’s call to split the MMR into three separate shots, which are not available separately and which experts say would be pointless, and has complained, without evidence, about pressure to fill newborns with vaccines. Kennedy greeted Miller’s promotion on X on November 6 with an instruction to “unleash the Kraken,” as the New York Post reported. He did not say what the Kraken was.

What the club costs

Membership of the club is not permanent. Canada’s outbreak is over, and if it stays over for twelve months the country can apply to have its status restored. The way out is the way in, in reverse: high vaccination coverage, fast and honest surveillance, clinicians told what they need to know, and public-health officials who say, without being asked, that the vaccine works.

The United States has the scientists, the money and the laboratories to do all of that. What it lacks is a secretary of health who will lead it. The most contagious disease known to medicine has already been unleashed. Kennedy’s own Kraken, whatever it turns out to be, will have to wait its turn.
