For two years the most reliable word in the Republican vocabulary was “woke.”

It was the word for everything the administration wanted gone. Diversity offices were closed because they were woke. Museum exhibits were rewritten because they were woke. Military bases got their Confederate names back because the old renaming had been woke. Generals were fired, books were pulled from base libraries and universities lost their grants, all in the name of curing the country of its wokeness. In the last weeks of the 2026 campaign the word carried the Republican closing argument almost by itself. James Talarico, a seminarian running for the Senate in Texas, became “Talafreako.” Dr. Annie Andrews, a pediatrician in South Carolina, was attacked for her past views on transgender children. Dr. Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan was called a “jihadist” by the president of the United States.

On 3 November 2026, America woke up.

It did so in both senses of the word. It woke up from a spell, and the people it elected were very nearly the roll call of everyone the anti-woke campaign had spent two years warning it about.

The roll call

Start with the list, because the list is the point.

Michigan elected Dr. Abdul El-Sayed, the son of Egyptian immigrants, as the first Muslim senator in American history. He introduced himself to his victory party in Detroit using the full Arabic pronunciation of his name. He had a message for the president, who had mangled that name and turned his middle name into an epithet. “I know you struggled with my name,” he said. “You can just go on and call me senator.”

New Hampshire elected Representative Chris Pappas, who becomes the first openly gay man to serve in the Senate. He barely mentioned the milestone on the campaign trail. As he told The New York Times, he thinks people, regardless of their politics, mostly want their neighbours to be able to be themselves.

Georgia elected Keisha Lance Bottoms, the first Black woman ever elected governor of an American state. Before 3 November only three Black governors had ever been elected in the country’s history: Douglas Wilder in Virginia, Deval Patrick in Massachusetts and Wes Moore in Maryland, who was re-elected the same night. On 3 November four states elected their first: Bottoms in Georgia, David Crowley in Wisconsin, Aaron Ford in Nevada and, it should be said, a Republican, Byron Donalds, in Florida. In one night the country elected more Black governors than in its entire history before.

New Mexico elected Deb Haaland, the first Native American woman to govern a state. Minnesota elected Peggy Flanagan, the first Native American woman elected to the Senate. Texas elected Gina Hinojosa, the first Latina to lead the state, and California elected Xavier Becerra, its first Latino governor. Ohio elected Dr. Amy Acton, its first woman governor. South Carolina elected Dr. Annie Andrews, the first woman the state has ever sent to the Senate by its own vote.

Hakeem Jeffries, meanwhile, is in line to become the first Black Speaker of the House.

None of these candidates ran on identity. Most of them ran on the price of gas. That is what makes the list remarkable. For two years the country was told that diversity was a plot against ordinary Americans. When ordinary Americans finally had their say, they didn’t seem to notice the difference.

The spell

The other kind of waking up was quieter, and it happened in pickup trucks.

The people who decided this election were not, for the most part, converts to anything. They were people who had believed a promise and watched it fail. Andrew Leyva, a 39-year-old blinds installer in Scottsdale, voted for Trump three times. He told Yvonne Wingett Sanchez of The Atlantic that he had expected $2 gas. He now spends nearly twice what he did a year ago, sometimes $300 a week. He didn’t vote in 2026. He thinks he may never vote again.

In Texas, Matt Viser and Nick Miroff of The Atlantic spent election eve in the corner of Bastrop that locals call “Elon Land,” home to the Boring Company, SpaceX and Starlink. Elon Musk had poured almost $200 million into Texas, most of it to help Ken Paxton. A SpaceX project manager there, a conservative in a Carhartt hat, had just paid $100 for three-quarters of a tank of gas. He told them Paxton should be in jail. Over several hours, in conversations with more than a dozen people whose livelihoods depended on Musk, they could not find one who was voting for Musk’s candidate.

At Paxton’s own party in Dallas, a Republican activist walked out of the ballroom looking stunned. He blamed the president for telling everyone to vote as if he were on the ballot. That, he said, was “absolutely insane.”

The waking up even reached the Rio Grande Valley, where Trump had turned Starr County Republican for the first time since 1892. Henry Cuellar, who represents it, said people used to tell him “El Trumpo va muy fuerte,” Trump is going strong. “Not anymore.” On 3 November Democrats won all four Texas districts that border Mexico.

This is the part of the result that should trouble the White House most. You can dismiss a Democratic voter as a Democrat. You cannot easily dismiss a man in a Carhartt hat in Elon Land.

Pretend I’m on the ballot

For two months Trump asked voters to pretend his name was on the ballot. Jonathan Lemire wrote in The Atlantic on election night that the voters listened, and then rejected him.

It was a strange closing argument for a man whose approval rating had fallen to 38 per cent. It became stranger in the final days. On 2 November, the day before the vote, Trump gave his last speech of the campaign beside the Reflecting Pool on the National Mall. It was the site of his renovation project, which had been undone by algae and a no-bid contractor, and he spent much of the speech on its pipes. In the closing week he also found time to praise the physique of an Ohio senator.

The Democrats, by contrast, said one thing over and over. Michael Scherer and Ashley Parker reported in The Atlantic that Jeffries spent two years insisting his party talk about little except the cost of living, even when his own members wanted to fight about everything else. Democratic strategists learned to turn corruption into a cost-of-living argument: things are expensive, they told their ad makers, because the system is rigged. One consultant reversed Trump’s most effective ad from 2024. That ad had said Kamala Harris was “for they/them” while Trump was “for you.” By 2026, the consultant said, Republicans were focused on “he/him,” and Democrats were focused on you.

It worked because it was true. Trump started a war with Iran that drove up the price of diesel. He imposed the costliest tariffs since Herbert Hoover. He spent the final week talking about ballrooms, monuments and a golf course on the Potomac. Jonathan Chait, also in The Atlantic, offered a verdict on the president’s supposed political genius: stripped of the strong economy he inherited the first time, Trump has “no more political magic than Jimmy Carter.”

The numbers

The verdict was not close. Democrats won the national House vote by about 13 points, the largest margin for either party in a congressional election since 1976, according to estimates by The New York Times. They flipped seven Senate seats: Maine, Iowa, Kansas, Ohio, North Carolina, South Carolina and Texas. Kansas had not elected a Democratic senator in nearly a century, and Texas had not elected a Democrat statewide since 1994. In South Carolina, as Madeleine Carlisle reported for The Atlantic, Andrews won rural Chester County, which Trump had carried by 17 points two years earlier.

Even the people Trump recruited came back. The Voter Trends estimates published by The New York Times show voters under 30 shifting 32 points toward the Democrats compared with 2024, and Hispanic voters 28. Counties with large Hispanic populations in Kansas swung by about 45 points.

What waking up does not mean

It would be easy to read all this as a conversion. It isn’t, and the people who won mostly know it.

On the same night, Arizona voters approved a measure barring transgender athletes from teams that match their gender identity. Florida elected a Republican governor and kept its Republican senator. Geraldo Cadava, writing in The Atlantic, noted that more than a third of Latinos, the group that swung furthest of all, still favoured deporting every undocumented immigrant. Idrees Kahloon pointed out in The Atlantic that the Democrats won partly by learning the lessons of 2024, including that they had drifted too far left on immigration and on transgender issues. And an Austin lobbyist who works with both parties offered Viser and Miroff the sourest summary of the night: people vote against people. If Democrats think they won because they had all the issues, he said, they didn’t.

That is fair. America did not wake up woke. It woke up from Trump. What it noticed, once it was awake, was that the country the president had warned it about, run by a Muslim doctor, a gay congressman from a New Hampshire diner family, a Black woman from Atlanta and a Latina from Austin, looked a good deal like the country it already lived in.

The morning after

Trump spent election night watching Fox News and, according to The New York Times, complaining about it. Once the losses were clear, he posted that he had not been on the ballot, and that if he had been, Republicans “would have won IN A LANDSLIDE!” It was an odd conclusion from a man who had spent two months asking the country to pretend he was on the ballot.

Jake Lundberg, writing in The Atlantic on election day, recalled the last president who tried this. In 1866 Andrew Johnson toured the country making the midterms a referendum on himself. One of the magazine’s own writers, E. P. Whipple, counted the “immense protuberance” of the first-person pronoun in his speeches. Johnson’s opponents gained enough seats to override his vetoes. Two years later he became the first president to be impeached.

History does not repeat that neatly, and Democrats will not have a veto-proof Congress in January. But it rhymes. A president who makes an election about himself should not be surprised when the voters take him at his word.

Rip Van Winkle slept for twenty years and woke to find the king’s portrait on the tavern wall replaced by George Washington’s. Trump has had a shorter nap and a ruder awakening. The country that went to bed on 2 November is not the one that woke up on the 4th. It is more diverse, a good deal angrier about the price of diesel, and newly unafraid of the word he spent two years trying to make it fear.
