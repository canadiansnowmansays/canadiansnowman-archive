Every deadline in a lawsuit is really just a countdown to a different kind of silence. On Friday, September 4, 2026, that countdown hit zero in The New York Times v. OpenAI and Microsoft, when both sides filed the last motions they are permitted to file before Judge Sidney H. Stein of the Southern District of New York either sends the case to trial or ends it himself. A ruling is expected in the coming weeks. Whichever way it goes, this was the moment the entire dispute — two and a half years of litigation, millions of pages of discovery, and the largest unresolved legal question hanging over the AI industry — got reduced to its two simplest possible questions: did the machines transform what they were given, and did they replace it.

Two questions, and a courtroom full of evidence for both

The Times’s position, laid out in its own filing, is that nothing about feeding millions of its articles into a language model transforms them into something new in the way copyright law requires. And separately, the paper argues, ChatGPT and Copilot don’t just resemble the news — they substitute for it, siphoning off the readers who would otherwise have clicked through to the original reporting. The Times wrote in its filing that the future of both journalism and responsible AI depends on preserving the incentive for humans to keep producing the work AI is trained on. That is the crux of a “market harm” argument under fair use: it isn’t enough to show that a use is transformative in the abstract, if that same use guts the market for the original.

Microsoft and OpenAI, unsurprisingly, see two different questions. Microsoft’s brief argues copyright law “does not permit rights holders to block transformative technologies” — pointing at Copilot as exhibit A. OpenAI’s brief goes further, arguing its products put the world’s facts “within reach of anyone who asks, in whatever form is most useful to them,” and that this is progress copyright law was never meant to obstruct.

What makes Friday’s filings worth a full essay, rather than a paragraph in next month’s roundup, is how each side chose to argue those two questions — because both sides reached well outside the four corners of copyright law to make their case, and the reaching itself tells you something about how thin the legal ground has gotten on both sides.

Byron’s daughter, Warhol’s silkscreen, and a pennant from 1951

Microsoft’s filing invokes Lord Byron — the poet who, the brief notes, believed literature and science were fundamentally at odds — only to pivot to his daughter, Ada Lovelace, one of the first people in history to describe what a computer could do. Lovelace, in Microsoft’s telling, saw the earliest computing machines as instruments of “poetical science,” creative tools rather than mechanical threats to creativity. It is an elegant argument and also, transparently, a rhetorical one: if the founding vision of computing already contained room for art and machine to coexist, then a language model trained on news articles is just the latest chapter in that story, not a rupture from it.

The Times reaches for a different kind of cultural ammunition — Andy Warhol’s 2023 Supreme Court loss, in which the justices found that Warhol’s silkscreened portrait of Prince, based on another photographer’s copyrighted image, did not qualify as fair use. The message is not subtle: even a transformation performed by one of the twentieth century’s most celebrated artists, using a wholly different medium, was not transformative enough to escape a market-substitution finding. If Warhol couldn’t clear that bar with a silkscreen, the Times is asking the court to conclude, why should a chatbot clear it by rephrasing an article.

And then there is OpenAI’s contribution to the brief-writing arts: a reference to a hypothetical web search for the 1951 New York Giants’ National League pennant — the team that famously lost that year’s World Series to the Yankees — dropped into a filing made in the Southern District of New York, the Giants’ old home turf. It reads as a wink rather than an argument: proof, OpenAI seems to want the court to notice, that its tools handle the same kind of everyday factual retrieval the Southern District’s own hometown history would produce, nothing more sinister than what a library card once did.

None of this is legal argument in the sense a first-year law student would recognize. It is advocacy dressed as culture, aimed at a judge before a ruling that will turn almost entirely on two much drier empirical questions: transformation, and substitution.

There is a reason both sides reach for cultural analogy rather than resting on doctrine alone. Fair use has always been a famously mushy four-factor test — the purpose and character of the use, the nature of the copyrighted work, the amount used, and the effect on the market — and none of the factors comes with a bright line attached. Lawyers on both sides know that a judge weighing those factors is, in practice, weighing a story about what kind of technology this is and what kind of harm it does or doesn’t do. Byron’s daughter and Warhol’s silkscreen are not decoration bolted onto the legal argument. They are the legal argument, translated into the register a judge is more likely to remember once the docket numbers blur together. It is worth noting, too, which side reached for which story: Microsoft chose an origin myth about computing and creativity coexisting; The Times chose a Supreme Court loss. Neither side chose a story about compromise.

An audience of more than one case

Judge Stein is not ruling in a vacuum, and both sides know it. A parallel suit brought by a group of authors against Meta reached its own fair-use ruling last year finding that training on legally acquired books could qualify as fair use — the same split outcome, want-it-both-ways logic this series covered when Anthropic’s book-piracy settlement was still working its way through approval. Getty Images has its own long-running suit against Stability AI over image generation, testing much the same transformation question in a different medium. None of these cases bind Judge Stein directly, but each one narrows the range of plausible outcomes the industry is now bracing for, and each new ruling becomes ammunition the next set of lawyers cite in the next set of briefs — exactly the way OpenAI’s Southern District brief cited a Giants pennant nobody asked about, because even a rhetorical flourish is more persuasive once it echoes a decision an appellate court somewhere has already blessed.

The empirical fight underneath the literary one

Strip the color away and the case comes down to competing expert witnesses arguing over web traffic. The Times contends its business has been measurably harmed by falling click-through rates, as AI-generated summaries increasingly stand in for search results that once pointed readers to Times journalism directly. OpenAI, citing its own experts, argues the opposite — that the Times’s traffic has not been “negatively impacted” by ChatGPT at all, and that the substitution the Times describes simply is not showing up in the data.

That fight over numbers is, in the end, more consequential than any of the literary references surrounding it, because market substitution is doing double duty in this case. It is both an independent fair-use factor on its own and the practical proof of the “transformation” question — if AI outputs are functioning as replacements for Times journalism in the market, that is itself strong evidence that whatever transformation occurred wasn’t enough to change what the output is for. Judge Stein’s ruling, whenever it lands, will have to resolve which set of experts he believes, and there is no institutional shortcut around that — no amount of Byron or Warhol substitutes for a finding of fact about click-through rates.

Why the wait now runs into next year

As of this writing, no U.S. court has taken a major AI copyright case all the way to trial, and only a handful have ruled on the central legal questions at all — a fact the case tracker maintained by Santa Clara law professor Ed Lee makes plain just by how short it still is. NYT v. OpenAI and Microsoft was always going to be one of the cases that filled in that record, whichever way Judge Stein rules. What Friday’s filings confirm is that his decision won’t just decide this case — it will hand every other newsroom suing an AI company, and every AI company defending itself, its first real judicial answer to the “transformative or substitutive” question at the center of the entire industry’s copyright exposure. Coming this deep into 2026, with a ruling not expected until the following weeks at the earliest, the honest expectation now is that the industry’s real answer on fair use arrives sometime in 2027 — whether from Stein directly, or from whichever appellate court inherits whatever he decides.

Discovery, meanwhile, keeps doing what discovery always does in a case this size: producing a paper trail neither side entirely wanted. Summary judgment motions are built almost entirely out of that trail — internal emails about training data acquisition, engineering notes about how outputs are generated, the kind of granular internal record that rarely survives contact with a courtroom without embarrassing someone. Settlements exist, in large part, to make that record disappear before a judge or a jury ever gets to read it in open court. The Anthropic book-piracy settlement did exactly that: $1.5 billion made an enormous liability question vanish without ever forcing a public accounting of how the pirated library was assembled or who inside the company signed off on it. NYT v. OpenAI and Microsoft has taken the opposite path. Neither side settled, discovery kept running, and the paper trail is now sitting in a public court file for Judge Stein — and eventually, whichever appellate panel inherits this case — to read in full.

That is the quiet stakes underneath the literary references and the traffic-data experts: whichever way Stein rules, the record he rules on will be the most complete public account yet of how a major AI company actually built its training pipeline, because nobody paid to keep it sealed.
