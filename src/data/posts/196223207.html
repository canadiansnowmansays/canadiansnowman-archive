The Vote That Started Everything

Indiana Senate chamber during/near the redistricting fight

December 11, 2025. The Indiana Senate chamber. Outside, protesters are audible through the walls, chanting vote no and Hoosiers fight fair. Vice President JD Vance is already on X, accusing the Senate President Pro Tempore of dishonesty in real time, before the vote has even been called. The White House has spent weeks lobbying, cajoling, and threatening. Trump himself has weighed in. Vance has visited Indiana twice. The pressure has been, by any measure, extraordinary.

The vote is 31 to 19. Against.

Twenty-one Republicans join all ten Democrats to kill the president’s redistricting demand. The Indiana Senate, where Republicans hold forty of fifty seats, has just told the President of the United States: no.

It was, as NPR noted at the time, the first time Trump’s redistricting campaign had been voted down by members of his own party anywhere in the country. Texas had complied. Missouri had complied. North Carolina had complied. Indiana did not.

The crime had been committed. The sentence would follow.

Last night, Indiana delivered its verdict.

The Results

Five of the seven Trump-backed challengers defeated the Republican incumbents who had voted against redistricting. One incumbent survived clearly. One race — the most watched of the night — ended in a coin flip.

The margins were not close where they weren’t close:

  • District 1: De Vries 75%, Dernulc 23%. A 52-point demolition.

  • District 21: Powell 65%, Buck 35%. Thirty points.

  • District 19: Fiechter 62%, Holdman 38%. Twenty-four points.

  • District 41: Davis 59%, Walker 41%. Eighteen points. Greg Walker — twenty years in the Senate, had already retired, un-retired specifically because he felt this fight demanded he stay — lost by eighteen points.

  • District 11: Schmutzler 59%, Rogers 41%. Eighteen points.

One incumbent survived convincingly: Goode in District 38, 54-36.

And then there was District 23. Spencer Deery — the man who gave the speech, who called mid-decade redistricting a violation of his conservative principles, who said he would resist a federal government that attempts to “bully, direct and control” the states “as long as I have breath” — finished at 50.0 percent to 50.0 percent with more than 95 percent of votes counted.

Half the Republican primary voters in his district agreed with him. Half did not. The machine, aimed directly at the face of the resistance, produced a coin flip.

The AP called the night: incumbents 1, Trump challengers 5.

What Was Actually Being Asked

Before we assess the verdict, the charge deserves restating — because the Loyalty Trials depend on obscuring the transgression behind the punishment.

Trump wanted the Republican supermajority in the Indiana Senate to redraw the state’s congressional map mid-decade — not after a census, not for any reason rooted in Indiana’s interests, but to eliminate the two Democratic House seats held by Frank Mrvan in the 1st district and André Carson in the 7th. The explicit purpose was to help Republicans hold the House in November by engineering two more safe seats before the election.

Partisan gerrymandering is as American as corn. But mid-decade map redrawing, outside the normal census cycle, at the direct request of a sitting president who needs the seats — that is something different. It is the nationalization of state legislative power. It is the White House telling a state senate: your maps are ours to command.

Senator Spencer Deery

Senator Spencer Deery said it plainly on the floor in December, in a speech that deserves to be on the record: “My opposition to mid-cycle gerrymandering is not in contrast to my conservative principles, my opposition is driven by them. As long as I have breath, I will use my voice to resist a federal government that attempts to bully, direct and control this state or any state. Giving the federal government more power is not conservative.”

A Republican state senator, in a Republican supermajority chamber, invoking federalism and states’ rights to resist a Republican president’s demand for federal control of state maps. By any traditional reading of conservatism, Deery was the conservative in the room. The president demanding compliance was not.

This is why the Loyalty Trials must obscure the charge. If you look at what was actually asked, the defendants look reasonable. So the prosecution changes the subject.

The Sentence

Five months after the vote, the sentence arrived — in the form of $8 million worth of advertising in races that have never seen more than a fraction of that spending.

The Club for Growth put in $2 million. Hoosier Leadership for America — a group aligned with Senator Jim Banks, one of Indiana’s two Republican senators and a close Trump ally — spent $4 million on television advertising. Governor Mike Braun, who had supported the redistricting demand, pledged to help primary the holdouts. Turning Point Action promised donations. The full apparatus of the Trump political machine descended on seven state Senate races that, in any ordinary year, would be decided by a few thousand voters watching local cable.

The ads were not subtle. One compared a Republican senator to toilet paper. Soft. Weak. Liberal. Another boosted a challenger by invoking Trump’s name four times in fifteen seconds. A third accused a senator of siding with Democrats against the president’s agenda — which was technically true in the same sense that a man who locks his front door has sided with burglars against the free movement of people.

Senator Greg Walker

Greg Walker’s story captures the human texture of what was happening. Walker had served in the Indiana Senate for twenty years. He had already announced his retirement — done, finished, ready to go home. Then came the redistricting fight. He voted no. And then he un-retired, reversing his decision specifically because he felt the political turmoil demanded he stay and fight. His daughter, living 100 miles away in Lafayette, started seeing TV ads against her father on local television. Walker estimated nearly a million dollars had been spent against him alone.

Last night, Walker lost by eighteen points.

This is what $8 million looks like when it lands on races built for $50,000. It looks like a senator’s daughter watching her father called a liberal on television in a city where he doesn’t even live. It looks like a twenty-year veteran, who came out of retirement to fight, losing to a challenger whose primary qualification was willingness to comply.

The Defence That Wasn’t Enough

The incumbent senators did not roll over. The Indiana Senate Republican caucus’s own campaign arm spent $1.8 million defending them. Fellow senators donated. The incumbents raised money, held events, made their case to voters who had been choosing them, in many cases, for a decade or more.

Their argument was correct: we represent Indiana. You elected us to represent Indiana. When a president — any president — demands that we redraw our maps to serve his congressional strategy, our job is to represent Indiana, not Washington.

Goode made it stick at 54-36. Deery made it to 50-50. The other five did not make it at all.

What separated the survivors from the fallen is not yet fully clear — the geography of each district, the specific challenger, the amount of money deployed, the personal relationships built over years. But the pattern is clear enough. Five of the seven senators who said no to the president of the United States were removed from office by Republican primary voters who said yes.

What the Verdict Means

The Indiana result is not just a story about Indiana. It is a signal broadcast to every Republican state legislator in America who might be considering whether to resist the next redistricting demand, the next executive order, the next instruction from a White House that has demonstrated it will spend $8 million to make an example of a state senator in a race that usually costs $50,000.

The signal is clear: the machine works. Local credibility helps — Deery’s coin flip and Goode’s survival suggest that long relationships and strong conservative records offer some protection. But Walker had twenty years and a conservative record and a compelling story and he lost by eighteen points. The machine overwhelmed all of it.

Lieutenant Governor Micah Beckwith, a redistricting supporter, said before the election that he didn’t want just conservatives — he wanted “conservatives who are willing to fight for conservative values.” What the Indiana verdict revealed is that “fighting for conservative values” has been redefined to mean compliance with presidential demands, regardless of whether those demands are conservative by any traditional measure.

Federalism is conservative. States’ rights are conservative. Resistance to federal overreach is conservative. Deery said all of that, on the floor, in December. Half his district agreed. The other half voted for the president’s preferred candidate.

The Loyalty Trials are not asking whether you are conservative. They are asking whether you are loyal. Indiana has now demonstrated that the distinction matters — and that loyalty, not conservatism, is the prevailing standard.

The Deery Question

Fifty percent to fifty percent deserves its own paragraph.

Spencer Deery gave the best speech of the redistricting fight. He articulated the constitutional and moral and political case against what Trump was demanding more clearly than anyone else in the chamber. He became the face of the resistance. And half his Republican primary electorate sided with him — half saw a conservative doing what conservatives are supposed to do, and voted accordingly.

The other half sided with the machine.

That split is, in its way, more interesting than the lopsided losses elsewhere. It suggests that the Loyalty Trial narrative is not universally accepted even within Republican primary electorates — that there is a constituency, in Indiana, for the older kind of conservatism. Whether that constituency can be built into something larger, in Indiana or elsewhere, is the question the Loyalty Trials series will be tracking.

For now: Deery survived, barely, and the five who lost did not survive at all. The machine is 5-1-1 in Indiana. It will draw its own conclusions.

The Next Courtroom

Trump’s political payback campaign extends beyond Indiana. Later this month, Georgia and Kentucky and Louisiana will hold their own Loyalty Trials — intraparty reckoning for Republicans who have crossed the president on redistricting, election certification, or other tests of compliance.

The Indiana verdict will be in the room for all of them.

The charge in Louisiana has been sitting on the docket for five years. The Callais ruling has given the prosecution new ammunition. The next courtroom opens soon.

The machine that just removed four long-serving Republican state senators in Indiana for the crime of representing their constituents over their president is the same machine that will be applied, with the same resources and the same logic, to every Republican who considers saying no to the next demand.

Spencer Deery still has breath. He used it to win by a coin flip. The four who lost used their breath too. In the end, $8 million was louder.
