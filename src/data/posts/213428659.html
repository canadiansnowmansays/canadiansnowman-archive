This series has spent a considerable portion of its recent instalments — Part IX’s tale of a recalled Missouri councilman, Part XII’s account of teenage activists auditing their own AI use, both tracing a widening public backlash against hyperscale data centres and the tax breaks, water draws, and power demands they bring to the communities hosting them. The through-line across those pieces has been political: local governments increasingly caught between the promised jobs and revenue of a data centre and the organised, sometimes bipartisan, opposition of residents who have to live next to one. It is worth pausing on a solution to that entire category of problem that has nothing to do with politics and everything to do with orbital mechanics: if the objection is to data centres on the ground, the answer, on this reasoning, is to put the data centre somewhere there is no ground at all.

The venture itself

SpaceX and Nvidia have announced a joint effort to develop a space-based version of Nvidia’s Vera Rubin compute architecture, deployed on satellites rather than in terrestrial buildings. SpaceX calls the satellite Starmind; the two companies introduced the Starmind AI1 compute payload on August 4, 2026. In late August Musk set the timetable: “SpaceX, in partnership with Nvidia, has designed a space-optimized Vera Rubin NVL72 system for launch to orbit in Q4 next year, with significant scale in 2028.” The described specifications are substantial for an orbital platform. As Simon Sharwood reported for The Register on August 25, 2026, each satellite would carry a full NVL72 rack (72 Rubin GPUs and 36 Vera CPUs) weighing roughly 1,800 kilograms and drawing 120 kilowatts, fed by a 210-kilowatt solar array and cooled by a liquid loop dumping heat through radiators. It is essentially a flying data centre, solar-powered, with no cooling water, no land acquisition, and, crucially for the politics this series has spent four instalments documenting, no local government to negotiate a tax abatement with and no neighbours to organise a recall campaign against. Nor is the ambition limited to a handful of satellites. Aamir Khollam reported for Interesting Engineering on August 4, 2026 that SpaceX has asked the Federal Communications Commission for permission to deploy as many as one million orbital data-centre satellites, at altitudes between 311 and 1,243 miles

Why “in orbit” solves the specific complaints on this ledger’s list

Read against Part IX and Part XII specifically, the orbital pitch answers almost every terrestrial objection this series has catalogued, point for point. Water consumption, the recurring complaint in drought-affected data-centre host communities, becomes irrelevant when cooling occurs via radiative heat rejection into the vacuum of space rather than evaporative cooling drawing on a local aquifer. Grid strain, the complaint at the centre of Kyle McGuffey’s Colorado Springs public-records discovery in Part XII, disappears when the facility generates its own power from solar arrays rather than drawing from a regional utility already straining to serve existing customers. Local tax-abatement politics, the mechanism this series traced through Loudoun County’s success and Independence, Missouri’s recall in Part IX, becomes moot when there is no municipality with jurisdiction over a facility in geostationary or low Earth orbit. It is, in a genuinely elegant if slightly unsettling way, a solution engineered specifically around the shape of the political backlash rather than around the underlying technical problem of where to put enough compute — which raises the obvious question of whether the backlash, rather than raw technical necessity, is actually the primary driver of the investment case.

The financing angle this series exists to track

This series’ actual mandate, as distinct from Asimov Gap’s governance focus, is the financial architecture underneath these announcements, and Starmind’s financing sits at an unusually direct intersection with Starbase Louisiana, examined in a companion piece in the To the Moon, Alice series. Launching satellites of this described mass and complexity at the volume this venture would require is precisely the “own cargo” Musk cited as the actual justification for building a spaceport capable of thirty-plus flights a day — not crewed missions, not conventional commercial satellite deployment, but the sustained launch cadence needed to loft an orbital compute constellation at genuine data-centre scale. SpaceX’s own description of the site leaves little doubt about the connection. NASASpaceFlight.com reported on August 25, 2026 that the 125,000-acre site at Pecan Island, in Vermilion Parish, is to have ten Starship pads, with construction beginning in 2027 and a first launch targeted for 2029, and that among its stated purposes is the Starmind system of “up to one million orbital data centers” in sun-synchronous orbit. The cadence target, at least 30 launches a day by 2030 across SpaceX’s Texas, Florida and Louisiana sites, is a number built around that cargo. That means Starbase Louisiana’s $100 billion price tag and Starmind’s still-unpriced orbital compute buildout are not two separate capital commitments to evaluate independently; they are the same bet, split across two press releases and, not coincidentally, two different corporate partners’ balance sheets. Nvidia’s involvement here also deserves scrutiny through the same lens this series applied in Part V to Nvidia’s $105 billion Piketon, Ohio guarantee and in Part VI to Oracle’s circular financing arrangements: a chip supplier co-developing the compute architecture for a customer’s orbital deployment, where that customer’s ability to actually deploy the hardware depends on a still-unproven Starship launch cadence, is a financing structure with the same basic shape this series has flagged repeatedly — vendor and customer each underwriting the other’s most speculative bet, in a way that makes either company’s standalone financial statements harder to read in isolation.

The Starlink Direct-to-Cell layer

Running in parallel, and drawing on the same Starship launch capacity, is SpaceX’s continued build-out of Starlink’s direct-to-cell service, following the company’s roughly $19.5 billion acquisition of EchoStar spectrum covering 65 megahertz of nationwide bandwidth — spectrum that allows satellites to communicate directly with ordinary mobile phones already in consumers’ pockets, without a dedicated satellite handset. This is a commercially nearer-term and more conventional business than orbital AI compute, but it competes for the same scarce resource: launch slots on a rocket that, as this series’ sister project has documented across ten instalments, has not yet demonstrated the reliable, high-cadence reusability its entire business case depends on. Every satellite launched to expand Direct-to-Cell coverage is a launch not available that week for a Star Mind compute satellite, and vice versa — a capacity allocation problem that Starbase Louisiana’s additional pads are explicitly designed to solve, assuming the site is actually built on anything resembling its stated 2029 timeline.

The depreciation question nobody is asking yet

There is a narrower, more technical financial question buried in this venture that this series flagged in a different context back in Part III, when examining Michael Burry’s argument that Nvidia and OpenAI’s circular financing arrangement understated real costs by roughly $176 billion through overly generous depreciation schedules on data-centre hardware. Terrestrial data-centre equipment — the racks, the chips, the cooling infrastructure — has an established, if contested, depreciation convention built up over decades of accounting practice and IRS guidance. Orbital compute hardware has no comparable convention, because nothing at this scale has ever been depreciated as an asset before. A satellite exposed to continuous radiation, micrometeorite risk, and thermal cycling in a way no terrestrial server rack ever experiences presumably has a shorter and more uncertain useful life than a data-centre building’s decades-long depreciation schedule assumes — and if a company chooses to depreciate an orbital compute satellite on a terrestrial-style schedule anyway, because no orbital-specific accounting convention yet exists to force otherwise, the same kind of understated true cost this series flagged in Part III could recur here, on hardware even less suited to a long depreciation window than the ground-based servers Burry was originally critiquing. Nobody currently on the record has raised this specific question about Star Mind. That silence is not evidence the question doesn’t matter; it is more likely evidence that the accounting conventions for orbital compute simply have not been worked out yet by anyone, on either side of the ledger, which is precisely the kind of gap this series exists to flag before the invoices start arriving.

The dot-com parallel, applied precisely

Readers of Part VI, this series’ examination of Larry Ellison and Oracle’s roughly 500 percent debt-to-equity position, will recognise the shape of what is happening here, because it rhymes closely with that instalment’s core argument: infrastructure being built at a scale and cost that only makes sense if a specific, not-yet-proven demand materialises on roughly the timeline projected. The dot-com era’s fibre-optic buildout is the standard comparison for this kind of overbuilt-infrastructure story, and it is usually invoked to make the optimistic case — the fibre eventually got used, the argument goes, even if the companies that built it went bankrupt first. That comparison is worth taking seriously here specifically because Starship’s launch cadence, Star Mind’s satellite deployment, and Starlink’s Direct-to-Cell expansion are all, structurally, the same kind of infrastructure overbuild: capacity constructed years ahead of confirmed demand, financed on the assumption that the demand will eventually catch up and make the early capital look prescient rather than wasted. The dot-com parallel does not tell you whether this bet will pay off. It tells you, with some historical confidence, that a meaningful share of the companies making an equivalent bet at the time did not survive to collect on it — and that the infrastructure itself, however useful it eventually proved to someone, is not the same thing as the specific balance sheet that financed it surviving intact.

The claim that came true, and what it is made of

The single figure in this story that most invited scepticism was Musk’s statement to SpaceX employees that AI-related revenue would exceed everything else the company does combined — larger than Starlink’s consumer and enterprise business, larger than launch services, larger than government and defence contracts. He did not say eventually. As Chris Lange reported for 24/7 Wall St. on October 3, 2026, Musk told staff in a 30-minute all-hands video posted on X on August 11, 2026: “Probably our AI revenue, not probably, definitely, our AI revenue will exceed all other SpaceX revenue probably in September, like next month.” The second-quarter numbers made that a steep climb: $2.56 billion of AI revenue against $5.25 billion from the connectivity and space businesses combined. Goldman Sachs had projected only $15.6 billion of SpaceX AI revenue for the whole of 2026.

He was right. SpaceX reported its third quarter on October 20, and the AI segment brought in about $7.6 billion, roughly 52 per cent of $14.58 billion in revenue, against about $5.3 billion from connectivity and $1.8 billion from space, investingLive reported that evening. Revenue overall rose 161 per cent, and net profit was $4.4 billion against the $1.57 billion analysts expected, Reuters reported. Quarterly figures cannot show whether the crossover happened in September itself, but over the quarter it happened, and this ledger had treated it as unlikely. That should be recorded plainly.

What the number is made of matters more than the number. None of that AI revenue comes from orbit. Reuters reported that SpaceX earns it “mainly by renting out data-center capacity to developers such as Anthropic, Google and Reflection AI” — data centres on the ground, the very category of building this essay began by describing as the thing Starmind is meant to escape. The same report noted that the AI unit has run large operating losses and “will need sustained investment before it can consistently generate profits.” The fastest-growing line in SpaceX’s accounts is a terrestrial rental business, and it is losing money.

That leaves the orbital half of the story where it was. There is still no public unit economics for orbital AI compute at anything resembling commercial scale — no cost per kilowatt delivered in orbit, no confirmed customer contracts, no comparison against an equivalent terrestrial data centre net of the political friction this series has spent four instalments cataloguing. Musk’s revenue call has earned him a better hearing on the next one. It has not supplied any of those numbers. Until it does, Starmind belongs filed where it was: possibly true, currently unaccountable, and worth exactly as much scepticism as any other number nobody outside the company can check.
