There is a particular kind of civic optimism that every small town knows. It arrives in the form of a great public meeting at which important men announce that great things are coming, and that the details will follow. The important thing is to think big. Stephen Leacock gave Mariposa, the little Ontario town of his Sunshine Sketches, a “whirlwind campaign” of exactly this kind: a drive to clear the church’s debt that generated splendid meetings, tremendous enthusiasm and almost no money.

On September 30, 2026, at Marine Corps Base Quantico, the whirlwind campaign came to Washington. The man at the front of the hall was the Secretary of Defense, and the audience was every governor in the United States.

The Performance

“We’re talking hundreds of thousands of acres with organic power generation, modern air defenses and cutting-edge technology,” Pete Hegseth told the room. “Think a fortress.”

He then explained how the fortress would be sited. There would be a competition. All fifty governors would be invited to bid, offering state land, hundreds of thousands of acres of it, to host what the Pentagon has named, with commendable modesty, “The Next Great American Base.” It would house between 15,000 and 25,000 troops, according to the Pentagon’s own summary of the six initiatives, reported by Susan Carpenter for Spectrum News. As the New York Times’s Helene Cooper and John Ismay reported on September 30, 2026, in “’No Beardos, No Weirdos, No Wimps’: Hegseth Lays Out His Military Vision,” tens of thousands of those troops would be brought home from overseas. Which overseas was not specified.

As Fox News reported in “Trump Pentagon launches competition for America’s next great military base,” it would be the first such installation built in some forty years. It would have off-grid power and modern air defences, high-end training facilities, healthcare and childcare. In keeping with a Trump executive order, it would have classical architecture. Governors would be judged on the quality and quantity of their land, the quality of local housing and schools, the level of community support, and their willingness to clear regulatory hurdles. The Pentagon promised to review the pitches publicly, to keep out what it called political favouritism.

“We are going to build them beautiful new bases from the ground up,” Hegseth said. Troops deserve “the gold standard.”

A fortress, then, with Doric columns. One pictures the governors already sketching.

The Paperwork

What was announced alongside it. The fortress has a sibling. The Secretary also launched an initiative called FORTRESS America, short for Federal Operations for Resilience, Troop Resources, Energy, Supply, and Survivability. Under it, every major military installation in the country will generate its own power, independent of the civilian grid. “If an adversary takes down the grid by cyberattack, by sabotage, or by any means, our bases stay lit,” Hegseth said. The plan also promises to bring home the supply chains for rare earths, specialty metals, semiconductors and propellants, and to build reserves sufficient to sustain operations through disruption.

These are not silly goals. Grid vulnerability is a real problem, and the Pentagon has worried for years about Chinese control of rare-earth processing. The difficulty is not the ambition. It is that the reports available on the day of the announcement contained no timelines, no specifications, no budget, no locations and no numbers beyond the troop count. An initiative with an acronym that long and a budget that short is a slogan with a filing number.

What the department can afford. Part II of this series set out the money. Congress passed a continuing resolution that funds the Pentagon at roughly last year’s level through December 11, 2026, about 44 percent below the $1.5 trillion the administration requested. Programmes cannot start under a stopgap. Military construction, the account that would pay for a new base, is among the slowest-moving in the whole budget even in good years. Projects are planned years ahead, approved line by line by Congress, and routinely delayed by environmental review and land acquisition.

The New York Times’s Greg Jaffe, in his September 30, 2026 analysis “Hegseth Talks Tough as Republican Doubts About His Leadership Grow,” reported that the budget gap will force the department to move money already set aside for barracks repairs into operations in the Middle East. The Government Accountability Office reported in August 2026 that the military’s maintenance backlog stands at about $285 billion.

The order of operations is worth spelling out. The department cannot fund the repair of the barracks it has. It has announced a competition to build a new base the size of a small city, which no one has designed, priced or put in a budget request, in order to house troops whose current stations it will not name.

What a base costs. No cost estimate has been published, so this essay will not invent one. It is enough to note the scale. A post housing 25,000 troops, with their families, schools, hospital, airfield and “organic power generation,” is roughly comparable in population to a mid-sized city. The Pentagon’s own long and painful history of building, closing and consolidating bases through the Base Realignment and Closure rounds, the last in 2005, suggests that such projects take a decade or more and cost billions before the first soldier unpacks.

What the columns mean. The classical architecture requirement deserves a moment of its own. In August 2025, Trump signed an executive order titled “Making Federal Architecture Beautiful Again,” directing that federal buildings favour classical and traditional styles. Applied to a military base, it means that somewhere in the fortress, between the air defences and the off-grid power plant, there will be pediments. The order may well produce handsome buildings. It will certainly not produce cheap ones, and it adds a design requirement to a project that does not yet have a design, a site or a budget.

What the last big rounds cost. The Base Realignment and Closure process offers the most useful guide to the price of rearranging the military’s footprint. The 2005 round, the most recent, was sold to Congress on the promise of savings. Its implementation costs rose from initial estimates of about $21 billion to roughly $35 billion, according to the Government Accountability Office, and the promised savings took far longer than expected to arrive. That round mostly moved units between existing bases. Building a new one from bare ground, for 25,000 troops and their families, is a different order of undertaking.

An imagined round of bids. With criteria no more specific than land, schools, community support and “regulatory cooperation,” a governor offering half a million waterless acres and a promise to suspend every state environmental law would, on paper, be competitive.

What a bidding war looks like. There is precedent for the competition, though not a military one. In 2017, Amazon invited North American cities to bid for its second headquarters and received 238 proposals. Cities offered tax breaks, land and, in some cases, embarrassing quantities of enthusiasm. After more than a year of public auditions, the company chose two sites, one of which it then abandoned after local opposition. Most of the bidders received nothing but a lesson in how much they had been willing to give away.

A state bidding for a military base would be offering public land and public money to the federal government. That is what Hegseth means by “regulatory cooperation and reduced bureaucratic hurdles”: governors who will waive their own environmental and planning rules. The competition is framed as merit-based. It is also a mechanism by which the Pentagon can let fifty governors outbid each other, in public, a few weeks before a national election, for a project that may never be funded.

What the fortress means for allies. The troops for the new base are to come home from overseas. Part IV of this series traced what the department’s earlier decisions on Romania and Poland did to the eastern flank. The new base raises the same question at greater scale. Which garrisons empty out to fill it? Germany, where Trump has already ordered 5,000 troops out? Japan? Korea? The Secretary did not say, and the allies have learned from experience that they may find out from a social media post.

The Bill

The governors. A competition announced on September 30 for a base without a budget is, at minimum, an invitation for every governor with acreage and an election to promise their voters something the Pentagon may never deliver. Some will spend real money on bid preparation. Some will commit land. One, eventually, may win a groundbreaking ceremony. If history is a guide, the others will win a press release.

The barracks. The soldiers who most need “the gold standard” in living conditions live now, in barracks the GAO described as chronically neglected. Every dollar and every planning hour spent on a fortress in an unnamed state is not spent on the mould, the broken air conditioning and the failing plumbing in the buildings they already live in. The young E-6s and O-3s at Quantico applauded the promise of beautiful new bases. They will go home to the old ones.

The taxpayer. A fortress for 25,000 troops will be one of the largest military construction projects in a generation. Announced first and costed later, it reverses the normal sequence of public spending, in which the bill comes before the ribbon. Congress, which Part II showed already struggles to get a seven-page answer from this Secretary on a $67 billion war request, will now be asked to finance a city.

The allies. Bringing tens of thousands of troops home is a legitimate policy choice, and one with real arguments behind it. Making that choice by announcement, without naming the countries affected, is the costly part. The allies who host American troops pay for that uncertainty in their own budgets and their own politics. Some, like Poland, would rather pay to keep Americans than watch them leave.

The calendar. The timing is also worth noting. The competition was announced on September 30, five weeks before the midterm elections, and invites governors, many of them on the ballot or campaigning for candidates who are, to compete publicly for thousands of federal jobs and billions in federal spending. No timeline has been published, and a site is unlikely to be chosen before November 3. But every governor who bids will be able to tell voters they are fighting for the base. A competition that cannot be won before the election, but can be campaigned on during it, is a peculiar kind of defence policy.

The idea itself. Grid resilience, domestic supply chains and better housing for troops are worthwhile goals. They deserve serious plans: costed, sequenced, briefed to Congress and funded over years. Instead they have become applause lines in a speech that also contained a slur list and an accusation of treason. When a good idea is introduced that way, it does not get debated on its merits. It gets filed with the speech.

Mariposa’s whirlwind campaign, it should be remembered, never did raise the money. It produced magnificent meetings, a great deal of excitement, and a story the town told about itself for years afterwards. Leacock was fond of the place. He simply couldn’t help noticing that enthusiasm and arithmetic are different things, and that the bill always arrives after the speeches.
