There are, by my rough count, more essays about climate change than there are functioning thermostats in the European Union, and after this week the latter number is falling fast. So I owe you an explanation before I add to the pile.

I am not a climate scientist. I am a retired businessman in a Toronto townhouse with a husky who has spent the last several summers lying flat on the kitchen tile like a bearskin rug that gave up. I have no models, no satellites, and no standing to tell you what the global mean temperature will be in 2070. What I have is the same thing I have always had in these pages: a Canadian vantage point, a long memory, and a constitutional inability to watch powerful people say absurd things without writing them down.

So this series is not going to be another recitation of parts per million. There is a genre of climate writing — earnest, footnoted, faintly funereal — that has been running continuously since roughly 1988 and has changed approximately zero minds. I have read a great deal of it. I do not propose to imitate it. The numbers are not in dispute among anyone who can read a thermometer, and I am not going to spend fifteen essays relitigating whether the planet is warming any more than I would spend fifteen essays relitigating whether the planet is round.

What interests me instead is the gap. The gap between what we built and what is coming.

Consider the houses. As the New York Times reported this week, during a heat wave that gave France its hottest day in recorded history, a great many British homes are nearly impossible to cool — because they were deliberately built to retain heat. Brick, small windows, thick walls, the accumulated architectural wisdom of an island that for four centuries understood cold as the enemy and warmth as the scarce and precious thing. Those houses are not wrong. They are simply built for a country that no longer exists. The climate they were designed for has quietly left, and the bricks did not get the memo.

A British home during a heat wave. Thick brick walls and small windows — sensible for centuries of damp cold — now turn interiors into ovens

That is the whole series, really, compressed into one image. We are a civilization living inside infrastructure — physical, legal, political, psychological — that was built for a planet we no longer have. The houses built to hold heat. The insurance markets built on the assumption that last century’s disasters predict next century’s. The electrical grids, the water treaties, the crop calendars, the building codes, the entire apparatus of normal life, all of it calibrated to a climate that is sliding out from under us a degree at a time. And the most interesting question is not whether this is happening. It is what happens to a house — and a country, and a politics — when the world it was built for goes away, and the people inside refuse to renovate.

I want to be precise about why this framing, and not another, because the choice of frame is the whole argument. There are, broadly, two ways the climate conversation usually gets conducted, and I find both of them useless. The first is the apocalyptic mode: the planet is doomed, the tipping points are upon us, the only honest response is despair or revolution. The second is the techno-optimist mode: someone clever will invent a machine, the market will sort it out, the genius of human ingenuity will save us as it always has. These two modes appear to be opposites, locked in eternal combat across the comment sections. They are in fact identical twins, because both of them excuse the reader from doing anything. The doomer says nothing can be done; the optimist says nothing need be done by you; and both of them let you close the browser tab with your conscience intact and your behaviour unchanged. The frame I am after is the duller, harder, middle one: a great deal can be done, much of it is not being done, and the reasons it is not being done are specific, nameable, and worth examining one at a time. That is the work. Not prophecy, not faith — accounting.

Here is where the Canadian vantage earns its keep, and why I think I have something to add that the thousandth American climate columnist does not.

Thawing permafrost in the Canadian North. What was once stable frozen ground is collapsing into thermokarst lakes, releasing ancient carbon and destabilizing the infrastructure built on top of it.

Canada is not a spectator to this. We are warming at roughly twice the global rate, and our North is warming faster still. We sit on top of the Arctic, which is the part of the planet coming apart most visibly — the permafrost that is supposed to stay frozen and increasingly does not, the boreal forest that is supposed to absorb carbon and increasingly burns it back into the sky. When the smoke from those fires drifts south and turns Manhattan the color of a nicotine stain, that is, for once, our emergency arriving on your doorstep — a reversal of the usual direction of these things that I intend to dwell on, because it is clarifying.

And yet Canada is also a petrostate. We are the Arctic’s victim and one of the world’s great exporters of the stuff that is melting it, a country that produces stirring speeches about climate leadership and then ships the oil sands abroad with the other hand. If I am going to ask hard questions of others in this series — and I am — I will start at home. There is no version of this argument in which Canadians get to stand on a hill and tut. We are in the house too. Some of us are selling the furnace.

This double position — victim and culprit, downwind and upwind, the country breathing in everyone else’s exhaust while exhaling a great deal of its own — is, I have come to think, the most honest place from which to write about climate at all. It forecloses the two cheap moves that ruin most climate commentary. The first cheap move is the posture of pure victimhood: they are doing this to us, the emissions come from over there, our hands are clean. The second is the posture of pure guilt: it is all our fault, the only honest response is penance. Canada’s actual situation refuses both. We are neither innocent nor uniquely culpable. We are implicated — in the precise sense that we are both harmed by the thing and party to it — and “implicated” turns out to be the correct moral stance for nearly everyone on this question, which is exactly why nearly everyone resists it.

So that is the plan. Over the coming essays I want to walk through the systems one at a time and watch each of them meet the new climate. I will begin with the heat itself and the homes that cannot shed it, then explain — because a reader is owed the mechanism and not just the misery — why the heat settles where it does, why western Europe and France in particular keep drawing the worst of it. Then north, to the faster-warming continent on our doorstep, and to the smoke that crosses borders that smoke does not recognize. Then the accountability, which is the heart of the thing: who profits from the delay, the framework I keep returning to that I have called TACO, the quiet death of my own country’s carbon tax, and a long hard look at the most powerful man on the continent and his insistence that the way forward is backward, into the coal mine. Then adaptation: air conditioning as politics, the insurance markets quietly pricing in what the politicians will not say, the water wars, with my own great-uncle Hugh Keenleyside and his river treaties making an appearance, as he tends to. And then the politics of who pays, which is where every climate argument eventually goes to die.

Ancient Roman domus with central fountain and shaded courtyard. For millennia, southern Europeans knew how to live with heat; postwar modern building largely abandoned that wisdom.

I should say plainly, one more time, what this series is not, because the temptation to slide into one of the two useless modes is constant and I will need you to hold me to it. It is not a counsel of despair. It is also not a brochure for any particular technology or party or saviour. It is an attempt to look, steadily and without flinching, at a set of arrangements that were built — sensibly, by intelligent people, for the world as it was — and that are now failing because the world has changed underneath them. The Romans, it turns out, knew how to keep a stone house cool in a Mediterranean summer; we will get to them, near the end, because some of the future is a return rather than an invention. The point throughout is not that we are doomed, and not that we are saved, but that we are living in a house built for winter, in a summer that is arriving early and staying late, and that the adult response is neither to weep nor to wait for a miracle but to understand the house, name what is failing, and begin — at last, and at home — to build its replacement.

A husky named Dior will appear from time to time, suffering theatrically. So will a closing section I am calling Degrees, in which I will set down one number per essay and let it sit there, the way a number sometimes says more than a paragraph.

Here is the first.

Degrees

44.3 — the temperature in Celsius recorded Tuesday in Pissos, in southwestern France, during the heat wave that opens this series. About 112 on the Fahrenheit scale my American readers prefer. The town of Pissos has roughly 1,500 residents and, until this week, no particular reason to be in the newspaper.
