There are two national songs in America.

Most people think there is one. They are wrong, and the confusion is itself instructive.

The first is the official anthem — “The Star-Spangled Banner,” written in 1814 after a minor battle in a minor war, notoriously difficult to sing, built around a single question: is the flag still there? It is a song about a symbol asking whether the symbol has survived. It does not ask what the symbol means. It does not ask whether the republic that flies it has earned another day. It asks only: can you see it? Is it still up?

The second song is not the official anthem, but it has always done the work the official anthem cannot. "The Battle Hymn of the Republic" does not ask a question. It testifies. Mine eyes have seen the glory. It proclaims. It exhorts. It insists that the nation is engaged in something larger than itself, answerable to something beyond the flag. It was written in a single night at the Willard Hotel in November 1861 by Julia Ward Howe, who received either four or five dollars for it and no byline. It became the closest thing America has to a sacred text — sung at the funerals of presidents, at the marches of the civil rights movement, at the moment Martin Luther King Jr. gave the last speech of his life, as The Atlantic has documented.

The Atlantic, in its July 2026 issue marking America’s 250th birthday, publishes both songs on its cover simultaneously. The juxtaposition is not accidental. One song asks a question. One song provides an answer. The question is whether, at 250, America can still provide the answer.

I am not American. I have never been American. I am a 74-year-old retired Canadian businessman and journalist, born in London, Ontario — a city Stephen Leacock, who is my lodestar and my model, once called the Mariposa on the Thames. I have lived my entire adult life in Toronto, across the longest undefended border in the world, close enough to watch America in real time and far enough away to see its shape.

I am not the first Canadian to notice this. David Frum, watching from the loyalist stronghold of Prince Edward County, Ontario — where the main street is literally called Loyalist Parkway — has spent Fourth of July weekends among the descendants of the Americans who bet against the republic in 1776 and lost. There is, he admits, a certain quiet satisfaction available to a Canadian standing on that road on America's birthday: the mutant Jack still flying, a two-hundred-and-fifty-year-old we told you so hanging in the air. I feel a version of that satisfaction too, and I want to be honest about it rather than pretend the Canadian vantage point is purely clinical. But the satisfaction fades quickly, because the thing being watched matters to more than the people watching it. It took the Loyalists a while to be proven right about anything. I am not sure they have been proven right yet.

I have been watching for a long time.

I watched in 1967, as a teenager on a school band tour through the American South, and felt for the first time the particular texture of a country that believed in its own exceptionalism with a fervour that made me simultaneously admiring and uneasy. I watched through the Nixon years, the Reagan years, the long strange carnival of the Clinton impeachment, the catastrophe of Iraq, the improbable election of Barack Obama and the even more improbable elections that followed. I watched, from across the border, as a country that had always managed — barely, bloodily, incompletely — to extend its founding promise to the next excluded group began, somewhere in the second decade of this century, to run that film in reverse.

I began writing about it seriously in May 2025, when I launched a Substack under the name CanadianSnowmanSays on Substack. In the fourteen months since, I have published over 1,215 essays — a number I find meaningful only because, 1,215 is the year of Magna Carta, 1,300 is the High Middle Ages at full flower, and 1,492 is Columbus, looming on the horizon. I am, in other words, living in a medieval essay count, waiting for the New World. My husky Dior, who has heard this observation before, remains unimpressed. In any case: north of a thousand essays on what is happening to the republic next door — on Trump accountability, AI governance, the Iran war, the gerrymandering wars, the slow dismantling of the institutions my neighbour spent two centuries building. I have done this because I believe, with the particular urgency that comes from proximity without citizenship, that what is happening in America is not a domestic matter. It never was. A nation that exports its idea of itself to the world — that defines itself by an idea rather than an ancestry — does not get to contain its crisis at the border.

This series is about that idea. Specifically, it is about what happens to a nation built on an idea when it can no longer agree on what the idea is.

The political philosopher’s term for what America has always been is a creedal nation. Not an ethnic nation — not a country built around shared blood, shared religion, shared ancestry. A creedal nation: one that defines membership by subscription to a set of propositions. Life, liberty, the pursuit of happiness. All men created equal. Government of the people, by the people, for the people. You did not have to be born here. You had to believe here. That was the bargain, and it was genuinely radical. One of my grandfathers was born to Jewish immigrants from what is now Belarus. The other came from Greek immigrants from a village above Sparta. In almost any other country in the world, the combination would have been irrelevant to civic life — they would have been neither fish nor fowl, neither fully belonging nor fully excluded. In America, the creed said: subscribe, and you belong.

It was, of course, a promise made in bad faith to half the population from the beginning. The creed said all men created equal in the same document that counted enslaved people as three-fifths of a person for purposes of congressional apportionment. The franchise that the young republic extended to white men at a rate unprecedented in the world excluded women, Black Americans, and the Indigenous peoples on whose land the experiment was being conducted. The history of America is substantially the history of people demanding that the creed mean what it says — and the resistance those demands have always generated.

But here is what The Atlantic identifies as the central vulnerability of the creedal model: a nation defined by blood and soil can survive a fractured narrative. Two ethnic nationalists can disagree violently about history and still share a country, because the country does not depend on the story. A creedal nation cannot do this. A nation bound together by a shared understanding of its own story falls apart when the story falls apart. The creed requires a narrative. The narrative requires agreement. And agreement, in America in 2026, is precisely what has become impossible.

This is not a new observation. But it has a new urgency. Because the story is not merely contested now — it is being abandoned. The hours devoted to social studies in American elementary schools have shrunk by a third. Survey courses in American history are vanishing from university campuses. The political left has, in significant parts of the academy, decided that the national narrative is irredeemably tainted and should be decentred rather than reformed. The political right has decided that the national narrative should be protected from complexity — that reverence and love are the appropriate responses to history, and that reckoning is a form of subversion. Between the post-Americans and the hyper-Americans, the mainstream school — the tradition that holds America as a work in progress, flawed and still becoming — has been left without institutional defenders.

Into that vacuum have stepped the white identitarians, as The Atlantic has noted, who have decided that “particular people” is the correct answer to the creedal nation’s oldest question. And the semiquincentennial celebrations — UFC on the South Lawn, a Formula 1 race through Washington, a statue garden featuring Davy Crockett, Julia Child, and Kobe Bryant — suggest that no one in a position of power has any idea what the 250th birthday is supposed to be celebrating, beyond the celebrity and spectacle that have become the default American idiom for occasions requiring meaning.

I want to be precise about what this series is and is not.

It is not a prosecution of America. I have spent a significant portion of my adult life studying this country, and what I feel toward it is closer to the complicated love one has for a difficult sibling — someone you know too well to idealize, too well to dismiss, and too well to stop watching. I have seen enough of America to know that its ideals, when it reaches for them, are genuinely transformative. I have seen what it has meant to the world to have an America capable of reaching for them. I am not indifferent to whether it can continue.

It is not a Canadian superiority argument either. Canada has its own unfinished business with its foundational story — its own residential schools, its own exclusions, its own convenient national myths. The Canadian outsider perspective I bring to this series is not a vantage point of moral superiority. It is simply a vantage point of outside — and outside, as Stephen Leacock always understood, is sometimes where the shape of things becomes clearest.

What this series is, is an attempt to examine seriously — in ten parts, over the weeks around America’s 250th birthday — how the American story came apart, who pulled at which threads, what remains, and whether anything can be rewoven. It draws heavily on the remarkable cluster of essays published in The Atlantic published this week: Appelbaum on the creedal nation’s vulnerability, Jake Lundberg on the two national songs, Gal Beckerman on the two kinds of patriotism, Thomas Chatterton Williams on the white identitarians, and the editorial note from Jeffrey Goldberg that frames them all. It draws on Jill Lepore, These Truths on Johann Neem’s taxonomy of the three competing schools, on Nikole Hannah-Jones’s unexpected path to patriotism through the people who chose to stay.

It also draws on a companion series published here on CanadianSnowmanSays: The American Contradiction: How Anti-”Woke” Politics Betrays the Nation’s Foundational Promise— ten parts examining the economic and civic cost of exclusionary politics across 250 years of American history. Where that series asked what exclusion costs, this one asks the deeper question underneath: what happens to the story itself when the argument about who belongs becomes irresolvable?

And it draws on fifty-some years of watching from across the border — watching the flag still flying, still asking whether it means what it says it means.

O say, can you see?

That is still, at 250, the right question. The anthem had it right, even if it didn’t know how right it was.

The Battle Hymn had the answer: Mine eyes have seen the glory.

The question for this series — and, I would argue, for America — is whether those eyes can still find it.
