On January 21, 2025 — the first full day of the second Trump administration — Larry Ellison stood beside the president in the Roosevelt Room while Trump called him “sort of C.E.O. of everything” and announced Project Stargate, a $500 billion, four-year plan to build data centers producing 10 gigawatts of computing power, enough to run roughly 10 million homes. Ellison, 80 years old and worth some $200 billion at the time, had come to Washington in his own Gulfstream, without a driver’s license, needing a staffer to vouch for him at the gate. Nineteen months later, he is worth tens of billions less, Oracle’s credit rating sits one notch above junk, and Wall Street analysts have started using his company as the barometer for whether the entire AI buildout is a sustainable transformation or the most expensive bubble in American history.

As Jonathan Mahler, Jim Rutenberg and Kirsten Grind reported for The New York Times in a piece published July 31 and updated August 5, 2026, Ellison’s path from that Roosevelt Room announcement to his current position runs through nearly every thread this series has been tracking separately — debt, circular financing, geopolitical entanglement, and a bet that capital expenditure alone could outrun the question of whether any of it would actually turn a profit.

The Scaling Hypothesis, Financed on Credit

Oracle’s transformation began, like nearly everyone else’s in this industry, with ChatGPT’s launch in November 2022 — a moment that turned Ellison, a database and cloud company executive who had stepped back from day-to-day control years earlier, into a man racing to avoid being left behind by the last technological shift of his career. The theory driving the entire buildout, the scaling hypothesis, held that AI capability scales directly with computing power — meaning whoever controlled the most capacity would eventually control the AI economy, regardless of whether the underlying business model was proven yet. That theory required Oracle to build data centers at a pace and scale its balance sheet could not support on its own, and Ellison chose debt, in nearly every available form — bonds, letters of credit, asset-backed securities — to close the gap.

The resulting numbers are genuinely startling relative to Oracle’s hyperscaler peers. The company’s debt-to-equity ratio reached roughly 500 percent — five dollars of debt for every dollar of shareholder equity — against Amazon’s roughly 50 percent and an even lower figure at Alphabet. Two Morgan Stanley credit analysts estimated Oracle’s debt and lease obligations could triple within three years, prompting a Financial Times headline blunt enough to function as its own analysis: the bank was telling clients to short the stock. Oracle kept borrowing anyway — $25 billion in bonds issued in a single February day, a bank credit line expanded to $10 billion, total debt climbing past $130 billion by the company’s own June disclosures. In March, Oracle was forced to scale back its own Texas Stargate site after banks began limiting their exposure specifically because Oracle was the tenant — a lender’s vote of no confidence delivered through the driest possible mechanism, a reduced credit commitment. That same month, the company laid off roughly 18 percent of its workforce without explanation, the traditional signature of a company drowning in debt and short on cash. By late July, Oracle’s stock was down 60 percent from its September peak, its credit default swaps — the market’s price for insuring against the company’s default — sat at record highs, and S&P had downgraded its rating to one step above what Wall Street calls a “fallen angel.”

A Timing Problem With No Easy Fix

Part of Oracle’s exposure, per the Times’ reporting, is simply a matter of when the money arrives relative to when it goes out. The company’s capital expenditures have been running years ahead of its revenue — it isn’t scheduled to begin collecting the tens of billions of dollars a year OpenAI owes it under the original Stargate deal until 2027, a gap this series has already flagged in Oracle’s counterparties: OpenAI itself doesn’t expect to reach profitability until 2030, is on the hook for hundreds of billions in commitments to other AI companies and chipmakers, and is burning cash at a pace that makes Oracle’s own bet on OpenAI’s eventual solvency look less like a calculated wager and more like a hope stapled to a spreadsheet. Oracle’s own June annual report, for what it’s worth, was candid enough to admit as much in its risk disclosures — the company could not guarantee it would successfully manage its outstanding debt, and warned explicitly that its customers might not be able to pay.

The Malaysia Problem

The financial exposure would be a self-contained story if it were the whole story, but Ellison’s AI pivot also dragged Oracle into the exact geopolitical fight this series covered at length in Parts LXXIX and LXXX under Asimov Gap — except Oracle, unlike Anthropic, appears to have simply walked through the open door instead of fighting at it. Oracle built one of the world’s largest data centers in Malaysia, and independent analysts at SemiAnalysis and the think tank ChinaTalk — whose assessments the Times could not independently verify but treats as credible — estimated the facility was feeding as much as 22.6 percent of China’s known AI computing power, largely to ByteDance. The Biden administration had been actively drafting a policy to cap American hyperscalers’ overseas computing power specifically to prevent this kind of leakage to Chinese firms through third countries; Oracle’s top Washington policy executive fought the plan aggressively, calling it one of the most destructive moves ever proposed against the tech industry. Forty-eight hours before that Biden-era restriction was set to take effect in 2025, the incoming Trump administration rescinded it — clearing the way for the Malaysia facility to keep operating exactly as it had been.

The TikTok entanglement runs even closer to the regulatory line. Oracle became the custodian of American TikTok user data in the deal structured to keep the app operating in the US, a deal that gave ByteDance 19.9 percent ownership of the joint venture — one-tenth of a percentage point under the legal cap — while explicitly barring ByteDance from any “operational relationship” with the US platform. Five former Biden national security officials told the Times the arrangement doesn’t come close to resolving their original concerns; one called it a clear violation of the spirit, and possibly the letter, of the law Congress passed. Oracle, simultaneously the guardian of TikTok’s American user data and a major infrastructure supplier to ByteDance’s AI ambitions in Malaysia, occupies a position that would be a conflict of interest anywhere else in the federal contracting world. Here, it is simply Tuesday.

The Family Business Gets a Media Empire

Ellison’s personal fortune, meanwhile, went somewhere the rest of this series hasn’t followed yet: into Hollywood. He backed his son David’s $8 billion acquisition of Paramount and, in 2025, personally guaranteed $45.7 billion of David’s $111 billion bid for Warner Bros. Discovery — a deal that, to close, required Gulf sovereign wealth funds from the UAE, Saudi Arabia, and Qatar to take a combined 38.5 percent stake in what would become one of America’s largest media conglomerates, owner of CNN among dozens of other properties. Twelve state attorneys general have since sued to block the deal, winning a temporary restraining order; the acquisition is currently delayed. Ellison’s personal guarantee now represents a considerably larger share of his net worth than it did the day he signed it, at the exact moment his Oracle shares — 1.1 billion of them, 346 million already pledged as collateral against personal loans — have lost well over half their peak value. The man who briefly became the world’s richest person last September by riding Oracle’s stock surge is now watching the same stock decline threaten both his AI infrastructure bet and his media empire simultaneously, financed by many of the same categories of foreign capital this series flagged as a national-security concern in an entirely different context two essays ago.

A Bubble That’s Made Him Rich Twice

The single most pointed fact in the Times’ reporting is also the most structurally elegant: the last time before 2025 that Ellison was declared the world’s richest person was April 2000 — the precise peak of the dot-com bubble, weeks before the Nasdaq began its 80 percent collapse into what analysts of the era called nuclear winter. Ellison survived that crash as a billionaire, eventually recovering and surpassing his prior fortune by 2018. Whether the current bet follows the same arc — a genuine transformation that happens to look, for a frightening stretch in the middle, exactly like a bubble — or ends differently this time is the question Wall Street can’t yet answer. One macroeconomic research firm, MacroStrategy Partnership, estimates the current AI bubble at 17 times the size of the dot-com bubble and four times the size of the 2008 housing crash; Gita Gopinath, the IMF’s former chief economist, has put the potential wealth destruction from an AI crash at $20 trillion — more than either of those two prior crises individually. Ellison himself, in a September call with analysts, described the coming wave in the most Ellison possible terms, comparing this moment to Formula 1: “Someone’s going to be better at this than anyone else, and multiple people are trying.”

The Island in the Background

It is worth noting, briefly, the scale of the personal life this entire financial structure is underwriting, if only because the contrast between the two registers — sovereign-debt-sized corporate exposure and a genuinely idiosyncratic private world — says something about how differently risk registers for a man at this altitude of wealth. Ellison owns the Hawaiian island of Lanai outright: 140 square miles, 47 miles of coastline, its own gas station and movie theater, roughly 3,000 residents who reportedly refer to him simply as “ownership.” He spent much of the pandemic there, hosting Bezos, Netanyahu, and Tony Blair at a single birthday party, and is currently building a house on the island for Elon Musk. None of this moves Oracle’s debt-to-equity ratio by a single decimal point, and that is precisely the point worth sitting with: a corporate balance sheet leveraged five-to-one against shareholder equity and a private life insulated by an entire private island are not in tension for Ellison the way they would be for almost anyone else on the Oracle org chart. The downside risk this series has spent five essays quantifying — stock declines, credit downgrades, state attorneys general lawsuits — is real to Oracle’s shareholders, its employees (18 percent of whom were laid off this spring), and the pension funds holding its now-junk-adjacent bonds. It is a rounding error to the man who built the strategy.

Where Oracle Sits in This Series’ Ledger

I’ve spent five entries in this series tracing the mechanics that make the broader AI buildout financially fragile — circular financing between Nvidia and its own customers, off-balance-sheet lease obligations, depreciation schedules that may be understating real costs by tens of billions of dollars, and now Nvidia’s own $105 billion guarantee structure for a single Ohio site. Oracle is what all of those mechanisms look like concentrated inside a single company rather than distributed across an industry. It carries the highest leverage of any major hyperscaler, its most important customer doesn’t expect to be profitable until the end of the decade, its infrastructure is entangled with the same Chinese computing-access questions this series covered from the Pentagon’s side under a different banner, and its owner has personally guaranteed tens of billions more against a media acquisition financed partly by the same category of foreign sovereign capital that worried the Biden administration when it showed up near AI data centers instead. If Oracle is the barometer this industry’s own analysts say it is, the reading it’s currently giving is not reassuring, and Ellison, twice now the richest man in the world at what may in hindsight prove to be exactly the wrong moment, has more reason than almost anyone else on earth to hope the barometer is broken.
