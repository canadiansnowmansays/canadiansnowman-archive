Can Sam Altman be trusted? The more important question: why is his trustworthiness the primary governance mechanism for transformative AI. Visual by Grok AI.

In 2015, Sam Altman texted Dario Amodei to say he would be ten minutes late to their first dinner together. His Uber had gotten into a crash.

Ronan Farrow and Andrew Marantz, who spent many months investigating Altman for a 16,000-word New Yorker profile, chose to present this detail without editorial comment. They noted it and moved on. The reader is left to do with it what they will.

There is a reason the detail resonates. It is not because an Uber crash, if true, is consequential. It is because, in the context of everything else Farrow and Marantz document, the reader finds themselves uncertain — genuinely uncertain — whether the most consequential technology executive in the world’s Uber actually got in a crash before his first dinner with the man who would leave his company and build his most serious competitor. We cannot know. There is no mechanism to know. The question of whether Sam Altman’s Uber crashed on the way to a restaurant in 2015 is, in miniature, the question this series has been asking for twenty-seven parts: who verifies the claims of the people building systems that could reshape civilization, and what happens when the answer is nobody?

The New Yorker profile is titled “Can Sam Altman Be Trusted?” This is not quite the right question. Or rather, it is the right question asked in the wrong register. The answer that Farrow and Marantz provide — carefully, forensically, with more sourcing and documentation than any previous account — is important. But the more important answer was given by Farrow himself, not about Altman, when Kevin Roose pressed him on why it matters who is running these companies at all.

“We have a technology emerging that could really affect us all in all of the existential ways you just mentioned,” Farrow said, “and we don’t have the regulatory guardrails to keep an eye on these folks. We are completely ceding the power to these individual companies and their whims, the mud fight between them, the quality control that each of them has or lacks.”

That is the Asimov Gap. Stated plainly, by one of the most accomplished investigative journalists in the world, after months of reporting on the man at the centre of it.

The Original Pitch

Ronan Farrow (left) and Andrew Marantz (right) produced the most documented account yet of the man at the centre of AI's governance vacuum. What they found wasn't a villain. It was a void.

Andrew Marantz made an argument on the Hard Fork podcast that cuts through the noise around the Altman profile more cleanly than any of the specific allegations.

The defence most commonly offered by Altman’s supporters, he noted, is that this is normal Silicon Valley behaviour — that telling different audiences different things, managing relationships strategically, emphasizing different aspects of the business to different stakeholders is simply how founders operate at this scale. Why, these defenders ask, is anyone even writing a 16,000-word piece about it?

“When you go back to what the original pitch was,” Marantz replied, “the defense of ‘why are you guys being so naive, this is a normal competitive business’ starts to feel a little more pressured.” Because when OpenAI was founded, the pitch was not a normal competitive business. It was a nonprofit. It was explicitly safety-focused. It committed to aggressive compliance with regulation. It framed itself, in Altman’s own words and in its founding documents, around avoiding what Altman himself described as an AGI dictatorship — the concentration of transformative AI power in the hands of any single actor.

Unconstrained by a need to generate financial return." The founding charter of OpenAI, December 11, 2015. It was publicly posted. It was sincerely believed by the people who left good jobs to join. It lasted about as long as most New Year's resolutions, and with considerably higher stakes.

“Were the people who believed that naive to believe it at the time?” Marantz asked. He was not being rhetorical.

This is the governance failure that precedes and underlies everything else in the Farrow-Marantz account. OpenAI’s founding pitch was a governance promise — a promise that this technology would be developed in a structure designed to prevent its capture by any single commercial interest. That promise was made to raise money, to recruit talent, to neutralize regulatory concern, and to distinguish OpenAI from the purely commercial AI development it was ostensibly founded to counterbalance. The promise is now largely inoperative. And the mechanism by which it became inoperative — gradually, through a series of decisions each of which was individually defensible, none of which was publicly authorized — is a case study in how private governance of transformative technology actually works in practice, as opposed to how it is pitched.

The Preponderance

Sam Altman at OpenAI DevDay, San Francisco, November 6, 2023 — twelve days before his board fired him for not being "consistently candid," five days before he was reinstated. Photo: Justin Sullivan / Getty Images.

The specific findings of the Farrow-Marantz investigation are worth stating plainly, because their cumulative weight is the point.

There is no single smoking gun. Marantz was explicit about this on the podcast — the memos and documents that have circulated about Altman in Silicon Valley for years “really shouldn’t have been a secret bulleted list, they should have been a 16,000-word New Yorker piece. Because they only really make sense when you lay them all out together in narrative form.”

What the piece lays out: an extraordinary preponderance of people who have had close, extended interactions with Altman — including people who supported him, gave him the benefit of the doubt, and had strong incentives not to criticize him — who have come away with active complaints and allegations that he lies repeatedly about things big and small. A Microsoft executive told the reporters there is “a small but real chance he’s eventually remembered as a Bernie Madoff or Sam Bankman-Fried level scammer.” An unnamed board member said Altman is “unconstrained by truth” and has “an almost sociopathic lack of concern for the consequences that may come from deceiving someone.”

These are not critics with obvious axes to grind. They are people who, at various points, stood to benefit from Altman’s success. The fact that they are speaking now, and speaking on record or near-record, is itself a data point about how the wind has shifted among the people who have watched him most closely.

One documented exchange captures the pattern at close range. During negotiations over a Microsoft investment, Altman assured Dario Amodei that the agreement contained no provisions overriding OpenAI’s safety commitments. Amodei pointed to the provision. He brought in a second colleague to confirm it was there. Altman’s response, according to contemporaneous notes and accounts, was: “Oh, well, who cares?” Moments later, he said he had never made the original claim. Amodei replied: “You just said it.” This is the quality of the interactions that led Ilya Sutskever, OpenAI’s chief scientist, to send the board a set of memos running to approximately seventy pages. The first item on the list: Sam exhibits a consistent pattern of lying.

Then there is the board investigation. After Altman’s 2023 firing and reinstatement — a moment that convulsed Silicon Valley and prompted enormous public discussion — the departing board members insisted on an outside law firm investigation as a condition of their exit. There was no written report. There was an 800-word press release describing a vague “breakdown in trust.” One of the two board members who oversaw the process has now confirmed explicitly, on the record, that a written report was not produced because, in their view, one was not needed.

The two board members who oversaw that non-investigation were Brett Taylor, a former Facebook executive who had previously been kept off the board on the grounds he was too close to Altman, and Larry Summers, the former Treasury Secretary and Harvard president. Summers has since stepped down from the OpenAI board following the disclosure of extensive correspondence with Jeffrey Epstein, including emails in which he asked Epstein for advice on pursuing a romantic relationship with a young mentee.

Farrow called this “quite extraordinary” for a nonprofit — a company whose structure, unlike a private corporation, implies public accountability and stakeholder obligations beyond its shareholders. The people who worked at OpenAI, who were told the company’s mission was humanity’s long-term benefit, had a reasonable expectation of knowing what the law firm found. They were told nothing.

The pattern that emerges from these details was identified by a former colleague quoted in the piece: Altman has “a track record of setting up elaborate guardrails to keep him in check and then skillfully navigating around them.” This is not described as a character flaw in isolation. It is described as a pattern — repeatable, documented across multiple contexts — that has consequences specifically because of the absence of any external structure capable of enforcing the guardrails when Altman navigates around them.

The AGI Dictator Problem

Here is the sentence from the Hard Fork discussion of the piece that this series needs to sit with.

“The fact that we’re having a discussion about AGI dictators at all,” Andrew Marantz said, “is insane. These guys know it’s insane, and yet this seems to be the race that they see themselves being in.”

He is right. And the insanity is structural, not personal.

The founders of OpenAI were not wrong that the character of the people at the frontier of AI development matters. They were not wrong that concentrated control of transformative AI is dangerous. They were not wrong that governance structures — oversight boards, nonprofit accountability, regulatory compliance — are necessary safeguards. They built a company explicitly on these premises. The premise was correct. The structure failed to enforce it.

A former board member named Sue Yun, speaking on record, offered what may be the most useful diagnostic frame. Rejecting both the “evil mastermind” interpretation and the “great businessman, nothing to see here” interpretation, she described something in between: a person who, in her words, believes the shifting realities of his sales pitches as he is saying them — driven by what she called a preternatural lack of doubt or reflection. This is a different kind of problem than dishonesty in the ordinary sense. A conventional liar knows what is true and chooses otherwise. What Yun is describing is someone for whom the distinction may not reliably exist. That is, in some respects, a more disturbing governance problem than a straightforward liar. A liar can, in principle, be caught and held accountable. Someone who believes their own shifting reality as they produce it is much harder to constrain — and much more likely to navigate around the guardrails with a clear conscience.

The lesson is not that Sam Altman is uniquely dangerous, or that a different individual at the head of OpenAI would have produced a different result. The lesson is that private governance of transformative technology does not work — not because the people involved are bad, but because the structural pressures are stronger than individual intention, and stronger than the governance structures individuals build for themselves. Demis Hassabis built an oversight board as a condition of sale to Google. It eroded. Jack Clark described the competitive dynamics that make even safety-focused labs choose speed over restraint. Altman founded a nonprofit and turned it into a for-profit. The Pentagon episode, examined in Part 25, showed the same pattern operating in a national security context — and on May 1, 2026, Pentagon CTO Emil Michael confirmed it publicly on CNBC: Anthropic remains blacklisted as a supply chain risk, he said, while Mythos is simultaneously a 'separate national security moment' requiring government-wide response. The NSA is reportedly using the model anyway. Michael's summary of the guardrails: 'Those are negotiable.' The pattern is not individual. It is systemic. Even Anthropic, the company founded specifically because of concerns about Altman's trustworthiness, is now swimming in Google's money — $40 billion committed as of this week — confirmation that the race to the bottom has no safe lane.

Ronan Farrow put it as plainly as it has been put: we are ceding power to these companies and their whims. The mud fight between them — the Musk-Altman rivalry, the Anthropic-OpenAI rivalry, the race to whatever the next Mythos-class capability turns out to be — is being conducted without the regulatory architecture that would make any of it accountable to the people who will live with the results.

This is what the Altman profile reveals, beneath and beyond the specific allegations about his honesty. It reveals the shape of the governance vacuum. A vacuum in which a man who may or may not have told the truth about his Uber can be, simultaneously, the most important single individual in the development of technology that could reshape civilization — and accountable to no public body, no democratic mandate, and no enforceable standard beyond the contractual obligations of the investors who funded him.

Sam Altman swears in before the Senate Judiciary Subcommittee, Washington, May 16, 2023. He told lawmakers regulation was essential. Two years later, there is none. Photo: Eric Lee / Bloomberg via Getty Images.

The Question That Matters

Kevin Roose asked Farrow whether it matters who is running these companies at all. He framed the counterargument cleanly: a very honest person could release a rogue superintelligence, and a manipulative person could release a perfectly aligned model. Why focus on the humans rather than the technology?

Farrow’s answer was not what the question invited. He did not defend the focus on Altman’s character as the correct lens. He used it to describe the governance void.

“The integrity of an individual figures in that,” he said, “and it’s important. It reveals the weaknesses in the system. If you have someone who potentially lies all the time, the important thing is to have the structures that account for that.”

The structures do not exist. That is the point of the profile. And that is the point of this series.

The question “can Sam Altman be trusted” is, finally, the wrong question — not because it is unimportant, but because it locates the problem in the wrong place. The right question is: what kind of system have we built in which the trustworthiness of one individual is the primary mechanism by which transformative technology is governed? And the answer to that question is not in the Farrow-Marantz profile. The answer to that question is the absence of anything else.

In 2015, Sam Altman texted Dario Amodei to say his Uber had gotten into a crash. Farrow and Marantz noted it. Neither editorialized.

We still don’t know what happened. We still don’t have the tools to find out. That’s the gap.
