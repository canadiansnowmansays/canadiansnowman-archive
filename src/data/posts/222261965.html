There is a cemetery in Santiago de Cuba where the grass is cut to razor-sharpness, the walkways are swept clean, and the honour guard changes every thirty minutes, all day long, rifles pointed skyward. The Santa Ifigenia Cemetery is where Fidel Castro’s ashes are kept — sealed inside a rounded granite boulder near the mausoleum of José Martí, Cuba’s great nationalist hero. Soldiers in neatly pressed uniforms goose-step in perfect unison, present arms, and are relieved by an identical set of soldiers, who march back. This ceremony occurs every half hour, every day, for an audience that is, by all accounts, essentially no one.

It was, Robert F. Worth wrote in The Atlantic on October 26, 2026, the only immaculately clean place he encountered in all of Cuba.

That sentence is worth sitting with for a moment.

In twelve original parts and three subsequent dispatches — now renumbered as Parts 17 through 19 of this series — we have analysed Cuba’s crisis from the outside in. We have followed the CIA task force assembled to fracture the island’s governing elite without provoking the kind of hard-line crackdown that would produce international sympathy for the regime. We have profiled Raúl G. Rodríguez Castro, nicknamed El Cangrejo, allegedly the preferred American-backed successor candidate. We have documented the shipping containers rotting at Mariel because Western carriers would rather absorb the loss than risk American sanctions, and the cancer drugs that stopped arriving, and the UN’s meticulous cataloguing of what it calls “overcompliance.”

What we have not had, until now, is a witness who actually went.

Worth spent nine days driving the island in August 2026 — an eighteen-hour journey from Havana to Santiago on roads cratered with enormous potholes, past horse-drawn carts more numerous than motorised vehicles, through a country that, as he put it, sometimes made him feel as if he were travelling backwards in time. His dispatch for The Atlantic is the most detailed ground-level account of Cuba’s condition that has appeared in a major English-language publication this year. It is not comfortable reading.

The State That Stopped Showing Up

The first and most consequential thing Worth observed was the near-disappearance of the Cuban state as a physical fact of daily life.

Police and soldiers, once omnipresent throughout the island, were barely visible. The Committees for the Defence of the Revolution — the neighbourhood surveillance network that kept Cuba’s population in line for six decades — had become something people openly laughed about. When Worth asked Cubans about the CDR, few seemed concerned about being overheard. In Parts 1 through 16 of this series, we traced how a government can progressively lose legitimacy over decades of failure. Worth’s reporting shows what the end stage of that process looks like from street level: not a dramatic collapse, but a quiet evaporation. The machinery of fear has not been dismantled — it has simply ceased to function as a credible threat.

The ration cards that guaranteed basic foodstuffs for every Cuban — one of the revolution’s foundational promises — were blank when Worth visited in August. The average monthly salary for a public-sector worker, which is to say a doctor, a teacher, or an engineer, was approximately 6,500 pesos. That converts to less than ten American dollars. A single carton of eggs cost up to 4,700 pesos. Banks, on certain days, limited cash withdrawals to 1,000 pesos per person — less than a dollar and a half. In far-eastern Cuba, Worth encountered fishermen who had abandoned refrigeration entirely and returned to traditional methods of drying and salting their catch, because there was no electricity to run the coolers.

In Santiago, on the seafront, a seventy-six-year-old woman and her adult daughter approached him. Both were, by his description, skeletally thin. Their only support had been a son who died of cancer. They now spend their days begging and collecting plastic bottles to trade for pesos. The state provides nothing. Their home is a ground-floor room where they sleep on metal bed frames topped with cardboard. When Worth asked whether there was electricity, the mother told him they did not even have a lightbulb.

That is what the government’s billboards, still cheerfully plastered across the island proclaiming that socialism meets all material and spiritual needs, are advertising to.

The Nurse Who Would Not Retire

Cuba’s healthcare system was, for decades, the revolution’s most morally defensible achievement and the most effective rebuttal to its critics. Castro sent hundreds of thousands of doctors overseas — an army of white coats that generated both foreign exchange and genuine goodwill. Whatever else could be said about the regime, its network of neighbourhood clinics reached communities that neither the Batista dictatorship before it nor many wealthier nations beside it had bothered to serve.

That system is now in free fall.

Worth travelled to Cayo Granma, a small island at the entrance to the bay of Santiago, where he found a single nurse responsible for all 870 residents plus the surrounding coastal population. Her name is Clara Luaces. She is fifty-five years old. When Worth arrived at her clinic — a room the size of a large closet, crowded with patients in near-forty-degree heat — she was managing a twelve-year-old boy with a fever, an IV drip in his arm, and a girl with a skin infection beside him.

Doctors have been abandoning the region so quickly that Luaces has lost count of how many patients she has inherited. The government stopped piping clean water to the island three months before Worth’s August visit. The resulting bacterial infections had begun killing people. “Even young people are dying on me,” she told him.

She will not retire. There is no one to take over.

This is the revolution’s promise, encountered at the end of its road. Not an ideological argument, not a propaganda film, not a conference paper — a fifty-five-year-old nurse adjusting a feverish boy’s pillow and calling him “sweetheart” because she is the only medical professional for miles, and she knows it.

Worth also visited a Catholic nursing home in Havana housing former civil servants and military officers — men who spent their careers in the regime’s service and now find themselves abandoned by both the state and their own families, most of whom have long since left the country. In one room he found a dozen old men whose beds were lined up a metre apart, each with a single narrow locker for everything he owned. Among them, a sixty-five-year-old Angola veteran who once had a large Che Guevara tattoo put on his arm at eighteen in a flush of revolutionary fervour. He showed Worth his battle scars. Worth asked what should be done to improve Cuba’s situation. Cuba needed to be bombed, the man said. Raúl Castro specifically. And Raúl’s daughter Mariela. And the prime minister.

A true believer who fought in Africa for this government now wants it bombed out of existence. If the regime needed a measure of how thoroughly it has exhausted its moral capital, that veteran with the faded Che tattoo is it.

The Church Fills the Vacuum

Into the space the state has vacated, the churches have moved — with what Worth describes as almost military efficiency.

In Baracoa, he met Pastor Raúl de Jesús Muñoz Munder, who has evangelised in the region for thirty-two years. Every week, his associate pastors report to him on conditions in their respective neighbourhoods: who is sick and needs medicine, who needs food, and how to get supplies to them. His congregation numbers approximately 1,100 regular members, but he provides aid to a substantially larger population. The Assemblies of God denomination alone has grown from two churches in the Baracoa region to forty over three decades. One Saturday evening Worth stumbled upon several different evangelical services simultaneously — each loud and raucous, congregants on their feet and clapping, a scene that still startles older Cubans accustomed to the more orderly Catholic traditions of their upbringing.

The Catholic Church, meanwhile, is reclaiming ground quite literally. In Havana, a priest named Iván Guerra Álvarez led Worth up a battered staircase to the Los Escolapios church, whose cloister and convent were seized by the revolution in 1959, its congregation exiled or jailed. The guards are gone now, along with the Fidel portraits and the secular schoolteachers who occupied the lower floors. The Church is in the process of recovering the magnificent eighteenth-century cloister from the ruins the state left it in. When Worth arrived, more than a hundred worshippers and schoolchildren filled the nave; a teacher dressed as an angel was giving Bible lessons; volunteers were distributing lunch.

“The Church will play a big role in the transition,” Guerra Álvarez told him. “We are the biggest institution in Cuba outside the government.”

This is not merely a spiritual story. The Trump administration’s $100 million aid package for Cuba, approved in May 2026, was conditioned on distribution through faith-based groups and humanitarian organisations. Washington has identified its transition partner. In Parts 17 and 18, we documented the CIA’s parallel effort to identify which elements of the Cuban military and security apparatus might be persuaded to shift their loyalties. The Church and the intelligence community, it appears, are converging on the same calculation from different directions.

Whether that produces a genuine democratic transition or simply delivers one form of institutional dependency in place of another remains the most consequential open question about Cuba’s near future.

The Anger That Finally Found Its Voice

Perhaps the most politically striking finding in Worth’s account is also the one most likely to be misread by those looking for simple answers.

In nine days travelling across the island, he did not encounter a single Cuban who blamed Donald Trump or the United States for their suffering. He raised the question directly: hadn’t Trump’s oil blockade, imposed after the American military’s capture of Venezuelan president Nicolás Maduro in January 2026, made things substantially worse?

“I don’t see any change since January,” a retired engineer named Guillermo Almeida told him. “That oil from Venezuela wasn’t reaching the people anyway.” Others agreed. The real turning point, most Cubans said, was the COVID-19 pandemic, which destroyed Cuba’s tourism industry and shrank the remittances from overseas family members that millions of households depend on to survive. “It seems to us,” another man told Worth, “that the real embargo is inside of Cuba.”

Let us be careful with that observation. It does not exonerate sixty-seven years of American policy — economic warfare, assassination plots, diplomatic isolation, and the systematic strangulation of a neighbour whose principal offence was refusing to be governed as Washington preferred. But it does reflect something real: a population that has lived through so many compounding failures and experienced the fruits of Venezuelan oil or Soviet subsidies so little that the additional suffering from Trump’s blockade barely registers against the baseline of permanent catastrophe.

What does register — what has finally burned through the last residue of revolutionary loyalty — is the anger. On a beach in Baracoa one evening, a man in his thirties, lying in a hammock with four friends and a bottle of sugarcane rum, launched without prompting into obscenities directed at Fidel Castro’s memory. Then the humour drained from his face.

“Cuba is frozen in time,” he said. “There’s no hope. It’s a sad thing to be born a Cuban. That’s what I think, and I don’t care; they can send me to prison if they want.”

The other men had gone silent.

They can send me to prison if they want. That sentence is the sound of a population that no longer believes the threat carries weight. The apparatus of fear that sustained the revolution for nearly seven decades has not merely been weakened. It has lost its credibility — and in a state whose entire governance model rested on manufactured fear, that is not a political setback. It is a terminal diagnosis.

Worth also noted one more detail, almost in passing, that carries its own kind of elegy. An eighty-one-year-old man in Santiago who had spent forty-five years as a singer in a band told him: there used to be a musical group on every corner of Santiago. Now there were none. The music — the thing Cuba exported to the world more successfully than any other product, the thing the island was known for long before the revolution and long after — has gone quiet.

The Guard Changes; the Country Has Moved On

And so we return to Santa Ifigenia Cemetery, where the grass is perfect and the honour guard marches every half hour, every day, for no one.

Watching those soldiers, Worth wrote, you could believe for a moment that you were somewhere else — in the Cuba Castro had dreamed of, where socialism is triumphant and everyone aspires to the example of a glorious revolution. Then a breeze swept across the grass, carrying the acrid smell of burning garbage, and he remembered where he was.

The changing of the guard at Fidel’s tomb is the perfect emblem of what remains of the Cuban state: an immaculate performance, conducted on a fixed schedule, for an audience of no one, surrounded by a country that has long since moved on. The ritual continues. The country has not.

What happens next is genuinely uncertain. The regime has suppressed any viable domestic opposition with enough consistency that no obvious successor coalition has formed. El Cangrejo and his alleged smuggling networks are positioning themselves. The Church is positioning itself. Washington, the CIA, and Marco Rubio are positioning themselves. The Cuban diaspora in South Florida — the same community whose intractable grievances have shaped American Cuba policy for sixty-five years — is positioning itself.

And Cuba’s population — who have already outlasted the Spanish Empire, the American occupation, the Batista dictatorship, the Soviet embrace, the Special Period of the 1990s, and sixty-seven years of the revolution itself — continue to wake up, collect plastic bottles, tend the sick with no medicine, improvise stoves from diesel engines, light candles in the dark, and wait.

At Santa Ifigenia, the honour guard completes its rotation. Another unit marches out. The music has stopped playing. And somewhere in Baracoa, in a hammock on a beach at dusk, a man who no longer fears prison drinks cheap rum and says what everyone else is thinking.

This series will keep watching.
