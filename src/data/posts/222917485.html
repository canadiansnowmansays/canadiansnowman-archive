On 29 October 2026, Sir Patrick Stewart’s representative announced that he had died at 86, after a brief illness with Alzheimer’s disease and Lewy body dementia, as ABC News reported. For seven seasons he opened Star Trek: The Next Generation by calling space the final frontier. Two days later, on 31 October, The New York Times launched a series on how the Trump administration is taking apart the American scientific research system. Among the casualties it named was a five-year study at the University of Pittsburgh into how stress and health problems earlier in life raise the odds of Alzheimer’s disease. The National Institutes of Health terminated it in August.

The coincidence is not an argument. It is, however, a reminder of what research is for. The disease that took one of the most recognisable voices of his generation is exactly the kind of problem that federally funded science has spent eighty years chipping away at, slowly and expensively, with no guarantee of when the breakthrough comes.

This series is about that eighty-year bargain, what it bought, and what is happening to it now. Its title comes from the document that started it.

A Report to the President

On 17 November 1944, Franklin Roosevelt wrote to Vannevar Bush, the engineer who ran the wartime Office of Scientific Research and Development. Bush’s office had organised the civilian science behind radar, the proximity fuse and the mass production of penicillin. Roosevelt’s question, in essence, was how the methods that had won a war be put to work in peacetime.

Roosevelt did not live to read the answer. Bush delivered it to Harry Truman in July 1945, under the title Science, the Endless Frontier.

The report made three claims that sound obvious today and were radical then. Basic research, the kind with no product in mind, is where the big returns come from. Only government can afford to fund it at scale, because no company can wait decades for a payoff it may not capture. And the government that pays should not decide what gets studied: that choice belongs to scientists, judged by other scientists on merit.

From that bargain came the National Science Foundation in 1950, a vastly expanded National Institutes of Health, and later NASA and the research laboratories of what became the Department of Energy. Washington paid. Universities and laboratories did the work. Peer review decided who got the money. By 2024, according to the Times, American universities were spending $65 billion a year of federal money on research, more than half of everything they spent on it.

It was, for a long time, the best deal any government ever struck.

What the Money Bought

The trouble with explaining basic research is that its payoffs arrive so late that nobody remembers who paid for them.

In June 1943, the US Army signed a contract with the University of Pennsylvania’s Moore School of Electrical Engineering. The Army wanted a faster way to calculate artillery firing tables. The result, as the US Army Research Laboratory records in its own history, was ENIAC, dedicated in February 1946: the first general-purpose electronic digital computer, programmed by six women recruited from the Army’s teams of human calculators. Its designers went on to build UNIVAC, whose first customer was the Census Bureau. Everything on the desk in front of you descends from that contract.

In 1948, the National Bureau of Standards ran the first atomic clock. It was no more accurate than the clocks it was meant to replace, according to its successor agency, NIST. Forty-seven years later, in 1995, the Air Force declared the Global Positioning System fully operational, running on the atomic clocks that line of work produced. Every map on every phone is a dividend from a government laboratory that, in 1948, had built a clock that kept no better time.

In October 1969, a Defense Department research agency sent the first message across a network it called ARPANET, from UCLA to the Stanford Research Institute, DARPA notes. The National Science Foundation later built the backbone that carried it to universities across the country, then handed it to commercial carriers in 1995. Two Stanford graduate students working on an NSF-funded digital library project in the mid-1990s turned their search experiment into a company called Google.

In 1976, researchers at the University of California, San Francisco, working on Public Health Service grants and a National Cancer Institute contract, showed that cancer-causing genes have counterparts in normal cells. That finding led to a Nobel Prize in 1989 and, by way of further NCI-supported work, to Gleevec, approved in 2001 in 72 days. The National Cancer Institute credits it with transforming the treatment of one form of leukaemia. The Human Genome Project, run by the NIH and the Energy Department, finished in 2003, more than two years early, according to the National Human Genome Research Institute.

On 1 April 1960, NASA launched TIROS-1, the first weather satellite. Its first picture of Earth was grainy and crooked. Today a five-day forecast is about as reliable as a one-day forecast was in 1980.

Look at the gaps between the spending and the payoff: 47 years from the atomic clock to GPS, 26 from ARPANET to the commercial internet, 25 from the oncogene paper to Gleevec. Marcia McNutt, who was president of the National Academy of Sciences until this summer, put it to the Times in five words: “Science is the long game.”

That is the point of this series. If the dividends take a generation to arrive, so does the damage.

What Is Happening Now

The Times identified three forces behind the strain: the job cuts and grant cancellations engineered by Elon Musk’s Department of Government Efficiency, the use of research funding as leverage against universities, and a campaign by budget director Russell Vought to bring political control over how Congress’s appropriations are spent.

The first thing to say is the administration’s best defence, because it is true. The federal government is still spending roughly $200 billion a year on research, about what its predecessors spent. When the White House asked Congress for 2026 to cut NIH by about 41 per cent and the NSF by about 57 per cent, Congress refused. The Congressional Research Service shows the final 2026 budgets for NIH, the NSF, NASA science and the Energy Department’s Office of Science all within a few per cent of the previous year. Scientists are still getting grants. Universities are still being paid.

The damage is underneath the totals.

The NIH spent its full budget in 2026, but the Association of American Medical Colleges counts about 7,500 fewer awards than two years earlier, because the agency paid for multi-year grants all at once. The same tracking found 5,776 NIH grants frozen or terminated since January 2025. In 2025, the Government Accountability Office ruled that the NIH had broken federal impoundment law by slowing the release of nearly $8 billion. The NSF ended its 2026 fiscal year with more than $1 billion that Congress had appropriated still unspent, AIP FYI reported.

Then there are the people. Federal science agencies lost nearly 95,000 staff between September 2024 and December 2025, about 12 per cent of their workforce, Government Executive reported from Partnership for Public Service data. The Pentagon’s own review of its research laboratories found they had lost about 14 per cent of their workforce, DefenseScoop reported. These are the labs the Times credits with helping develop radar and satellite communication. The Energy Department, home to the administration’s own flagship AI science effort, lost about a quarter of its staff.

The universities took a separate blow. The administration froze research money at Columbia, Harvard and other elite schools, officially over complaints about antisemitism on campus. Columbia agreed to pay more than $220 million to have its funding restored, the Associated Press reported, and Brown, Cornell and Northwestern reached deals of their own. Harvard sued, won in federal court in September 2025, and is now defending that ruling before the First Circuit, The Harvard Crimson reported. The next generation has noticed. At 55 leading research universities, international applications to doctoral programmes fell 21 per cent for fall 2026, and total PhD admissions fell 15 per cent, according to the Association of American Universities.

And then there is the rule. On 29 May 2026, the White House budget office proposed, in the Federal Register, that political appointees review federal research awards, and that no award be used to promote what it called “anti-American values.” Nearly half a million people filed comments. Congress has barred it from being finalised before 11 December. If it becomes final, it reverses the central principle of Bush’s report: the people who pay will decide what is studied.

The administration’s case deserves a hearing, and this series will give it one. The White House says it is ushering in a new golden age of American science, built around artificial intelligence and big national missions. Its officials argue that much university research had drifted into ideology and that a shake-up was overdue. A former White House aide told the Times that the system may simply cost more than it returns. Those claims will be tested, part by part, against the record.

Somebody Else’s Harvest

Here is where a Canadian has to declare an interest.

On 27 August 2026, Ottawa announced the first 64 holders of its new Eddie Goldenberg Research Chairs, part of a $1.7-billion, twelve-year programme to recruit more than 1,000 researchers, according to Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada. Forty-eight of the 64 came from American institutions, The PIE News reported. Sara Seager, the MIT planetary scientist, is now at the University of Toronto. Phil Zamore, who led the RNA Therapeutics Institute at UMass Chan Medical School, is moving his lab to McGill, WBUR reported.

Canada is not alone, and it is not the most generous bidder. The European Union put €500 million into a programme called Choose Europe for Science. Applications from US-based researchers for European Research Council starting grants rose from 116 in 2025 to 169 in 2026, DUB reported. The Netherlands, France, Germany, Spain and Austria have all opened programmes aimed at the same people. And in July 2026, Omar Yaghi, who shared the 2025 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, left the University of California, Berkeley, for a full-time chair at Tsinghua University in Beijing, where he will lead an institute using artificial intelligence to discover new materials. Those are two fields the White House says it intends to dominate.

It would be easy, from Toronto, to treat this as a windfall. It is better understood as a transfer. The United States trained these people, paid for their laboratories and built the networks that made them productive. Every American-trained researcher Canada recruits is a return on an American investment, collected by someone else.

How This Series Works

The Endless Frontier runs to eighteen parts. After an essay on how the system actually works, four lineages, told over six essays, trace what federal research bought in the machine, the network, the body and the sky. Each is paired with an essay on what is happening now to the system that produced it: the grant freezes, the fight with the universities, the money Congress approved that never arrived, the laboratories hollowed out by their own government, and the pipeline of students that is already thinning. Two essays follow the researchers north to Canada and across the Atlantic and Pacific; another measures how fast China is closing the gap; and one tests the administration’s golden-age claims against the record. The series ends by asking the question Bush never had to: once a system like this is broken, can it be rebuilt, and how long would that take?

Alongside the essays, The Seed Corn Ledger keeps the running account in three columns: what American science bought, what is under strain, and who is gaining. It is updated weekly. Farmers have a saying about seed corn: you can eat it this winter, but then there is nothing to plant in spring.

Vannevar Bush called science an endless frontier. He meant that there would always be more to discover. He did not promise that America would always be the one discovering it.
