This Week in New York — Friday, September 25 to Monday, September 28, 2026

On the afternoon of Friday, September 25, 2026, a rumour went round the United Nations that Xi Jinping would leave the White House, take the short hop north, and speak for China in the General Assembly after all. Reporters began rearranging their evenings. The Associated Press, in its weekend column of small bites from the UN, described how it settled the question. Someone inside the compound who would know checked for the one thing that always precedes a Chinese head of state: an advance team. There was none. The rumour was dead. “The Chinese don’t improvise,” the someone said.

They don’t. Vice-President Han Zheng spoke for China on Saturday, exactly as scheduled, and Xi flew home to Beijing from Washington without passing through New York.

The first three dispatches in this series followed the Assembly day by day. This last one covers the tail of the week, from Friday to the close of the general debate on Monday, September 28. Its subject is the one the rumour pointed to. The eighty-first session was, for many of the people who matter most to it, a place to be absent from, or to send a deputy to, or to use for purposes that had nothing to do with the room.

The empty chairs

Michael Crowley put the case plainly for The New York Times on September 25, 2026. When Xi visited Barack Obama in September 2015, he also came to New York and addressed the General Assembly. This time he did not bother. Jeffrey Prescott, a former Biden administration official now at the Carnegie Endowment, said the symbolism “could hardly be more devastating for the U.N.” Narendra Modi of India, the leader of the world’s most populous country, stayed away too.

Fairness requires the other reading, and Crowley gave it. China has increased its funding to the UN under Xi and sought more influence throughout its ranks. Some observers think Xi’s absence was less about the UN’s stature than about showing that China is America’s equal: a state visit to Washington shared with a speech in New York would have been a smaller state visit. And the Washington summit itself produced little more than a promise to meet again.

So the room everyone skipped was not really upstaged by anything grand. It was upstaged by the habit, now well established among the powerful, of treating the United Nations as optional.

The spoils answer back, politely

On Tuesday, September 22, Donald Trump defended the American takeover of Venezuela’s oil with a maxim that Thomas Wright of The Atlantic, quoted in Dispatch Three, took to be the old order’s obituary: “To the victor belongs the spoils.” On Wednesday, September 23, the Assembly heard from the vanquished.

Delcy Rodríguez became Venezuela’s acting president after an American military raid in January 2026 seized Nicolás Maduro and flew him to the United States to face drug-trafficking charges. He is being held, the Associated Press noted, only a few miles from UN headquarters while he awaits trial. Rodríguez’s trip to New York was the first by a Venezuelan head of state since 2018, when Maduro himself came to address the Assembly.

I expected a speech of defiance. It was nothing of the kind. Rodríguez thanked Trump and his administration for their willingness to resume relations, and called a new agreement between the two countries one of the most significant ever signed between them. She had met Trump briefly on the sidelines the day before; according to reporting carried by Al Jazeera, they discussed restructuring Venezuela’s billions of dollars in sovereign debt. “I wish to state this very clearly before this General Assembly,” she said. “Elections will be held in Venezuela.” She gave no date. The Venezuelan people, she said, would determine the right moment.

Her best line was addressed to her own country, still recovering from a double earthquake on June 24, 2026: “A nation cannot only be reborn by rebuilding buildings; rather, it is reborn when it rebuilds trust in its institutions.” Critics, the same report noted, say Washington has dropped its demand for free elections in favour of oil and investment deals with the interim government.

Put the three days side by side. On Tuesday, the victor claimed the spoils. On Wednesday, the government the victor had installed thanked him from the same podium. On Saturday, as we shall see, Russia’s foreign minister stood at that podium and demanded the release of the man the victor had taken. At no point in any of this did anyone ask the United Nations for permission, or suggest that its permission was relevant. The Assembly was the place where each party announced what had already been done.

Moscow’s rival rulebook

Sergey Lavrov has addressed the General Assembly for more than twenty years, and his speeches have the comfortable predictability of liturgy. Saturday’s had the usual hymns. He quoted Vladimir Putin on “the dictatorship of a single hegemon” that “has run amok.” He said the objectives of the “special military operation” in Ukraine “will be achieved.” According to UN News, he called Maduro’s capture an “extrajudicial detention” and demanded his release, called for the American blockade of Cuba to be lifted, denounced the killing of Iran’s supreme leader and said the strikes on Iran’s nuclear facilities had done “irreparable damage” to the non-proliferation system.

Two passages were new. The first was on Security Council reform, and it illustrates the point Part XI of this series makes about the lock on the Charter. Russia, Lavrov said, supports permanent seats for Brazil and India and more representation for Africa, but opposes seats for Germany or Japan. Every permanent member supports reform, as long as it can veto the reformers.

The second was an announcement. Russia, Belarus and their allies, he said, had begun work on a “Eurasian Charter on Diversity and Multipolarity in the 21st Century.” It was a remarkable thing to announce from the podium of the organization founded on the actual Charter: a rival rulebook, launched in the chamber of the one it proposes to supplement.

The same afternoon produced the week’s most honest diplomatic exchange. Germany’s foreign minister, Johann Wadephul, met Lavrov for about twenty minutes, the first meeting between the two countries’ foreign ministers since the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, as the Associated Press reported. Wadephul urged Russia to abandon its “dangerous path of escalation.” They shook hands. Lavrov said afterwards that “nothing new came from” the conversation.

Nothing new came from it, and it happened anyway. That is the thing the building still does better than anywhere else: it puts people who will not otherwise speak to each other within twenty metres of a coffee machine. In his own speech, Wadephul offered a sentence that could serve as the Charter’s preamble for the age of artificial intelligence: “Whenever power becomes global, responsibility must become global, too.”

The deputies

Han Zheng’s speech on Saturday was, as the Associated Press put it, China using its UN address as usual to promote global development as the answer to the world’s problems. “Protectionism produces no winners,” he said, which was a pointed remark to make the day after his president left Washington. He called for safeguarding the post-war international order and opposed “unilateral military actions that violate the U.N. charter and circumvent Security Council authorization,” which was more pointed still.

What he left out mattered more. Chinese speeches at the UN routinely include a paragraph on Taiwan. Han’s did not. A day after a Trump–Xi summit that ended with a promise to meet again, Beijing chose not to raise its most sensitive grievance before the world. Silence, in diplomacy, is also a sentence.

It was not hard to see why Beijing felt it could afford one. Washington has been withholding a $14 billion arms package for Taiwan that congressional leaders approved early this year, and President Trump has called such sales a “very good negotiating chip.” On Sunday, September 27, the American ambassador to China, David Perdue, told Fox News that Trump had gone further during the visit and asked Xi whether he would like to buy some American weapons himself, as Edward Wong reported for The New York Times. The State Department replied that U.S. law prohibits arms sales to China and that there was “no offer or plan.” China had no need to mention Taiwan at the United Nations. The subject was being handled elsewhere, on better terms.

India’s external affairs minister, S. Jaishankar, spoke for Modi on the same day. He argued that the case for “reformed multilateralism” “only gets stronger day after day,” and described a “4F crisis” of food, fuel, fertiliser and finance falling hardest on countries with the least say over the wars that caused it. As The Quint reported, he also answered Pakistan, which had addressed the Assembly the day before, by calling it a “serial practitioner of terrorism.” “Friendship and goodwill cannot co-exist with terrorism,” he said. It was the week’s clearest example of two members using the hall to speak past each other, and a reminder that the case for an Indian permanent seat is being made by a government that does not intend to share the room gracefully.

Moral cowards, redefined

On Thursday, as Dispatch Three recorded, Benjamin Netanyahu told the delegates walking out of his speech that any “moral cowards” still in the hall should leave. On Saturday, Iceland’s foreign minister, Thorgerdur Katrín Gunnarsdóttir, took the phrase back.

“We have heard the words ‘moral cowards’ from this podium,” she said. “To me, moral cowards are those leaders around the world who refuse to sit down and negotiate a just and lasting peace. Those who deny access to humanitarian aid for people in desperate need. And moral cowards are those leaders who repeatedly go against and disrespect international law.”

It is the prerogative of small countries at the United Nations to say what large ones would rather not hear. Iceland has no army. It has, for five minutes a year, a microphone.

Canada’s wider view

Canada’s foreign minister, Anita Anand, used her time to say what Canadian governments have spent a year signalling and rarely said so baldly in public. “We can no longer afford to be overly reliant on any one partner,” she told the Assembly, according to the Associated Press account carried by The Canadian Press. She listed new economic ties with Europe, India, China and Africa, higher defence spending in line with NATO commitments, and, in a detail that will not have been lost on Washington, a new Canadian consulate in Greenland.

She closed with a sentence that is half pitch and half warning: “In a rapidly changing world, Canada is a reliable partner, and we are ready to work together.” It echoed Mark Carney’s argument, made since his Davos speech in January, that middle powers must look out for themselves and for the idea of international order. It also echoed Anthony Albanese’s speech on Thursday, recorded in Dispatch Three, asking for a seat at the Security Council’s table for the countries that are neither great powers nor loud ones. Two middle powers, in one week, made the same case from the same podium. Whether anyone at the top table heard it is another matter.

Monday: the last day

The general debate resumed on Monday, September 28, under its official theme, “Restoring trust, managing transformation: A United Nations that delivers for all,” and ran to its close. Saturday had been dominated, as UN News summarized it, by artificial intelligence and migration, with Singapore’s Vivian Balakrishnan insisting that “human beings must remain in control and accountable” and Jamaica’s Kamina Johnson Smith warning against AI that would “deepen old inequalities and create an entirely new global divide.” Monday was the tail of the list: the smaller delegations, the rights of reply, and the thinning hall.

It also brought one more fight about memory. The Assembly held a high-level meeting to mark twenty-five years since the 2001 Durban Declaration against racism, and was expected to adopt a political declaration reaffirming it. The United States and Israel walked out of the original Durban conference over what they called its anti-Israel and antisemitic language. This time, Israel campaigned for a boycott, and Israel’s ambassador, Danny Danon, said more than 40 countries stayed away, a record, surpassing the 38 who boycotted the twentieth anniversary in 2021. “The world is sending the UN a clear message,” he said. “Antisemitism should not be celebrated.” A declaration against racism, boycotted as a vehicle for hatred by a fifth of the membership, is as neat an illustration of the institution’s week as any.

The Security Council, meanwhile, met on the Middle East, as settler violence and settlement expansion continued in the West Bank and the Board of Peace plan for Gaza that Abbas mourned on Thursday remained stalled, its remaining phases, including Hamas’s disarmament and Israel’s full withdrawal, not carried out. And behind closed doors, the Council went on with the straw polls to choose António Guterres’s successor. No recommendation is expected this week. Al Jazeera reported that Guyana’s Carolyn Rodrigues-Birkett leads the informal poll. Nicholas Kristof, in The New York Times on September 26, 2026, voiced the fear that Washington, Beijing and Moscow will settle on “some innocuous dark horse nonentity.” Part XI of this series explains why that fear is structural.

What the week told us

Five days of speeches produced what they always produce: an enormous record of what the world’s governments want the world to think they think. Read in sequence, this year’s record says something unusually clear.

The powerful acted first and explained afterwards. The United States seized a head of state and threatened annihilation; Russia announced a rival charter; China and India sent deputies. The middle powers, Australia, Canada, Germany, Iceland, used the podium to argue for the rules the powerful had just set aside. And the building went on doing its unglamorous work in the corridors: a German and a Russian shaking hands over nothing new, an Iranian foreign minister offering a timetable, a Venezuelan president promising elections to the people she was really addressing.

This series began with a Canadian’s question about whether the Parliament of Man is still worth talking to. The week’s answer was a Chinese one. The powerful don’t improvise. They decide in advance which rooms matter. For this year, at least, they decided this one mattered less.

The flashbacks begin with the first time it happened: when an American president built a room for the world, and the United States Senate declined to enter it.
