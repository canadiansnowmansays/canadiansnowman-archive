Stephen Gordon September 24, 2026

Sometime in early September 2026, the Director of the Federal Bureau of Investigation ordered his agency to stop cooperating, in part, with the Royal Canadian Mounted Police — the national police force of one of America’s closest allies, a partner the Bureau relies on for terrorism and cybersecurity cases along a shared border of nearly nine thousand kilometres. He did this, according to reporting by the New York Times’s Maggie Haberman, Jane Bradley, Jonathan Swan, Adam Goldman, and Matina Stevis-Gridneff, not because of an intelligence failure, a security breach, or a diplomatic incident. He did it because Canada’s public broadcaster had reissued an internal style-guide memo about how its journalists should describe the September 11 attacks — and the broadcaster had already reversed that guidance before the FBI’s cooperation ever stopped.

That sequence is the whole story, and it is worth sitting with before moving to the details: the grievance was resolved, and the punishment happened anyway.

What CBC Actually Did

The controversy began with a memo, reported by a columnist at The Toronto Sun, in which CBC News reminded its staff of a long-standing editorial policy: that the word “terrorism” should be attributed to an expert or authority rather than used as the network’s own unqualified label, particularly for breaking or contested events. This is not, whatever the framing that followed, a policy unique to September 11 or invented to relitigate it. Many major news organisations, this newspaper’s own conventions on contested political violence included, maintain some version of an attribution standard precisely because “terrorism” is a legally and politically loaded term, and a newsroom’s job is description, not adjudication. The distinction CBC’s critics elided, deliberately or not, was between a style rule for how a word gets sourced and a substantive claim that the attacks were something other than terrorism. Nothing in the memo said the latter.

None of that nuance survived contact with social media. On August 27, Kash Patel posted to X: “Any agency in Canada that doesn’t publicly reject this bastardization of history, and an insult to the souls lost during our largest terrorist attack in US history will no longer have friend in this FBI… Not to mention our heroes that responded in the aftermath.” He closed with an American flag and “WE WILL NEVER FORGET 9-11.” Pete Hoekstra, the U.S. Ambassador to Canada, amplified the post the following day, warning that “failure to recognise and confront radical and terrorist ideologies significantly endangers our efforts to establish and harmonise a shared national and economic security partnership” — explicitly linking a wire-service style question to the architecture of bilateral security cooperation.

Facing that pressure, CBC News moved quickly. By August 28, the network’s head of standards had issued updated guidance stating that “terrorism” could be used to describe the events of September 11, 2001, without attribution. Canada’s culture minister, Marc Miller, called the reversal a “welcome change to what was a piece of pedantic and foolish guidance that caused legitimate anger.” The dispute, on its face, had concluded exactly the way such disputes are supposed to conclude: public pressure, a policy review, a change, an on-the-record statement from a government minister. That is the system working.

The Punishment That Followed Anyway

And yet, according to the Times’s reporting, within days of that resolution, elements of FBI–RCMP cooperation quietly stopped. FBI officials cancelled meetings with their Canadian counterparts and told them they would be out of touch for some time. The halt was not total — it was specific to the FBI rather than the wider U.S. intelligence community, and not every strand of FBI–RCMP collaboration was affected — but it was real enough to unsettle officials on both sides, and it lasted roughly until the anniversary of the attacks itself, on or around September 11. One official with knowledge of the episode told the Times that the Americans had sought “some sort of apology” from the Canadian side, though it remains unclear whether one was ever issued.

Read that timeline again in order. The memo existed. Patel threatened. CBC reversed course within a day. Canada’s own government publicly welcomed the reversal. And only after all of that had already happened did the FBI actually withdraw cooperation — not to compel the change, which had already occurred, but, on the available evidence, to extract something further: contrition, an apology, an acknowledgement of fault that went beyond the policy correction itself. This is not a director using leverage to fix a problem. The problem, by his own administration’s stated standard, was fixed. This is a director continuing to withhold cooperation as punishment for the fact that the offence had occurred at all — a distinction that matters enormously for understanding what kind of institution the FBI has become under his direction.

A New Register for TACO

Readers of this series know the TACO framework — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — as a way of reading Patel’s conduct in congressional hearings and press disputes. This episode forces an expansion of that framework, because it doesn’t fit any of the four categories cleanly. The Threaten stage is unmistakable: the August 27 post, amplified by the sitting U.S. ambassador, explicitly conditioning “friendship” — meaning operational cooperation between two allied law enforcement agencies — on Canadian institutions publicly rejecting a wording policy. What should have followed, once CBC accommodated the demand in full within 24 hours, was some version of stand-down. Instead, the Bureau moved to punish the ally regardless, in a mode this series has not previously had to name: not Threaten, not Accommodate, not Capitulate, not quite Obfuscate — call it Retaliate After Compliance. It is the logical end point of grievance-as-governance: a demand that cannot actually be satisfied by meeting it, because satisfying it was never really the point. The point was the demonstration of who holds power over whom.

This matters because it answers, more clearly than any hearing exchange this series has documented, the question of what actually drives Patel’s conduct. Parts VII through IX of this series have chronicled a pattern of counter-attack in place of accountability — meeting scrutiny with statistics, meeting a Republican senator’s attempted rescue with confusion, meeting a direct question about lying to Congress with a non-answer, three times. Each of those episodes could, charitably, be read as a man poorly suited to hostile questioning. This episode cannot be read that way. There was no hostile questioning here. There was a foreign public broadcaster’s internal style memo, a demand, immediate and complete compliance with that demand, and a punishment delivered anyway, weeks later, against an allied national police force that had nothing whatsoever to do with the memo in the first place. The RCMP does not run CBC’s newsroom. It answers for none of CBC’s editorial choices. It was collateral.

Why the Target Matters

This series has, until now, documented Patel’s conduct primarily in its domestic register — his treatment of Congress, of journalists, of his own agency’s career staff. Tuesday’s revelation, however, moves the pattern into a different and more consequential arena: the machinery of allied national security cooperation. The RCMP works with the FBI on terrorism and cybersecurity investigations that, by definition, protect American lives as much as Canadian ones. Cutting that cooperation, even temporarily and even partially, is not an act of institutional culture; it is an act with operational consequences for ongoing casework on both sides of the border. U.S. allies globally have watched the current administration wield tariffs and rhetoric against Canada — the “51st state” taunts, the accusations that Canada is “ripping off” the United States — and wondered whether traditional national security cooperation would eventually be treated the same way, as another lever to pull. Until this month, there was no confirmed instance of that happening. Now there is.

It is worth being precise about what did and did not happen, since the Bureau’s silence invites speculation in both directions. The FBI declined to comment to the Times. The White House did not respond to questions about whether the President or his advisers knew about the pause as it occurred. The RCMP, for its part, offered only a diplomatically minimal statement — “We value our relationship with our U.S. counterparts, as we work closely to respond to threats to national security and maintain situational awareness of emerging risks” — and referred all substantive questions back to the Bureau. Nobody with direct knowledge of the decision has explained, on the record, why cooperation was suspended after the underlying grievance had already been resolved, or what, specifically, would have satisfied whatever the Americans were actually seeking. That silence is itself a data point. An institution confident that its actions were proportionate and justified does not typically decline to explain them.

The Pattern, Completed

Set against the rest of this series, the episode completes a picture rather than complicating it. Part VII documented a director who meets questions about his own conduct with a laminated sheet of statistics. Part VIII documented a director whose commitment to a bright-line statute protecting American elections narrowed, under pressure, into deliberate ambiguity. Part IX documented a director who could not recognise a lifeline offered by a sympathetic senator of his own party, and whose one publicly stated concession — the alcohol-screening pledge — evaporated without a trace once the cameras moved on. This tenth instalment adds the clearest evidence yet that the pattern is not confined to how Patel handles being questioned. It extends to how he wields the power of his office when no one is questioning him at all — when the only audience is an allied government he has decided, for reasons that remain officially unexplained, needs to be shown who is in charge.

The Trade War Backdrop

None of this happened in isolation from the broader deterioration in U.S.–Canada relations this year. Since returning to office, President Trump has repeatedly insisted that Canada ought to become “the 51st state,” accused the country of “ripping off” the United States on trade, and imposed a series of punishing tariffs on Canadian goods. Prime Minister Mark Carney has, for the most part, absorbed these attacks with the practised patience of a leader who understands his country has more to lose from escalation than the United States does — a dynamic this series has tracked at length elsewhere. What made national security and law enforcement cooperation different, until this month, was precisely that it had appeared insulated from all of that. Tariffs are a trade lever. Rhetoric about statehood is a political provocation. The FBI’s relationship with the RCMP was, by contrast, understood on both sides of the border as a functional necessity — a channel that exists because terrorists and cybercriminals do not observe the 49th parallel, regardless of who is taunting whom in Washington. Allied security officials worldwide have spent much of the past year quietly wondering whether the Trump administration’s willingness to weaponise trade and rhetoric would eventually extend to intelligence and law enforcement sharing as well. This episode is the first confirmed instance of that line being crossed, however briefly, and it should be read by every U.S. ally, not only Canada, as a signal about what channels are and are not considered off-limits when Washington wants to make a point.

It is also worth noting what did not happen. Carney’s own government did not need convincing that September 11 was an act of terrorism; Canadian officials, this series would note, have consistently described it that way in their own communications, and Carney himself did so again in a statement marking the 25th anniversary just last week. Twenty-four Canadians died in the attacks — a fact Canadian governments of every political stripe have commemorated without prompting for a quarter-century. The dispute, in other words, was never actually about whether Canada honoured the memory of September 11. It was about the editorial independence of a single public broadcaster’s sourcing policy, a policy CBC’s own leadership corrected within a day once it drew unwanted attention. That the correction did not end the matter says everything about what the matter actually was.

Canada’s government secured the correction it wanted from CBC within a single news cycle. It did not, on the available evidence, secure an explanation from Washington for why that correction wasn’t enough.
