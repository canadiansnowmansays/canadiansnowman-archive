If the first tier of the Reversal Ledger is government by signature, the second is government by paperwork, and paperwork is where the ratchet first begins to bite.

Most of what the federal government actually requires of anyone is not in statutes. It is in regulations: the detailed rules agencies write to carry out the laws Congress passes. A statute might tell the Environmental Protection Agency to protect the public from hazardous air pollutants. The regulation says which pollutants, from which plants, at what limits, measured how, by when. Under the Administrative Procedure Act of 1946, an agency writing or repealing a significant rule must publish a proposal, invite public comment, respond to the serious comments and explain its reasoning. Then the final rule can be challenged in court.

This takes time. The Biden administration’s Title IX rule took three years and drew about 240,000 comments. A major environmental rule commonly takes two years from proposal to final text, and longer once the lawsuits begin. Twenty-three reversals in the ledger sit in this tier. None of them can be undone by a signature. Each would need a new rule, written the slow way, and in most cases defended in court against people who would rather it had never been written.

The method nobody notices

Before looking at what was rewritten, look at how much was simply abandoned.

When an industry dislikes a new rule, it sues. When the rule’s authors are still in office, they defend it. When they are not, their successors have a choice. They can defend the rule as the government’s position, which is the tradition. Or they can tell the court that they agree with the challengers, and let the judge strike it down.

Five rules in the ledger were ended that way. In January 2025, weeks before the inauguration, a federal judge in Kentucky vacated the Biden Title IX rule; the incoming administration did not appeal, and on 29 September 2026 the Education Department formally removed it, without a fresh round of public comment. A federal court in Mississippi struck down the gender-identity provisions of the Affordable Care Act’s anti-discrimination rule; the Department of Health and Human Services announced in June 2026 that it would not enforce them. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s $8 cap on credit card late fees was vacated in April 2025 with the bureau’s agreement. Its rule keeping medical debt off credit reports was vacated three months later. The Federal Trade Commission’s ban on noncompete clauses, which would have freed an estimated thirty million workers from agreements not to join rivals, was vacated in September 2025 when the commission stopped defending it, and removed from the books in February 2026.

This is the cheapest way to end a regulation. There is no public comment period, because the agency is not writing a new rule. There is no reasoned explanation to be attacked, because the court did the work. The industry that sued gets everything it asked for, and the agency gets to say that its hands were tied by a judge. For the restorer, it is a trap. A rule vacated by a court cannot simply be reissued; the new version must answer the court’s objections, and if the court’s reasoning was sweeping, there may be little room left.

What was rewritten

The rest of the second tier was undone through the full process, or is in the middle of it.

Civil rights enforcement. Executive Order 11246, which Lyndon Johnson signed in 1965 to bar discrimination by federal contractors, was revoked on the second day of the administration; on 21 August 2026 the Labor Department removed the regulations that carried it out. Six departments, among them Justice, Education and Health and Human Services, have stripped “disparate impact” provisions from their civil rights rules, ending the long-standing practice of investigating policies that are neutral on their face but fall hardest on one group. The Justice Department dismissed the consent decrees that would have reformed the police in Minneapolis and Louisville. A Transportation Department rule that took effect on 25 September 2026 ended the presumption, in place since a programme authorised under Reagan in 1983, that women- and minority-owned firms are disadvantaged when bidding for federal contracts; every firm must now prove it individually.

Housing and veterans. The duty of local governments receiving federal housing money to “affirmatively further” fair housing, written into a 2015 Obama rule, was cut in April 2025 to “any action rationally related to promoting fair housing.” The Department of Housing and Urban Development has proposed rescinding its rule on disparate impact under the Fair Housing Act. And a Veterans Affairs rule that took effect in January 2026 ended the department’s provision of abortion in cases of rape, incest or a threat to the patient’s health, leaving only an exception for the life of the mother.

The civil service. A final rule issued on 6 February 2026 created “Schedule Policy/Career,” the successor to what Trump’s first administration called Schedule F. In June about 8,000 positions were moved into it, stripping their holders of the protections against dismissal that career civil servants have had since the Pendleton Act of 1883.

Land and water. The Bureau of Land Management rescinded its 2024 rule recognising conservation as a legitimate use of public land. The Fish and Wildlife Service finalised a rule on 14 July 2026 removing habitat destruction from the definition of “harm” to an endangered species, a definition written under Gerald Ford in 1975 and upheld by the Supreme Court in 1995. The government-wide regulations implementing the National Environmental Policy Act, which Richard Nixon signed on the first day of 1970, were removed in January 2026; each agency now writes its own. The Agriculture Department has proposed rescinding the 2001 Roadless Rule, which protects 58 million acres of national forest from road-building and most logging. The comment period, extended once, closes on 6 October.

Climate and air. The 2009 finding that greenhouse gases endanger public health, the legal foundation for nearly every federal climate rule, was repealed on 12 February 2026, ending federal greenhouse gas limits for vehicles. Most power-plant carbon standards were repealed on 17 September 2026; health groups sued the same day, and more than twenty states and cities followed on 1 October. The 2024 strengthening of the mercury and air toxics standards was repealed in February. The National Climate Assessment, required by a law George H.W. Bush signed in 1990, has been abandoned: about 400 authors were dismissed in April 2025 and past reports were taken offline. Dozens of appliance efficiency standards have been postponed or withdrawn.

Money. The Corporate Transparency Act of 2021, which requires shell companies to disclose who really owns them, was passed over Trump’s veto. A Treasury rule finalised on 14 August 2026 exempted every domestic company. Only foreign-registered companies still have to report.

The clock

Here is the arithmetic a future administration faces.

Suppose a new president takes office on 20 January 2029 and makes restoring this tier a priority. The agencies need new political appointees, which takes months. The rules need to be drafted, which takes months more. A proposal might appear in late 2029. Comments close in early 2030. The agency reads and answers them, and the final rule appears late in 2030 or in 2031. Lawsuits follow at once, usually in a court chosen by the challengers. By the time the rule has survived an appeal, it may be 2032, and a presidential election is due that November.

The Biden administration’s experience shows how tight this is. Many of its most important rules were finalised only in its last year, in 2024. That left them exposed to the Congressional Review Act, which lets a new Congress strike down recent rules by simple majority, and to the incoming administration’s decision not to defend them. Rules that take most of a term to write can be gone in the first months of the next.

The ratchet runs both ways

It would be misleading to suggest that only one side reaches for the ratchet. The method is older than this administration, and the restorers have used it too.

Every outgoing administration of the last forty years has rushed rules through in its final months, a practice Washington calls “midnight regulation,” precisely to make them harder for a successor to undo. George W. Bush’s administration did it in 2008. Obama’s did it in 2016. Biden’s did it in 2024. In April 2024 the Biden Office of Personnel Management issued a rule designed specifically to make it harder for a future president to strip civil servants of their protections, a ratchet aimed at the Schedule F plan everyone expected. It held for less than two years before a new rule replaced it.

The opposite shortcut produced the opposite weakness. When the Obama Education Department issued its Title IX guidance on campus sexual harassment in 2011, it did so by letter rather than by regulation, without public comment. That made it quick to issue, and just as quick to withdraw, which the next administration did in 2017. Title IX has now been rewritten five times in fifteen years, a calibration case to set beside the Mexico City Policy: every shortcut taken to build a rule quickly is a shortcut available to the next administration to destroy it.

The court that changed the rules

There is a further complication, and it is the reason this tier is harder to restore than it would have been even five years ago.

For forty years, under a 1984 decision called Chevron, federal courts deferred to an agency’s reasonable reading of an ambiguous statute. If the Clean Air Act did not say exactly how the EPA should regulate a pollutant, the agency’s choice would usually stand. In June 2024, in Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo, the Supreme Court overruled Chevron. Courts must now decide for themselves what a statute means.

That decision cuts both ways in principle. A repeal can be struck down for misreading a statute just as a new rule can. In practice it favours the reverser, because repealing a rule usually means doing less, and doing less is rarely held to violate a statute. Restoring an ambitious rule means persuading judges, many of them appointed by the party that repealed it, that Congress clearly authorised it. Some of the rules in this tier rested on readings that Chevron would have protected and Loper Bright may not.

The endangerment finding is the clearest case of what is at stake. In 2007, in Massachusetts v. EPA, the Supreme Court held that greenhouse gases are air pollutants under the Clean Air Act and that the EPA must decide whether they endanger public health. The 2009 finding answered that they do. The 2026 repeal argues, among other things, that the Act was never meant to address global climate change. Twenty-four states and a coalition of health groups have challenged the repeal in the D.C. Circuit. If the courts uphold it on that reasoning, the repeal stops being a rule a future EPA can rewrite and becomes a legal holding that forecloses any such rule. A second-tier reversal would become a fourth-tier lock.

What the delay costs

The ledger’s last column records, for this tier, a particular kind of loss: the loss that happens in the interval, while the rule is gone and before its replacement arrives.

Harassment complaints that were not investigated under the narrower Title IX rule will not be investigated later. Habitat destroyed while destroying it no longer counted as “harm” to an endangered species stays destroyed. Projects approved without full environmental review are built. Carbon emitted by power plants freed from their limits stays in the atmosphere for centuries. A refrigerator or furnace sold under a weakened efficiency standard will run for fifteen years. Roads cut into a roadless forest are not uncut, and trees logged are not unlogged.

Sixty years of compliance work under Executive Order 11246, and the federal office that oversaw it, are gone. Rebuilding that capacity, even if the order is reissued on the first day, will take years of hiring and training. The same is true of the disparate-impact cases never brought, and of the police reforms in Minneapolis and Louisville, where a new decree would need a new investigation lasting years.

The middle of the arc

Poe’s prisoner, watching the blade descend, works out that it moves on a fixed schedule. He can count the swings and estimate when it will reach him. The second tier of the ledger offers the same grim comfort. Nothing here is permanent, but the timetable for undoing it runs longer than a presidential term, and every year on that timetable is a year in which the interval does its damage.

The lesson for anyone planning a restoration is the same as in Part I, sharpened. A rule written in a hurry at the end of a term is a rule written to be repealed. If a future administration means to rebuild this tier, it will need to start on the first day, finish within the first two years, and ground every rule in statutory language clear enough to survive judges who no longer defer. Better still, it will need Congress to put the most important of them into law.

Which is the subject of the next part, and the place where the blade descends further: the reversals that no agency can repair, because only Congress can.
