On the afternoon of Wednesday, July 22, 2026, U.S. Energy Secretary Chris Wright sat down with his Saudi counterpart, Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman, and signed what eight months earlier had been mere ceremony — a “joint declaration” issued for the cameras at Mohammed bin Salman’s White House state dinner. Now it had teeth. The two men signed a “123 agreement,” the formal instrument of American civilian nuclear cooperation, along with a companion pact on safeguards. Wright assured the public that the arrangement upheld “the highest standards of nuclear safety and nonproliferation.” He credited the president with launching an “American nuclear renaissance.”

By Thursday morning, the president himself was busy undoing it. And by Thursday evening, the rest of the world had already started drawing its own conclusions — not about Saudi Arabia, but about Washington.

A Condition, Discovered a Day Late

At some point Thursday, July 23, Donald Trump posted online that the deal his own energy secretary had signed less than twenty-four hours earlier was, in his words, entirely dependent on Saudi Arabia joining the Abraham Accords — the framework that has normalized relations between Israel and Bahrain, the UAE, Morocco, and Sudan since 2020. There had been no mention of any such condition in Wednesday’s announcement. White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt confirmed the reversal to reporters anyway, framing it as though it had been the plan all along: the president was the “final dealmaker,” she said, and had raised the Accords “on numerous occasions.” Pressed for specifics, she was unambiguous about what happens if Riyadh declines: “If they don’t join the Abraham Accords, the deal is off.”

There is a small, telling detail buried in Leavitt’s briefing: asked whether Israel had pushed Trump into adding the condition, she said, “Not to my knowledge.” Which means the single biggest complication to a deal Washington had already signed — one that could sink thirty years of nuclear cooperation and tens of billions of dollars in reactor contracts — may have originated from nowhere in particular except the president’s own head, sometime between Wednesday’s signing ceremony and Thursday’s Truth Social post.

Saudi Arabia has said for years, consistently, that it will not recognize Israel without a credible pathway to Palestinian statehood — a pathway this administration has shown no interest in building, and one that Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, who welcomed Trump’s new condition anyway, adamantly opposes. Riyadh’s Center for International Communication did not respond to requests for comment. Nobody, it seems, checked with the Saudis before the president decided to renegotiate his own administration’s paperwork in public.

Trump didn’t stop at reintroducing a dead diplomatic precondition. He also declared, flatly, that the deal contains no enrichment rights at all: “There will be no enrichment of material.” This is not what officials familiar with the actual text have said. According to reporting on the agreement’s terms, it includes a two-year study period during which American and Saudi officials will assess whether it’s economically viable for the kingdom to enrich its own uranium — the very provision nonproliferation hawks have been sounding alarms about since the deal became public.

“He’s Not Always Connected to Reality”

Here is the strategic logic underneath the whiplash, once you strip away the confusion: Trump appears to be taking a deal he already signed and turning it into a hostage. The nuclear cooperation Riyadh has wanted for five years is now framed as something Saudi Arabia only gets to keep if it delivers the one concession — recognition of Israel — that every American president since Biden has failed to extract. Whether that leverage is real is a separate question from whether Trump is trying to use it as leverage. He plainly is.

In Jerusalem, the reaction to all of this was less relief than exhaustion. Yoel Guzansky, a Saudi and nuclear proliferation expert at Israel’s Institute for National Security Studies, waved off the president’s Truth Social diplomacy entirely: “We shouldn’t look at what Trump just said; we should look at what’s in the agreement.” Of the president’s grasp on the details, Guzansky was blunter still — he’s “not always connected to reality.” Tamir Hayman, a former head of Israeli military intelligence, took the leverage theory seriously: the two-year enrichment study, he suggested, conveniently gives Trump a lever to keep pressuring the Saudis toward normalization for as long as he’s in office — which, Hayman noted, might outlast Netanyahu, who faces an election as soon as October.

Netanyahu himself stayed almost entirely silent on the substance of the nuclear deal — the one that alarmed Israeli officials when it was signed Wednesday, raising fears of a regional arms race and of Washington charting an independent course in the Gulf. He said only that the U.S.-Israeli war against Iran had “created the possibility to expand the circle of peace.” It is easier, evidently, to celebrate a hypothetical breakthrough with the Saudis than to explain why your closest ally just handed your most complicated neighbor a nuclear program you were never offered any say over.

Line up the week in order. Wednesday: the deal gets signed without the enrichment ban or the intrusive inspection regime that nonproliferation experts wanted — the same “gold standard” restrictions the UAE accepted back in 2009. Thursday: the president adds a precondition nobody announced beforehand, then contradicts his own administration’s description of what the deal actually permits. In between, Riyadh says nothing, Jerusalem says thank you without saying anything useful, and Congress — which has ninety days to review the agreement and would need a veto-proof majority in both chambers to stop it — waits to see whether the transmission of the deal even happens on schedule, since Trump could hold it up himself while he argues with the Saudis over a condition he invented.

This is not diplomacy. This is a man discovering, a news cycle late, that his own signature might need a rider attached — one he can point to later, however the story breaks, as proof he was tougher on Israel’s behalf than the deal he’d already approved actually was.

Eight months ago, in Part I of this series, Trump told a reporter that Jamal Khashoggi’s murder was something MBS “knew nothing about,” and that we could “leave it at that.” This week, his own energy secretary handed the man the CIA said ordered that murder a legal pathway to nuclear fuel — and the president spent the next morning pretending he hadn’t.

Congress Gets Its Ninety Days

None of this sat well on Capitol Hill even before Trump’s Thursday reversal muddied things further. Senator Ed Markey of Massachusetts, who has spent years trying to force congressional approval requirements onto exactly this kind of nuclear pact, warned the deal risked “igniting an arms race in the region.” Representative Gregory Meeks, the ranking Democrat on the House Foreign Affairs Committee, said it raised “deep concerns” and needed real limits on enrichment and reprocessing. Representative Brad Sherman was more pointed still: a president waging war to stop nuclear proliferation in the Middle East, he argued, has no business handing out a proliferation pathway to Riyadh as a party favor. Andrea Stricker of the Foundation for Defense of Democracies called it simply “a bad U.S. nuclear deal” Congress should block.

The math working against them is the same math that has worked against every effort to check this president: Congress would need to pass disapproval resolutions in both chambers, then assemble a veto-proof majority to make them stick. Henry Sokolski, who runs the Nonproliferation Policy Education Center, framed the deeper problem — not what Riyadh does with the deal, but what everyone watching Riyadh does next: “What new standard is the Saudi deal going to set for the UAE, Turkey, and Egypt?”

A Name From 1953

To understand why the rest of the world is watching this so closely, it helps to go back seventy-three years. In 1953, Dwight Eisenhower stood before the United Nations and offered a deal: nuclear technology and know-how, in exchange for a promise to use it peacefully. They called it Atoms for Peace. Analysts now reach for a less flattering name to describe what’s happening in 2026: nuclear hedging.

Vipin Narang, a nuclear security professor at MIT who worked on nonproliferation policy in the Pentagon under Biden, put it plainly: “We’re back in an era of nuclear hedging.” Narang expects “a cascade of states looking for the capacity to pursue” a weapons-capable program — not because every government wants a bomb, but because fewer of them trust that Washington will still be there if they don’t have one.

Saudi Arabia’s deal is unusual in ways that matter. It could eventually let the kingdom enrich its own uranium and limit which sites international inspectors are allowed to visit — concessions absent from any recent American civil nuclear agreement, and a sharp break from the 2009 pact with the UAE, in which Abu Dhabi gave up the right to produce its own fuel entirely. That arrangement became known as the nonproliferation “gold standard.” Riyadh’s deal has none of it. Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman has never hidden the strategic logic: if Iran gets a bomb, he’s said, Saudi Arabia will get one too. Gary Samore, who advised on arms control under Clinton and Obama, worries less about Riyadh specifically than about what comes next: “What’s to stop Russia from making a deal like this?” Once the United States has stopped holding the line on enrichment for one government, it has a much harder time holding it for any other — especially with rival nuclear suppliers like Russia and China happy to seize on the precedent themselves.

Seoul Asks the Obvious Question

Nowhere is the double standard sharper than in South Korea. Since the 1970s, Washington pressured Seoul to abandon its own weapons ambitions and has barred it from enriching its own reactor fuel ever since — even after South Korea joined the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty, accepted rigorous IAEA safeguards, built one of the largest civilian nuclear industries on Earth, and watched North Korea build an arsenal on its doorstep. The restriction held through a 2015 treaty revision that still required Washington’s written consent for any enrichment.

Now Washington has apparently waved that standard away for a government that has never operated a single reactor and has openly floated building a bomb. Leif-Eric Easley, an international studies professor at Ewha Womans University in Seoul, said the message from the Trump administration is that it’s “willing to make major geopolitical bargains with less emphasis on international norms” — a lesson South Korean negotiators are unlikely to forget the next time they sit across the table from Washington. South Korea’s government says only that it is watching “trends in U.S. foreign policy” closely; unofficially, the question in Seoul is a simple one — why Saudi Arabia, and not us?

Europe Stops Waiting

The hedging isn’t confined to Asia and the Gulf. Japan’s defense minister said this week that the country needs to confront the role nuclear weapons play in its own defense, despite a decades-long aversion rooted in Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Poland’s president has said his country should pursue its own program. Germany’s chancellor has joined a nuclear steering group with France.

France, already one of Western Europe’s two nuclear powers, has been the most explicit about filling the gap. President Emmanuel Macron declared in March that “the next half-century will be an age of nuclear weapons,” announcing a shift in French doctrine toward “forward deterrence” — using France’s arsenal to protect territory beyond its own borders. Paris has since sent a nuclear-capable fighter to a German airbase, a gesture of exactly the kind of extended protection the United States has offered its allies since the Cold War, and increasingly seems less inclined to guarantee.

Matthew Fuhrmann, a security studies professor at the University of Texas, thinks this is the real story hiding underneath the Saudi deal: “The best nonproliferation tool the U.S. ever had is its military alliances.” Every country hedging toward its own enrichment capability — Seoul, Tokyo, Warsaw — is a country quietly concluding that the American security umbrella isn’t as reliable as it used to be. France’s offer to backstop its neighbors may blunt some of that anxiety in Europe. It does nothing for the Gulf, where no such umbrella exists.

“The Erosion of Trust”

Suzanne Maloney, an Iran expert who directs the Foreign Policy program at the Brookings Institution, connects the Saudi deal directly to the war Washington and Israel have been waging against Iran this year. The deal, she said, is “a tangible confirmation of the erosion of trust and confidence in Washington.” Gulf states have no realistic alternative to American security guarantees, she added — but they’ve also watched the limits of those guarantees up close, and are moving to expand their own autonomy accordingly.

There’s a bitter irony buried in that logic. The war against Iran was supposed to set back Tehran’s nuclear ambitions and, in theory, make everyone else’s hedging less urgent. Instead, watching a rubble-strewn Iranian nuclear program get bombed appears to have taught the rest of the region — and the rest of the world — the opposite lesson: that the fastest way to avoid being bombed is to already have the capability nobody wants to strike.

Even He Knows

None of this required Saudi Arabia to actually build a weapon. It only required the United States to blink on the standard it spent decades enforcing — for the one government in the region that has said, out loud, exactly what it would do if its rival got there first, and whose de facto ruler the CIA still believes ordered the murder of an American resident and Washington Post columnist in 2018.

Hanan Elatr Khashoggi still doesn’t know where her husband is buried. Congress still has ninety days to act on a deal signed in confusion and amended by tweet. And experts on three continents — in Jerusalem, in Seoul, in Paris — doubt Washington will use them.

Things happen. Even he knows.
