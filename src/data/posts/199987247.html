DRAMATIS PERSONAE

Judge Kathleen M. Williams — Federal judge, Southern District of Florida. Still unimpressed. Increasingly specific about it.

Todd Blanche — Acting Attorney General. Signed the document. Said “Not me.” Still here.

Boris Epshteyn — Trump’s outside legal adviser. Former client of Blanche’s. Helped recruit Blanche into government. Then negotiated against him. Declined to comment.

Trent McCotter — Blanche’s senior deputy. Rising star. Author of the phrase “patriotic marketing gimmick.” Here in an advisory capacity he would prefer not to define.

Norman Eisen — Counsel for 35 former federal judges. Still enjoying this. More so now.

The Chorus — 35 retired federal judges. They have read the Times piece. They have underlined things.

The Trump Organization — Present in spirit. Briefly considered filing a claim against their own fund. No decision was made.

The 25-Page Memo — IRS Chief Counsel. Still never entered into evidence. Increasingly difficult to ignore.

The Treasury Department’s Top Lawyer — A Trump appointee. Resigned. Not present. His absence is noted.

Act I

The courtroom, Miami. June 12, 2026. 9:07 a.m. Seven minutes late. Counsel for the President has seventeen more pages of notes than last time.

Judge Williams has read the New York Times.

Everyone knows she has read the New York Times.

JUDGE WILLIAMS: Before we begin, I want to note for the record that since this court’s order of May 29th, the New York Times has published a detailed account of the negotiations that produced the settlement agreement now under scrutiny. I have read it. I have several questions that did not appear in my original order. We will get to those. Counsel, you were going to tell me whether this court was the victim of a fraud.

COUNSEL FOR THE PRESIDENT: Your Honour, the United States maintains that the settlement agreement was reached through a good-faith process involving —

JUDGE WILLIAMS: Who was in the room?

COUNSEL FOR THE PRESIDENT: I beg your pardon?

JUDGE WILLIAMS: Who negotiated this agreement? On the government’s side, specifically.

COUNSEL FOR THE PRESIDENT: The Acting Attorney General and senior members of the Department of Justice —

JUDGE WILLIAMS: And on the president’s side?

COUNSEL FOR THE PRESIDENT: The president’s outside counsel, including —

JUDGE WILLIAMS: Including Boris Epshteyn?

A pause.

COUNSEL FOR THE PRESIDENT: Mr. Epshteyn played a coordinating role —

JUDGE WILLIAMS: Mr. Epshteyn was a former client of the Acting Attorney General.

COUNSEL FOR THE PRESIDENT: That is —

JUDGE WILLIAMS: He helped recruit the Acting Attorney General into his current position.

COUNSEL FOR THE PRESIDENT: The Acting Attorney General’s appointment was —

JUDGE WILLIAMS: And then Mr. Epshteyn negotiated against the Acting Attorney General in a settlement that benefited the man who appointed the Acting Attorney General, audited the Acting Attorney General’s former client, and was signed by the Acting Attorney General alone.

Silence.

THE CHORUS: In our combined experience, we have presided over cases involving conflicts of interest that were described, variously, as “troubling,” “extraordinary,” “without precedent in this circuit,” and, on one occasion in the Ninth, “breathtaking in its audacity.” We are updating our vocabulary.

COUNSEL FOR THE PRESIDENT: The department’s legal analysis concluded that the arrangement was consistent with —

JUDGE WILLIAMS: Who conducted that analysis?

COUNSEL FOR THE PRESIDENT: The Office of Legal Counsel.

JUDGE WILLIAMS: Run by T. Elliot Gaiser.

COUNSEL FOR THE PRESIDENT: Correct.

JUDGE WILLIAMS: A former clerk for Justice Alito.

COUNSEL FOR THE PRESIDENT: His credentials are —

JUDGE WILLIAMS: I’m not questioning his credentials. I’m mapping the room. Carry on.

Act II

The same courtroom. Trent McCotter has been called.

He is, as described, a rising star. He would prefer to be rising somewhere else today.

NORMAN EISEN: Mr. McCotter, you proposed that the fund’s amount be set at $1.776 billion.

TRENT McCOTTER: The figure was chosen to reflect —

NORMAN EISEN: You described it, according to people familiar with the matter, as a “patriotic marketing gimmick.”

A longer pause than the ones before it.

TRENT McCOTTER: The characterisation of my internal communications —

NORMAN EISEN: Is it accurate?

TRENT McCOTTER: The fund was designed to provide meaningful compensation to Americans who had been victimised by —

NORMAN EISEN: Mr. McCotter. A marketing gimmick is a device designed to make something appear more attractive than it is. Is that a fair definition?

TRENT McCOTTER: In a commercial context —

NORMAN EISEN: We’re in a federal context. You proposed naming a taxpayer-funded compensation scheme after the year of American independence in order to make it more attractive than it was. To whom were you marketing it?

McCotter looks at his hands. They are the hands of a rising star. The trajectory is under review.

THE CHORUS: We have seen creative legal reasoning. We have seen ambitious statutory interpretation. We have seen, in one memorable case in the Fourth Circuit, a lawyer argue that the word “shall” meant “may” and the word “may” meant “shall not.” We have not seen a senior Justice Department official describe a federal compensation fund as a marketing gimmick and expect the description to stay internal.

NORMAN EISEN: The fund was modelled on Keepseagle v. Vilsack. You’re familiar with Keepseagle?

TRENT McCOTTER: The department considered it an appropriate precedent —

NORMAN EISEN: Keepseagle was overseen by a federal judge. After years of litigation. With analysis of claims and evidence. Your fund was negotiated in private, over several weeks, by lawyers loyal to the president, without judicial oversight of any kind, and was described by its architect as a marketing gimmick. In what sense is that Keepseagle?

McCotter opens his mouth. Closes it. Opens it again.

TRENT McCOTTER: The structural parallels —

NORMAN EISEN: Thank you, Mr. McCotter.

Act III

Todd Blanche returns to the stand. He did not expect Act III. Nobody told him there would be an Act III.

NORMAN EISEN: Mr. Blanche, when you appeared on CNN following publication of the settlement agreement, you were asked who designed its terms. You said there had been negotiations between outside counsel and the Justice Department. You then said, and I quote: “Not me.” Is that accurate?

TODD BLANCHE: I was describing the process by which —

NORMAN EISEN: You signed the audit immunity provision.

TODD BLANCHE: I did.

NORMAN EISEN: Alone. No one from the IRS signed it.

TODD BLANCHE: The agreement reflected a resolution of —

NORMAN EISEN: Mr. Blanche, the audit immunity provision protects the president from an IRS investigation that, according to the Times, could have cost him more than $100 million. You signed it. You said “Not me.” Which part of it was not you?

Blanche looks at Judge Williams. Judge Williams looks at Blanche. She has her glasses off again. This is becoming a habit.

THE CHORUS: In forty years on the federal bench, we developed certain reliable indicators. A witness who answers a question about a document they signed by saying “not me” is not clarifying. They are auditioning. The performance is not going well.

JUDGE WILLIAMS: Mr. Blanche, I have one further question, and I want a direct answer. The Treasury Department’s top lawyer — a Trump appointee — resigned when this agreement became public. Do you know why?

TODD BLANCHE: I can’t speak to the motivations of —

JUDGE WILLIAMS: A Trump appointee resigned rather than be associated with this arrangement. Does that give you any pause?

The longest pause of the morning.

TODD BLANCHE: Your Honour, the President of the United States —

JUDGE WILLIAMS: Yes?

Blanche says nothing. There is, it turns out, no sentence that begins “The President of the United States” that ends well in this courtroom today.

Somewhere off-stage, the Trump Organisation briefly considers filing a claim against its own fund.

No decision is made.

THE CHORUS: We are going to need more pens.

Curtain.

Or rather: recess. Fifteen minutes.

It always is.

— End of Act III —

The actual hearing begins June 12, 2026.

The script may differ. The questions will not.

* This remains a work of satirical anticipation. The “patriotic marketing gimmick” quote is real. The “Not me” is real. The Trump Organisation’s brief deliberation about filing claims against its own fund is real. The Treasury Department lawyer’s resignation is real. The author continues to regret that satire and reality have become increasingly difficult to distinguish, and notes that the difficulty is not diminishing.
