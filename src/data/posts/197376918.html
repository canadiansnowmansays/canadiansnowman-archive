By Stephen Gordon | CanadianSnowmanSays

Calvin Duncan spent years in a Louisiana prison for a crime he did not commit. While incarcerated, he taught himself the law and became what people in the system call a prison lawyer — someone who helps other inmates navigate the legal machinery that holds them. He struggled throughout his incarceration to access his own court records — records he was legally entitled to, records that might have helped him prove his innocence. He was eventually exonerated in 2021. He earned his law degree from Lewis and Clark Law School in 2023.

And then, in November 2025, the voters of Orleans Parish elected him Clerk of Criminal Court — the very office that had controlled access to the records he’d fought to obtain as a prisoner — with 68 percent of the vote. A landslide against an incumbent.

Governor Jeff Landry signed legislation eliminating the position before Duncan could take office.

The bill was sponsored by State Senator Jay Morris of West Monroe. Morris said it wasn’t personal — that he wasn’t even familiar with Duncan’s story when he filed it. Morris also rejected an amendment that would have let Duncan serve out the four-year term he’d been elected to before the office was eliminated. He said it would be better for Duncan to never start the job at all rather than serve and then be unable to run again. He said it wasn’t personal.

If it wasn’t personal, the amendment would have passed.

Morris also happens to be the legislator most aggressively advancing proposals to redraw Louisiana’s congressional map to eliminate its majority-Black districts. When State Senator Gary Carter confronted Morris about this pattern — the clerk position eliminated after a Black man won it, the congressional districts eliminated after Black voters repeatedly used them — Morris denied any racial motivation. Chairman Caleb Kleinpeter briefly paused the hearing to let the temperature drop.

The temperature did not drop.

In Louisiana, a map has never been just a map. It is a story the state tells itself about who counts. And this week, the Louisiana legislature is telling that story in the most direct terms it has used since Reconstruction: when a Black man wins an office, eliminate the office. When Black voters elect members of Congress, eliminate the districts.

The Testimony

Gary Chambers Jr. is a civil rights activist and former United States Senate candidate from Baton Rouge. He testified before the Senate and Governmental Affairs Committee on May 9th, and his testimony deserves a full accounting because it is the most direct statement made in any legislative chamber during this entire redistricting cycle.

On the state’s rankings — Chambers went through them one by one: 50th in the nation overall. 50th in crime. 46th in education. 44th in healthcare. 48th in infrastructure. 49th in the natural environment.

“But you got time to take away clerks of court seats. Your priorities are jacked up, and your state continues to lose as a result.”

On the pattern of the legislation: “You reconstructionists. And it’s racist. And every one of your votes reflects your moral character and the lack thereof.”

On the redistricting itself: “If you are taking seats from Black people, which we are entitled to by the Constitution of this country, then you are a thief in my opinion. And I’m entitled to that opinion.”

On faith: “I go to church just like you all. I don’t know if we read the same Bible. Do good. Seek justice. Help the widow, the orphan, the oppressed, and the poor. Isaiah 1:17.”

On the cowardice beneath the procedural language: “You come with these white supremacist tactics because you have the numbers, but you don’t have the courage. Because if you were really visionary leaders, you’d run against these Black men with fair maps.”

On the economic indictment: “Georgia beats you every year. Why? Because they don’t have a problem with Black people prospering.”

On the Calvin Duncan situation specifically — the collision of the redistricting and the clerk elimination: “The government is a job farm. You all are opening up prisons in rural parishes to create jobs for your constituents and trying to take away Calvin Duncan’s job in New Orleans where he creates jobs for Black constituents.”

He closed: “This is weak, self-serving and cowardice. And you’re reconstructionists. And history will remember you.”

The committee did not vote that day. It adjourned. The redistricting continues.

The Four Who Stood Together

Since Reconstruction, Louisiana has sent exactly four Black members to the United States Congress.

That same day, all four stood together in the Louisiana State Capitol to testify against the proposed maps that would eliminate the districts that sent them there.

Cleo Fields. Troy Carter. Bill Jefferson. Cedric Richmond.

Fields, whose district is at the centre of the Callais ruling, released a statement: “Since Reconstruction, Louisiana has sent only four Black members to the United States Congress. This morning, the four of us stood together in the Louisiana State Capitol to make clear that the voices of Black Louisianans must be heard, respected, and protected.”

The image of those four men in that room is the series in a single frame. Everything this series has been examining — the 1992 bargain, the three-step dismantling of the VRA, the cascade of redistricting that followed Callais, the Georgia comparison, the pipeline that connects staff positions to congressional seats to generational political leadership — is present in the fact that there are only four of them. In a state where Black residents have comprised between a third and half the population for the entirety of its history. Four.

The committee is expected to vote on new maps before the legislative session ends June 1. The maps under consideration would eliminate one or both majority-Black districts. Senator Ed Price, a Democrat, has proposed a map preserving both. All four Black congressmen testified in support of Price’s proposal. The Republican supermajority will decide the outcome.

What Landry Said on 60 Minutes

La. Gov. Jeff Landry

Governor Jeff Landry appeared on CBS’s 60 Minutes last week to discuss the redistricting and the suspension of Louisiana’s House primaries — a suspension that came after 45,000 absentee ballots had already been cast.

CBS correspondent Cecilia Vega pressed him: “As we sit here right now, more than 45,000 ballots have been returned. What happens to those?”

“Those ballots are discarded and those voters will vote again in November,” Landry said.

When Vega observed that he was saying this as if it were not a big deal, Landry was unapologetic. “It’s not my fault. If anybody has a grievance, take it to the United States Supreme Court.”

It’s not my fault. Take it to the Supreme Court.

The Supreme Court that handed down Callais. The Supreme Court that, on the same day Virginia filed its emergency application seeking to restore a voter-approved map, cleared the path for Alabama to eliminate its second majority-Black district. The Supreme Court that rejected Virginia’s appeal on May 15th in a one-sentence order — no vote count, no reasoning, no noted dissents. The Supreme Court that Landry is directing 45,000 Louisiana voters to address their grievances to is the same Supreme Court that created the legal environment in which their ballots were discarded.

Landry also said: “You cannot say that we are all created equal and that states must treat everyone equal under the law and then allow a law to sort people based upon race.” He said he wants Louisiana to be “finally unshackled from the decades of litigation.”

Representative Troy Carter, a Black Democrat whose district may be eliminated, took particular offence to Landry’s choice of the word “unshackled.” He did not explain his objection in the 60 Minutes segment. He did not need to.

What Happened on Saturday

On Saturday — the same day Louisiana held its Senate primary under the shadow of the redistricting suspension — Senator Bill Cassidy finished third in his own Republican primary with roughly 25 percent of the vote. He is the first elected incumbent senator to lose renomination since Richard Lugar in 2012. Trump-endorsed Representative Julia Letlow led with 45 percent; State Treasurer John Fleming followed at 28. They will meet in a June 27 runoff. Cassidy’s offence was voting to convict Trump after January 6th. His concession speech was the most direct rebuke of Trump’s retribution politics any departing Republican has delivered: “Our country is not about one individual. It is about the welfare of all Americans, and it is about our Constitution. And if someone doesn’t understand that and attempts to control others through using the levers of power, they are about serving themselves.”

In one week in Louisiana, the maps were redrawn, the ballots were discarded, the office was eliminated, and the senator who voted his conscience was removed. The state is being reorganised from top to bottom.

Alabama: The Door Cleared

On May 11th, the Supreme Court cleared the path for Alabama to use a new voting map — the map the state drew in 2023 when ordered by a federal court to create a second majority-Black district, and then refused to use when the court rejected it as insufficient.

That map, which would return Alabama to a single majority-Black district, now goes back to the lower court for reconsideration under the Callais standard. The practical outcome is nearly certain: the lower court will apply the new standard, find the map permissible, and Alabama will eliminate the district that elected Shomari Figures in 2024.

Figures is forty years old. He is a former congressional aide and White House staffer. He said in an earlier interview that “none of us working on Capitol Hill would have gotten there without that foot in the door.” The foot in the door was the district. The Supreme Court has now cleared the path to close it.

Figures was the second Black congressman Alabama had sent to Washington. He is likely to be its last, for the foreseeable future.

The dissent from Justice Sonia Sotomayor, joined by Kagan and Jackson, accused the majority of acting “without any sound basis” and “without regard for the confusion that will surely ensue.” The majority issued its order in a single paragraph. The lower court will do the rest.

The Map Has Always Been the Story

The Louisiana Illuminator published an essay last week that contains the most precise description of what this series has been trying to document across twenty-eight instalments.

“In Louisiana, a map has never been just a map. It is a story the state tells itself about who counts. Before it was paper, it was the bill of sale. Before it was a precinct, it was a parish boundary that decided whose body produced the next generation of laborers.”

The map has always been the story. The redistricting war that began in Texas last summer, that consumed California and Virginia and Tennessee and Louisiana and Alabama and South Carolina, that produced Callais and the Virginia Supreme Court ruling and the emergency applications and the court-packing proposals and the yard signs and the food pantry and the four Black congressmen standing together in Baton Rouge — all of it is the same story the map has always told.

The question is who gets to be in it.

Gary Chambers named what the legislature was doing in the clearest terms anyone has used this entire cycle: reconstructionists. The reconstruction of a racial order that the Voting Rights Act interrupted. Not the post-Civil War Reconstruction that briefly expanded Black political power — that is the one the state’s Republicans are reversing. The other kind. The restoration. The one that followed Reconstruction and lasted until 1965 and is now, with the Supreme Court’s help, being reassembled piece by piece, map by map, district by district, office by office.

Calvin Duncan won an office. They eliminated the office.

Shomari Figures won a district. The Supreme Court cleared the path to eliminate the district.

Four Black men have represented Louisiana in Congress since Reconstruction. They stood together in the Capitol and asked to keep their seats. The Republican supermajority will vote before June 1.

The View From Here

I have been watching this from Toronto since May 15, 2025. One year. Twenty-eight instalments of this specific series. The redistricting war has moved faster and gone further than I expected when I began tracking it.

The New York Times reported on May 17th that fewer than a tenth of the 435 House seats up in November are likely to be competitive — and that the number has been shrinking with every map redrawn. Florida went from fourteen competitive districts to one. Texas went from two to zero.

The thing Gary Chambers said that I keep returning to is the simplest: “If you were really visionary leaders, you’d run against these Black men with fair maps.”

He is right. The maps are being redrawn because Republicans have concluded they cannot win these districts with fair maps. That is the admission hidden inside every procedural justification, every colorblind argument, every invocation of partisan rather than racial motivation. If you could win with fair maps, you would use fair maps. You are not using fair maps.

Calvin Duncan won his office with fair rules. They changed the rules.

Shomari Figures won his district under a court-ordered map. The court order is being reversed.

The four Black congressmen from Louisiana won their seats under the Voting Rights Act’s protections. The protections are being removed.

Democracy fights back. In Baton Rouge, it fought back in a Senate hearing room on May 9th, in the voice of a man who taught himself the law in a prison cell and watched an elected office get eliminated to prevent him from holding it. It fought back in four men standing together to say their voices must be heard.

The committee adjourned without a vote.

On May 13th — four days after Chambers told the Louisiana committee that “Georgia beats you every year” because Georgia doesn’t “have a problem with Black people prospering” — Governor Brian Kemp called a special session of the Georgia legislature for June 17 to redraw the state’s congressional and legislative maps. Not for 2026. For 2028. Kemp’s calculus is different from Landry’s or Alabama’s — he is not racing to change maps before this November’s midterms, where early voting for next week’s primaries has already begun. He is racing to change them before Republicans potentially lose the power to draw them at all. The target is the Second Congressional District, represented by Sanford Bishop since 1993 — the only Democratic-held seat in the state outside Greater Atlanta. The current map gives Republicans nine seats to Democrats’ five. The next map is intended to make that arithmetic permanent, while the legislature still can.

Chambers was right about Georgia on May 9th. By May 13th, it no longer mattered.

The redistricting continues — but not everywhere, and not without resistance that deserves naming.

On May 12th, South Carolina’s Senate voted to halt its redistricting effort, with five Republican defectors blocking the return to session. The man who led them was Shane Massey, the Republican Senate leader — and what he said on the floor deserves more than a passing sentence.

Massey spoke for roughly 45 minutes from prepared remarks. Trump had called him personally the week before — the president’s first direct conversation with him — to ask if South Carolina could help sweep the state’s congressional delegation. “Look, I hope you can help us out,” Massey recalled the president saying.

Massey voted no anyway.

He did not pretend the vote was costless. He acknowledged explicitly what had happened in Indiana — where Republican legislators who refused Trump’s redistricting demands lost their primaries to Trump-backed challengers just weeks earlier. “That’s a possibility, if not a likelihood,” he said of his own political fate, “and I’m comfortable with that, because my conscience is clear.”

His speech was not a defence of Democrats or a repudiation of partisan redistricting in principle — he was clear that he had drawn lines to favour Republicans in 2021 and had no qualms about it. His argument was different and more fundamental. “Too many people in power just want to do whatever it takes to stay in power,” he said. “They’ll do whatever it takes to keep it, but I ask to what end? What do you do with it when you’ve attained it?”

He bristled at the outside pressure: “I cannot in good conscience surrender this authority that has been preserved to, for and by the states and merely take orders from those who are not in South Carolina.”

He closed with Benjamin Franklin’s observation after the Constitutional Convention of 1787 — that what had emerged from their efforts was “a republic, if you can keep it” — and applied it directly to the moment.

“Maybe we become convinced that the only way to preserve the republic is to implement policies that are contrary to the founding ideas of the republic,” Massey said. “Maybe we turn on ourselves. Maybe 250 years in, maybe we can’t keep it.”

But if that was going to happen, he said, “by God, it’s not going to be because I surrendered it. I’m voting no.”

Clyburn — who stood alongside three fellow Black congressmen in Baton Rouge to testify against Louisiana’s maps — will keep his seat for this cycle. The four who stood together won one of the battles they were fighting. And in South Carolina, a Republican Senate leader invoked the Founders to hold the line against a president of his own party.

That same day, the Missouri Supreme Court upheld the Republican congressional map drawn last year to flip an additional House seat. The cascade has limits. It also has confirmed gains.

Gary Chambers named what is happening in Louisiana a reconstruction. Shane Massey, in South Carolina, named what resisting it requires: a conscience, and the willingness to accept the consequences of having one.
