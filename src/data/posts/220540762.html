There is a phrase that keeps turning up in the farewell statements of women leaving Donald Trump’s second administration. It is “the honor of a lifetime.” Kimberly Guilfoyle used it on October 13, 2026, on her way out of the American embassy in Athens. Karoline Leavitt used a version of it in August, on her way out of the White House briefing room. It is a gracious phrase, and a durable one, and in this administration it has become something close to a door-closing sound.

Nobody, so far as I can find, has had to use it on the way out of a Pentagon office, an FBI director’s suite or the Commerce Department. Those doors, in Trump’s second term, appear to open only one way.

This short series is about that asymmetry. It is not, I should say at the outset, an argument that every woman who left deserved to stay. Several of them gave their critics, me among them, ample material. It is an argument about the threshold: about how much it takes to remove a woman from Trump’s government, how much it takes to remove a man, and why those two numbers are so far apart.

We begin with the departures, in order.

March 5: Noem

The first to go was Kristi Noem, the Homeland Security Secretary and the public face of the administration’s mass-deportation campaign. NPR reported on March 5, 2026 that Trump had removed her and named Oklahoma Senator Markwayne Mullin to replace her.

The proximate cause was a Senate oversight hearing two days earlier, on March 3. Senators of both parties went after a Homeland Security advertising campaign, reported at roughly $220 million, in which Noem herself featured prominently. Noem told the committee the president had approved the ads. The president said he had not. That, as Erica L. Green reported for the New York Times on October 14, 2026, appears to have been the moment the floor gave way.

The broader context was considerably worse than an ad budget. Two American citizens, Renée Good and Alex Pretti, had been killed by federal immigration agents in Minneapolis, and Noem had faced sustained criticism for how she characterized them afterward. The Guardian also reported on her personal relationship with a senior deputy. A reasonable person could conclude she should have gone. Many reasonable people, including Republican senators, said so.

What is interesting is the manner of her going. Trump did not fire her so much as reassign her, announcing that she would become “Special Envoy for The Shield of the Americas,” a hemispheric security initiative unveiled that weekend at his Doral resort. It was a firing dressed as a promotion. I wrote about that sleight of hand in an earlier, unpublished draft, and the costume has not improved with age.

April 2: Bondi

Four weeks later it was Pam Bondi’s turn. Trump announced on April 2, 2026 that his attorney general would be leaving for an unspecified new job in the private sector, as KTLA reported, quoting the AP. Every major outlet confirmed within hours that she had been fired. Her deputy, Todd Blanche, Trump’s former personal criminal defence lawyer, became acting attorney general.

Bondi’s sins, as the White House understood them, were twofold. She had mishandled the release of the Epstein files, satisfying neither the survivors nor the president. And she had failed to deliver the convictions of Trump’s enemies that he had very publicly demanded. The prosecutions of Adam Schiff and Letitia James, among others, had gone nowhere. Trump was also, the Times reported, unhappy with how she performed on television and before Congress. A House Oversight subpoena for her testimony on the Epstein matter was scheduled for April 14.

Bondi was a loyalist of the purest kind. She had defended Trump at his first impeachment trial and campaigned for him three times. None of that bought her the soft landing Noem received. “We love Pam,” the president wrote, and showed her the door.

Her replacement was confirmed on August 7, 2026, by a vote of 50 to 49, Bloomberg Government reported. His name was Todd.

April 20: Chavez-DeRemer

Lori Chavez-DeRemer’s exit was the quietest of the four, and the most clearly deserved on the facts as reported. The Labor Secretary left on April 20, 2026. Her departure was announced not by Trump on Truth Social but by communications director Steven Cheung, who said she was leaving for the private sector.

Behind the press release sat an inspector general’s investigation. As the AP reported via KTAR, the department’s inspector general was examining material showing that Chavez-DeRemer, her senior aides and members of her family had routinely sent personal messages and requests to young staff. She also faced allegations that she drank on the job and had aides plan official trips mainly for personal reasons. Several of her staff had already been placed on leave. Chavez-DeRemer denied wrongdoing and blamed “deep state actors.”

I will return to those allegations in Part II, because two of them, drinking on the job and personal travel at public expense, are going to sound very familiar.

Her deputy, Keith Sonderling, took over as acting secretary and was nominated to the post permanently on June 29. His name was Keith.

May 22: Gabbard

The fourth was Tulsi Gabbard, the director of national intelligence. She resigned on May 22, 2026, effective June 30, saying her husband, Abraham, had been diagnosed with a rare bone cancer. The AP reported that her principal deputy, Aaron Lukas, would serve as acting director.

Of the four, this is the departure I am least willing to fold neatly into the pattern, and I want to be careful with it. A spouse’s cancer diagnosis is a reason to leave any job, and Gabbard should be taken at her word about it.

It is also true that her relationship with the White House had been deteriorating for months. She had declined to tell Congress that Iran posed the imminent threat Trump had cited to justify his war. Trump had publicly described her, on March 29, as “a little bit softer on that issue.” According to reporting in the Guardian cited by Fortune, the president had been canvassing his cabinet about whether to replace her. An intelligence-community whistleblower had separately accused her of withholding intelligence for political reasons. Both things can be true at once: a family crisis and a job that was already slipping away.

Her replacement, in an acting capacity, was Aaron.

The arithmetic

So: March 5, April 2, April 20, May 22. Four cabinet-level women gone in eleven weeks. Four men in their chairs.

As Fortune put it on May 26, 2026, the women were the only cabinet members to have left, and every original man was still at the table. Trump began his second term with eight women in cabinet-level posts, by the Times’ count. Four remain: chief of staff Susie Wiles, Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins, Education Secretary Linda McMahon and Small Business Administrator Kelly Loeffler.

Kathryn Dunn Tenpas of the Brookings Institution has tracked the composition of presidential administrations since Reagan. She told the Times that she has never before seen a president’s first wave of cabinet departures consist entirely of one sex. This is not a partisan pamphleteer. This is the person whose spreadsheets everyone else borrows.

It is worth recalling what Trump himself said about this cabinet at a Women’s History Month event in 2025. He boasted that it held more women than any Republican cabinet in history. That boast, as the Times noted drily, is no longer true.

Below the cabinet line

The pattern does not stop at the cabinet table.

Elise Stefanik was Trump’s first choice for ambassador to the United Nations, nominated in November 2024. In March 2025 her nomination was withdrawn because House Republicans could not spare her vote. She stayed in Congress, waiting. Then, in May 2025, Trump gave the UN job to Mike Waltz, his national security adviser, who had just been removed from that post for adding a journalist to a Signal chat about bombing Yemen. More on Waltz in Part II.

Stefanik, adrift, announced a run for governor of New York in November 2025. On December 19, 2025, she abandoned it and said she would not seek re-election to Congress either, as the Deseret News reported. When Gabbard resigned in May, Senator Jim Banks floated Stefanik as an obvious, easily confirmed replacement. The job went, on an acting basis, to the deputy.

Then there is Guilfoyle. On October 7, 2026, the Wall Street Journal reported that, days before her July 2025 confirmation hearing, she had asked a Trump donor, Eric Deters, to pay down a $100,000 American Express bill, in a way she suggested would leave no trace. According to messages published by the Journal, she wrote to him: “Honey please I need you to get this done for me today.” That detail comes via the Honolulu Star-Advertiser. Deters says he never paid. Guilfoyle’s lawyer disputes the messages’ authenticity.

Six days later, on October 13, 2026, the new White House press secretary, Katie Zacharia, opened her first briefing. It lasted fourteen minutes and took questions from three outlets, as Hawaii News Now reported. One of those questions produced the day’s only news: the White House had asked Ambassador Guilfoyle to resign.

The following day Trump allowed that she had been doing a good job, said he had not liked what he read, and then put a little distance between himself and the woman who had been engaged to his eldest son. She was not family, he told reporters; she had, he supposed, dated his son some time ago. Guilfoyle was a Fox host, a 2020 campaign finance chair and a fixture at Mar-a-Lago. She had been, for years, about as close to family as one gets without a surname.

The press secretary who announced Guilfoyle’s exit did not last much longer. Katie Zacharia was herself out on October 16, after seven days in the job. Her story belongs to Part III.

The biggest exit came last. On November 6, 2026, three days after Republicans lost both houses of Congress, Trump announced that Susie Wiles, his chief of staff and the first woman ever to hold the post, would be replaced by Stephen Miller. The day before, Harmeet Dhillon, who ran the Justice Department's civil rights division, had resigned after clashes with the department's leaders. Wiles's case also belongs to Part III, because until November 6 she was the best evidence the administration had against this series.

What the exits have in common

Set aside the particulars and the five cases share a shape.

Each woman was removed, or eased out, within days or weeks of the problem becoming a public embarrassment. Noem went two days after a bad hearing. Guilfoyle went six days after a bad story. Bondi went a fortnight before a subpoenaed deposition. The speed is the tell. These were not careful personnel decisions taken after deliberation. They were reflexes.

Each departure was also preceded by some failure of personal loyalty, as Trump measures loyalty. Noem contradicted him under oath about the ads. Bondi failed to deliver the prosecutions he wanted. Gabbard would not give Congress his Iran line. Chavez-DeRemer embarrassed him. Guilfoyle embarrassed him more. The Times put it plainly: in many instances the women appeared to have failed some kind of loyalty test.

And in each case, a man took the chair.

None of this, on its own, proves anything about motive. A defender of the administration could say, with some justice, that each of these women had real problems. Several did. That defender could add that Trump has also removed men: the FDA commissioner, Marty Makary, was pushed out, and Joe Kent left the counterterrorism centre over the Iran war. Both are true, and both sit below cabinet rank, which is rather the point.

The question this series asks is not whether these women had problems. It is whether the men around them had problems of the same size, and what happened to those men.

That is where we go next.
