Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche testifies before the Senate Appropriations subcommittee on May 19, 2026. Senator Jack Reed called him "the president's consigliere." Blanche replied: "Your perspective is completely wrong, senator, respectfully." Tom Hagen was always polite, too.

In every great crime family, there is a moment when the personal lawyer becomes the institutional lawyer. When the man who used to get the Don out of trouble becomes the man who makes trouble disappear before it arrives. When the consigliere stops defending the family and starts running the legitimate front.

In The Godfather, that man was Tom Hagen — adopted son, Columbia Law graduate, the only person in the Corleone organization who could sit across from a United States Senator and make threats sound like favours. Hagen’s genius was not violence. It was paperwork. He made the family’s business look like business.

On May 19, 2026, Todd Blanche sat before the United States Senate Appropriations subcommittee and performed the most flawless Tom Hagen impersonation in the history of American governance. He did not need a script. He had been rehearsing for this role his entire career, though neither he nor the casting director knew it until recently.

Senator Jack Reed of Rhode Island, who apparently watches the same films I do, looked across the hearing room at the acting attorney general and said the quiet part at Senate volume:

“You’re a very gifted lawyer, but from my perspective, you have very little faith to the Constitution and the people of America, and you’re the president’s consigliere.”

“Your perspective is completely wrong, senator, respectfully,” Blanche replied.

Respectfully.

Tom Hagen was always polite, too.

Let us trace the promotion.

Eighteen months ago, Todd Blanche was a criminal defense attorney. His client was Donald J. Trump, who at the time was facing federal charges for hoarding classified documents in a bathroom at Mar-a-Lago and for his role in the January 6th attack on the Capitol. Blanche’s job was straightforward, if ambitious: keep the Don out of prison.

He succeeded — not through legal brilliance, though he is by all accounts a capable lawyer, but through the mechanism that has resolved every legal crisis in the Don’s life since 1968. Trump won the election. The cases evaporated. Jack Smith packed his boxes.

In a normal country, the defense lawyer goes home after the charges are dropped. He sends an invoice. He updates his LinkedIn. Perhaps he writes a book.

In the Corleone model, the defense lawyer gets promoted.

Trump made Blanche the acting attorney general of the United States — the nation’s chief law enforcement officer, the head of the Department of Justice, the man responsible for the impartial administration of federal law. The criminal defense lawyer for the Don became the Don’s chief enforcer, installed at the top of the very institution that had tried to hold the Don accountable.

Tom Hagen never had it this good. Tom Hagen had to pretend the family was in the olive oil business. Blanche told the United States Senate that a $1.8 billion fund to pay the president’s allies “is unusual, but it is not unprecedented.”

We’re in the olive oil business, Senator.

Here is what the consigliere accomplished in a single week.

On Sunday, May 18, the president dropped his $10 billion lawsuit against the IRS — a case so transparently a shakedown that the presiding judge had ordered both sides to explain whether an actual legal dispute existed, or whether the president was suing himself. The consigliere’s Justice Department had been preparing to settle. The judge was about to expose the arrangement. So the Don folded the hand and played another.

Minutes after the lawsuit was dismissed, the consigliere announced the creation of a $1.776 billion “Anti-Weaponization Fund” — administered by a five-member commission that Blanche would appoint, whose members the president could fire at will, with no eligibility criteria, no evidentiary standards, no compensation caps, and no court oversight.

The money would come from the Judgment Fund — a perpetual congressional appropriation requiring no additional vote from Congress. The consigliere had found the family’s chequing account, and it required no co-signer.

On Monday, the consigliere quietly posted a one-page document on the Justice Department’s website.

This is where the Tom Hagen comparison transcends metaphor and becomes operational procedure. The one-page document — signed by Blanche, released without announcement, discovered by reporters rather than presented to them — expanded the IRS settlement to include a provision barring the agency from pursuing tax claims against the president, his family, or his businesses.

Not the fund. Not the $1.776 billion. A separate, additional gift. The consigliere, having already created a slush fund for the Don’s allies, had also — almost as an afterthought, almost as a footnote — made the Don’s tax problems disappear.

The IRS is required by its own procedures to audit the president’s returns annually. Federal law prohibits the president from instructing the IRS to start or stop audits. But the law contains, as the New York Times reported, “a carve out for the attorney general.”

The attorney general. The consigliere. The man who, eighteen months ago, was defending Trump against criminal charges. That man now has the legal authority to tell the IRS not to audit his former client, and he has exercised it, in a one-page document, posted quietly on a government website, on a Monday afternoon.

Tom Hagen made problems go away through paperwork and polite conversation. Todd Blanche made the president’s tax liability go away through a one-page PDF and a broadband connection.

If Coppola had written this scene, his editor would have cut it for being too obvious.

The next morning, the consigliere went to the Senate.

The hearing was ostensibly about the Justice Department’s budget. In practice, it was about the $1.8 billion, and Blanche knew it, and everyone in the room knew he knew it, and he sat there with the composure of a man who has already signed the documents and is merely enduring the theatre.

The Democrats came armed. The consigliere parried.

“This is unusual, but it is not unprecedented,” he said of the fund, citing the Keepseagle settlement — an Obama-era class action in which Native American farmers received compensation for documented discrimination by the USDA, administered under court oversight, with defined eligibility criteria, capped payments, and independent claims review.

The comparison is instructive in the way that comparing a hospital to a chop shop is instructive. Both involve sharp instruments. The similarity ends there.

But the consigliere was not making a legal argument. He was performing one. The audience was not the senators. It was the cameras. The purpose was not to convince. It was to provide a soundbite — not unprecedented — that could be repeated on Fox News by people who would never read the Keepseagle decision and would not understand it if they did.

This is what the consigliere does. He makes the indefensible sound merely unusual.

Then came the Hagerty moment, and here the Godfather parallel required no embellishment whatsoever.

Senator Bill Hagerty, Republican of Tennessee, complained to Blanche that the special counsel, Jack Smith, had seized his telephone records during the January 6th investigation. Hagerty was not asking a question. He was presenting a grievance. He was, in the language of the Family, coming to the Don with a request on the day of the Don’s daughter’s wedding.

And the consigliere obliged.

Blanche called the seizure of lawmakers’ phone records “in some ways the worst form of abuse by the Department of Justice.” He suggested — on camera, in a Senate hearing, under oath — that Republican lawmakers whose records were seized might receive money from the fund.

Read that again.

The acting attorney general of the United States, sitting before the Senate, publicly suggested that Republican members of Congress could receive financial payments from a fund he administers, for the inconvenience of having been investigated by the Justice Department he now runs.

Tom Hagen distributed envelopes. Todd Blanche distributes them on C-SPAN.

There is one more detail, small enough to miss in the cascade of enormities, and it is the detail that matters most.

Brian Morrissey, the Treasury Department’s top lawyer, resigned on Monday — the day the fund was announced.

He said nothing publicly. Three people familiar with the matter confirmed his departure to the Times. No statement. No press conference. No dramatic exit interview. He simply left.

In every crime film, there is this character. The accountant. The company man. The person who understands exactly what the books say and cannot make himself sign them. He does not go to the press. He does not become a whistleblower. He does not testify before Congress. He just quits. He goes home. He pours a drink. He stares at the wall.

Brian Morrissey stared at the wall.

He is the man who could not be the consigliere. And his departure tells you everything about what the consigliere is being asked to do.

Vice President Vance, filling in as press secretary — a casting choice that suggests the White House communications operation is running a skeleton crew or a dark comedy, possibly both — was asked whether people convicted of assaulting police officers during the January 6th attack would be eligible for payments from the fund.

He declined to rule it out.

“I don’t rule things out categorically when I know nothing about a person’s individual circumstances,” Vance said.

This is, technically, the language of due process. It is also the language of a man who knows the answer is yes and is not yet prepared to say so on camera. The consigliere’s fund has no eligibility criteria. That is not an oversight. It is the design. When anyone can qualify, no one can be excluded, and the question of who gets paid becomes entirely a matter of the consigliere’s discretion.

The Don pardoned the January 6th rioters on his first day back in office. The consigliere will now pay them. And the vice president, when asked about it directly, offered the verbal equivalent of a shrug.

The Family takes care of its people.

In The Godfather Part II, Tom Hagen visits Frank Pentangeli in federal custody to discuss what the Romans would call an honourable exit. He does not threaten. He does not plead. He simply explains how things are. The family will take care of Frank’s people. History will remember him well. All he has to do is take a bath and open a vein.

Pentangeli understands. He does what is asked.

Todd Blanche is not asking anyone to open a vein. He is asking something far more sophisticated and far more dangerous: he is asking the institutions of American democracy to accept, quietly and politely, that the Department of Justice now operates as the Don’s personal disbursement office. That the IRS will not audit the president. That the acting attorney general will decide which of the president’s allies receive taxpayer money. That the fund requires no congressional approval, no court oversight, no eligibility standards, and no public accounting.

He is asking the Republic to take the bath.

And he is doing it respectfully.

The Enforcers Series documents, with all appropriate sardonic gravity, the ongoing transformation of American governance into something that would have seemed familiar to the Corleone family and deeply alarming to the Founders. The series will continue for as long as the material keeps presenting itself, which, at this rate, will be approximately forever.
