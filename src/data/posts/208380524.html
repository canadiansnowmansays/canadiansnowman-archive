Readers of this series may remember a photo caption from Part XIII, back in early June: Trump threatening not to let the Gordie Howe International Bridge open unless the United States was handed half ownership of a structure Canada paid for almost entirely on its own. That thread sat unresolved for weeks. This week it finally closed, and the way it closed tells you most of what you need to know about where this relationship actually stands.

I. Fourteen Years, Ninety Minutes, No Traffic

The bridge opened Friday. Ceremonially, at least — cars and trucks won’t actually cross until today, currently scheduled for noon. What should have been the culmination of more than two decades of planning and $6.4 billion Canadian in construction became, instead, a Canada-only event: a ribbon cutting and Indigenous drumming ceremony held not at the international boundary in the center of the bridge, where it was originally planned, but on a stretch of pavement at the Canada Border Services Agency’s inspection plaza, safely on the Canadian side. The joint ceremony had already been cancelled once, in June, at what reporting suggests was the Trump administration’s own request. This time, Canada did the cancelling — its infrastructure minister’s office calling a celebratory joint event “inappropriate” in light of the tariffs announced days earlier.

II. Named in a Different Country

It’s worth sitting with how much has changed since anyone agreed to call this thing the Gordie Howe International Bridge. The name was chosen in 2012, honoring the Red Wings legend as a shared symbol for two countries that, at the time, were about as close as the relationship gets — Barack Obama was broadly admired in Canadian polling, and cooperation on trade and border security was the default assumption rather than the exception. The New York Times’ own framing of the contrast is hard to improve on: Trump is, this week, “as disliked in Canada as Mr. Obama was acclaimed.” A bridge conceived as a monument to closeness opened, fourteen years later, as a monument to its absence.

III. The Moroun Shadow

The stalling wasn’t only about tariffs. Buried in the backstory is a nearly two-decade fight with Michigan’s Moroun family, who own the century-old Ambassador Bridge upstream and had every commercial incentive to delay a taxpayer-funded competitor. Michigan lawmakers refused to contribute funding, leaving Canada to pay for the whole project itself. In February, following a meeting between trucking magnate Matthew Moroun and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, Trump threatened on social media to block the bridge’s opening entirely — the threat this series first flagged back in Part XIII. The Times reports Moroun had donated $1 million to a pro-Trump super PAC the month before that meeting. None of this proves the donation bought the threat. It’s the kind of timeline this series has learned not to look away from either.

IV. The Deal That Might Be Worth Nothing

A tentative agreement reached this month gave the U.S. a share of toll revenue for fifteen years — reported as roughly 5 per cent of Canada’s construction cost, or about $320 million Canadian, according to Carney’s own estimate. Trump praised the deal. What he didn’t mention is what the bridge authority’s own financial forecasts say about it: a loss of nearly $100 million Canadian in the current year, a smaller loss the year after, and only a modest $18 million profit by 2028 — before any of Canada’s construction costs are even repaid. The American share is calculated off surplus revenue. For the foreseeable future, by the operator’s own numbers, there may not be much surplus to share.

V. What the Ceremony Actually Sounded Like

Trump couldn’t resist narrating the snub from the sidelines, posting mid-ceremony that Canada disinviting the U.S. was “fine, considering they are paying substantial TARIFFS to the United States” — and separately claiming the 2012 governing agreement “no longer stands,” a claim Canada’s infrastructure minister, Gregor Robertson, directly disputed on the spot. Windsor’s mayor, Drew Dilkens, delivered the line of the day without naming names, telling the crowd everyone knew which one person Gordie Howe might want to “check into the boards” — and letting them fill in the blank themselves. Ontario Premier Doug Ford, characteristically, tried to keep the door open anyway: “I know Canadians love Americans.” Dilkens, for his part, was blunter about what the moment actually was: “It’s regretful that this is what it’s come to. It just speaks to where we’re at with our relationship with the United States.”

VI. The Howes Were There

Whatever else got diminished this week, this part wasn’t. Gordie Howe’s youngest son, Dr. Murray Howe, attended and spoke at the ceremony — the family the bridge was actually built to honor showing up for it, even with almost everything else about the day scaled down or cancelled around them. Marty and Murray Howe have been present at this bridge’s milestones going back years, long before any of this political noise attached itself to the project; whatever Ottawa and Washington made of this week, the Howes got their moment.

VII. The Ledger

Dilkens ended his remarks with something closer to patience than grievance: he’s hopeful that someday the bridge can close for a few hours for the celebration this opening should have been, once the relationship is “a little stronger than it is today.” That’s a fair note to close this piece on too. The bridge is real. The tolls are real, if not yet profitable. The tariffs are real. And the two countries that built a bridge together in 2012 to symbolize how close they’d become couldn’t manage to stand on it together in 2026 to open it.
