IN THE COURT OF PUBLIC OPINION STATE OF TEXAS and STATE OF FLORIDA, Petitioners, v. FLOCK SAFETY, INC., Respondent

PETITION FOR DISSOLUTION OF PARTNERSHIP (Filed jointly, on grounds discovered only after several thousand cameras had already been installed)

I. PARTIES

Petitioner State of Texas, represented by Governor Greg Abbott, a man not previously known for his hesitance around law enforcement technology, hereby petitions this Court for dissolution of its arrangement with Respondent.

Petitioner State of Florida joins this action, represented by Governor Ron DeSantis, who wishes the Court to know that he has, as of this filing, discovered the concept of a “digital A.I. surveillance state,” a phrase he introduced to reporters this week with the tone of a man reporting a burst pipe rather than a policy his own administration had funded for several years.

Respondent Flock Safety, Inc. is a company that sells small black cameras on poles, and — per its own public defense — “important public safety tools,” a description it maintains even now, mid-filing, with the wounded dignity of a party who cannot quite understand what it did wrong.

II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

  1. Respondent’s product line, the license-plate reader, was adopted with enthusiasm across thousands of jurisdictions, including but not limited to every police department in Texas and Florida that could find room in its budget.

  2. Petitioners’ law enforcement partners spent this same period publicly extolling the Respondent’s product. As recently as the week before this filing, the Jacksonville Sheriff’s Office — an agency within Petitioner Florida’s jurisdiction — released a documentary-style video celebrating the cameras’ role in solving a kidnapping. One week later, the same office paused its 150-camera programme, on instruction from the very state that had, until that point, raised no objection whatsoever.

  3. This is not, the Court notes, a case of new information. In East Texas, an officer was found to have used the Flock network more than ten thousand times to track an ex-girlfriend and others — a fact Petitioner Texas now cites as grounds for its objection, despite the fact that the capability enabling that abuse was present in the product on the day it was purchased, and every day since.

  4. Petitioners’ objection, in other words, is not that the marriage changed. It is that the marriage was, this whole time, exactly what several journalists, a few judges, and one persistent veteran in Norfolk, Virginia had already pointed out that it was.

  5. This office notes, for the record, that it flagged several of these same concerns three weeks prior, in a Sunday Satire piece titled “FLOCK SAFETY HOME EDITION™,” at a time when neither Petitioner had yet noticed anything amiss. The Court is invited to draw its own conclusions about who arrived at this realization first, and why it took a sheriff’s own promotional video to get the rest of the room there.

III. GROUNDS FOR DISSOLUTION

Petitioners allege irreconcilable differences, namely:

A. Respondent’s product does exactly what it was designed to do, and this turns out to be the problem. The cameras were purchased to find stolen cars. They also, as a structural feature rather than a bug, log every other car that happens to drive past — a detail that was true in the pitch to city councils and remains true today. Petitioners are shocked, shocked, to discover that a comprehensive vehicle-tracking network comprehensively tracks vehicles.

B. Respondent’s customer base has begun to include people Petitioners did not personally authorize. Reporting has connected Flock’s data with immigration enforcement queries — a use case the Court understands was neither part of the original pitch nor, evidently, part of anyone’s due diligence before the check cleared.

C. Oversight, where it exists, relies substantially on the good judgment of whoever is logged in. This was disclosed. It was disclosed in trade press, in local news, and in at least one Substack essay this office is aware of. It was not, however, disclosed to Petitioners in a font large enough to notice until an election cycle made noticing convenient.

D. Respondent has taken the position, through its chief executive, that the entire arrangement is “an ongoing debate probably forever,” which Petitioners had previously understood to be a feature and now understand to be a warning label.

IV. AS TO PETITIONER TEXAS SPECIFICALLY

Governor Abbott’s directive instructs police departments to stop spending state money on Flock cameras — a distinction load-bearing enough to deserve its own paragraph. The Dallas Police Department, for instance, will deactivate 321 cameras purchased with state funds, while at least 300 others purchased with local funds will continue operating exactly as before. The Court finds this less a divorce than a legal separation with a very specific joint bank account still open. Representative Mitch Little, to his credit, has floated an actual ban, on the sensible-sounding theory that making law enforcement’s job easier is not, strictly, the Legislature’s job — a theory that would have applied equally well at any point over the past several years.

V. AS TO PETITIONER FLORIDA SPECIFICALLY

Governor DeSantis’s order removes the systems from state roads only, which has nonetheless prompted full shutdowns in Jacksonville and Miami-Dade County, while Polk and Orange Counties intend to keep running cameras on every road that isn’t technically a state one. Sheriff Grady Judd of Polk County offered the Court perhaps the filing’s most honest sentence, observing that “technology already knows everything about all of us,” and that government’s remaining job is simply to make sure it’s used appropriately — a sentence that is either a resolution to this whole dispute or an admission that there isn’t one, depending on how much faith the reader has left.

Sheriff Bill Prummell of Charlotte County, for his part, has filed what the Court will treat as an amicus brief in Respondent’s favour, on the theory that “criminals are jumping up and down for joy” at the state’s decision — a claim the Court cannot verify, criminals not typically being available for comment, but which it notes was not raised as a concern back when the cameras were merely spying on the law-abiding.

VI. RESPONDENT’S ANSWER

Respondent, through counsel, states that its technology remains “an important public safety tool” and that it supports “bipartisan legislation that puts strong privacy, transparency, and accountability guardrails in place” — a sentence Respondent has apparently kept on file for exactly this occasion, requiring no new drafting, since it works equally well whether the surveillance state is under fire from the left, the right, or, as here, both flanks at once.

VII. INTERVENORS

The Court notes with interest that this is not, strictly, a partisan filing. Mayor Andrew Ginther of Columbus, Ohio, a Democrat, paused his city’s programme this same week over a separate officer-misuse investigation. Los Angeles has done likewise. Americans for Prosperity’s Tennessee chapter celebrated a unanimous Knox County vote to remove the cameras entirely, in an ordinance signed by outgoing Mayor Glenn Jacobs — better known to some as the professional wrestler Kane, whose signature move, the Court will note without further comment, was traditionally delivered from behind.

Professor Barry Friedman of the NYU School of Law’s Policing Project offered the filing’s closest thing to a thesis statement, remarking that he struggles to imagine a greater affront to conservatives than the government surveilling multitudes of blameless people — which raises the obvious question of why it took several thousand cameras and one very persistent Norfolk veteran’s lawsuit for that affront to register.

VIII. EXHIBIT A — TIMELINE OF AFFECTION

The Court, for completeness, enters into evidence the following abbreviated chronology, which it invites the reader to examine for the precise moment at which anything material actually changed:

  • Recent years: Flock Safety cameras begin appearing by the tens of thousands on American roads, eventually reaching over 100,000 units feeding a single company’s database, under contract with some 7,000 law enforcement agencies. No governor objects.

  • An unspecified prior period: An East Texas officer is found to have queried the network more than ten thousand times to track an ex-girlfriend and others. No governor orders a shutdown.

  • The week before this filing: The Jacksonville Sheriff’s Office releases a documentary-style video praising its 150 cameras for helping solve a kidnapping. No governor objects.

  • Last week: Governor Abbott directs Texas police departments to stop spending state funds on the cameras.

  • Days later: Governor DeSantis’s administration orders the systems removed from Florida’s state roads, and Governor DeSantis discovers, aloud, in front of reporters, the phrase “digital A.I. surveillance state.”

  • This week: The Jacksonville Sheriff’s Office, having praised the cameras seven days earlier, pauses its programme.

The Court notes that the technology did not change across this timeline. Only the willingness to be photographed standing next to it did.

IX. RELIEF REQUESTED

Petitioners request:

  1. That this Court recognize the dissolution as sincere, notwithstanding the fact that several hundred locally funded cameras will remain running throughout the proceedings;

  2. That the record reflect Petitioners’ objection is to the visibility of the arrangement rather than its existence, the latter having been visible, to anyone checking, for years;

  3. That Respondent be permitted to keep its subscription revenue from the roughly 6,700 law enforcement agencies not currently filing for divorce; and

  4. That the Court decline to ask who, exactly, is expected to believe that a surveillance network built on 100,000 cameras and marketed for over a year as indispensable has only just this month become “out of control.”

X. CLOSING STATEMENT

The Court observes that this filing does not represent a change of heart so much as a change of headline. The cameras did not become more invasive between the Jacksonville sheriff’s kidnapping video and his pause order one week later; the cameras did exactly what they had always done, in front of everyone, the entire time. What changed was the willingness of two governors who built careers on being tough on crime to notice that the crime-fighting tool in question also happens to be a warrantless, judge-free, subscription-based ledger of where every citizen in their state has driven, and with whom, and how often — the sort of thing this office, and a great many others, had been mentioning for some time.

The Court takes no position on whether this is a genuine conversion or an election-year filing of convenience. The Court notes only that “a bit like the Middle Kingdom” was, three weeks ago, a satirical flourish, and is now, this week, a bipartisan finding of fact.

SO NOTED.
