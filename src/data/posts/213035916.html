On the afternoon of Tuesday, August 25, the White House published a document titled “President Trump Is Finally Ending Canada’s Free Ride.” It runs to twelve claims, each prefaced with the word FACT in capitals, each hyperlinked to a source. It is the administration’s fullest public statement of why the negotiations collapsed and why the tariffs are justified.

I read it, and then I read what it links to. This part is about the second thing, because a document’s citations tell you more about its authors than its assertions do. Anyone can assert. Sourcing is where the work either exists or doesn’t.

Three findings, in ascending order of importance. But first, the document itself, laid out beside the record.

I. The Chatbot

The seventh FACT concerns Canadian protectionist barriers, and asserts that they have “driven market share to foreign competitors who face none of the same restrictions.”

The phrase “driven market share” is hyperlinked. It does not link to trade data, to a Commerce Department analysis, or to an industry filing. It links to x.com/i/grok — a conversation with the chatbot on Elon Musk’s platform, preserved at conversation ID 2092265068880650465.

I want to be precise about what this is and isn’t. The link includes a text fragment pointing at a Globe and Mail article, which suggests someone asked Grok a question, Grok cited the Globe, and rather than linking the Globe directly, the drafter linked the conversation with the chatbot. That is marginally less absurd than citing an AI’s unsourced output as fact. It is still the official position of the United States government on Canadian auto market share resting on a link to a chatbot session.

There is a version of this that would be defensible: use the tool, verify what it tells you, cite the underlying source. Someone did the first step and skipped the other two. In a document published by the Executive Office of the President, about a trade war with a treaty ally, in which every other claim carries a hyperlink precisely to establish that the claims are checkable.

II. The Claim With No Source At All

Now the first FACT, which is the one that frames the entire document:

“Canada is joined only by the People’s Republic of China in choosing retaliation over negotiation.”

That is the sentence the whole argument rests on. It is the sentence that places Canada in the same category as America’s principal strategic rival, and that this series examined at length in Part XXV. It is also the only claim on the page with no citation of any kind. Every other FACT is hyperlinked, some of them three or four times over. This one is bare.

The reason may simply be that it cannot be sourced, because it isn’t true in any useful sense. Canada spent eighteen months negotiating, matched the first tariffs in March 2025, then withdrew most of its counter-tariffs in September 2025 as a good-faith gesture, retaining them only on steel, aluminum and autos — the three sectors where American tariffs continued without CUSMA exemption. Canadian negotiators were in Jamieson Greer’s offices for up to ten hours a day in the week before the collapse. Whatever else that is, it isn’t choosing retaliation over negotiation.

A drafter who believed the claim could be sourced would have sourced it. The blank space where a link should be is the document telling on itself.

II. The Number the White House Quietly Corrected

This is the important one.

On Monday, August 24, the President posted that Canada does “95% of their business with the U.S., with us, the exact opposite!” That figure is the entire basis for the accompanying assertion — “WE DON’T NEED CANADA, THEY NEED US” — because at 95 percent Canada has no alternatives and no exit, and submission becomes the only rational course. Part XXV noted that the actual figure is roughly 72 percent, and that the twenty-three-point gap is what converts a lopsided relationship into a total one.

The following day, his own White House published the eighth FACT: “Canada sends roughly three-quarters of all its goods exports to America.” The words “three-quarters” are hyperlinked to the Office of the United States Trade Representative’s own Canada country page.

So within twenty-four hours, the administration’s official document, citing the administration’s own trade agency, published a figure materially lower than the one the President had asserted. Nobody announced a correction. Nobody was going to. The fact sheet simply used the real number and moved on, and the Truth Social post remains up.

This series has spent twenty-six parts trying to be careful about the difference between what can be established and what is merely asserted. It is worth pausing on a case where the administration did the work itself, in public, and evidently expected nobody to read both documents in the same week.

IV. In Fairness

I want to be more careful than the fact sheet was, so here is the case against overstating this.

The document is not uniformly self-referential, and I have seen it described that way. It cites the BBC twice, the Wall Street Journal, Reuters, the Globe and Mail, BNN Bloomberg, the Canada Border Services Agency’s own tariff schedule, Statistics Canada, the U.S. Census Bureau, and a KPMG press release. Those are real outside sources, and several of the underlying facts are simply true. Canada’s over-quota dairy tariffs are genuinely in the range described. Eight of ten provinces did pull American liquor from government shelves. American vehicle exports to Canada did fall.

What’s wrong with the document is not that its numbers are invented. It is that the numbers are wrapped in causal stories the numbers don’t support, and that the sourcing quietly reveals which parts the drafters could stand behind and which they couldn’t.

Two examples. The dairy claim characterises Canadian tariff-rate quotas as functioning as a “near-total ban” — and cites, for that characterisation, the International Dairy Foods Association. Twice. The IDFA is the American dairy lobby. Its own statement notes that the punitive over-quota rates apply only when American exports exceed the quota negotiated under USMCA, which they generally do not. American dairy sales into Canada have grown substantially since 2016. A near-total ban that permits a growing billion-dollar trade is a strange kind of ban.

The other is the retaliation figure. The tenth FACT says Canada “just announced an additional $27.6 billion in tariffs on American businesses,” and links to Canada’s own Department of Finance page. That’s correct, and it is also the same value as the American tariffs imposed on Canadian goods four days earlier — which is what “dollar for dollar” means and what Canada said it would do. Describing a matched response as “doubling down” requires the reader not to notice that the two numbers are identical. The link the White House provides is where a reader would find that out.

V. What the Document Is Actually For

Set the sourcing aside and consider the artefact.

It is published on the day Canada released its retaliation schedule, and it is addressed to an American audience rather than a Canadian one. Its purpose is not to persuade Ottawa of anything. It is to establish, before the September 8 counter-tariffs land on American importers and before those costs reach American consumers ten weeks out from the midterms, that the resulting pain is Canada’s fault.

Read that way, the document’s weaknesses stop being errors. The uncited China comparison does work that a citation would only complicate. The lobby-sourced dairy characterisation is more useful than the quota mechanics. And a chatbot link is perfectly adequate for a claim nobody is expected to check, in a document nobody is expected to check, because the point is the twelve capitalised FACTs scrolling past on cable, not the hyperlinks underneath them.

Which is also why the three-quarters figure got through. The drafters weren’t hiding a correction. They just needed a number that would survive contact with a journalist, and the President’s number wouldn’t.

VI. Forty-Eight Hours

There is a postscript, and it arrived while I was writing this.

The fact sheet was published Tuesday afternoon. On Wednesday, Jamieson Greer sat down with CBC News and was asked about the Canadian cultural rules — the discoverability requirements that oblige streaming platforms to surface French-language films and shows for Canadian subscribers, which Carney had named on Saturday as one of the reasons he ended the talks.

Greer’s answer: the rules had been “highlighted” by the American side, but they were not a make-or-break issue. “President Trump, myself and the U.S. government recognizes the importance and sensitivity of the French language in Canada.” And then, unambiguously: “There is no way we would let a good deal go by for something like this. This is not something where we push hard or condition or red line.”

On Thursday morning, Dominic LeBlanc pocketed it. “Canada welcomes that the U.S. is now withdrawing its positions on discoverability and labeling and is confirming that measures to promote French language and Canadian culture will not be subject to future U.S. trade actions.” He added that Canada looked forward to “further constructive U.S. clarifications on their other positions, which would create the possibility of a mutually beneficial trade agreement that respects Canadian sovereignty.”

Note what that sentence does. It accepts the concession, declines to call it sufficient, and names the remaining problem — the other positions, which include the demand that Canada align its tariff policy toward third countries with Washington’s. That was always the larger sovereignty question, and it is untouched. But it is the first movement in either direction since midnight on the twenty-first, and it came from the American side.

Which brings us back to the document. On Tuesday the White House published twelve reasons Canada was in the wrong. By Wednesday the trade representative was on Canadian television saying one of the disputed items was never a red line and would not be allowed to cost anyone a deal. By Thursday Ottawa had a written American assurance to point at.

I don’t think this was coordinated, and I don’t think the fact sheet was insincere in the ordinary sense. I think it was a communications product with a communications purpose, and that the people conducting the actual negotiation were never especially constrained by it. That gap is worth naming, because a reader who took the twelve FACTS as a statement of the American position would have been wrong about the American position within thirty-six hours.

And then, on Thursday, the President signed an order renaming Lake Ontario. Half of which is in another country, and remains, for the moment, named what it has always been named on the Canadian side. Greer spends Wednesday assuring Canadians that Washington understands sensitivity; his President spends Thursday renaming their lake. The professionals and the principal are not running on the same track, and anyone trying to read this dispute has to decide, day by day, which track is the real one.

VII. The Ledger

Twenty-six parts in, and the pattern this series identified in Part XXII — that the justifications shift because the justifications were never the point — now has documentary form. There is a page on whitehouse.gov listing twelve reasons for this trade war. Its central claim is unsourced. One of its supporting claims rests on a chatbot conversation. Its most-cited authority on dairy is the American dairy lobby. And its own figure for Canadian export dependence is twenty-three points below the one the President gave the day before.

None of that means the underlying grievances are fictional. Some of them are real, and this series has said so since Part XXII. It means the document assembling them was built to be read at speed and not at all closely, which is a reasonable bet about how such documents are read, and a poor one to make in a week when both governments have asked their publics to take a great deal on faith.

It also means the fact sheet has been overtaken by its own administration, which is the most useful thing it did. A reader who wanted to know the American position on Canadian cultural policy would have got it wrong from the White House and right from a CBC interview two days later. The louder document was the less reliable one.

Canada, for its part, has published a ninety-nine page tariff schedule with every line item listed. Whatever else is unequal in this relationship, that particular asymmetry runs the other way.
