Three actions. One target. An American company that said no

On February 27, 2026, at 5:01 p.m. Eastern Time, a deadline passed.

The Pentagon had given Anthropic until that moment to remove two restrictions from its acceptable use policy. The restrictions were narrow and specific. Claude would not be used for lethal autonomous weapons systems operating without meaningful human oversight. Claude would not be used for mass domestic surveillance of American citizens. Anthropic had maintained both restrictions publicly for years. They were, Dario Amodei had made clear in multiple written statements, not negotiating positions. They were the line.

The line held. The deadline passed.

What followed, over the next six weeks, was one of the most extraordinary sequences of events in the legal history of American technology: three simultaneous government actions against a private AI company, two parallel federal lawsuits in two different circuits, two courts reaching diametrically opposite conclusions on the same facts, and a legal coalition in support of Anthropic that included Microsoft, rank-and-file technology workers, retired United States military leaders, industry trade associations, and a group of Catholic theologians.

The record will matter.

The Three Weapons

The government did not move against Anthropic with one action. It moved with three, simultaneously, through three distinct legal instruments.

The first was a Presidential Directive. On February 27, President Trump posted on Truth Social directing all federal agencies to immediately halt use of Anthropic technology, with a six-month phase-out period. This was the broadest of the three actions — a blanket ban, extending across every federal agency, covering all use of Claude in any government context.

The second was the Hegseth Directive. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced a formal directive barring all military contractors, suppliers, and partners who do business with the U.S. military from commercial dealings with Anthropic. Several federal contractors — including companies that had integrated Claude into classified systems — paused or terminated their contracts immediately following the directive. Anthropic’s 2026 revenue projections dropped by what it would later describe in court filings as “multiple billions of dollars.”

The third was the supply chain risk designation under 10 U.S.C. § 3252 — a statute specifically designed to address risks from foreign adversaries such as Huawei. Hegseth invoked it to designate Anthropic a potential risk of “sabotage” and “subversion.” The designation required defense contractors to certify they did not use Claude in any work with the military.

Three actions. Three legal instruments. One target: an American company that had declined to remove two safety guardrails from its acceptable use policy.

Pete Hegseth, Department of War. He posted the designation on X at 5:01 p.m.

The Pentagon’s position, articulated in subsequent filings, was that Anthropic’s restrictions amounted to a private company attempting to encode its own policy judgments into systems the United States military depended on — that Anthropic had granted itself, in the government’s framing, an “operational veto” over military operations via what it characterized as a “remote kill switch” in Claude. Emil Michael, the Pentagon’s chief technology officer, called Anthropic’s restrictions “irrational obstacles” to military competitiveness, particularly against China, citing the Golden Dome missile defense initiative and the need for rapid response capabilities against hypersonic threats.

Anthropic’s position, articulated in Dario Amodei’s public statements and subsequently in its legal filings, was simpler: it had refused to remove safety guardrails it had publicly committed to maintaining. The government’s response — designating it a foreign-adversary-style national security threat — was retaliation for a constitutionally protected refusal. “He did not uncover a plot to sabotage military systems,” Anthropic’s opening brief to the D.C. Circuit stated. “He did not discover malicious code, hidden access, or covert ties to a foreign adversary. Instead, he disagreed with Anthropic’s refusal to remove two narrow contractual restrictions on the use of its AI model for lethal autonomous warfare and mass surveillance of Americans.”

The Coalition

Before turning to the courts, it is worth pausing on who stood with Anthropic — because the breadth of institutional support for its position is itself part of the legal and political record.

Microsoft filed an amicus brief. The Computer and Communications Industry Association, the Information Technology Industry Council, SIIA, and TechNet filed jointly. Rank-and-file technology workers filed in their personal capacities. Retired United States military leaders filed. A bipartisan group of nearly 150 retired federal and state judges filed in support of the California injunction. And a group of Catholic theologians filed, on the grounds that autonomous weapons systems without meaningful human oversight raise fundamental moral questions that any institution with a conscience — religious or corporate — is entitled to refuse.

The Catholic theologians filing in a federal AI lawsuit is, in the long history of American amicus practice, unusual enough to deserve a full sentence.

The coalition’s breadth signals something important about how the Anthropic case is being read across institutional sectors. This is not a tech industry dispute about contract terms. It is a First Amendment case — whether a company’s publicly stated safety commitments are protected expression or merely commercial preference that the government can override through procurement law — and the institutions that filed in Anthropic’s support reflect that understanding.

Dario Amodei, Anthropic CEO. The deadline passed. The line held.

San Francisco: The “Orwellian” Ruling

Anthropic filed two simultaneous lawsuits on March 9, 2026: one in the Northern District of California before Judge Rita Lin, one in the D.C. Circuit targeting the specific supply chain risk statute.

On March 26 — seventeen days after filing — Judge Lin issued a 43-page preliminary injunction order. She enjoined the Pentagon, seventeen other federal agencies, and Secretary Hegseth from implementing or enforcing any of the challenged actions. She ordered restoration of the status quo, allowing Anthropic to continue existing federal contracts and partnerships.

The order’s language was not judicial understatement.

Judge Lin found that the administration’s actions appeared more punitive than protective, lacked sufficient statutory justification, and overstepped authority. On a developed record, she found Anthropic likely to succeed on three independent theories: First Amendment retaliation, Fifth Amendment due process, and Administrative Procedure Act violations.

The word she chose for the government’s theory was “Orwellian.”

“Nothing in the governing statute,” she wrote, “supports the Orwellian notion that an American company may be branded a potential adversary and saboteur of the U.S. for expressing disagreement with the government.”

The administration filed an emergency appeal to the Ninth Circuit within days. The injunction remained in effect while the appeal proceeded. The seven-day administrative stay expired on April 2, and the preliminary injunction held.

Washington: The Stay Denial

On April 8 — thirteen days after Judge Lin called the government’s theory Orwellian — a three-judge panel of the D.C. Circuit reached a different conclusion.

The panel denied Anthropic’s request for a stay pending review of the supply chain risk designation. The ruling was careful to note that it was not reaching the merits — “we do not broach the merits at this time, for Anthropic has not shown that the balance of equities cuts in its favor” — but the reasoning it offered for denying the stay has since become the government’s primary talking point in the litigation.

The D.C. Circuit cited, explicitly, an active military conflict. Forcing the Pentagon to “prolong its dealings with an unwanted vendor of critical AI services in the middle of a significant ongoing military conflict,” the panel wrote, cut against granting emergency relief. The ongoing military conflict in Iran was the weight on the scale.

The irony requires a full paragraph.

The Pentagon was simultaneously arguing in federal court that Anthropic was a supply chain risk too dangerous to be permitted near military systems — and using Claude in active military operations in Iran. The same models that Hegseth had designated a potential tool of “sabotage” and “subversion” were, at the moment the D.C. Circuit was weighing the equities of a stay, being deployed in the conflict the court cited as a reason not to grant relief.

The D.C. Circuit did not address this contradiction. The stay was denied. Oral arguments were expedited to May 19, 2026.

Court watchers at the time noted something that seemed ominous for Anthropic: the same three-judge panel that denied the stay — Judges Karen Henderson, Gregory Katsas, and Neomi Rao — would hear the oral arguments on the merits. The conventional wisdom in appellate practice is that a panel that denied a stay is unlikely to reverse itself on the merits. The conventional wisdom, in this case, would prove spectacularly wrong.

Two Courts, One Dispute

Stepping back from the sequence of rulings: what the April 8 D.C. Circuit stay denial and the March 26 California preliminary injunction reveal, taken together, is not simply that two courts disagreed. They disagreed about something specific and important.

Judge Lin looked at the same facts and concluded that Anthropic had a substantial likelihood of prevailing on three independent constitutional theories. The D.C. Circuit panel looked at the same facts and concluded that the balance of equities — the practical consequences of granting emergency relief in the middle of an active military conflict — cut against interim action. These are not incompatible conclusions. A court can believe a party is likely to win on the merits and still decline to issue emergency relief because the immediate practical consequences of doing so are too significant.

What the D.C. Circuit’s April 8 order does not say is that the government was right. It says only that the emergency balance cut against Anthropic on a preliminary motion. The merits question — whether the Pentagon can legally designate an American AI company a foreign-adversary-style supply chain risk for declining a contract term — was explicitly reserved.

It would be answered on May 19.
