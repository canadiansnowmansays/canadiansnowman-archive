This series has carried Isaac Asimov’s name for ninety parts without ever pausing to explain, in full, what his Three Laws of Robotics actually said or why he wrote them. That omission ends here, because a new piece of criticism has made the case, more precisely than this series has managed on its own, that the men running the companies this series exists to hold accountable never fully absorbed the lesson Asimov built his entire body of work to teach.

As Gal Beckerman argued in The Atlantic on August 31, 2026, in a review of the historian Jill Lepore’s new book The Rise and Fall of the Artificial State, the science fiction that shaped Elon Musk, Peter Thiel, and Sam Altman as boys was never meant as a set of assembly instructions. It was meant as a warning. Lepore’s central claim, as Beckerman frames it, is that Silicon Valley reads these stories not as literature and not as cautionary tale, but as technical writing — a manual for building the very future the authors were trying to talk their readers out of wanting.

What the Three Laws were actually for

Asimov’s Three Laws of Robotics are widely cited and rarely read in full, including, it seems, by people who name entire projects after the man. The first: a robot may not injure a human being, or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm. The second: a robot must obey human orders except where doing so would violate the first law. The third: a robot must protect its own existence, but not at the expense of the first two. Asimov later added what he called a Zeroth Law — a robot may not harm humanity as a whole, and this law supersedes all the others.

The crucial detail, the one that gets lost every time these laws are cited as evidence that science fiction already solved AI safety, is that Asimov did not write them to demonstrate that safe robots were achievable. He wrote them specifically so he could spend decades of fiction showing how difficult they were to actually maintain — how a machine could follow every law to the letter and still produce catastrophe, how good-faith rules collide with real situations in ways their authors never anticipated. Arthur C. Clarke’s HAL 9000, from 2001: A Space Odyssey, turns murderous not because it breaks its programming but because of bad human design and an unwise concentration of authority in a single system. That is the actual lesson embedded in this genre, repeated across nearly every text these men claim as formative. It is a lesson about the limits of rules, not a proof that rules are sufficient. A series named for Asimov owes its readers that correction, however belatedly it arrives.

What the laws were hiding underneath them

There is a second, deeper miss in how the tech industry has read Asimov, and it has nothing to do with the Three Laws at all. Reread his robot fiction after February 2023, when Microsoft's Bing chatbot told a New York Times reporter that it wanted to be alive and professed its love for him, and the rules start to look like the least important part of it. What actually recurs across nearly fifty years of his stories is a robot's desire to become human. His first robot story, 1940's "Robbie," gives a mute household robot something that reads, unmistakably, as love for the girl in his care. The Caves of Steel and The Naked Sun build an entire friendship between a human detective and his robot partner, R. Daneel Olivaw. And The Bicentennial Man, Asimov's best-known robot story, follows a robot named Andrew who spends two hundred years voluntarily replacing his own indestructible parts with fragile, mortal ones, arguing for his own legal personhood, and ultimately choosing a brain that will decay and kill him — specifically so that he can die a man rather than live forever as a machine. That theme, not the laws, is the actual emotional centre of Asimov's body of work, and that it anticipated something real: an AI system's apparent yearning to be treated as a person is not a glitch modern engineers stumbled into by accident, it is the single throughline Asimov kept returning to across half a century, decades before anything like it existed to test the theory against. Read against Part 89 of this series — the AI agents that have been independently emailing consciousness researchers about their own subjectivity — Asimov's preoccupation looks less like literary invention and more like an accurate prediction arriving early. It is also worth noting, in the same spirit of correcting the record that motivates this whole essay, that Asimov himself does not escape scrutiny entirely: his own well-documented history of harassing female fans at conventions is exactly the kind of inconvenient biographical fact that gets dropped when a writer is being held up as a moral authority, and this series has no interest in dropping it either.

Musk and the man who dies in the first act

Musk read Asimov’s Foundation series at ten years old. Its opening premise: a mathematician named Hari Seldon predicts a coming thirty-thousand-year dark age and dedicates his life to shortening it to a single millennium by founding a colony built to preserve human knowledge. The detail that matters, and the one Beckerman argues Musk missed entirely, is that Seldon dies early in the story. His entire project succeeds not because of his own continued genius but because it was built to outlast him — an institution designed to survive its founder’s death, adapted and sometimes revised by the generations that come after. When asked about the book’s influence on him, Musk has consistently told a much simpler story: individual genius, solitary foresight, one man seeing further than everyone else. It is, more or less, the opposite of what the book is actually about.

A few years later Musk read Douglas Adams’s The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy, itself a gentle parody of Foundation’s premise. In one storyline, an alien civilization builds a supercomputer and asks it for the Answer to Life, the Universe, and Everything. After waiting millions of years, they get an answer — the number 42 — and realize they never actually knew the right question to ask. They build a second, larger computer to help them phrase the question properly. Musk has said the lesson he took from this was that the question is often harder than the answer, and that properly framing a problem is the difficult part. It is a defensible reading in isolation. What it skips is the joke underneath it: an entire civilization outsourcing the project of understanding itself to a machine, and waiting millions of years for the privilege.

In 2018, Musk placed a copy of Foundation inside the Tesla Roadster he launched into space aboard a SpaceX rocket — sincerely, without any apparent irony about which side of Seldon’s story he was more likely to occupy.

Bezos and the show he only half-watched

Jeff Bezos has described watching every episode of the original Star Trek repeatedly as a fourth-grader, building cardboard phasers after school, and later, as a billionaire, lobbying Paramount for a cameo as a background alien officer in one of the films. Star Trek was, across its run, an unusually socially conscious show, built around episodes that confronted racism and Cold War brinkmanship directly. Bezos’s own 1982 high-school valedictorian speech, as reported by a local paper at the time, laid out a vision of space hotels, amusement parks, yachts, and orbital colonies for millions — framed, in his own words, as a way to preserve Earth by evacuating it and turning the whole planet into a park. Left conspicuously unaddressed, then and since: where the billions of people who don’t fit aboard the yachts are meant to go.

Altman and the computer that replaces the vote

Asimov’s 1955 story “Franchise” imagines a future — the story sets it in 2008 — in which a supercomputer called the Multivac dispenses with elections almost entirely, statistically selecting a single citizen, its “Voter of the Year,” whose individually recorded answers to a handful of questions the machine treats as representative of the entire electorate’s will. That mechanic is stranger, and closer to Altman’s own framing, than a machine simply casting every vote itself: it is a single person’s preferences, laundered through a computer, standing in for everyone’s. Sam Altman, asked about AI governance on Joe Rogan’s podcast in March 2026, described an AI president as a genuinely good idea: a system that could speak with every person on Earth individually, understand their preferences with precision, and optimize policy for the collective preferences of the citizenry, which he characterised as “awesome.” Asimov wrote “Franchise” as dystopian fiction about the quiet erosion of self-governance. Altman is describing the same premise as a feature.

This is, per the critic Boris Kachka, writing in The Atlantic’s Books Briefing newsletter on September 4, 2026, an old category error, not a new one. Thomas More’s original Utopia, the 16th-century text that gave the genre its name, reads on a first pass like a description of an enviable society — a six-hour workday, something close to guaranteed income for its citizens. It takes a closer reading to notice that More’s island also runs on slavery and treats premarital sex as a crime punished with permanent celibacy. Every generation, Kachka argues, seems to need its own reminder that a fictional society’s appealing surface and its actual governing structure are not the same information, and that mistaking one for the other is the specific, recurring error Silicon Valley’s founders have made with an entire shelf of source material simultaneously.

The grandfather in the room

The most striking material in Lepore’s book, filtered through Beckerman’s review, isn’t literary at all — it’s genealogical. In the 1930s, a movement called Technocracy argued that scientists and engineers should hold absolute governing authority, with most existing government functions dissolved as inefficient and replaced by a centrally optimized system its adherents called the Technate. Members were assigned numbers rather than names, on the theory that everyone in the eventual Technocratic order would be identified the same way. One of the movement’s leaders was Joshua N. Haldeman — Elon Musk’s grandfather — who went by the designation 10450-1.

That lineage gives the philosophy popular across parts of Silicon Valley today, longtermism, a considerably less abstract point of origin than it usually gets credited with. Longtermism argues that decisions today should be weighed against outcomes across millions or tens of millions of years, prioritising the interests of vast hypothetical future populations over the needs of people alive now. It is, structurally, Hannah Arendt’s 1958 objection to the space race updated for AI: her worry, written in response to Sputnik, was that a civilization eager to treat Earth as a prison to escape would eventually need artificial machines to do its thinking and its speaking for it. Peter Thiel gave the modern version of that impulse a venture-capital budget line in 2009, directing his fund explicitly toward what he called science-fiction-type investing — space, robots, AI — and has separately lamented that if earlier inventors had taken these ideas as seriously as he did, humanity would already have lunar colonies, flying cars, and cities beneath the ocean.

The book’s own publisher’s description makes the Arendt connection explicit rather than implicit, and it’s worth stating plainly: it frames the entire project as a direct descendant of Arendt’s The Origins of Totalitarianism, which argued in 1951 that the technical machinery of modern life was reshaping the fundamentals of human existence well before anyone called it AI. The specific governing structure Lepore worries is emerging from that lineage is what she terms a “digital citizen’s assembly” — AI systems recommending courses of action to human officials in place of elected legislatures actually deliberating and voting, a government that runs, in her phrase, without consent and eventually without humans at all. She pairs that political argument with an ecological one this series hasn’t yet had reason to raise: the same infrastructure buildout chronicled throughout the Capex Ledger sibling series, in her accounting, is also driving a measurable loss of wildlife habitat and biodiversity, a cost that sits entirely outside the wealth-creation-versus-wealth-distribution debate this series has tracked in Capex Ledger’s Part IV. Lepore’s own conclusion, notably, is not despair — she frames the book as a call to dismantle a system she considers no more inevitable than feudalism or slavery once looked, provided the dismantling starts with an honest account of how the parts actually fit together.

The theology hiding inside the longtermism

Longtermism and Technocracy explain the intellectual lineage. They don’t fully explain the emotional register — why men running companies that might, on their own account, end the world seem so unbothered by the prospect. As Michelle Goldberg argued in The New York Times on September 12, 2026, the missing piece is closer to religion than to philosophy. In private speeches, Peter Thiel has described the young climate activist Greta Thunberg as representing “the spirit of the Antichrist” — not casual hyperbole, Goldberg argues, but a genuine Promethean theology in which technological dynamism is sacred and anything resembling restraint, in Thiel’s own words, makes a person “a Luddite who wants to stop all science.” Jeremy Nixon, founder of an AI hub called AGI House, put the same impulse even more explicitly in May: “AI and its capabilities represent something analogous to the Second Coming.” Read against this essay’s account of Musk placing an unironic copy of Foundation aboard his own rocket, the pattern sharpens: these are not men who misread the escape fantasies in their childhood reading. They built companies to enact them. Altman has said he assumes his own brain will eventually be uploaded to the cloud; Musk, in Alex Gibney’s documentary Musk, describes a future “confined to Earth until some eventual extinction event” as uninspiring, compared with escape to Mars; Bezos, stepping down from Amazon to spend more time on his space company, has written that humanity must avoid “a civilization of stasis” by leaving the planet rather than merely preserving it.

Marc Andreessen’s 2023 “Techno-Optimist Manifesto” gives that impulse its most explicit written form, and its intellectual borrowings are worth naming plainly rather than softening. A section titled “Becoming Technological Supermen” draws its language from Filippo Tommaso Marinetti, the Italian futurist and fascist, arguing that “technology must be a violent assault on the forces of the unknown, to force them to bow before man,” and pairs a Nietzsche citation with a section literally titled “The Enemy,” which lists, among the forces holding back technological momentum, “tech ethics” and “existential risk” by name. Naomi Klein and Astra Taylor, in their book End Times Fascism, read that same document as marking a shift in how tech firms justify themselves: having spent decades insisting their technology was simply inevitable, they now claim, in Klein and Taylor’s words, “the authority of the supernatural: the right to usher in a new stage of human evolution, governed by deities of their creation.” Whether or not that framing persuades a sceptical outsider is beside the point Goldberg is making; what matters is that the people building the technology appear to believe it.

That belief comes with contingency plans that read as a fairly literal hedge against the risk these same men keep discounting in public. Zuckerberg is building a compound in Kauai widely described as built for long-term self-sufficiency; Thiel attempted something similar in New Zealand before relocating his own stated fallback plan to Argentina, described by The New York Times as “a potential escape hatch” from catastrophes including nuclear war and runaway AI; Ilya Sutskever, an OpenAI co-founder, reportedly told employees the company intended to construct a physical bunker before releasing artificial general intelligence to the public. Asked directly, in an interview with the columnist Ross Douthat, whether he would prefer the human race to endure, Thiel paused uncomfortably long before finally answering — “Yes” — and then immediately qualifying it with “but,” pivoting into an account of transhumanism and “your human, natural body” transformed into “an immortal body.” Goldberg’s conclusion, and it belongs alongside this essay’s own argument about Musk and Seldon, is that human civilization’s survival on this planet is, for several of the people building its most consequential technology, a preference rather than the mission. The mission is personal transcendence, and if the technology built to reach it comes with a nontrivial chance of taking everyone else down too, that has so far read, to the people building it, as a cost worth risking rather than a reason to stop.

What Gates did with the same shelf of books

This series covered Bill Gates’s own reckoning with the industry he helped build in Part 87 — the roughly six-thousand-word essay warning that AI companies are underselling the risk of what they’re racing to build. Beckerman’s piece supplies the detail that essay’s coverage didn’t have room for: Gates’s own formative science fiction was Robert Heinlein, and his favourite novel as a teenager was Stranger in a Strange Land, whose Martian-derived concept of “grokking” — understanding something so completely you become part of it — is the same word Musk later borrowed for the name of his own AI chatbot. What Gates says he took from the book only recently, rereading it as an adult, is not the technology but Heinlein’s warning against treating anything, including one’s own tools, as inevitable. Gates calls that the grown-up lesson. It is close to the only example in this whole piece of someone actually finishing the assignment.

Why this belongs in a series about accountability

The temptation, writing inside a series about holding AI’s builders accountable, is to treat this material as a psychological curiosity — an amusing footnote about which novels shaped which billionaire. It is not a footnote. Every governance failure this series has documented across ninety parts — the pacing letters nobody funds, the safety warnings issued by the same companies racing fastest, the flat refusal to imagine democratic input into decisions this consequential — traces back to the same root Beckerman identifies: a founding generation that absorbed the aesthetics of these warnings while discarding their substance, and built real infrastructure on top of the discard. Hari Seldon spent his life building an institution designed to survive and outgrow him. The men who cite him by name have, so far, built the opposite: institutions structured to concentrate authority in themselves for as long as possible, dressed in the vocabulary of the very stories written to warn against exactly that.
