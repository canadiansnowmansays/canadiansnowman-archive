By Stephen Gordon — CanadianSnowmanSays

“I was born to a single mom who worked overtime at a hotel to provide for me. And the luckiest day in both of our lives was the day we met my adoptive dad, Mark Talarico — the man who gave me his last name. Every Saturday morning, my dad would mow our lawn. And then, without anyone asking him, he would go next door and mow our neighbour’s lawn. Because she was elderly. He never talked about it. He just did it. Because that’s what a man does. Call me old-fashioned. But a man takes responsibility. He upholds his commitments to his family and his neighbours. He does what’s right even when no one is watching. Real men serve others. Weak men serve themselves.”

— James Talarico, June 4, 2026

There has been a lot of talk in this race about what makes a real man.

The Republicans started it. Within hours of Ken Paxton’s runoff victory on May 26, the attack playbook was distributed and the nicknames were deployed. “Low-T Talarico.” “Six-Gender Jimmy.” “James Talafreako.” On Fox News, White House immigration czar Stephen Miller informed viewers that Texas Democrats had bravely nominated “their first transgender Senate candidate.” Talarico is not transgender. Miller elaborated: “He’s clearly transitioning into a female. When Talarico goes in for a blood test, when he gets a physical, blood doesn’t come out.” Jesse Watters, providing what the Atlantic described as “live-action canned laughter,” noted that Talarico is “37 and not married” and had been photographed “swallowing large sticks of meat.” He quickly clarified — for the record — that Talarico is “not gay and not vegan.” The clarification did not slow the innuendo.

James Talarico credit Danielle Villasana / Getty

A Florida Republican congressional candidate named Dan Weldon diagnosed the core problem: “It’s a huge problem for the Democrat Party that you take one look at the men they run for elected office and just know that they couldn’t name a single obscure wide receiver from the early 2000s.” An unnamed commentator mused about whether Talarico wears “frilly underwear.” The conservative podcaster Allie Beth Stuckey called his theology “blasphemous.” Ben Shapiro called him a “pretend Bible teacher.” Michael Knowles said he represented “the modern, weak, sappy version of that same awful replacement of Christianity that has destroyed our civilization” and was “satanically wrong.”

As the Atlantic’s Helen Lewis observed this week, the attack campaign is “both obviously coordinated and unusually overt.” The strategy, as Democratic presidential contender Rahm Emanuel has described it, is to associate the entire left with being “weak and woke.” Not manly, in other words. Talarico’s youth, his aw-shucks niceness, his willingness to discuss the Annunciation as a story about consent — all of it reframed as the consequence of low testosterone. His concern for gender-nonconforming children repackaged as proof of freakishness. His failure to identify an obscure wide receiver from the early 2000s as disqualifying.

The Republican Party has settled on its theory of the case. The only remaining question is whether the theory survives contact with the evidence.

The Proof of Concept

Allow us to examine the alternative.

Ken Paxton, the Republican nominee, is the man Stephen Miller called “a real conservative, patriotic, God-fearing, and truly beloved statewide figure.” Lewis allows herself a single aside: “Ken Paxton? Truly beloved? Now, that’s comedy.”

The comedy has layers.

In 2018, Paxton confessed to senior staff that he had conducted an extramarital affair. In 2023, the Texas House — controlled by his own party — voted 121-23 to impeach him for corruption and bribery related to his relationship with Austin real estate developer Nate Paul. The allegations included using the powers of his office to investigate Paul’s enemies, delay foreclosures on Paul’s properties, and interfere with police investigations. In return, according to impeachment testimony, Paul paid for Paxton’s home renovations and hired Paxton’s girlfriend so the two could meet in secret in Austin. The Republican-controlled Senate acquitted him.

In July 2025, Angela Paxton — his wife of 38 years, also a state senator — filed for divorce. “Today, after 38 years of marriage, I filed for divorce on biblical grounds,” she wrote. In December 2025, the sealed divorce records were unsealed after media organizations sued, producing roughly 300 pages. Days before winning the Republican nomination in May, Paxton allowed a man who had acknowledged abusing a young boy to serve just 29 days in jail. Talarico has described this as giving “Epstein-style sweetheart deals to pedophiles.”

This is the MAGA vision of masculinity in practice. As Lewis summarizes it with surgical precision: “Real men eat barbecue, pocket unattended stationery, and cheat on their wife.” The archetype is not a patriarch. It is a perpetual adolescent — endlessly irresponsible and endlessly indulged. The more scandals accumulate, the more they become proof of virility. Paxton didn’t merely have an affair; he had his girlfriend put on the payroll. He didn’t merely face allegations; he got his own party to back down. That’s not weakness. That’s power. That’s dominance. That, according to the current Republican theory of gender, is what men do.

Talarico’s response to the “Talafreako” label was to start selling merch using the phrase. His response to the vegan accusations was to note that he has been eating barbecue “since before Ken Paxton’s first indictment.” His response to Stephen Miller’s blood test comments — delivered on a campaign stop in East Texas — was a single raised eyebrow and the reminder that his opponent once had his home renovations paid for by a man he was supposed to be investigating.

The counterpunches land because the contrast is already doing the work.

Dan Cogdell, a defense attorney for Ken Paxton, at the State Capitol in Austin during Mr. Paxton’s impeachment trial in 2023.Credit...Jordan Vonderhaar for The New York Times

There is one more data point worth noting, delivered yesterday. Dan Cogdell — the Houston lawyer who defended Paxton against the securities fraud charges, helped negotiate the settlement, and stood beside him through the entire impeachment trial — has endorsed James Talarico. “I stood behind the man for eight years,” Cogdell told the New York Times. He added, with the dry precision of a man who has spent decades in courtrooms: “As a criminal defense lawyer, I wouldn’t probably endorse the vast majority of my clients, current or former, if they were running for Senate.” Cogdell made the maximum personal contribution to Paxton’s Senate campaign last year. He has since concluded that Paxton is “just an absolute rubber stamp” for Trump and has “lost sight of his core mission, which is to represent the people of Texas.”

The man who saved Ken Paxton is now trying to stop him.

The Father and the Lawn

Here is what James Talarico chose to talk about on June 4, when the masculinity attacks had reached full velocity.

Not his voting record. Not his fundraising totals. Not his polling lead. He talked about his father.

Mark Talarico is not a politician. He is the man who fell in love with a single mother from Laredo and adopted her son and gave the boy his name. Every Saturday morning, he mowed the lawn. And then, without being asked, he walked next door and mowed the elderly widow’s lawn too. He never mentioned it. It was simply his responsibility — not just to his immediate family, but to Nidita, the neighbour.

“That just always struck me,” Talarico said in his Jamie Kern Lima interview, “as like — that’s exactly what a man, a father should be. Someone who takes responsibility. Someone who does the right thing even when no one’s watching.”

This is the counter-theory. Not a policy position. Not a theological argument. A lawn mower and a widow and a man who never talked about it.

There is a line in the Bluesky post Talarico put out on June 4 — the one that has been circulating since yesterday — that deserves to be read slowly: “A man does what’s right when no one is watching. He upholds his commitments to his family and neighbors. He doesn’t lie, cheat, and steal his way through life.” He didn’t name Paxton. He didn’t need to.

Lewis notes that Talarico “does not share the worst tic of ‘peak woke’: the kind of doctrinaire haughtiness that declares that not only are there six genders, but anyone who disagrees must be shunned as a bigot.” He has thrown his former self under the bus on several of his more linguistically adventurous statements from the 2021-2022 era — the six biological variations, the “neighbors with a uterus,” the non-meat campaign. He says now that there are two sexes, men and women, and that he “missed the mark” with language that was “cringey.” He is willing to argue his case with people outside his tribe. He went on Joe Rogan, for two hours, and Rogan — a swole, conservative man’s man who endorsed Trump in 2024 — told him: “You need to run for president. We need someone who’s actually a good person.”

The Republicans’ response to the Rogan clip was, presumably, to check whether Rogan had ever eaten a vegan meal.

The Backfire Problem

There is a structural problem with the Low-T strategy that Lewis identifies and that the Republican operatives running it seem to have decided to ignore.

The party already underperforms with female voters. And the most senior women in the Trump administration — Tulsi Gabbard, Pam Bondi, Kristi Noem, Lori Chavez-DeRemer — have all been forced out and replaced by men. Female swing voters have televisions. They can see what the party thinks of them.

The masculinity campaign is also, at its core, a campaign about the nature of American Christianity — and that is a fight the Republicans may not win as cleanly as they assume. Talarico’s Jesus is meek and mild, a man of heart rather than ego, a barefoot rabbi who walked into the seat of power and flipped the tables of injustice. The MAGA Jesus, as Paula White-Cain illustrated at the White House Easter lunch this year, is a man who was “betrayed and arrested and falsely accused” — a familiar pattern, she told the room. The fight over Talarico’s masculinity is therefore also a fight over which Jesus is the real one, and that is a fight that is, at minimum, contested.

Stuckey calls Talarico’s theology “blasphemous.” Knowles calls him “satanically wrong.” Meanwhile, Emma Borders — a Southern Baptist from East Texas who hadn’t attended church in over a decade — saw a Talarico Easter sermon online and sobbed. She started attending St. Andrew’s, then volunteered with the homeless ministry and the food pantry. “Now you couldn’t drag me out,” she said. Jonathan Westerfield contacted the campaign to find out where Talarico was preaching; when he learned the candidate wasn’t speaking that Sunday, he came to hear the pastor behind the candidate — Jim Rigby. He brought his father, a pastor, who compared Talarico to Jimmy Carter.

The pews are filling up. The polls are tightening. And the strategy that was supposed to bury Talarico is functioning, at least in part, as his best advertising.

As Lewis observes with the understated precision that is her signature: “This is a strategy born not of manly strength, but of submissive desperation.”

What November Will Decide

Texas has not elected a Democrat to statewide office since 1994. The structural disadvantage is real. The Cook Political Report rates this seat “Leans Republican.” And yet: Paxton’s primary turnout was soft, Talarico is polling ahead of where Beto O’Rourke stood at this stage of the 2018 race, and 21% of undecided voters in a state defined by economic anxiety are sitting with a Republican nominee who is best known for corruption, a contested divorce, and sweetheart deals for child abusers.

The party chose this. Steve Bannon said so explicitly: the Paxton endorsement was as much a vote of no confidence in John Thune as it was a vote for Paxton. Trump wasn’t trying to win Texas. He was punishing the Senate. The collateral damage is Ken Paxton’s electability — and potentially, as we documented earlier in this series, the very gerrymander Republicans drew to lock in their congressional advantage for a decade.

In the meantime, James Talarico keeps telling the story of his father and the lawn.

A man mows his neighbour’s lawn without being asked. He doesn’t announce it. He doesn’t post about it. He just does it because it is his responsibility — not just to his family, but to an elderly widow on the other side of the fence who never had to wonder whether anyone was watching out for her.

Real men serve others.

Weak men serve themselves.

Texas votes in November.
