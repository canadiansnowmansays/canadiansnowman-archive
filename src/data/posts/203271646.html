In automation theory, there is a word for someone whose machine makes them stronger. You put on glasses, and the lenses become part of your sight. You ride a bicycle, and the wheels become part of your legs. You play a harmonica instead of making mouth noises. You are, in the language Cory Doctorow uses (as discussed in Charlie Warzel’s Atlantic podcast), a centaur — a human and machine fused into something more capable than either alone.

There is a second word for the opposite arrangement. A reverse centaur is not enhanced by the machine. A reverse centaur is conscripted to serve it — assigned the one task the machine cannot quite do itself, and then driven at the machine’s pace rather than their own, because the capital sunk into that machine is depreciating every hour it sits idle, and somebody has to make sure it never does.

Fifty parts into this series, that distinction may be the cleanest summary available of what is actually at stake in the AI transition. Not whether the technology works. Whether the human standing next to it is being made larger, or being used up.

I. The Machine That Wants You Exhausted

Doctorow’s case study is the Amazon delivery driver, and it is worth sitting with the mechanics rather than the metaphor.

The van plans the route. The van times the stops. The driver’s job is to make the algorithm’s prediction true regardless of traffic, regardless of whether the address the van assigned them is, in physical reality, a mile from where they parked. Cameras and sensors score the driver’s eye movements, penalizing a glance toward something the system did not flag as a hazard, even when the human correctly identified a risk the model missed. Investigative reporting has documented Rivian delivery vans with air conditioning that shuts off automatically after the driver has been out of the seat for as little as thirty seconds — a system drivers have tried to defeat just to survive a delivery route in summer heat.

None of this is incidental to the design. It is the design. “It’s hard to overstate,” Doctorow says, “how much automation in that van is about seeing if you can get the person to work so hard that there is just no slack left for them.” The reverse centaur is not used by the machine. The reverse centaur is used up by it.

The same configuration, Doctorow argues, is coming for knowledge work, and the radiologist example makes the contrast with the alternative explicit. Imagine a hospital is offered an AI system that flags chest X-rays for a second look once or twice a day — a tool that makes radiologists slower but catches what they would otherwise miss. That pitch does not sell, because hospitals are not paying half a million dollars a year for a tool that increases their costs and their accuracy in equal measure.

The pitch that does sell is different: fire nine-tenths of the radiology staff, keep the remainder to rubber-stamp the AI’s output, and run them as close to the line of human collapse as the system allows. In that configuration — clicking “okay, okay, okay, okay” five times a minute on a model that is usually right — Doctorow’s point lands with the force of something self-evidently true: the radiologist, not the AI, will eventually kill a patient. And the system will not blame the AI. It will blame the human who failed to catch the one case in five hundred that the system already knew it would eventually get wrong.

This is automation blindness, and it has a precise mechanical explanation. TSA screeners train constantly to spot one thing — weapons — that almost never appears, because actual attempted weapon smuggling is statistically near zero. The skill of vigilance atrophies precisely because it is never exercised against the real target. A human asked to supervise an AI that is right the overwhelming majority of the time will, by the same mechanism, lose the capacity to catch the rare case where it is wrong — not through negligence, but through the predictable decay of a skill nobody is permitted to use.

II. Who It Does It To

Doctorow’s recurring formulation deserves to be stated exactly as he states it, because it compresses fifty parts of this series into nine words: “The most important thing about a gadget isn’t what it does, it’s who it does it for and what it does it to.”

This single sentence resolves something that has puzzled observers of the AI debate for two years: how two equally honest people, using the identical tool, arrive at opposite conclusions about whether it is good. One programmer reports that an AI coding assistant has made their work more enjoyable and more creative — because they control when and how to use it. Another reports that nine of their ten colleagues were fired, and their job is now to mark the surviving bot’s homework on code quality they describe as the worst they have ever shipped.

Both are telling the truth. The technology is not the variable. The configuration of power around the technology is the variable. A tool that you choose to use, on your own terms, is a centaur arrangement. A tool that has been used to eliminate your colleagues and convert your remaining labor into a supervisory function performed at machine speed is a reverse centaur arrangement. Same software. Opposite experience. The difference is entirely a function of who is in charge of the deployment decision — a question this series has been asking, in different language, since Part 1.

III. The Accountability Sink

There is a companion concept, borrowed by Doctorow from the writer Dan Davies, that explains a specific and underappreciated harm: the accountability sink.

An accountability sink is any system, person, or process that can be blamed for a foreseeable mistake so that no one with actual power has to be. Doctorow’s case study is almost too perfect to need elaboration. A man flying to his grandmother’s funeral asked Air Canada’s customer service chatbot how a bereavement fare worked. The bot told him to buy a full-price ticket, attend the funeral, and submit a death certificate afterward for a partial refund. He did exactly that. Air Canada’s human staff then told him that was never the policy, that bereavement fares had to be arranged before travel, and that he was owed nothing.

He pursued the claim through every level of the airline’s appeals process and eventually won a judgment for roughly seven to eight hundred Canadian dollars — a multi-month ordeal for an amount that should never have been disputed in the first place. From the airline’s perspective, the chatbot’s accuracy was never the point. The bot is the accountability sink: it absorbs the blame for bad information, while the airline absorbs the savings of not staffing a competent human customer service operation, and individual passengers absorb the cost of being wrong about which entity to trust. Air Canada has, in Doctorow’s framing, one or fewer real competitors on most of its routes — captive customers do not require accountability, only the appearance of an answer.

The mechanism generalizes far beyond one airline. Any institution that can route a customer’s reasonable reliance on an AI’s stated answer into a dispute it has no obligation to honor has discovered a way to monetize plausible deniability. The chatbot did not make a mistake on Air Canada’s behalf. It made the mistake Air Canada was always going to find a way to make, with a more convincing voice and a more sympathetic interface than the one it replaced.

IV. The Bubble’s Internal Logic

This series has, since Part 48, treated the economics of the AI transition as a governance question — who is taxed, who is paid, who decides. Doctorow supplies something this series has not yet had: an explanation for why the bubble has grown larger than every technology bubble that preceded it, rooted not in hype but in the mechanics of how growth companies are valued.

A share price is a claim on future earnings. A company whose earnings are expected to keep growing trades at a high multiple of this year’s revenue, because investors are pricing in the growth still to come. The moment markets begin treating a company as mature rather than growing — once a firm has saturated its addressable market — its multiple collapses, and with it, the stock-based currency that let the company hire talent and acquire competitors cheaply. This is, in Doctorow’s account, the actual engine behind the succession of bubbles in technology over the past two decades: a company facing the terror of being re-rated as mature reaches for the next narrative that lets it claim it is still a growth story. Web3. The metaverse. Now AI.

What makes the AI bubble different in scale, on Doctorow’s account, is not greater credulity but a more genuine underlying technical achievement — a real computer science breakthrough with returns to scale that, for a period, were real: spend more, get proportionally more capability out the other end. Those returns to scale are now plateauing. The capital expenditure forecast for AI infrastructure has nonetheless climbed from roughly $700 billion to a projected $1.4 trillion in 2027, by Goldman Sachs’s own estimate — a sum Doctorow describes as making “tulip bulbs look like rational conduct” and “the South Seas look like an achievable objective.”

The unit economics, by his account, do not support the valuations. A company is profitable only if revenue from new customers and increased usage increases profit rather than diminishing it — and the repeated pattern across the industry, by his telling, is the opposite: usage-based pricing tested at cost-covering levels has met resistance described internally as customers being unable to afford even steep discounts off the actual cost of serving them. “Giving away hundred-dollar bills,” in his phrase, “will have a high demand.”

The numbers, where they have since become available, corroborate Doctorow’s account more precisely than he could when he made it. James Surowiecki, writing in The Atlantic days after the SpaceX IPO, identified the mechanism driving the record run of AI-sector public offerings: these companies are not going public to let early investors cash out, the traditional reason a tech company lists shares. They are going public because they are burning capital at a rate private markets alone can no longer sustain. OpenAI reportedly lost $38.5 billion last year, according to financial statements the blogger Ed Zitron uncovered. SpaceX’s AI business burns through roughly $1 billion a month. The pattern extends beyond the three companies filing IPOs: Alphabet announced it would sell $85 billion in new stock earmarked for AI infrastructure; Oracle moved to raise $20 billion; Meta is reportedly weighing a comparable offering. JPMorgan Chase estimates that companies will sell $1.5 trillion in shares over the next two years to fund the buildout.

Surowiecki notes that this reverses a two-decade pattern: since 2003, public companies in aggregate have returned more money to shareholders than they have raised by selling new stock. The AI capital expenditure race is now inverting that relationship industry-wide, and the historical precedent is not encouraging. As the economist Owen Lamont has put it, when firms are selling shares en masse, the rational individual response is to sell as well — a surge in stock supply this large has, historically, tended to overwhelm demand rather than reflect durable value. The volatility that hit SpaceX’s stock in the days immediately following its record debut, erasing much of the average investor’s paper gains within a week, is the bubble’s internal logic working exactly as Doctorow describes it: capital chasing a growth narrative that the underlying unit economics have not yet justified.

This is the connective tissue between the taxation debate of Part 48 and the governance failure of Part 49. An industry valued on a growth story it cannot yet support has every incentive to extract maximum labor value from the reverse centaur configuration before the music stops — and every incentive to resist the kind of “statutory process that is transparent, fair, clear, and grounded in technical facts” that Anthropic itself, per Part 49, says it wants. Growth narratives and accountable governance pull in opposite directions. The bubble needs speed. Democratic process needs time.

V. The End State of Oligarchy Is Not Stability

The essay’s final argument is also its most consequential for this series, and it arrives by way of the French economist Thomas Piketty, whose research across three centuries of capital flows established what is now treated as close to an empirical law: absent deliberate intervention, the rate of return on capital exceeds the rate of economic growth, indefinitely. Wealth concentrates not occasionally but structurally. The share of total wealth held by the top decile grows faster than the economy grows, without exception, until something breaks the pattern by force.

Piketty’s documented historical pattern is that when concentration crosses certain thresholds, the outcome is never quiet stability. It is upheaval — revolution, world war, depression. Doctorow’s application of this to the current moment is the essay’s sharpest line: “the end state of oligarchy is not stability. The end state of oligarchy is chaos.”

This reframes a question this series has asked from Part 1 in language drawn from outside the AI debate entirely. The billionaires most enthusiastic about AI’s capacity to eliminate the friction of human disagreement are not a stable ruling class consolidating a permanent advantage. They are, on this reading, accelerants toward their own eventual instability — increasingly isolated from the population whose cooperation, in Piketty’s longer view, every durable social order ultimately requires. Doctorow’s observation that Elon Musk refers to people who disagree with him as NPCs, and that a strain of effective altruism rationalized harming presently living people in service of hypothetical future trillions, is not a digression. It is a description of the psychological precondition for oligarchic rule: a genuine inability to register other people’s experience as equally real to one’s own.

The same logic, in Doctorow’s account, explains the appeal of AI to a specific kind of politician — not the boss who wants to fire workers, but the executive who wants to govern without the friction of a permanent civil service that knows how government actually functions and will say so. “This is what Trump wants to do with DOGE,” Doctorow says directly. Remove everyone who understands the machinery of government, replace them with a chatbot, and an executive’s idea becomes policy without the collision against institutional memory that a functioning bureaucracy is specifically designed to provide. A government without bureaucrats is the public-sector mirror of a workplace without workers — and Doctorow’s verdict on both is identical: it does not work, because the friction it eliminates was never the problem. The friction was the system functioning correctly.

VI. What Doctorow Does Not Take Far Enough

This series exists because Asimov’s frame — laws built before the capability exists, not improvised after the damage is done — keeps proving prescient in ways its author could not have anticipated. Doctorow’s reverse centaur framework deserves the same treatment Asimov’s Three Laws received fifty parts ago: genuine credit for naming the mechanism, alongside an acknowledgment of where the frame runs out.

Doctorow is right that the configuration of power, not the technology, determines whether an AI tool produces a centaur or a reverse centaur. He is right that bosses have been “infinitely horny for firing workers and replacing them with machines... since forever,” and that AI’s specific appeal to capital is the promise of finally eliminating the moment where an employee who knows better tells the boss their idea is dumb. He is right that the accountability sink lets institutions extract the savings of automation while routing its costs onto the people with the least power to dispute them.

What the reverse centaur framework does not by itself answer is the question this series has organized itself around: who has the standing to change the configuration? Doctorow’s prescription — puncture the bubble, regulate the way Glass-Steagall once structurally separated investment banking from commercial banking, refuse to stipulate to the AI industry’s framing of its own inevitability — is a theory of resistance. It is not yet a theory of governance. The Friday Night Order chronicled in Part 49 demonstrates that even a government nominally willing to regulate AI can do so through an opaque, unaccountable process that replicates the exact failure mode Doctorow diagnoses in corporate AI deployment: a decision made by people with power, imposed on people without it, with no transparent standard either group can appeal to.

The reverse centaur, in other words, is not only a feature of the relationship between worker and employer. It is increasingly the relationship between citizen and state wherever AI governance decisions are made by executive fiat rather than statute. The worker rubber-stamping the radiologist AI’s output and the public absorbing a Commerce Department order issued with ninety minutes’ notice are, in the structural sense Doctorow’s framework reveals, occupying the same position: conscripted to bear the costs of a system whose terms they did not set and cannot appeal.

Fifty parts in, the gap has not narrowed. It has acquired a vocabulary precise enough to describe exactly how it operates — and a historical frame, courtesy of Piketty, for how badly it tends to end when nobody closes it in time.
