Every January since 1906, Denver has hosted the National Western Stock Show. Ranchers bring their best animals to the city, judges walk the rings, and buyers take notes. It is the oldest kind of audition in the American West, and nobody pretends it is anything else.

In the second week of November 2026, the city hosted a different sort of livestock show. At least eight Democrats who might run for president in 2028 spent three days in Denver’s River North Art District at a conference titled, with admirable honesty, “What’s Next.” As Reid J. Epstein, Lisa Lerer and Laurel Rosenhall reported for The New York Times on 15 November, it was part candidate cattle call and part speed dating for potential campaign staff. The would-be candidates headlined panels, worked the donors and scouted for talent at happy hours with ambitious operatives.

Nobody has declared. The election was ten days old. But the midterms, the reporters wrote, had given Democrats licence to talk openly about who would run and what they would bring to a contest that could draw more than two dozen candidates. They took the licence and ran. Not even the nationwide ground stop of 13 November, which stranded some of the party’s brightest rising stars in the conference’s final hours, slowed them down. Between cocktails and rebooked flights, the jockeying carried on.

Who paid for the ring

Before looking at the contestants, look at the people who built the ring.

The conference was hosted by a new group called Majority Democrats, which comes from the party’s moderate wing and says it exists to foster a new generation of leaders. According to the Times, its biggest donors include the hedge-fund billionaires Stephen and Sue Mandel, the former New York mayor Michael R. Bloomberg, and James and Kathryn Murdoch. The setting was lavish. Framed Art Deco posters of the featured speakers lined the walls; one consisted only of the word “raimondo,” six times over, in lowercase blue and purple, for Gina Raimondo, the former commerce secretary. Some guests wore hats reading “Tax and Spend Liberal,” a joke that works best for people who will never be accused of it.

The reporters called it an opening salvo from the centrists in the fight over the party’s direction. That is fair, and it is not a scandal. Every faction is entitled to hold a conference, and the left will hold its own. But this series has spent much of its life on money. Part IX, “The Money and the Slogan,” worried that Democrats lacked both. Part XVI noted that the money mattered less than anyone expected in 2026: Republicans outspent Democrats across the Senate battlegrounds and lost nearly all of them. The lesson the donors in Denver seem to have drawn is not that money matters less. It is that it matters earlier. The time to shape a field is before there is one.

The contestants

Josh Shapiro, the governor of Pennsylvania, framed his ambition as a family argument. “We have got to have an ideological conversation within our family, within our party,” he told the Times. “Whatever comes in the future, I’m going to be a part of that.”

Elissa Slotkin, the Michigan senator, made the case for the biggest field possible. “There’s a lot of people who have basically been running for the past two years, right?” she said. “We should accept a big primary. I think that we should not try to whittle it down.” She said she would decide by early 2027 whether to run, and that at least a dozen potential candidates had already called her to talk about Michigan.

Cory Booker of New Jersey, who ran in 2020 and coasted to re-election this month, said he was weighing another campaign as a healer in a divided country. He spoke of “a realignment that’s happening that undermines what our algorithms tell us and what the 24-hour networks tell us.” Raphael Warnock of Georgia, senior pastor of Ebenezer Baptist Church in Atlanta, described the candidate the party needed in terms that sounded remarkably like a description of himself. “We suffer not from a poverty of resources, but a moral imagination,” he said.

Even people well off the national radar asked not to be counted out. Joe Neguse, the Colorado congressman, argued that anyone with a microphone and new ideas would find an audience. The midterms’ biggest winners took victory laps. James Talarico, the senator-elect from Texas, told the hall on Thursday that “a supermajority requires a super agenda.” Rob Sand, Iowa’s governor-elect, urged his colleagues to follow his example.

Some of the contenders did not come at all, which is its own kind of statement. JB Pritzker of Illinois and Wes Moore of Maryland sent senior aides. Mark Kelly of Arizona went to California instead, leading journalists and social-media influencers on a tour of the aircraft carrier he served on as a Navy pilot and then visiting the space shuttle Endeavour, which he once flew, at its new exhibit in Los Angeles. “I just don’t have a typical background,” he said, and allowed that this could cut either way.

Gavin Newsom of California was further away still, at a United Nations climate summit in Turkey, where he told the Turkish press that he would decide in late January whether to run. It is the most specific timeline he has offered. Before leaving, he offered a warning this series would happily endorse. “We tend to wildly over-read midterm elections in this country,” he said.

And one man took himself out of the ring. Chris Van Hollen of Maryland used his Denver appearance to rule out a presidential run, so that he could challenge Chuck Schumer for Senate majority leader instead. Part XIX took up that fight. His choice has a mirror image in the room. Slotkin, Booker, Warnock and Kelly all sit in the Senate, and Schumer's allies argue, as Jonathan Lemire reported for The Atlantic, that a caucus whose members will spend 2027 in Iowa and New Hampshire needs a veteran minding the floor.

The early states

The most revealing scenes were not on stage. One of the happy hours was for Democrats from the first six states on the party’s 2028 primary calendar, and the reporters watched senior aides to would-be candidates mingle with the people whose endorsements would soon be in demand. Don Scott, the speaker of the Virginia House of Delegates, said he had already heard from seven prospective candidates. “If you want to be president, you gotta come to the mother of presidents,” he said. “They’re going to have to touch some of that dirt in Virginia.”

That is how an American party chooses its leader. In Westminster, a party picks its leader through a vote of members, or of MPs, or some mixture of the two, and the winner becomes leader of the opposition with a shadow cabinet behind her. The candidates are known, the electorate is defined and the contest has an end date. The United States has no such office. Its opposition has no single leader until a nominee emerges from a primary season that lasts more than a year. In the meantime, the job is filled by an audition: whoever can draw a crowd, a donor or a speaker of a state house.

The contrast with Ottawa is stark. When Justin Trudeau announced on 6 January 2025 that he would step down, the Liberal Party ran a full leadership race, with debates, a membership vote and a declared winner, in nine weeks. Mark Carney won it on 9 March with almost 86 per cent of the vote and was prime minister five days later. Canadians may complain about many things in their politics, but nobody there has to watch a party audition for two years before it picks someone to oppose the government. American Democrats will spend the next fourteen months doing exactly that, and the first round of judging took place over cocktails in Denver.

David Axelrod, who ran Barack Obama’s campaigns, summed up where that leaves the party. “This is the first election I can remember where the field is wide open and there’s no front-runner,” he said. “We have a whole new cast of characters.”

Big field, small lesson

Slotkin’s case for a big primary deserves to be taken seriously, because it has history on its side as well as against it. A party fresh from a landslide has more credible candidates than it can use, and trying to clear the field for one of them would look like the kind of establishment manoeuvre the voters just rejected. The 2026 class proved that candidates no one in Washington had picked could win where no one expected.

The case against comes from the last open Democratic primary. Part XV described Project 270, the effort led by Rahna Epting and Xochitl Hinojosa to publish monthly polling on how potential Democratic candidates fare against Republicans in the battleground states. Its founders were explicit about why. In 2020, a crowded field spent a year arguing about the fine print of liberal policy and put off the general-election voters who would decide the outcome. A big primary is healthy only if it is an argument about how to win those voters, not a contest over who can best please the people in the room.

The room in Denver was a particular room. The donors were from one wing, the hats told a joke about the other, and the most eloquent warning came from a governor who was not there. The danger is not that the centrists organised first. It is that whoever organises first gets to define what the argument is about.

What a loyal opposition owes

Newsom is right that midterms get over-read. Part XV said it before the votes were counted: a wave is not a mandate. Part XVI, the audit of the series, found that the opposition won mostly because the government lost, and that the party’s own favourability had barely moved. Van Hollen put the same point in Denver: the voters fired Trump, but they have not yet hired the Democrats.

While the contenders auditioned, the majority they will run on had already gone to work. On 18 November Robert Garcia, the top Democrat on the House Oversight Committee, sent a dozen notices to members of the Trump family, the vice president and current and former officials, asking them to preserve records on targets ranging from the family’s cryptocurrency company to the handling of the Epstein files. A day earlier, Jamie Raskin, Gregory Meeks and Sydney Kamlager-Dove had told Cantor Fitzgerald, the firm Howard Lutnick once ran and his sons now run, to preserve its records on Nvidia and its business in Asia, as Michael Gold reported for The New York Times. The firm says the claims are inaccurate. The White House has already warned, as Part XVIII noted, that this kind of oversight could be treated as a crime; legal experts call that claim dubious. Garcia said the investigations would “lay the groundwork for legislation which helps all Americans.” Whoever wins the nomination in 2028 will inherit what this Congress finds, or fails to find.

Two years is not long. The new majority has to show, in that time, that it can lower prices, end a war and clean up a government, all with a president holding a veto pen. If the next two years go well, the 2028 nominee will inherit a record to run on. If they go badly, no amount of Art Deco will help. The cattle call in Denver was a reasonable thing for ambitious people to hold. It was also a little early. The voters who decided 2026 are not watching auditions. They are watching the gas price.

At the National Western, the judges are named in advance and the animals are judged on what they are. In a presidential primary, the judges are the voters of six early states, and after them the rest of the country. A loyal opposition’s job between now and then is to make sure the cattle call stays a show, and does not turn into an auction.
