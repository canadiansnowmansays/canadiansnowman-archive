Troop deployments are the most expensive form of diplomacy a country practises. A brigade in Poland costs money, but its real value is what it says: we are here, and if you come for this ally, you will meet us first. Like any message, it only works if it is consistent. A brigade that arrives, is cancelled, and is then restored by a presidential phone call says something too, though not what the sender intended.

This instalment is about the messages Pete Hegseth’s Pentagon has sent NATO’s eastern flank, and about what Republicans in Congress say they were told along the way.

The Performance

In the Quantico speech, Hegseth announced that tens of thousands of American troops now stationed overseas would come home to a vast new base, a “fortress,” somewhere in the United States. The New York Times’s Helene Cooper and John Ismay, in their September 30, 2026 report “’No Beardos, No Weirdos, No Wimps’: Hegseth Lays Out His Military Vision,” noted that he did not say which countries would lose them.

It was a striking omission for an audience of allies who have spent the past eighteen months reading every Pentagon statement for exactly that information. In the Secretary’s telling, bringing troops home is strength: fewer foreign entanglements, more American acres. The performance is the announcement. The paperwork, as the record shows, is a series of decisions taken without the people who were supposed to be consulted, and in at least one case reversed by the President within days.

The Paperwork

Romania, October 2025. The pattern began on NATO’s south-eastern edge. On October 29, 2025, the Pentagon announced it would not maintain the rotational U.S. brigade in Romania, a unit that had been a fixture of the alliance’s posture on the Black Sea since Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine.

The objection came from the Republican chairs of both Armed Services committees. In a joint statement released by Senator Roger Wicker’s office on October 29, 2025, “Chairmen of Senate and House Armed Services Committees: We Strongly Oppose the Pentagon’s Decision to Scale Back the U.S. Troop Presence in Romania,” Wicker and Representative Mike Rogers said they “strongly oppose the decision.” They complained that the decision had not been coordinated with Congress, with the combatant commanders or with NATO allies. They added that it sent the wrong signal to Russia at the very moment President Trump was pressing Moscow towards peace talks.

They also pointed to the law. The fiscal 2026 National Defense Authorization Act, passed by both chambers, required that no changes be made to the U.S. posture in Europe without a thorough review. When the two Republican chairmen of the committees that write the defence bill publicly accuse the Pentagon of ignoring it, the dispute is not partisan. It is institutional.

Poland, May 2026. Then came Poland, America’s most eager ally on the eastern flank. In May 2026, the Pentagon halted the deployment of a Fort Hood armoured brigade to Poland, a unit of about 4,000 soldiers whose nine-month rotation had already begun, with some troops already in the country.

The President reversed it. On May 21, 2026, Trump announced that 5,000 troops would deploy to Poland, citing his friendship with the Polish president, Karol Nawrocki. As John Vandiver reported for Stars and Stripes on May 22, 2026, in “Poland troop boost adds new twist to Pentagon’s Europe plans,” the announcement came with no timeline and no word on where the troops would come from, and it left the Pentagon’s own cancellation hanging in the air. NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte welcomed it. Secretary of State Marco Rubio described the wider posture review as “not a punitive thing.”

According to Wall Street Journal reporting summarised by The New Voice of Ukraine, Trump questioned Hegseth about the cancellation in a phone call, stressing the importance of good relations with Poland. The same reporting said the cancellation had surprised officials because it was Germany, not Poland, that had criticised American strategy on Iran. Trump had ordered 5,000 troops out of Germany instead. The Pentagon’s spokesman, Sean Parnell, said the President and the Secretary “communicate constantly and are in lockstep regarding U.S. troop movements in Europe.”

The lockstep took a few weeks to arrive. As Vandiver reported again for Stars and Stripes on July 7, 2026, in “Poland says US revives previously canceled troop deployment,” Poland’s defence minister, Władysław Kosiniak-Kamysz, announced that the suspended rotation was returning and that American troops would fill it in the coming weeks. The unit was likely the same Fort Hood brigade. Vandiver noted that about 5,000 soldiers and their families had faced repeated uncertainty about a nine-month deployment that was on, then off, then on again.

What Congress says it was told. The Times’s Greg Jaffe, in his September 30, 2026 analysis “Hegseth Talks Tough as Republican Doubts About His Leadership Grow,” reported that Republicans in Congress have complained that Hegseth and his staff misled or lied to them about the decisions to pull Army brigades out of Poland and Romania. Representative Don Bacon of Nebraska, a retired Air Force brigadier general who sits on the House Armed Services Committee, was blunt: “We didn’t think we were treated honestly.”

Bacon is not a habitual critic of Republican administrations. He is a hawk and a former wing commander at Offutt Air Force Base, and he has spent his career defending exactly the kind of forward presence that Hegseth has been dismantling. In September he told Time that Hegseth “would not be on my team” and had not been a very good leader.

What Poland offered. The Polish response to all this has been to offer to pay. Poland’s deputy defence minister, Paweł Zalewski, said his country would fund garrison construction to support a permanently stationed U.S. brigade. Its existing bases at Poznań, Powidz and Żagań lack the family housing and commissaries needed for permanent stationing. Warsaw has estimated the cost of a permanent facility at roughly $3 billion. By June 2026, Stars and Stripes reported, Poland was seeking up to 15,000 U.S. troops.

None of this is new. In September 2018, during Trump’s first term, Poland’s then-president Andrzej Duda offered about $2 billion towards a permanent American base and suggested it could be called “Fort Trump.” The offer was a piece of flattery so naked that it became a joke, but it was also a sincere statement of Polish strategy: Warsaw wants Americans on its soil permanently, and it will pay for the privilege. Nawrocki, Duda’s successor, has revived the idea. Eight years and two American administrations later, the base still does not exist, and the rotation that stands in for it has been cancelled and restored within a single summer.

The contrast with Germany is instructive. Germany, the host of the largest American garrison in Europe, criticised American strategy on Iran, and Trump ordered 5,000 troops out. Poland praised the President, and he ordered 5,000 troops in. Whatever one thinks of either decision, the logic is not strategic. It is personal. The eastern flank is being garrisoned according to which head of state has most recently pleased the President, and the Secretary of Defense, whose job is to give the President military advice on exactly this question, appears to learn the outcome at the same time as everyone else.

There is a sad comedy in this. On September 30, Hegseth invited all fifty American states to bid against each other for the honour of hosting a new base. Poland has been bidding for one for years, with its own money, and keeps being told it may or may not get a brigade depending on the week.

The Bill

Deterrence. Forward-deployed troops deter by being predictable. A Russian planner looking at the eastern flank in October 2025 saw a brigade leave Romania. In May 2026 he saw one cancelled in Poland. A week later he saw the President order 5,000 troops in. In July he saw the cancelled rotation revived. The cumulative signal is not American weakness. It is American inconsistency, which an adversary may find more useful, since it invites testing.

Alliance trust. Allies plan around American commitments: where to put their own brigades, what to buy, when to spend political capital at home on defence budgets. Poland now spends more than 4 percent of its GDP on defence and has made itself the model NATO ally. It has been rewarded with a deployment cancelled without warning by the Pentagon and restored by a presidential phone call. Every other eastern-flank capital has drawn its own conclusions about whose word counts in Washington.

Congressional trust. The law required a thorough review before changes to the European posture. The Republican committee chairmen say they were not consulted on Romania. Republican members say they were not dealt with honestly on Poland. Trust between a secretary and the committees that write his budget is not sentiment. It is how a $1.5 trillion request gets read in good faith rather than line by line with a sceptical pencil. Part II of this series showed what happens when that trust runs out.

The soldiers. The most direct cost falls on about 5,000 soldiers and their families at Fort Hood, who were told they were deploying for nine months, then told they weren’t, then told they were again. A military family can plan around a long deployment. What it cannot easily plan around is a deployment whose existence depends on a phone call between two men in Washington.

The law. Congress is not without tools. It wrote a requirement into the fiscal 2026 defence bill that changes to the European posture follow a thorough review, precisely because members of both parties feared decisions of this kind. The two Republican chairmen have said publicly that the requirement was not honoured on Romania. The obvious next step is a firmer statutory floor on European troop levels, of the kind Congress has written before, with funding restrictions that bite if the Pentagon ignores it. That would be an unusual thing for a Republican Congress to impose on a Republican administration. That such a step is even conceivable is a measure of how far trust has fallen.

The fortress. All of which gives the September 30 announcement a peculiar texture. Hegseth intends to bring tens of thousands of troops home to a fortress that does not yet exist, from countries he will not name, on a schedule no one has seen. If the past year is any guide, the allies will learn which of them is losing troops from a social media post. The Republican chairmen will learn from the allies. And the President may, or may not, reverse it on the phone.

From Canada, a country that has stationed its own battle group in Latvia since 2017 and has just committed to making it a brigade, the American pattern is hard to watch. The point of standing on the eastern flank is that you stay standing there, whoever is defence minister. It is not complicated. It is just inconvenient for a Secretary who would rather announce than consult.
