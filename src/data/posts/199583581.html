The ruins of Mohenjo-daro, Indus Valley Civilization (~2500 BCE). One of the world’s earliest planned cities with advanced urban infrastructure.

The first mistake the West makes about India is assuming it began when the British arrived.

The second mistake is assuming it began with the Mughals. The third is assuming it began with Alexander. India did not begin with any of them. India was ancient when all of them were young. When the Roman Republic was still figuring out plumbing, the subcontinent had already built cities with drainage systems, produced mathematical concepts the world would not rediscover for a millennium, and generated philosophical traditions that still quietly underpin how a billion people think about duty, time, and the relationship between the individual and the cosmos.

If you want to understand why Indian democracy works — or more precisely, why it works when it shouldn’t — you have to start here. Not with institutions, not with constitutions, not with elections. With the civilization that made all of those things both possible and perpetually unstable.

I. The Cities That Shouldn’t Exist

Sometime around 3300 BCE — roughly contemporary with the earliest Sumerian settlements in Mesopotamia and a full eight centuries before the Great Pyramid at Giza — a civilization began to coalesce along the Indus River and its tributaries in what is now Pakistan and northwest India. We call it the Indus Valley Civilization, or sometimes the Harappan civilization, after Harappa, one of its major cities. The people who built it left no name for themselves that we can read, because their script — found on thousands of small seals and tablets — has never been deciphered.

What they left instead is infrastructure.

Reconstruction of life in the Indus Valley. Sophisticated sanitation and city planning long before most ancient civilizations

Mohenjo-daro, the best-excavated of the Harappan cities, contained features that would not reappear in urban planning for thousands of years. A grid street layout. Standardized brick sizes across hundreds of kilometres. A Great Bath — a large, waterproofed public pool — whose purpose remains debated but whose engineering is not. And most remarkably, a drainage system. Nearly every house in the city had a private bathroom connected to a covered municipal drain. The Romans would later be celebrated for their sewers. The Harappans had something comparable fifteen centuries earlier, and we still don’t know who designed it.

There is something else notable about the Harappan ruins, something that has puzzled archaeologists for a century: there are no palaces. No obvious temples on a monumental scale. No evidence of a warrior-king class. No great tombs stuffed with gold. The settlements show evidence of trade, craft production, granaries, and a sophisticated weight-and-measure system — but none of the usual signatures of a hierarchical ancient state. Either the Harappan elite were extraordinarily modest, or this civilization organized itself on principles that don’t map neatly onto the pharaohs-and-ziggurats framework the West uses as its default template for antiquity.

This matters. It is the first hint of a pattern that will recur throughout Indian history: the subcontinent does not organize itself the way Western theory predicts it should. Its civilizations are more distributed, more layered, more internally contradictory than the models allow for. The Indus Valley had no Pharaoh. Modern India has no single centre of gravity either. The pattern is older than anyone realized.

The Harappan civilization declined around 1900 BCE, likely due to shifts in river patterns and a prolonged drought. It did not collapse in a single catastrophe. It faded. Its people dispersed eastward and southward, carrying skills, habits, and — almost certainly — ideas that seeped into everything that followed. Whether the Vedic culture that succeeded it was an indigenous evolution or the product of migration from Central Asia is one of the most politically charged questions in Indian historiography today, because the answer implicates the founding mythology of Hindu nationalism. We will return to this in a later essay. For now, note the heat. In India, even archaeology is political.

II. The Vedic World

Vedic priests (rishis) performing a yajna (fire ritual). Central to the Vedic world — hymns, sacrifice, and the cosmic order that gave rise to concepts of dharma.

Sometime between 1500 and 500 BCE — the dates are debated, the cultural impact is not — the Vedic tradition took shape across the northern plains of the subcontinent. The Vedas, composed in Sanskrit and transmitted orally for centuries before being written down, are among the oldest religious texts in continuous use anywhere on earth. They are hymns, rituals, philosophical dialogues, and cosmological speculation. They are also the seedbed of the caste system.

The Rigveda, the earliest of the four Vedas, contains a hymn called the Purusha Sukta — the Hymn of the Cosmic Man — in which the primordial being is sacrificed and his body divided into four parts: his mouth became the Brahmins (priests and scholars), his arms the Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), his thighs the Vaishyas (merchants and farmers), and his feet the Shudras (servants and labourers). Below even the Shudras, unmentioned in the hymn because they were considered outside the body entirely, were the people who would later be called Untouchables — the Dalits, the broken people, the humans deemed so polluted by their birth that their shadow could contaminate a Brahmin’s food.

Artistic representation of the Purusha (Cosmic Man). The Vedic origin story that divided society into varnas and profoundly shaped Indian social structure for millennia.

I will say this plainly because the series demands it: caste is the single most important social fact in Indian history, and it is not history. It is present tense. When B.R. Ambedkar — himself a Dalit, himself a man who had drunk from separate water fountains as a child in the twentieth century — wrote India’s constitution in 1949, he did not merely draft a legal document. He performed an act of civilizational defiance. The man whose ancestors were considered subhuman by the religious texts of his own country wrote the supreme law that declared all citizens equal. We will spend an entire essay on Ambedkar. He deserves it. Most of the world has never heard his name, and that is a failure of Western education, not of his significance.

But the Vedic period gave India more than caste. It gave India the Upanishads — philosophical texts of such depth and sophistication that Schopenhauer, reading them in translation two millennia later, called them “the consolation of my life.” It gave India the concepts of dharma (duty, cosmic order, righteous conduct — there is no single English translation, which is itself instructive), karma (the moral consequence of action across lifetimes), and samsara (the cycle of death and rebirth). It gave India a view of time that is fundamentally different from the Western one — not linear, not progressive, not marching toward a culmination, but cyclical. Vast. Incomprehensibly long. The Hindu cosmic calendar counts time in yugas spanning millions of years. A civilization that thinks in those terms does not panic the way the West does. It also does not hurry the way the West does, which has implications for everything from economic policy to climate negotiations.

III. The First Empires

India’s first great political unification came not from the Vedic heartland but from its margins. Chandragupta Maurya, born around 340 BCE, founded the Maurya Empire by overthrowing the Nanda dynasty and, according to Greek sources, met Alexander the Great — or at least Alexander’s generals — during the Macedonian’s brief incursion into the northwest. Whether or not the meeting happened, the timing is instructive. Alexander turned back at the Beas River in 326 BCE, defeated not by Indian armies but by the mutiny of his own troops, who looked at the subcontinent and decided they had gone far enough. India has been exhausting invaders ever since.

Chandragupta built an empire that stretched from modern Afghanistan to Bengal, governed from Pataliputra — near modern Patna in Bihar — and administered by a bureaucracy described in the Arthashastra, a treatise on statecraft attributed to his advisor Chanakya that reads like Machiavelli written fifteen centuries early. The Arthashastra is coldly pragmatic about the use of spies, the management of treasury, the conduct of war, and the responsibilities of a king. It is also, notably, pragmatic about religious diversity — the text assumes a multi-faith society as a baseline condition of governance, not an aberration to be resolved.

But it is Chandragupta’s grandson who commands the most attention, and who remains the most astonishing figure in Indian antiquity.

IV. Ashoka

Ashoka Pillar at Sarnath with the famous Lion Capital. The 24-spoked wheel on India’s flag originates here — a symbol of dhamma and ethical rule from the 3rd century BCE.

Ashoka Maurya inherited an empire and expanded it through war. In approximately 261 BCE, he conquered the kingdom of Kalinga — modern Odisha — in a campaign of extraordinary brutality. By his own account, carved into rock edicts that still survive, a hundred thousand people were killed, a hundred and fifty thousand deported, and many times that number perished from the war’s aftermath. Ashoka won. And then he did something no conqueror before him and very few after him have done.

He stopped.

The Rock Edicts of Ashoka, carved on pillars and boulders across the subcontinent, record a transformation that historians still debate but cannot dismiss. After Kalinga, Ashoka converted to Buddhism and renounced military conquest. He proclaimed a policy of dhamma — not dharma in the later sectarian sense, but a broader ethical code emphasizing nonviolence, tolerance, compassion for all living beings, and respect for differing religious traditions. He sent missionaries — not soldiers — across Asia. He built hospitals. He planted trees along roads for shade. He issued edicts calling on his subjects to treat all religions with respect and to refrain from praising their own faith by denigrating others.

Read that last line again. In the third century BCE, an Indian emperor carved into stone a prohibition against religious chauvinism. He did it seventeen centuries before the European Enlightenment and twenty-three centuries before the BJP’s current governing project.

Ashoka is the great ghost in Indian political history. Gandhi invoked him. Nehru invoked him. Ambedkar, who converted to Buddhism himself, invoked him. The modern Indian state placed his Ashoka Chakra — the twenty-four-spoked wheel from his pillar capital — on the national flag. He is claimed by everyone and fully owned by no one, because his example is simultaneously inspiring and embarrassing. He represents a road India took once and has never quite managed to take again.

Close-up of Ashoka’s Rock Edict. The emperor’s own words, carved in stone, proclaiming nonviolence and religious tolerance after the Kalinga War.

He also represents something the West has never fully reckoned with: the subcontinent produced a philosophy of nonviolence and religious pluralism as state policy at a time when Rome was still perfecting crucifixion. The moral infrastructure of India is not borrowed from the Enlightenment. It predates the Enlightenment by two thousand years. This does not mean India has lived up to it — no country lives up to its best ideas — but it means the ideas were there first, indigenous, rooted in soil and stone and carved edicts that you can still read today.

V. The Classical Age and the South

The Western narrative of India has a persistent northern bias, drawn largely from the Gangetic plain, the Maurya and Gupta empires, and the later Mughal capitals of Delhi and Agra. But India is also the Deccan Plateau, the Tamil coast, the Malabar shore, the Dravidian south — a world of languages, literatures, and political traditions that were flourishing long before the Mughals arrived and that maintained their distinctiveness long after the British left.

The Sangam literature of Tamil Nadu — poetry dating roughly from the third century BCE to the third century CE — constitutes one of the great literary traditions of the ancient world, and most people outside India have never heard of it. The Chola dynasty, based in Tamil Nadu, built a maritime empire that reached Southeast Asia, leaving Hindu temples in Cambodia, Indonesia, and Vietnam that still stand. The spice trade connected Kerala to Rome — actual Rome, not metaphorical Rome — as early as the first century CE. Indian Ocean commerce linked the subcontinent to East Africa, the Persian Gulf, and China in a trading network that preceded European maritime expansion by a millennium.

The towering gopurams of a great Chola temple — evidence of the maritime and cultural reach of southern Indian kingdoms.

This matters for the argument of the series because it establishes something essential: India was never a single political entity before the British, and the south was never merely an appendage of the north. Tamil identity, Kannada identity, Malayalam identity — these are not regional quirks within a unified national story. They are autonomous civilizational threads, woven into the Indian fabric but never fully dissolved into it. When my roommate Rikesh speaks Gujarati and my roommate Gurneet speaks Punjabi and they both switch to Hindi to talk to each other, they are performing — without thinking about it — an act of negotiation that has been happening on this subcontinent for three thousand years.

VI. The Arrival of Islam

Islam arrived in India not once but repeatedly, and not only by the sword — though the sword was certainly part of it. Arab traders brought Islam to the Kerala coast as early as the seventh century CE, establishing mosques that are among the oldest outside the Arabian Peninsula. The Sufi mystics who followed in later centuries built their appeal not on conquest but on spiritual accessibility, offering a direct relationship with God that cut across the caste hierarchies of Hindu society. The appeal was real, particularly for Dalits and lower-caste communities for whom Islam’s theoretical egalitarianism represented liberation from a birth-assigned prison.

The military invasions came separately. Mahmud of Ghazni’s raids in the early eleventh century. The establishment of the Delhi Sultanate in 1206. And then, in 1526, the arrival of Babur — a descendant of both Timur and Genghis Khan — who founded the Mughal Empire.

The Mughals are where the Western narrative typically picks up India’s story, and they deserve attention, but not the version the West usually tells. The Mughal Empire was not simply “Muslim rule over Hindus.” It was something far more complicated — a hybrid civilization that produced extraordinary art, architecture, literature, and governance systems, and that at its best represented a conscious experiment in religious coexistence.

Akbar, who ruled from 1556 to 1605, is the figure who embodies this most fully. He abolished the jizya — the tax on non-Muslims. He married Hindu Rajput princesses not merely as political alliances but as genuine cultural integration. He established a policy called Sulh-i-Kul — peace with all — and convened a hall of religious debate, the Ibadat Khana, where Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Jain, Zoroastrian, and even atheist scholars argued theology in his presence. He attempted, late in his reign, to create a synthetic faith called Din-i-Ilahi — the Religion of God — that drew from all traditions. It failed as a religion. It succeeded as a statement of intent.

Akbar presiding over religious discussions in the Ibadat Khana — Hindus, Muslims, Christians, Jains, and others debating freely under imperial patronage.

Akbar’s great-grandson Aurangzeb reversed much of this. He reimposed the jizya, destroyed Hindu temples, and pursued a more explicitly Islamic state. The Mughal Empire’s decline is often dated from his reign — not because Islamic rule was inherently unstable, but because the pluralist compact that Akbar had built was the thing that actually held the empire together, and Aurangzeb broke it.

There is a direct line from this story to the present. The BJP’s Hindutva project frames Indian history as a narrative of Hindu victimhood under Muslim rule — the temples destroyed, the forced conversions, the centuries of subjugation. This narrative is not entirely false. Temples were destroyed. Conversions happened. But it is catastrophically incomplete, because it erases Akbar, erases the Sufi saints, erases the centuries of Hindu-Muslim cultural synthesis that produced Urdu, Hindustani classical music, Mughal architecture, and the composite culture that Indians actually lived in for five hundred years. The Taj Mahal was built by a Muslim emperor using Hindu craftsmen, and it is the defining symbol of India. That fact alone should complicate any narrative of civilizational warfare.

The Taj Mahal — built by Mughal Emperor Shah Jahan. A monument to love, craftsmanship, and the synthesis of Hindu and Islamic artistic traditions.

VII. What the Civilization Teaches

I began this essay by saying that the first mistake the West makes about India is assuming it began when the British arrived. The deeper mistake is assuming that India’s history is a prelude to its present — that everything before 1947 is backstory, context, scenery for the main event of modern nationhood.

It isn’t. The civilization is the main event. Everything that follows — the Raj, Partition, independence, democracy, Modi — is a series of arrangements imposed upon or negotiated within a civilizational reality that is older than most countries’ entire histories. India’s democracy doesn’t work because of its institutions alone. It works because the civilization learned, over millennia, how to hold contradictions together without resolving them. Hindu and Buddhist. Vedic and Dravidian. Northern and Southern. Sanskrit and Tamil. Caste hierarchy and Ashoka’s egalitarian edicts. Akbar’s pluralism and Aurangzeb’s orthodoxy.

India does not resolve its contradictions. It contains them. And that, as we will see in the essays that follow, is both its greatest strength and its most persistent vulnerability.

The British did not understand this. They saw contradiction and assumed chaos. They imposed resolution — borders, categories, census classifications, a single administrative language — and called it civilization. What they actually did was light a fuse. The explosion came at midnight on August 15, 1947.

But before we get to the British, we need to understand the instrument they used to pry India open. It was not an army. It was a company.
