This series opened with a suspicion, stated plainly in the introduction: that this week’s General Assembly session would probably look like last year’s, and that Tuesday’s American address in particular would probably be dark. It was, in the end, darker than expected — not in tone, which was oddly buoyant in places, but in substance. Within the space of about ninety minutes on the Assembly floor, the President of the United States told the world’s assembled leaders that he faced a choice between letting Iran “rebuild and create a far greater country than it ever was before” or, in his own words, deciding to “annihilate the Islamic republic and do it quickly, never giving them a chance to kill and destroy people and countries again.” It was, by any reasonable reading, a threat of national annihilation delivered from the one podium on earth built, on paper, for the opposite purpose. And it was, within the hour, quietly undercut by the same government’s own diplomats, sitting a few floors away with the foreign minister of the country just threatened, discussing what both sides were calling a pathway to a deal.

Before Trump spoke, two other men made this series’ argument for it

It is worth pausing on the order of Tuesday’s proceedings, because the two speeches that came before Trump’s did something this series has spent seven essays trying to demonstrate with historical evidence, and did it in about fifteen minutes combined, entirely unprompted. António Guterres, delivering his final address as Secretary-General before stepping down in December, told the Assembly that building a better future “begins with a recognition, especially by superpowers, that their power isn’t so super” — a line that could serve as an epigraph for this entire series’ argument about the Council’s structural limits. Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva of Brazil, speaking next by the Assembly’s traditional protocol, went further still, telling the chamber that member states “are all held hostage” by the Security Council — a direct, one-sentence summary of Part IV’s entire thesis, delivered by a sitting head of state on the opening morning of the session, before the week’s dominant story had even happened yet.

The threat, and the choice as Trump framed it

Trump’s own address ran long even by his own standards, opening with an extended account of domestic economic and immigration claims — several of which the Times’s own fact-check, published the same afternoon, flagged as exaggerated or unsupported, from a claimed $21 trillion in foreign investment (roughly double the White House’s own figure) to a “largest tax cuts in American history” claim that actually ranks sixth. He called the assembled leaders “great leaders” this year, a notably warmer register than last year’s “your countries are going to hell,” before turning to Iran with considerably less warmth. He framed the choice explicitly around the calendar of American domestic politics — “they’re waiting to see how I do in the midterm election,” he said of Tehran, adding that he expected a deal only after November’s vote — and around a military campaign he called “Operation Epic Fury,” claiming Iran’s navy, air force, and missile manufacturing capacity had all been effectively destroyed. Iran’s own armed forces responded within hours, in a statement carried by state media, calling the speech “exploitation of the U.N.’s podium to legitimize aggression, insecurity and global banditry” and dismissing the annihilation framing as a “baseless” threat reflecting Washington’s own “impasse.”

The threat was not this year’s only headline-grabbing moment, but it was the one that produced the sharpest reaction from the press corps covering the session — the Times’s own correspondents, watching from the room, called it “jarring” and “remarkable,” and noted it echoed an earlier 2026 social media post about Iranian civilisation that legal experts had already flagged as describing something close to a war crime.

It is worth stating plainly, for a series built around this document, exactly what was being set aside. The Charter’s Article 2(4) — drafted, as this series’ third essay covered, largely by American negotiators at San Francisco — commits every member state to refrain from “the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state,” except in self-defence or with the Security Council’s own authorisation. A sitting American president threatening, from the Assembly’s own podium, to eliminate another member state’s government “with no chance of survival” is not a grey area under that text; it is closer to as direct a rejection of the Charter’s founding prohibition as the podium has hosted in the organisation’s history — delivered, moreover, by the government whose own delegation did more than any other to write that prohibition in 1945.

The same afternoon, a different story

What happened next is, for this series’ purposes, more interesting than the threat itself. Barely an hour after Trump left the podium, he told reporters at the UN that American and Iranian officials had just concluded a “very good meeting” lasting three hours, with another already scheduled. Within a few more hours, the details sharpened: Iran’s foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, had met on the sidelines with Trump’s special envoy Steve Witkoff and his son-in-law Jared Kushner, the meeting mediated by Pakistan and Qatar, both of whom have spent months quietly pushing Washington and Tehran back toward the table. Iranian state television reported Araghchi’s own conditions for any deal — lifting the US naval blockade on Iran’s southern shores, releasing Iran’s frozen funds, ending hostilities on every front — as the price of reopening the Strait of Hormuz. Araghchi did not spend his day in New York looking like the foreign minister of an isolated pariah state; he spent it meeting the European Union’s top diplomat and the foreign ministers of Britain, Italy, Austria, Qatar, and Azerbaijan, posting warm, smiling photographs of each encounter to his own social media, a visible and deliberate rebuttal to the isolation Trump’s speech had just implied.

This is, in miniature, the exact argument this series’ fifth essay made about the General Assembly’s theatre: the spectacle and the substance run on separate tracks, and the spectacle is what gets remembered. A threat of annihilation, delivered to a packed chamber and covered by every wire service on earth, will be what most people recall about Tuesday, 22 September 2026. The three-hour meeting between Witkoff, Kushner, and Araghchi, mediated quietly by two Gulf states with every incentive to see the war end, will not be — even though it is, on the available evidence, considerably more likely to determine what actually happens to Iran than anything said from the podium.

A P5 power calls out its own Council, and a king states the pattern plainly

Two other speeches on Tuesday deserve more attention than the day’s dominant story will likely leave them. Emmanuel Macron, addressing the Assembly for the final time as France’s president before stepping down next May, asked the chamber directly “whether we want to live in this state of nature, with a kind of return to the law of the jungle, or in a civilised international society,” and then did something no essay in this series has yet had occasion to report a sitting P5 head of state doing from the Assembly floor: he called for Security Council reform, explicitly, as a member of the Council himself, saying it “had been paralyzed in the face of violations of international law” and that “we cannot accept this new order of impunity.” This is Part IV’s argument, made by France, about France’s own institution, in public, on the record — worth remembering when this series reaches its own essay on reform later on.

King Abdullah II of Jordan, speaking earlier in the day, put the Council’s structural problem even more starkly, without using the word “veto” once. Cataloguing the tools the international community has used against other states — “sanctions, asset freezes, embargoes, diplomatic isolation” — he told the Assembly that “with Israel, resolutions end where they began — on paper. The result is a state without restraint.” It is, almost word for word, this series’ argument about resolutions without enforcement, delivered by the head of state of a country that has spent the past seven months absorbing missile strikes meant for American forces stationed on its own soil.

Macron’s own Gaza remarks — that the Palestinians’ plight was “a spectacle that shames us all” — drew an immediate, sharp rebuttal from Israel’s prime minister’s office, which called the comment a “grotesque absurdity” and pointed to the killing of an Israeli man in the West Bank days earlier as evidence that Palestinian armed groups bear responsibility too. Both sides have a documented case worth stating rather than adjudicating here: UN humanitarian office figures put West Bank deaths at more than 1,000 Palestinians killed by Israeli forces and civilians since the Gaza war began, roughly 80 of them this year, against more than 40 Israelis killed in the West Bank over the past three years. Neither side’s account was invented for Tuesday’s floor debate; both numbers are simply what the UN’s own monitors have recorded.

The Gaza chorus was, by Tuesday’s end, genuinely loud: Turkey’s Erdogan accused Israel of “a genocidal mind-set that can never get enough of killing,” prompting Israel’s delegates to walk out of the hall mid-speech; Qatar’s Emir Tamim called the situation “a genocide in every sense of the word and a stain on the conscience of all humanity”; Palestinian officials themselves were, for the second year running, denied US visas to attend at all.

Two further speeches, easy to lose beneath the day’s louder news, are worth a line each for what they add to this series’ longer arc. South Korea’s Lee Jae Myung called for “an end to this decades-old war on the Korean peninsula” — a reminder, coming seventy-six years after the armistice this series’ sixth essay covered in detail, that the Korean War has technically never actually ended, only paused, and that a serving South Korean president felt the need to say so from this same podium in 2026. And Vietnam’s To Lam, addressing the Assembly as a confident middle power rather than a former combatant, warned against a “might makes right” approach to world order — a message aimed as much at Washington’s tariff threats against Vietnam’s own export economy as at any of the week’s larger wars.

Ukraine, and a claim still unconfirmed

On the sidelines, Trump met separately with Ukraine’s Volodymyr Zelensky, who told reporters he hoped the war would end “before winter” and that the American administration would help get there. Trump, for his part, claimed Vladimir Putin was “willing to meet” Zelensky face to face for the first time in seven years — “He’ll meet. He’s willing to meet. He wants to see the war end.” Russia had not responded to the claim as of Tuesday evening. It is worth treating this, for now, as exactly what it is: an American president’s characterisation of a Russian president’s private intentions, offered to reporters on a sidewalk rather than stated by Putin himself — the kind of claim this series’ evidence-based instincts suggest holding at arm’s length until Moscow says it in its own voice.

The Ukraine story has its own quiet-diplomacy-beneath-the-noise parallel to Iran’s, worth noting briefly. Earlier this month, Witkoff and Kushner made their first-ever joint visit to Ukraine, arriving fresh from hours of talks with Putin in Moscow — a trip the United States reportedly used to secure a temporary, mutual halt to strikes on both capitals for the duration of the envoys’ presence. The Times described the visit, accurately, as “talks about how to resume talks,” after months in which the Iran war had left the Ukraine peace process in what one report called “a deep freeze.” The pattern across both wars this week is now consistent enough to name directly: the loudest public rhetoric and the quietest actual diplomacy are, this September, running almost entirely independently of one another.

A veto, in real time, over who runs the institution itself

The week’s quietest story may end up being its most structurally revealing. The Security Council is, in parallel with everything above, working through its confidential process to select Guterres’s successor — a monthslong sequence of secret straw polls, each candidate marked “encouraged,” “discouraged,” or “no opinion” by all fifteen Council members, before a single nominee is put to the Assembly for a procedural vote requiring nine votes and no veto from any of the five permanent members. Seven candidates remain after Chile’s Michelle Bachelet withdrew last week; Costa Rica’s Rebeca Grynspan, currently head of the UN’s trade and development body, holds a narrow lead in the most recent poll. And Russia has already signalled it will veto Rafael Grossi, the IAEA chief and one of the seven, over his handling of the Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant standoff, while Washington has separately made clear its own objections to at least one other candidate over her comments on Gaza. This is Part IV’s entire argument, playing out in real time, over the single most consequential appointment the UN system makes: even choosing who leads the organisation is subject to exactly the great-power veto this series has spent seven essays tracing back to San Francisco. Whoever eventually takes the post in January — after Africa, Asia, and Western Europe supplied the last three Secretaries-General, Latin America is considered “due,” and a woman has never yet held the job in the UN’s 81-year history — will do so only because no permanent member chose to block them, not because the Assembly’s 193 members preferred them to anyone else.

Tuesday closed with Trump signing the long-delayed Greenland security agreement — a deal he described as securing “permanent control,” while Denmark’s prime minister described the same document as recognising Danish “sovereignty and territorial integrity,” two governments describing one piece of paper in terms that cannot both be fully true — and with the Cuban delegation walking out of the hall after Trump declared their government “an absolutely failed state” that “will fall.” It was, on balance, a day that gave this series’ central argument more free, unsolicited confirmation than seven essays of archival research had managed on its own: great powers say what they like from the podium, and the institution’s actual capacity to act remains exactly where San Francisco left it in 1945 — dependent, in the end, on whether the five states holding a veto can be persuaded to agree.

The week is not finished. Zelensky and Iran’s own president, Masoud Pezeshkian, are still to speak.
