This is the instalment that every series like this one dreads writing, because the honest answer to the question in its title is: not currently. Not with the political actors in place. Not with the conditions as they stand. Not in any timeframe that will help the people who are suffering now.

That is not despair. It is diagnosis. And diagnosis is the prerequisite for treatment, even when the treatment is long, expensive, and uncertain.

Let me be precise about what does not exist, what might exist, and what would have to change for any of it to become real.

What Does Not Exist

The two-state solution — an independent Palestinian state alongside Israel, roughly along the 1967 borders with mutually agreed land swaps, with East Jerusalem as its capital and a negotiated arrangement on the refugee question — is the solution that the international community has endorsed for fifty years, that every American administration since Carter has nominally supported, and that is currently physically impossible.

It is physically impossible because 700,000 Israeli settlers in 279 settlements have made a contiguous Palestinian state in the West Bank structurally incoherent. The settlers cannot be removed by the Israeli political system as currently constituted — the political coalition that would authorize their removal does not exist and is not close to existing. The settlers themselves have made clear, through their political representatives and through the violence of their most extreme members, that they regard the West Bank as their permanent home and will resist removal by any means available to them.

700,000 settlers in 279 settlements. The two-state solution, mapped.

It is politically impossible because the Palestinian political system is fractured beyond any current capacity for unified negotiation. The Palestinian Authority governs the West Bank with diminishing legitimacy and growing corruption, unable to deliver either security or economic progress, propped up by international donors and Israeli security cooperation that its own population regards as collaboration. Hamas governs Gaza and will not recognize Israel’s right to exist. The two Palestinian governments have not held elections since 2006, have not achieved meaningful reconciliation despite multiple attempts, and cannot present a unified negotiating position to any future peace process. A peace process requires a partner, and the Palestinian political system currently cannot produce one.

It is strategically impossible because the Israeli government that would have to negotiate it — that would have to freeze settlements, withdraw from the West Bank, divide Jerusalem, and address the refugee question — is the most right-wing in Israeli history, composed of politicians who do not want a Palestinian state, have said so publicly, and regard the current military campaign as an opportunity to resolve the Palestinian question by means other than negotiation.

The one-state solution — a single democratic state between the Jordan River and the Mediterranean Sea, with equal citizenship for all residents regardless of ethnicity or religion — is the solution that the trajectory of events is producing by default, and it is also currently impossible.

It is impossible because a single democratic state with equal citizenship for all of Israel’s Jewish citizens and all Palestinians in the West Bank and Gaza would produce an Arab majority within a decade, ending the Jewish character of the state. No Israeli government will accept this. No Israeli political movement that has the capacity to govern will accept this. The entire premise of Zionism — the creation of a Jewish state as the national home and refuge of the Jewish people — is negated by a single democratic state in which Jews are a minority. The one-state solution that the occupation is creating by default is not a democratic one-state solution. It is an apartheid one-state reality in which Jews have full rights and Palestinians have none, administered under a system of military control that the international community has characterized as a form of apartheid.

The confederation solution — two states with open borders, shared institutions, joint management of shared resources, and freedom of movement between them — is the most intellectually interesting of the proposed alternatives, advocated by thinkers including the Israeli writer Daniel Barenboim and the Palestinian intellectual Sari Nusseibeh. It addresses the practical problem that the settlement enterprise has made territorial separation impossible without forced evacuation, by proposing that settlers could remain in a Palestinian state as citizens subject to Palestinian law, and Palestinian citizens of Israel could remain in Israel. It is currently politically impossible because it requires both parties to accept a level of integration that neither current political leadership is prepared to accept — Israeli settlers living under Palestinian law, Palestinian refugees accepting symbolic rather than literal return.

None of these solutions exists. All of them require political conditions that do not currently obtain. The honest position, stated plainly, is that the conflict is currently irresolvable by any mechanism available in the existing political environment.

What Might Change

Political environments are not permanent. The conditions that make the conflict currently irresolvable are real, but they are contingent — they depend on specific political actors, specific economic arrangements, specific international configurations that are not fixed forever.

On the Israeli side, the political conditions that would need to change for any solution to become possible are: the collapse or significant weakening of the religious nationalist right as a governing force, the emergence of a political leadership prepared to genuinely confront the settlement enterprise, and a shift in Israeli public opinion sufficient to sustain a government that negotiates in good faith. None of these changes is imminent. All of them are conceivable over a longer timeframe — particularly as the cost of the current trajectory becomes clearer in security, international isolation, and economic terms.

The International Criminal Court arrest warrant for Netanyahu — for war crimes and crimes against humanity in Gaza — is a signal of that international isolation. It does not constrain Netanyahu’s domestic political survival, but it constrains Israel’s international relationships in ways that will compound over time. Israeli scientists, athletes, artists, and academics are already experiencing exclusion from international institutions. Israeli businesses are facing boycott pressures. Israeli universities have lost international partnerships. These are not decisive pressures. They are accumulating ones, and accumulating pressures eventually produce political changes even in systems designed to resist them.

On the Palestinian side, the political conditions that would need to change are: the reform or replacement of the Palestinian Authority with a leadership that has genuine popular legitimacy, the integration of Hamas into a political framework that requires it to accept a two-state solution as an interim arrangement, or Hamas’s replacement by a political force capable of representing Palestinian interests in a negotiation. None of these changes is imminent either. The Palestinian political system has been deliberately fragmented by decades of Israeli policy — the isolation of Gaza, the undermining of the PA, the prevention of Palestinian elections — in ways that have made unified Palestinian political leadership structurally difficult to achieve.

On the American side, the conditions that would need to change are: a domestic political shift that removes the veto power of the pro-Israel lobby over American Middle East policy, a president willing to apply genuine pressure on Israel — conditioning military aid, supporting UN resolutions, withdrawing diplomatic cover — as Eisenhower did in 1957 and Bush 41 briefly did in 1991. These changes would require either the decline of the domestic political constituencies that currently prevent them, or a president of unusual political courage willing to spend the capital required.

What was framed as a long-term structural shift in Part XII of this series appears to be arriving faster than the long-term framing suggested. The Iran war has collapsed Republican support for Israel among younger voters at a speed that has surprised even the conflict’s most attentive analysts. When the most committed pro-Israel enforcer in Trump’s orbit privately tells the president he will likely be the last pro-Israel president America ever has — and the president agrees — the political architecture that has sustained American policy for seventy-five years is not merely eroding. It is being acknowledged as terminal by its own guardians. That does not mean the next president will condition military aid or withdraw Security Council cover. Political trajectories move faster than political institutions. But the window in which unconditional American support for Israeli conduct is a domestic political asset rather than a liability is closing, and it is closing faster than any previous analysis of this conflict suggested it would.

The Iran ceasefire of April 2026 — mediated by Pakistan, extended indefinitely by Trump, and immediately violated by both sides — has not changed this calculus. If anything, it has illustrated it. With American attention turned toward Iran negotiations, Israeli bombardment of Gaza accelerated. A Lebanon ceasefire agreed June 3 between Israel and the Lebanese government was rejected within twenty-four hours by Hezbollah as "absurd and humiliating." The Strait of Hormuz remains a negotiating chip. The frozen Iranian assets remain frozen. The talks in Pakistan produced a pause, not a peace. The window may be closing on unconditional American support for Israeli conduct, but the institution has not yet caught up with the trajectory. The gap between where American politics is going and what American policy currently does remains the central fact of this conflict.

What the International Community Can Do

The International Court of Justice, The Hague. The law moves slowly. It moves.

The international community — meaning the UN system, the International Court of Justice, regional organizations, and the governments of states that are not the United States — has more leverage than it has exercised, and less leverage than its rhetoric implies.

The ICJ proceedings initiated by South Africa are genuinely consequential. They have established that Israel’s conduct poses a plausible risk of genocide — a legal finding that has real consequences for states that continue to provide military support, since the Genocide Convention creates obligations for all signatories to prevent genocide when they have the capacity to do so. The legal proceedings will continue for years and their ultimate findings will shape the legal framework within which Israeli conduct is assessed.

The ICC arrest warrant for Netanyahu is similarly consequential over a longer timeframe. It does not mean Netanyahu will be arrested — the United States has made clear it will not enforce it, and Israel is not an ICC member. But it means that Netanyahu cannot travel to 124 countries that are ICC members without risk of arrest. It isolates Israel diplomatically in ways that compound slowly but accumulate.

Arms embargoes — already imposed by some European governments on certain weapons categories — are a tool that more governments could use. Canada’s review of military export permits to Israel is a step in this direction. It does not stop the war. It does signal that the international community is beginning to distinguish between diplomatic support and material enablement.

The BDS movement — Boycott, Divestment and Sanctions — which models itself on the international campaign against South African apartheid, is controversial, contested, and politically charged in ways that make it difficult to evaluate objectively. Its effectiveness in changing Israeli government policy has been minimal. Its effectiveness in changing the conversation in Western societies — in universities, in cultural institutions, in trade unions — has been real, though contested. Whether economic and cultural pressure of the kind that BDS advocates can produce political change in Israel, in the way that it produced political change in South Africa, is genuinely uncertain. South Africa’s white minority did not abandon apartheid because it was economically unviable. It abandoned apartheid because a combination of internal resistance, international isolation, and American political pressure made the cost of sustaining it exceed the benefit. Whether equivalent conditions can be created for Israel is the central strategic question for those who believe external pressure is the only remaining lever.

What Israelis and Palestinians Can Do

Within the current political environment, the most important thing Israelis can do is build and sustain the political movements that are capable of challenging the religious nationalist right. Those movements exist — Peace Now, B’Tselem, Breaking the Silence, Combatants for Peace — and they are small, politically marginal, and subjected to legal and social pressure from the current government. They represent the conscience of Israeli society and the seed of a political future that the current government is trying to prevent. They deserve international support, attention, and solidarity.

The Israeli hostage families who have marched and protested and demanded accountability from their own government have demonstrated a moral authority that transcends political categories. Their demand — that the government that failed them on October 7th be held accountable and that their loved ones be returned by negotiation rather than rhetoric — is a demand that cuts through the political noise in ways that formal opposition cannot. They are the most important political voice in Israel right now.

The hostage families. The most important political voice in Israel. The government has not listened.

Within the Palestinian political system, the most important thing that can happen is the emergence of a new generation of political leadership — one that is not corrupted by the Oslo-era accommodation with occupation, not imprisoned in the armed resistance theology of Hamas, and capable of representing Palestinian national aspirations in a way that can build international coalitions and negotiate with Israeli partners. That leadership is not currently visible at the political level. It exists — in Palestinian civil society, in the diaspora, in the universities and professional organizations and human rights groups that have been documenting and resisting the occupation for decades. The question is whether it can emerge into political relevance before the physical conditions for a Palestinian state are entirely destroyed.

The Honest Accounting

This series began in Jerusalem, where everything begins. It has traced the compound interest on the original sin through three thousand years of sacred geography, four centuries of Ottoman management, a century of British betrayal, seventy-five years of American enabling, fifty-eight years of occupation, and the current catastrophe whose death toll continues to rise as these words are written.

The accounting is this: a legitimate refuge for a people who had been persecuted for millennia was built on terms that required the dispossession of a people who had nothing to do with that persecution. The dispossession was underwritten by Western power, sustained by Western money, protected by Western vetoes, and is currently producing a catastrophe whose scale the Western democracies that enabled it are struggling to acknowledge honestly.

Gaza, 2026. The compound interest, paid in rubble

The Palestinian people — 5.9 million registered refugees and their descendants, 2.9 million living under military occupation in the West Bank, 2.2 million living in the rubble of Gaza, millions more in the diaspora — are paying the compound interest on decisions made in London, Washington, Tel Aviv, and New York by people who are dead, and by their successors who continue making the same decisions.

The Israeli people — 9.7 million citizens of a state that has genuine legitimacy and genuine security needs — are also paying compound interest, in the form of a security environment that every military campaign makes worse rather than better, an international isolation that is accelerating, an internal political dynamic in which the most extreme elements of their society have achieved governing power, and the moral cost of an occupation that corrupts the institutions and values of a democracy.

There is a way out. It requires, on the Israeli side, a government with the political courage to confront the settlement enterprise, to negotiate in good faith, and to accept that a Jewish state living in genuine peace with its neighbours is more secure than a Jewish state maintaining permanent military control over a population that will never accept that control. It requires, on the Palestinian side, a political leadership with the legitimacy and the strategic capacity to negotiate and implement an agreement that the Palestinian people can accept. It requires, on the American side, a president willing to apply genuine pressure rather than offer endless rhetorical support that changes nothing. It requires, on the international side, the sustained application of legal, economic, and diplomatic pressure that makes the status quo more costly than a negotiated solution.

None of these requirements is currently met. All of them are achievable in principle. The gap between principle and current political reality is the measure of the work that remains.

I am a Canadian journalist writing from Toronto, watching a conflict that has been building for a century on a screen that brings its human cost into my living room with a clarity and immediacy that no previous generation of outsiders has had to witness. I have no power to resolve it. I have the power to describe it honestly, to name the actors and their decisions, to refuse the comfortable evasions that allow powerful governments to pretend that the status quo is sustainable or that the suffering is someone else’s problem.

The suffering is everyone’s problem. It was made by everyone’s decisions — or by the decisions of the governments that everyone’s taxes fund and everyone’s votes sustain.

That is the most a journalist can offer. That the accounting exists. That the names are on the record. That the compound interest has been calculated and its payers identified.

The payment is overdue. It will be made, eventually, because history always collects. The only question is whether it is made through negotiation and justice, or through the continuation of a catastrophe that has already cost too many lives to count and shows no sign of stopping.

Jerusalem is still there. The Dome of the Rock still rises gold above the Old City. The Western Wall still holds the prayers of two thousand years of exile. The Church of the Holy Sepulchre still marks the site of the world’s most consequential death and resurrection.

Master KG is still singing Jerusalema. The world is still dancing.

The city waits.
