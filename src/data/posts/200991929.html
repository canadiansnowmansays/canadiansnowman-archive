He was fifteen minutes late.

The chiefs of the Department of Health and Human Services’ thirteen operating divisions had gathered, as they do every Tuesday at 10:30 a.m., in the secretary’s suite to brief leadership on their activities. These are the people who run Medicare and Medicaid, the FDA, the CDC, the NIH — the agencies that together provide health care to 40 percent of the American population and stand between the public and the next pandemic.

Robert F. Kennedy Jr. arrived fifteen minutes after they did.

"Thank you for putting up with my dysfunctional self," he told the room, by way of apology, as reported by The New York Times.

It was, in its way, the most honest thing he has said since taking office. More honest than the promise not to change the childhood vaccine schedule. More honest than the assurance that leucovorin could treat autism. More honest than the declaration that measles outbreaks are “not unusual.”

He said it as a self-deprecating joke. The people in the room did not find it especially funny. Several described him, to reporters at The New York Times, as “checked out.” He spends the meetings scrolling on his phone.

This is the man responsible for protecting the health of 340 million Americans.

The Schedule

Let us begin with the basics, because they are remarkable.

When Kennedy is in Washington — and he travels frequently, on a “Take Back Your Health” tour that has taken him to Ohio, California, Arizona, Florida, and Wisconsin, spotlighting dairy farmers and addiction recovery programs and regenerative farms — he arrives at HHS headquarters at approximately 10 a.m. and leaves by 4 p.m. He spends much of his day in closed-door meetings with a small circle of handpicked advisers. He has little direct engagement with career staff. He is, according to a dozen people who have had direct contact with him as secretary, single-mindedly focused on food recommendations, pesticide exposures, and hunting for evidence to support his long-held beliefs about vaccines.

When he is not in Washington, he is sometimes in Scottsdale, Arizona, where his son and daughter-in-law live, or in Palm Beach, Florida, where he stays at the mansion owned by Dr. Mehmet Oz, the director of the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services and one of his closest allies. After Memorial Day, Kennedy posted video of himself on Oz’s patio, in a suit and tie, capturing two black racer snakes. One of them bit him.

His management of the Tuesday division chief meetings tells the fuller story. At the outset of his tenure, he was rarely present, either virtually or in person. Since Chris Klomp’s elevation to chief counselor in February — a White House-directed intervention to stabilize a department the White House itself had concluded was dysfunctioning, as STAT News reported — Kennedy now shows up once a month. When he does attend, multiple people in the room describe the same scene: he appears disengaged, scrolling on his phone. Checked out.

This is the man who, when asked by The New York Times about the Ebola outbreak now spreading through central Africa — a WHO-declared public health emergency of international concern, with more than 500 suspected cases and 139 deaths, and six Americans already exposed — offered three words to ABC News: “Yeah, we’re working on it.”

That was his first public comment on the outbreak. It was followed by nearly three weeks of silence.

The Architecture of Absence

The Secretary responsible for pandemic preparedness, busy removing snakes from his colleague’s patio while the U.S. scrambled to respond to Ebola.

No single person can run the Department of Health and Human Services. It is too large, too complex, too consequential. Former Secretary Tommy Thompson spent a week at each of the department’s operating divisions at the outset of his tenure, then made frequent trips to Capitol Hill to advocate for the department. Former Secretary Kathleen Sebelius met with division chiefs regularly — “I was really schooled by the people who had been there before and who knew what the hell they were doing,” she told the Times.

Kennedy’s approach has been different. He is deeply mistrustful of career officials, and has surrounded himself with a small circle of loyalists, most of them holdovers from his presidential campaign or his years at the anti-vaccine group Children’s Health Defense. All decisions, all requests to meet with the secretary, flow through one person: Stefanie Spear, his longtime adviser, who runs meetings on his behalf, accompanies him on official trips, and maintains a private spreadsheet of more than fifty policy projects — a spreadsheet to which his own policy team has no access.

“Just run that by Stefanie,” is Kennedy’s characteristic response when asked a question.

This arrangement has produced results. When a gunman opened fire on CDC headquarters last August — Kennedy was fishing with Native American leaders in Alaska — a communications response was held up for hours while Spear sought White House approval for a statement. Kennedy is on his third top spokesman; the first two quit in frustration over Spear’s control of communications. He has run through two chiefs of staff. Thomas Corry, the HHS assistant secretary of public affairs, lasted less than two weeks after being sworn in — he resigned abruptly after clashing with Kennedy over the muted response to the Texas measles outbreak, with sources telling CNN that Spear appeared to effectively run communications within HHS, sidelining everyone else.

In February, the White House moved to force Spear into a diminished role, citing frustration with her performance and the belief that she had struggled to work with others. The White House then publicly called this "absolute fake news," as MSNBC reported. The dysfunction, on available evidence, continued.

Kennedy has also been out of the loop on decisions that are nominally his to make. When Dr. Tracy Beth Hoeg, the FDA’s top drug regulator, was fired by FDA leaders in May, the secretary did not learn of it until after it happened.

The Empty Offices

No Surgeon General. Acting leaders at CDC, FDA, and half of NIH. The empty offices of American public health.

Into this management vacuum, add the vacancies.

There is no surgeon general. Around half of the twenty-seven institutes and centers at the NIH are run by acting directors. The acting chief of the National Institute for Allergy and Infectious Diseases — the agency that would normally lead the U.S. scientific response to an Ebola outbreak — was recently fired. The nation’s top drug regulator was fired. The leader of the FDA quit last month under pressure over tobacco policy. The CDC has had a confirmed director for twenty-nine days since Kennedy took office; it is currently run on an acting basis by Dr. Jay Bhattacharya, who already has a full-time job as director of the NIH and who has no prior experience in public health.

Eleven acting directors oversee operations at the CDC. Eight acting officials run the FDA, including the acting commissioner. The CDC's principal deputy director recently resigned, as did Kennedy's choice to help lead the vaccine advisory committee — all as catalogued this week by Maryland Matters and Protect Our Care.

And then there is the vaccine advisory committee itself. In a normal year, the CDC’s expert panel known as ACIP — which has guided the agency’s vaccine recommendations for more than sixty years — would convene in June to weigh in on the autumn flu shot. This, as The Atlantic reported Sunday, is not a normal year. In March, a federal judge effectively suspended ACIP on the grounds that Kennedy had violated lawful procedure when he hastily remade the panel’s roster last June. There is currently no functional ACIP. No committee exists to guide this autumn’s flu and COVID immunization campaigns. More than half of American children receive their vaccines through the federal Vaccines for Children program, which depends entirely on ACIP to determine eligibility — and that program is now in legal limbo.

“Even those of us who follow this 24/7 don’t completely understand where anything stands right now,” Michelle Fiscus, a pediatrician and the chief medical officer of the Association of Immunization Managers, told The Atlantic.

The administration has the option of doing nothing, letting the rest of the vaccine-delivery pipeline operate as best it can in ACIP’s absence. As vaccine-law expert Dorit Reiss described it to The Atlantic, that would be a tactic of “rock the boat as little as possible, to reduce embarrassment.” The closing assessment of The Atlantic’s reporting deserves to be quoted in full: “If the administration’s goal was to shrink the government’s involvement in infectious disease, in this way, it’s succeeding.”

Senator Angela Alsobrooks of Maryland, speaking at a virtual conference with Protect Our Care this week, called it what it is: “How shameful it is, at a time when the worst measles outbreak in decades is still raging, and as Ebola and hantavirus threaten the globe, there is no one in control in these critical positions. It is an unmitigated disaster that could have, and should have, been avoided.”

Dr. Michael Osterholm, director of the University of Minnesota Center for Infectious Disease Research and Policy, put the management failure in terms a business audience might understand: “If the CEO lacked deep expertise in the company’s business and the leaders of its most important divisions were missing, investors would revolt. Here, the stakes are much higher. The mission is protecting the health and safety of the American people, and we’re confronting serious disease threats without permanent leadership in some of our most important public health agencies.”

The Man Responsible for Preparedness

And then there is John Knox.

The Administration for Strategic Preparedness and Response — ASPR — is the agency responsible for pandemic preparedness, for standing up field hospitals, for quarantine facilities. It is the agency that helped bring COVID-19 under control. Historically, it has been led by doctors, military officers, or career public health officials.

Under Kennedy, it is led by John Knox, a former Los Angeles firefighter who founded the group Firefighters4Freedom during the pandemic to fight vaccine mandates. Knox has no background in public health management. Six current and former senior HHS officials told MSNBC that Kennedy had placed an unqualified activist atop one of the federal government’s most critical health agencies. Under Knox, ASPR has lost experienced scientists and senior staff. Key roles have been filled with, in the words of those officials, conspiracy theorists and advocates of false COVID treatments.

Knox has also pushed to cut funding for CDC experts who advised ASPR officials on the national stockpile. It was coming down, as one former official put it, to where Knox’s box was on the org chart.

This is the agency standing between the United States and the next pandemic.

The Outbreak

The US has completed its withdrawal from the World Health Organization, the US Department of Health and Human Services said. Fabrice Coffrini/AFP/Getty Images

The Ebola outbreak now spreading through central Africa — the Bundibugyo virus strain, for which there is no approved vaccine — is the first major outbreak since the Trump administration demolished its global health architecture.

USAID was shuttered last year. The U.S. withdrew from the World Health Organization in January, a move Georgetown public health law expert Lawrence Gostin described to CNN as 'the most ruinous presidential decision in my lifetime.'" The dismantling of these institutions did not cause the outbreak. It has shaped the response.

The Hill reported that experts say the absence of American infrastructure likely delayed detection of the virus. CNN reported that U.S. infectious disease researchers at what was once Anthony Fauci’s agency were barred from directly communicating with the WHO during the outbreak — initially permitted to attend meetings only in listen-only mode. The WHO’s Contingency Fund for Emergencies, which the U.S. had historically been among the largest donors to, has received just $5.4 million in contributions in 2026, nearly exhausting it, with other donors unable to fill the American gap — as KFF documented this week.

The pandemic preparedness office’s budget was cut by $240 million. The agency is led by the firefighter.

Kennedy has left the department’s Ebola response to Bhattacharya, the health economist with no prior public health experience who is simultaneously running the NIH. When children were dying of measles in Texas earlier this year, Dr. Demetre Daskalakis, who led the response, asked repeatedly to brief Kennedy and was rebuffed each time.

It was the same pattern, at a larger scale, with higher stakes.

“Yeah, we’re working on it.”

The Performance and the Record

Kennedy’s allies argue that his management style is different from his predecessors’, not worse. He is leading in other ways, they say — taking stands on healthy eating, tackling chronic disease, challenging powerful interests. Mehmet Oz, in a statement issued through the health department’s own communications machinery, said: “You do not come to Washington to challenge powerful interests, disrupt decades of business as usual, and demand accountability to make friends. You do it to deliver results.”

Calley Means, a close Kennedy adviser, offered a more candid version at a Harvard forum: “Very predictably, you get into governing, you get into a toxic political environment, and a situation where there’s trillions of dollars of interest and you have the burden of having to do policy. Nobody executes perfectly.”

Nobody executes perfectly. This is technically true, in the way that it is technically true that Ebola is not nothing, and that having eleven acting directors at the CDC during a global public health emergency is not ideal.

What Kennedy has delivered: a flipped food pyramid. Agreements with medical schools to expand nutrition education. Some food makers abandoning artificial dyes. A podcast, launched in April. A “Take Back Your Health” tour that has visited Ohio, California, Arizona, Florida, and Wisconsin. Snakes on Oz’s patio.

What he has not delivered: a permanent CDC director. A surgeon general. Stable leadership at the NIH, the FDA, ASPR, or any of the major operating divisions. A public health response to the worst measles outbreak in decades that included briefings from the people managing it. A comment on the Ebola outbreak that ran longer than six words.

Trust in federal health agencies has collapsed during his tenure, according to KFF polling from February — across partisan lines, which is the detail that worries Republican senators most. It is why the White House has been managing his public appearances with increasing care, keeping him away from vaccine commentary, redirecting his energy toward food dyes and seed oils, putting his agenda on what Bloomberg called a “low-risk messaging diet” ahead of the midterms.

It is why, as we documented in the previous entry of this series, Marco Rubio reclaimed the Gavi file from him, and the White House installed a CDC director he would not have chosen himself.

The containment operation is real. But containment presupposes a contained. The man being managed is the Secretary of Health and Human Services. The man checking his phone during the Tuesday morning briefings is responsible for every agency that stands between the American public and the thing that comes next.

He shows up once a month now. That is progress.

He scrolls through his phone when he gets there. That is the record.

Thank you for putting up with his dysfunctional self.
