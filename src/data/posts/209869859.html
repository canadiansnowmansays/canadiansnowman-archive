Every promise SpaceX made to investors on August 4, 2026 has a date attached to it. Mobile V2 satellites launch this year. Commercial Starlink Mobile service arrives by the end of 2027. Global direct-to-device coverage — the thing that would make “Who Owns the Air” a settled question rather than an ongoing series — arrives by 2028.

Five parts into this series, we now have enough evidence to ask the only question that actually matters: is that plausible? Not “is it ambitious” — everything Elon Musk says on an earnings call is ambitious, including his aside about scaling to a thousand times Earth’s economy via lunar manufacturing. The question is narrower and more useful: given everything documented in Parts I through V, what specifically has to go right, and what’s most likely to go wrong?

What’s already working in SpaceX’s favour

Start with the case for optimism, because it’s genuinely stronger than the “two years is impossible” instinct suggests. The entire argument of Part I was that spectrum sovereignty makes a traditional buy-everywhere strategy too slow for this timeline — and SpaceX isn’t running that strategy. Outside the US, it’s running the MVNO-style partnership model, confirmed in Shotwell’s own words in Part III: “we operate that through local telco providers.” That model has already proven it can move from signed deal to national commercial service in as little as two and a half years in Canada, and in mere months in Ukraine, where necessity erased the usual timeline entirely. Part IV showed four completely different national logics — crisis, absence, protectionism, convergence — all independently resolving in SpaceX’s favour. That’s not a coincidence produced by one clever argument. It’s evidence that the underlying product genuinely solves a real problem for enough different kinds of governments and carriers that the deals keep closing regardless of which specific justification a given country reaches for.

Inside the US, Part III’s femtocell revelation removes the single biggest objection to the domestic timeline — that becoming a true fourth carrier would require the kind of decade-long, hundred-billion-dollar terrestrial buildout that broke Dish Network. If Shotwell and Musk are right that existing rooftop Starlink dishes can be incrementally converted into small cellular base stations, SpaceX may be the first company in telecom history to build a nationwide terrestrial network largely out of hardware its customers already own, installed for an entirely different purpose. That’s not a normal capital expenditure problem. It’s closer to a software rollout riding on physical infrastructure that already exists.

What has to go right, in order

None of that optimism is unconditional, though. Line up the dependencies and the 2028 promise has at least four separate execution risks that all need to resolve favourably, plus one further risk that’s less about execution than politics.

First, the satellites themselves need to fly on schedule. Musk’s own language on the earnings call was aspirational rather than committed — V3 satellites are “about an order of magnitude more capable” than V2, with a targeted tenfold increase in launch volume, but SpaceX’s own critical-mass target for V3 broadband capacity is roughly a thousand satellites by the second quarter of 2027, which leaves less than a year of runway between that milestone and the stated end-of-2027 mobile commercial launch. Any meaningful delay to Starship’s flight cadence — and Starship, however impressive Flight 13’s heat-shield results were, is still a developmental vehicle attempting to reach a launch rate nobody has ever sustained — pushes the entire downstream timeline back with it.

Second, the femtocell concept has to actually work at the radio-frequency engineering level, at scale, without the kind of interference or reliability problems that would undermine the entire “we don’t need towers” pitch. Shotwell’s own reluctance to discuss capital expenditure for this piece of the plan reads as much like an admission that the engineering isn’t fully solved as it does trade-secret protection. This is the single largest technical unknown in the whole series, and it’s one no amount of spectrum ownership or clever business-model design can paper over if the physics doesn’t cooperate.

Third, the international partnership pace has to hold up against a genuinely improving field of competitors. Part II documented AST SpaceMobile’s slower but potentially superior architecture, Amazon Kuiper and Eutelsat OneWeb both approaching meaningful scale, and wholesale carriers like Veon explicitly playing multiple satellite operators against each other rather than committing early. Every year that passes without Starlink locking in exclusive or near-exclusive positions in more markets is a year AST’s bigger antennas have to mature and start winning carriers away on quality, particularly once broadband — not just texting — becomes the actual battleground.

Fourth, and perhaps most consequential: the domestic and international strategies have to keep working simultaneously without one undermining the other. Part III showed all three major American carriers closing ranks against Starlink Mobile with a joint spectrum-pooling venture the moment SpaceX signaled retail ambitions at home — a level of hostility SpaceX doesn’t face anywhere in its international partnerships, where it’s a welcomed supplier rather than a resented competitor. If the US retail fight turns genuinely ugly — regulatory challenges, an aggressive incumbent response, a slower-than-planned femtocell rollout — it’s an open question whether that drags down investor confidence and capital allocation for the international expansion running in parallel, given that both draw on the same balance sheet and the same executive attention.

Fifth, and more speculative than any of the previous four: the possibility that a foreign-owned satellite constellation carrying an increasing share of a country’s domestic phone traffic eventually triggers the same kind of national-security scrutiny that has already been applied to other cross-border technology infrastructure in recent years. Every country in Part IV granted SpaceX access for its own locally specific reasons — necessity, absence of an incumbent, a bet against a domestic telecom billionaire, competitive convergence — but none of those approvals happened in a vacuum insulated from broader geopolitics. A constellation that starts as a convenient satellite-messaging feature can, at sufficient scale, start to look to a government like critical communications infrastructure controlled by a foreign private company, a materially different category of concern than the one that governed any of this series’ four case studies at the time each deal was signed. Nothing in the public record suggests this is imminent in any market this series has covered. But it’s the kind of risk that tends to arrive quickly and reshape a timeline overnight, rather than announce itself gradually the way spectrum negotiations or engineering delays do, and it belongs on the list of things that could complicate the 2028 promise even though it isn’t currently on it.

The market’s own verdict, so far

It’s worth taking seriously what SpaceX’s own stock did on the day all of this was announced. Shares rose more than nine percent during the regular session on the strength of the revenue beat, then reversed by more than eight percent after hours once investors had time to digest the spending plans and the “very ambitious outlook,” in one analyst’s phrasing. That reversal is itself a kind of verdict: the market believes the growth story, and simultaneously isn’t yet convinced the execution risk across Starship, Starlink V3, mobile service, and AI infrastructure — four enormous bets, running concurrently, on one balance sheet — is fully priced in. Nothing in this series should be read as more confident than the market itself was on the day the promises were made.

It’s also worth noting what the company chose not to disclose, because the gaps in Shotwell’s and Johnsen’s answers are as informative as what they did share. Shotwell declined twice, in slightly different words, to discuss the capital expenditure required for the femtocell buildout — a level of reticence that stands out against how freely the call’s other numbers flowed. Johnsen, similarly, kept the connectivity segment’s guidance framed around broadband and enterprise growth rather than offering a specific mobile-revenue projection, even while confirming the qualitative scale of the ambition. A management team happy to give analysts a six-hundred-billion-dollar addressable market figure, and pointedly unwilling to give a capital expenditure number for the plan to capture it, is a management team that either hasn’t finished the internal math or doesn’t want the market pricing it in yet. Either reading argues for treating “end of 2027” as a stated intention rather than a modeled certainty.

What “2028” will actually mean if it arrives on schedule

Even in the best case, it’s worth being precise about what success looks like, because it isn’t “Starlink Mobile available identically everywhere.” Part IV’s four case studies suggest the honest picture is closer to a patchwork arriving at different speeds for different reasons: Ukraine-style crisis markets moving fastest because there’s nothing to negotiate around; leapfrog markets like much of Africa moving quickly because there’s no incumbent to displease; protectionist markets like India moving at whatever pace a government’s internal politics allow; and saturated, competitive markets like Japan and Canada settling into whatever split between Starlink and AST each country’s specific carrier landscape produces. “Global coverage by 2028” will likely be true in the sense that matters to SpaceX’s investors — service available in the overwhelming majority of countries — while remaining false in the sense that matters to any individual customer wondering whether their specific carrier, in their specific country, will actually be running on Starlink rather than a rival constellation.

The question this series opened with

Which brings this back, finally, to where the introduction to this series began. Who owns the air? For the moment, still nobody, in the pure sense — spectrum remains sovereign, licensed, and jealously guarded everywhere this series looked. But something has genuinely shifted since this series’ first instalment. The practical answer to “who owns the air” is drifting, market by market, toward whichever company can most quickly convince a government or a carrier that opening the door serves their own interests better than keeping it shut. SpaceX has, so far, been remarkably good at making that case — not through one universal argument, but through four or five different arguments, deployed country by country, each one calibrated to whatever that specific government or carrier actually needed to hear.

There’s a final irony worth sitting with, and it’s the one this series keeps circling back to without ever fully resolving: for all the spectrum deals, satellite constellations, and thousand-satellite production targets, the thing most likely to determine whether an ordinary consumer in a country like Canada actually switches to Starlink Mobile has nothing to do with any of it. It’s whether Starlink’s price beats what that consumer’s existing carrier will quietly offer over the phone, the moment they threaten to leave. Every satellite launched, every megahertz acquired, every carrier signed — all of it, in the end, still has to clear the oldest and least glamorous bar in this entire industry: is it actually worth switching for.

That’s a strange note to end a series about rockets and orbital constellations on, but it’s the honest one. This series opened by asking who owns the air, and closes by suggesting the more useful question was never really about ownership at all. Spectrum will keep being licensed, satellites will keep launching, and governments will keep treating the sky above their territory as sovereign right up until the moment a foreign company offers them a good enough reason to open the door. What decides whether any of it changes an ordinary person’s life is smaller and cheaper than anything this series has spent six parts describing: a phone call to a retention desk, and whether the person on the other end can still beat the price of looking up.

Two years from now, we’ll know whether the answer was yes.
