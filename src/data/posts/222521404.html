THE BIPARTISAN SOCIETY OF BALLOT COMPOSERS Established 1972 Motto: “Generally Considered Legal”

FOREWORD FROM THE SOCIETY’S SECRETARY

This manual is issued to operatives of both major parties. The Society takes no side. In more than fifty years we have found that both sides eventually need us, and both sides eventually deny having met us.

The Alaska revision was prompted by events. Members who have been reading the newspaper will know which ones. Members who have been in the newspaper are asked to stop reading here and call their lawyers, who, in at least one recent case, were already on the group chat.

CHAPTER 1: A BRIEF HISTORY OF THE CRAFT

The Society traces its origins to the spring of 1972, when a young California lawyer travelled the country recruiting volunteers to make life difficult for a field of presidential candidates. His methods were crude by modern standards: forged letters, fake flyers, unordered deliveries to opponents’ dinners. But his objective was sound, and it remains the foundation of our discipline. Choose your opponent before the voters do.

He served four and a half months. The Society does not regard this as a failure of strategy. It was a failure of execution, and of certain colleagues who later went shopping for plumbing. Members are reminded that the Society employs no plumbers, owns no wigs, and has never held a meeting in an underground garage.

Our founders had their own word for the craft. The publisher has asked us not to print it. Members of a certain age will know it; younger members may ask a retired senator.

A Note on Canadians. Do not forge letters about Canadians. One such letter in 1972 is still remembered north of the border, and the candidate concerned was later seen weeping in the New Hampshire snow, or possibly just standing in it. Canadians remember these things. They are polite about it, which is worse.

CHAPTER 2: FINDING YOUR SULLIVAN

2.1 Begin with the voter file. Assign a junior staffer to search the state’s voter records for every resident who shares a name with the incumbent. In a state with fewer people than the city of Toronto, expect a short list. Do not tell the staffer why. They will work it out, and they will remember.

2.2 Rank your candidates. The ideal Sullivan:

  • shares the incumbent’s name exactly, with at most a different middle initial;

  • lives somewhere reachable only by air or sea, which is inconvenient for reporters as well as for voters;

  • is retired, and therefore free in the summer;

  • dislikes the other side’s leader and likes yours;

  • has never donated to your side. (In practice he always has. See Chapter 8.)

2.3 Uncommon names. If the incumbent’s name is unusual, consider a different career. Republican members should note that the surname Peltola is not widely held in Alaska. Do not waste a summer looking.

CHAPTER 3: RECRUITMENT IN THE FIELD

3.1 Approved venues. Irish pubs, brew pubs, delis and small-town cafés. Never an underground garage. That venue has been compromised since 1972.

3.2 The pitch. Tell the recruit you are looking for independent-minded candidates and that your backers will support them with outside spending. Do not say who the backers are. If the recruit asks who is paying, smile and order another round.

3.3 Recruits from the other party. Have them change their registration before the filing deadline. Ideally, do this weeks before the deadline, not days. A registration switch just before the deadline is the first thing the other side’s lawyers will look for, and the first thing a judge will hear about.

3.4 Divine guidance. Some recruits will tell you they believe a higher power has called them to run. Do not correct them. Do not take credit either. This is the only situation in which modesty and deniability call for exactly the same behaviour.

3.5 Rejections. Not everyone will say yes. Some will say no and then answer a reporter’s questions in great detail. Assume every person you buy a beer for will one day be shown your photograph.

CHAPTER 4: COVER, ALIASES AND SOCIAL MEDIA

4.1 Aliases. A first name is a sufficient alias. Surnames invite questions. Our founding era, which favoured full false identities, is not a model.

4.2 Telephones. Your burner number should not carry the area code of your home state.

4.3 Email. Your email address should not contain your home state’s nickname. This is especially important if you come from a state whose nickname is a nut.

4.4 Social media. Do not post a photograph of the airport you have just flown into. If you must, post a photograph of a different airport.

4.5 When the story breaks. Make your accounts private. Members should know that doing so the same week the story breaks is itself a kind of statement, but it is the only one available.

CHAPTER 5: CLEARING THE BALLOT

This chapter serves both sides. Read the half that applies to you this cycle, and keep the other half for next cycle.

5.1 For the recruiting side. Once your Sullivan has advanced, a member of your own party may be occupying a slot you would prefer to give to someone more useful. Meet them at a café. Thank them, in writing, for their sacrifice. The vacancy will be filled by whoever finished next, ideally another long shot from the other party.

5.2 For the defending side. Ask the state’s election officials to remove the Sullivan as a decoy. Expect the courts to rule that officials have no power to judge a candidate’s motives. Recruit attorneys general from other states to file a brief. Expect to lose anyway.

5.3 Reciprocity. Two years ago, the side now defending was on the other side, helping a candidate stay on the same state’s ballot while he ran his campaign from a prison cell in New York. Every tactic in this manual has been used by the people currently complaining about it. The Society does not call this hypocrisy. It calls it a business model.

CHAPTER 6: MONEY AND PAPERWORK

6.1 Payments. Your allied super PAC may pay a firm you co-own. The PAC will later say it never paid you for any services. Both statements can be true at once in the same way that both Sullivans can be on the same ballot.

6.2 Unexplained filings. If your name appears on a request for broadcast advertising time on behalf of a PAC you say you have never heard of, the approved explanation is auto-fill.

6.3 A lesson from the founders. Cheques leave trails. Our founding era was undone, in part, by a single cheque that turned up in a Miami bank account. Since then the money has become less traceable and the people far more so.

CHAPTER 7: COMMUNICATIONS DISCIPLINE

7.1 Group chats. Do not type anything into a group chat that you would not want read aloud by a reporter, and then by a judge, and then by your mother.

7.2 Emojis. An emoji is a signed statement. Three heart emojis are three signed statements.

7.3 Focus groups. Do not joke in writing about how easily the participants could be fooled. Those voters will eventually read about it, and they vote.

7.4 Spelling. Misspelling your operative’s first name three different ways in your texts is not tradecraft. It just looks like you do not know him, which, according to the Chapter 8 denial, you do not.

7.5 Recording. Our founders were undone less by what they did than by what they recorded. Tape has since given way to group chats, which are worse. Tape at least had to be switched on.

CHAPTER 8: DENIAL

8.1 The approved statement. “Our campaign has had no involvement in any candidacy but its own.” Issue it early and often.

8.2 Detailed questions. Decline to answer all of them. The reporter will note in print that you declined to answer all of them. This cannot be avoided.

8.3 Superseded statements. If a later statement contradicts an earlier one, describe the earlier one as “inoperative.” The founders’ term remains in good standing.

8.4 The candidate. “The candidate was not directly involved” is not deniability. It is the sentence that confirms the candidate was indirectly involved.

8.5 Personnel. Fire staff gently. A former campaign manager is the natural predator of the ballot composition operative. Members should know that retractions do not work: a newspaper that has already conducted the interviews is not obliged to forget them.

CHAPTER 9: WHEN THE OTHER SIDE’S JUSTICE DEPARTMENT SUES

Step 1. Determine which side controls the Justice Department. Only one side has one at a time. If it is yours, turn to Appendix B, which the Society does not publish because the department writes its own.

Step 2. Read the trigger. If the lawsuit only takes effect when your candidate wins, congratulations. Your opponents have predicted your victory in a federal court filing.

Step 3. Do not panic. The suit is not designed to win in court. It is designed to be quoted on the night of the count.

Step 4. Note the difference. Your trick was carried out by private operatives using a voter file. The lawsuit was filed by the government, against a state, about votes not yet cast. The Society regards ballot composition as a private matter between consenting operatives. A government that sues to hold back a count has entered a different line of work. Our founders, who also worked in government, remain the best-known example of where that line of work leads.

Step 5. Accept both facts. No one can ask a secret-ballot voter which Sullivan they meant, so the lawsuit’s remedy cannot be carried out. Your trick, on the other hand, can be investigated by anyone with a voter file and a newspaper subscription. These two facts do not cancel each other out. Members should stop hoping that one of them will.

Step 6. If the president announces that he ordered the lawsuit himself, do nothing. He has done your work for you.
