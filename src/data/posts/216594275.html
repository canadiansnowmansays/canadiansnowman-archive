Essay 1,812 is about the only contest in the historical record that ended in a scoreless draw and produced three victors, and I must declare a conflict of interest at the top, as this series does: I am one of the victors. I live in the city that got burned in it. I ride an e-bike every morning along the shore of a lake it was fought across. My country’s founding myth was assembled substantially from its parts. The Numbers Game has covered many wars with disputed numbers; this is the only war in the files where the outcome itself is a number in dispute — nil–nil on the official ledger, and one–nothing in three different directions on the ledgers that matter, which are the ones nations keep in their heads.

The facts first, kept honest, because the mythologies will get their turn and they will need supervision.

In June of 1812, the United States declared war on Great Britain — the first declaration of war in the young republic’s history — over a docket of genuine maritime grievances: the Royal Navy, locked in its death-struggle with Napoleon, was seizing American ships trading with France and, more inflammatory still, boarding American vessels to impress sailors it claimed as British subjects, a practice that treated the distinction between the two English-speaking peoples as a clerical error the press-gang was entitled to correct. Thousands of American seamen, by the American count, were taken this way. The grievances were real. And the war aim, in the influential quarters that mattered, was not confined to the grievances: the invasion and absorption of British North America — the Canadas — was openly anticipated, and Thomas Jefferson, in the season of the declaration, supplied the quotation that every Canadian schoolchild eventually meets, predicting that the acquisition of the country as far as Quebec would be a mere matter of marching. I have been carrying that phrase for eighteen hundred essays. A mere matter of marching. It is the Most Fortunate Armada of North America, essay 1,588’s adjective reborn as a gerund: the outcome booked before the event, the result declared in the results column while it still lived in the future. Readers of this series know exactly what happens next to ventures that pre-count their conclusions, and it happened to this one on schedule.

The marching was attempted, repeatedly, across the Niagara and Detroit frontiers, and it went badly in ways that assembled, engagement by engagement, the founding shelf of Canadian memory: Isaac Brock, the British general who took Detroit with a bluff and an alliance and died at Queenston Heights in October 1812 leading a charge uphill, becoming in death the first hero of a country that did not yet exist; Tecumseh, the Shawnee leader whose confederacy fought beside the British for a homeland the peace would betray, and whose death in 1813 deserves and will get more than this clause; Laura Secord, who in June 1813 walked some thirty kilometres through occupied country to warn of an American attack, and whose reward was to be forgotten for decades and then commemorated, in the fullness of Canadian gratitude, chiefly by a chocolate company; the outnumbered stands at Châteauguay and Crysler's Farm that turned back the drives on Montreal. None of these, I am obliged to report, were fought principally by the ancestors of modern English Canada's imagination — the war was carried by British regulars, Indigenous confederacies, and French-Canadian militia in proportions the myth has always quietly rearranged. The count is the count. My country's founding battles were won substantially by people my country then spent a century declining to count properly, and a Numbers Game essay written from Toronto owes that sentence its place.

I have been inside Isaac Brock. I should explain. On Queenston Heights, above the river where he fell, stands his monument: a column fifty-six metres high with the general on top, and inside it a spiral staircase of 235 steps up to a small platform under his boots. I climbed it as a boy. All I kept of the visit is the staircase itself, narrow and winding, the stone close on both sides, round and round and up. The column reopened this July after a two-million-dollar restoration, so the staircase is still there for the next small boy who has no idea who the man was. Neither did I. I did not know I was climbing a war that ended nil–nil, or that this was the second column, the first having been blown up in 1840, probably by a sympathiser of the failed rebellions of 1837, which tells you the meaning of 1812 was still being argued with gunpowder a generation later. Brock lies underneath it, beside his aide-de-camp John Macdonell, who was mortally wounded leading the next charge up the same slope. The general has been buried four times, and there is something quietly Canadian about a national hero who has spent two centuries being moved to make room. I have no memory of counting the steps, which, given the man that small boy turned into, is the one part of the story I find hard to believe.

And Toronto — then York, the little capital of Upper Canada — enters the ledger in April 1813, when an American force landed, took the town, and burned its Parliament buildings before withdrawing. I want it noted with appropriate parochial feeling: they burned my city's legislature. The books of the Assembly, the Speaker's ceremonial mace — carried off; the mace was returned, I am delighted to report, in 1934, by order of President Roosevelt, a mere hundred and twenty-one years later, which by the institutional-lag standards of essays 1,582 and 1,633 qualifies as practically overnight. And sixteen months after York burned, in August 1814, a British force landed in Chesapeake Bay, brushed aside the defence at Bladensburg, walked into Washington, and burned the public buildings of the American capital — the Capitol, and the executive mansion whose scorched sandstone walls were afterward painted over in a colour that gave the building the only name anyone now uses for it. The burning of Washington is remembered in the United States, when it is remembered, as an outrage from a clear blue sky. It was, among other things, an invoice. York was the itemized entry; Washington was the settlement; and I record, in the interest of the full ledger, that the British commander spared the private homes of the city, that the whole occupation lasted roughly a day, and that a hurricane and tornado arrived mid-burning as if the weather, essay 1,588's most reliable auditor, wished to review the work personally.

Now the settlement, which is where the essay has been heading. By late 1814, both sides had had enough — the British because Napoleon’s fall had ended the maritime grievances at their source and Europe was the actual ledger; the Americans because the war was stalemated, the treasury drained, and New England contemplating secession over it. At Ghent, in Belgium, on December 24, 1814, the diplomats signed a peace whose operative principle was status quo ante bellum — the state of things before the war. No territory changed hands in the final account. No boundary moved. Impressment, the stated cause, went unmentioned in the treaty, the practice having already expired with the war that occasioned it. Two and a half years, tens of thousands dead across the armies, the fleets, the Indigenous nations, and the fevers that killed more than the muskets did — and the official score, certified in ink by both parties, was nil–nil. The most honest treaty in this series’ files: a document that declined to pretend anything had been won, because nothing had.

And then — I have been saving this, because it is the purest specimen of a phenomenon this series was invented to collect — the war’s most famous battle was fought after the war was over. News of the Christmas Eve treaty had to cross the Atlantic by sail, and it had not yet arrived when, on January 8, 1815, at New Orleans, Andrew Jackson’s improvised army shattered a British assault in half an hour of slaughter, the most lopsided American victory of the entire conflict — a victory achieved in a war that had legally concluded fifteen days earlier. The battle changed nothing, could change nothing, was fought over a result already filed in Belgium. And it changed everything, because it was the last number in, and the last number in is the one the public remembers: New Orleans arrived in the American press entangled with the news of peace, the sequence blurred, and the national memory recorded — has recorded ever since — a war that ended in triumph, sealed by Jackson’s guns, when the documentary truth is a draw signed before the guns spoke. It made Jackson a president. It made “we won” the American answer. The information lag of a sailing ship, filed under essay 1,609’s lead-time ledger, manufactured a victory out of a stalemate, and no correction ever caught the story, because corrections never catch stories; this series has watched them fail to since a disputed door in Wittenberg.

So attend to the final accounting, because it is unique in the record. The treaty says nil–nil. And three peoples walked away with a win. The Americans won, in their telling: they had stood up to the empire a second time, humiliated the Royal Navy in single-ship duels, and closed at New Orleans — the “Second War of Independence.” The British won, in theirs, insofar as they noticed the war at all: a colonial sideshow held to a draw with a fraction of their strength while the real business was settled at Waterloo — the empire retained everything, mission accomplished, next file. And the Canadians won most of all, in the telling I was raised in: invaded by a neighbour ten times their size who had booked the conquest as a matter of marching, they were still there when the music stopped — and for a people whose entire strategic condition, then and now, is continuing to exist beside the United States, still-being-there is not a euphemism for victory; it is the entire definition of it. Three national memories, one treaty, and the treaty is outvoted three to nothing. Here is the idea at the centre of this essay, and it may be the most important one in the whole project: a war’s outcome is not an event; it is an entry — and the entry is made separately, in each nation’s books, by each nation’s bookkeepers, and the reconciliation is never performed. There is no auditor above the national ledgers. Ghent could certify the boundaries; it could not certify the meaning, because meaning is posted domestically, and every country runs its own accounts payable of grievance and receivable of glory. The one party with no victorious ledger to keep — the Indigenous confederacies, who lost Tecumseh, the war, and at Ghent the British-sponsored buffer state that had been the whole point of their alliance — kept the only honest books of the conflict, and the honest books, as usual in this series, belonged to the people history invoiced.

Dior’s regard for territorial stalemates is professional: she has maintained one for six years against the husky two fences down, a war of daily declarations across an unmoving boundary, in which no ground has ever changed paws and both parties parade home victorious every single morning. Status quo ante bellum, renewed at dawn, in perpetuity. I asked her once, in the spirit of Ghent, whether the dispute might be settled by treaty. Her look conveyed what every foreign ministry knows and none admits: that the dispute is the settlement — that two parties who can win every day without cost have negotiated the most stable peace available on this earth, and that only a species that writes its ledgers down could be foolish enough to want it resolved.

The count moves on, as it does, and it leaves the battlefields for a while, thankfully, for a ship’s deck and a sharper set of eyes. The next landmark is essay 1,835, and it concerns a seasick young naturalist, five weeks among some black volcanic islands off the coast of Ecuador, and the greatest data-collection error in the history of science — a labelling failure, a column left blank, that very nearly cost the species its deepest discovery about itself. He forgot to write down which island. That is the whole mistake. What it nearly cost, and how the books were reconstructed, and what was in them — that is the essay, and after three wars’ worth of ledgers, a story about finches will be a mercy for us both.

Nil–nil, three winners, one honest set of books, and the last number in took the memory. Somewhere under the lake I ride beside, the war sleeps in both languages, still undefeated on every side.

See you at 1,835, when the column is blank and the answer is in it.
