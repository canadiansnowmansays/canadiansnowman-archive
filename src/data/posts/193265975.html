The notice went up on a Wednesday. Maggie removed a lost tabby notice from the bulletin board outside the Small Animal Clinic to make room for it, mentioned it to four clients before noon, and thought no more about it. Gerald had posted identical notices at the feed store, the municipal hall, and St. Andrew’s-on-the-Avon United Church — Reverend Faye had asked him for a copy when she saw him in the Tim Hortons on a Tuesday morning, and he had produced one from his left cardigan pocket, which she received without visible surprise, because eleven years in Elsinore had taught her that Gerald’s pockets were not to be underestimated.

The Elsinore Farm Bureau’s public meeting would be held at the Rotary Hall on Ontario Street, third Thursday of May, seven o’clock. The subject was the American tariffs on Canadian agricultural goods.

The hall held one hundred and twenty people. One hundred and fourteen came.

Gerald had prepared a summary document.

It ran to thirteen pages. The original eleven-page version had covered the tariff schedule by agricultural category — grain, livestock, dairy, processed goods — the timeline of implementation, the applicable retaliatory measures undertaken by the Canadian government, and the projected impact on Perth County’s primary agricultural sectors based on figures from the Ontario Ministry of Agriculture, Food and Rural Affairs. He had added two pages in February and revised them again in March.

The additions covered the following: on February twentieth, the United States Supreme Court had ruled, six votes to three, that the tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act did not have congressional authorisation. Chief Justice Roberts, writing for the majority, had noted that the president had claimed the independent power to impose tariffs on imports from any country, of any product, at any rate, for any amount of time, on the basis of two words in a 1977 statute. “Those words,” Roberts had written, “cannot bear such weight.” The IEEPA tariffs were struck down.

Within seventy-two hours, the president had imposed replacement tariffs of ten percent on most imports under a different statute. These tariffs were valid for one hundred and fifty days. They were in effect through July twenty-fourth. They would expire unless Congress voted to extend them.

The president had, separately, stated that two of the Supreme Court justices who voted against the tariffs — both of whom he had personally appointed — made him sick.

Gerald read all thirteen pages in the same measured voice he uses for all summary documents, which is the voice of a man who considers it his job to convey accurate information completely and who has never felt that editorial emphasis serves this purpose.

It took twenty-six minutes. The room was very quiet throughout.

When Gerald finished, he thanked the room for its attention. He sat down.

Mayor Doug spoke next. He wore his good coat. He said that Elsinore stood with its farming community in this difficult time, and that the municipal council was monitoring the situation closely, and that he had written to the MP for Perth-Wellington expressing the community’s concern.

Someone in the back row said that standing with the farming community was not a trade policy.

Mayor Doug acknowledged this. He said he understood the frustration. He sat down.

Wayne spoke briefly. He noted that forward bookings from American tourists for the summer season were down seventeen percent compared to the same period last year, and that several group bookings had been cancelled with reference to the current political climate. He presented these figures without editorial comment. The room looked at him for a moment. He sat down.

Reverend Faye sat in the fifth row, slightly left of centre. She was not a farmer. She was there because her congregation contained farmers, and when something matters to your congregation it matters to you, and she had learned in eleven years at St. Andrew’s-on-the-Avon that showing up is a form of theology that requires no footnotes.

She had not brought a notebook. She did not need one. She listened with the complete attention of a woman who was simultaneously present in the Rotary Hall and already, somewhere in a quieter part of her mind, in next Sunday’s pulpit, finding the shape of something she could not yet name. She had been preparing a sermon for three weeks on the subject of what we owe each other across borders. She had three drafts. None of them were right yet. She was listening for what was missing.

She did not speak when the floor opened. She did not need to. She had what she came for.

Two Sundays later she preached a sermon at St. Andrew’s that did not mention the Farm Bureau meeting, did not mention the tariffs, did not mention Perth County agriculture by name. It was about the parable of the wheat and the tares, and about the difficulty of knowing, from the ground, which is which, and about patience as an act of faith rather than passivity. Three farmers in the congregation felt specifically understood. They could not have said exactly what she said.

The Swan Warden attended. She was not a farmer. She was present because she attends public meetings in Elsinore, which is a thing she does, and has done for twenty-two years, because someone should. She sat in the second row. She took notes in her dark green notebook in her small, precise handwriting. She said nothing until the floor opened, at which point she raised her hand and noted that the Avon River watershed was downstream of several of the affected agricultural operations, and that any significant change in farming practice resulting from economic pressure — fallow land, changed chemical application, altered drainage — would have downstream implications that the Swan Habitat Preservation Fund of Perth County would need to monitor. She said she was noting this for the record and would be submitting a formal environmental observation to the municipal council.

She sat down. Gerald wrote a note to create a file for it.

The Perth County farmer sat in the third row. He had driven in from his farm that morning for a different reason entirely — a part for the tractor that the dealer in Mitchell had to order from Listowel — and had seen the notice at the feed store and stayed.

When the floor opened, he was asked to speak. He stood.

He spoke for four minutes.

He talked about what the farm grew and where it went. He talked about the American buyers he had sold to for eleven years. He talked about February, when the IEEPA tariffs were struck down, and about the forty-eight hours after, when his buyers had briefly called to discuss resuming normal terms, and about the seventy-two hours after that, when the replacement tariffs arrived and the conversations stopped again.

He talked about the fuel costs, the equipment costs, the seed costs. He talked about the margin, which had become very narrow.

He said he did not know what the solution was. He was not a trade lawyer. He was not a constitutional scholar. He understood the legal situation was complicated and the courts were doing what courts were supposed to do.

What he understood was that the man who created the tariffs had said the judges who struck them down — including two he had personally appointed — made him sick. And that within three days the tariffs had come back under a different law.

He said: those words cannot bear such weight.

He was quoting Chief Justice Roberts. He did not say this. He had read it in the summary Gerald had circulated before the meeting and it had stayed with him, the way certain precise sentences stay. He sat down.

The room was quiet in a different way than before. Several people looked at their hands. Reverend Faye, in the fifth row, wrote three words in the margin of her order of service. Gerald noted, in the margin of his own summary document, the time: eight forty-seven.

Carly had come to cover the meeting for the Beacon. She was in the back row with her notebook and her phone, and she had been writing steadily since Gerald began his summary, and she wrote through Wayne’s numbers and through the Swan Warden’s observation and she wrote down every word of the farmer’s four minutes, word for word, which she had not done for anyone else, because when someone speaks in a way that is clearly complete and complete in a way that is clearly true, you write it down.

She filed her story that night. Norman read it the next morning, removed one sentence he considered editorialising, and moved the farmer’s quote to the top of the story, above the fold. He told Carly the next day that leading with the quote was the correct instinct. She said she had almost put it at the bottom. He said she had been right not to. This was the longest editorial conversation they had had in eight months.

The story ran on page one. Gerald filed the clipping.

Jim had walked past the Rotary Hall at six forty-five with the labrador. He had seen the trucks in the parking lot, the lights on inside, the number of people arriving. He had looked up the current tariff situation online that morning — it had taken fourteen minutes to read the Ontario Federation of Agriculture summary he had bookmarked in February when the SCOTUS ruling came down.

His assessment: the legal situation was in flux, the replacement tariffs were temporary pending congressional action, and the odds of a clean resolution before the July expiry were not good.

He walked the labrador home. He went home himself.

He told Maggie at Sunday dinner that the farmer had spoken, which was notable. Maggie said the farmer had brought his border collie in the following morning. The pad that had been torn in February was fully healed. She had told him the dog was in good shape. He had thanked her, paid, and left. She had not asked about the meeting. He had not mentioned it. The dog was fine.

Jim passed the bread. He said he’d been thinking about something the Chief Justice wrote in the tariff ruling. He said it seemed like it meant something larger than tariffs, though he couldn’t quite say what.

Maggie said she thought she knew what he meant.

They ate their dinner.

A Note for the Record

The Supreme Court ruled six to three. Those words cannot bear such weight. Within seventy-two hours, new tariffs arrived under different authority. The cost to Perth County farms did not change. The legal basis changed. The cost did not.

The man who imposed the tariffs has never, to anyone’s knowledge, seen a farm. He considers the inconvenience of farmers a variable in a negotiation about something else entirely, which is either a statement about his priorities or a description of what happens when the people making decisions about the land have never stood in it.

In Perth County, the seed was in the ground before the ruling and the seed was in the ground after it. The farmer drove home from the meeting and was up before dawn because the work does not pause for legal proceedings any more than it pauses for trade negotiations. He put the seed in. Same as every year.

In Perth County, this is either faith or stubbornness. It has never been considered necessary to determine which.
