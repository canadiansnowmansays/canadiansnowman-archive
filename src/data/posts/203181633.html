There are more Indians living outside India than there are Canadians living in Canada.

The Indian diaspora — counting both citizens and people of Indian origin — numbers approximately 32 million, spread across every inhabited continent, embedded in the economies and politics of countries from Fiji to the United Kingdom, from the Gulf states to the Caribbean, from East Africa to the corner stores of Toronto. It is the largest diaspora in the world, and it is not a single thing any more than India itself is a single thing. The Gujarati motel owner in Texas, the Sikh truck driver in British Columbia, the Tamil engineer in Singapore, the Keralite nurse in the Persian Gulf, and the Punjabi farmer in New Zealand share a country of origin and almost nothing else.

I live inside this diaspora. I have been living inside it for two decades, in a townhouse in Toronto where the breakfast table accommodates Gujarati, Punjabi, Hindi, English, and Dzongkha. The men who have passed through my house over those years — roommates, boarders, whatever the word is for people who share your kitchen and your mortgage payment — have been drawn overwhelmingly from the Indian migration stream that has reshaped Canada more profoundly than any other demographic force since the postwar European immigration wave.

This essay examines where they came from, why they left, what they carry with them, and what it means — for India, for Canada, and for the idea of democracy — that a nation of 1.4 billion people has exported 32 million of its citizens to the world.

I. The Waves

Indian emigration is not new. It is as old as Indian civilization. Tamil traders sailed to Southeast Asia two thousand years ago and left Hindu temples in Cambodia, Indonesia, and Vietnam. Indian merchants operated in the ports of East Africa and the Persian Gulf long before the British arrived. The diaspora has roots that predate colonialism by centuries.

But the modern diaspora — the one that put Indians in my kitchen and in the CEO’s office at Google — was shaped by three distinct waves.

The first wave was colonial. Beginning in the 1830s, after the abolition of slavery in the British Empire, the colonial government established a system of indentured labour that transported Indian workers to British plantation colonies: Mauritius, Trinidad, Guyana, Fiji, South Africa, Malaysia, and East Africa. The system was, in the blunt assessment of Hugh Tinker’s landmark study, “a new system of slavery.” Workers were recruited — sometimes by deception, sometimes by coercion, always from the most vulnerable populations — and shipped to distant colonies under contracts that bound them for years, in conditions that bore a striking resemblance to the servitude the abolition was supposed to end.

The descendants of these indentured labourers form the oldest layer of the Indian diaspora. They are the Indo-Trinidadians, the Indo-Fijians, the Indian South Africans. Gandhi began his political career among them. Their communities have produced prime ministers (Trinidad), coups (Fiji), and one of the most distinctive cultural fusions in the world — the Indo-Caribbean identity, which blends Hindu and Muslim traditions with Creole culture in ways that would be unrecognizable in the subcontinent.

The second wave was professional. Beginning in the 1960s and accelerating after the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 in the United States and parallel liberalizations in Canada, the UK, and Australia, educated Indians — doctors, engineers, scientists, academics — began emigrating to the West. This was the brain drain wave, and it was driven by a combination of push factors (the licence raj, limited opportunity, bureaucratic suffocation) and pull factors (Western economies hungry for skilled labour, immigration policies that selected for education and professional credentials).

This wave produced the Indian-American and Indian-Canadian professional class that has, over two generations, become the highest-income immigrant group in both countries. It produced the doctors who staff rural hospitals across North America, the engineers who built Silicon Valley, and the academics who populate every major university. It also produced a diaspora elite that maintains deep connections to India — financially, culturally, politically — while being thoroughly assimilated into the professional cultures of their adopted countries.

The third wave is post-liberalization. Beginning in the 1990s and surging in the 2000s and 2010s, this wave is larger, more diverse, and more complex than its predecessors. It includes IT professionals like my roommate Rikesh, who came to Canada equipped with the technical education that post-1991 India excels at producing. It includes Punjabi workers like Gurneet, who came through diaspora networks that have been operating for over a century. It includes international students — India is now the largest source of international students in Canada — many of whom come on study permits with the intention of transitioning to permanent residency.

This third wave has changed the character of the diaspora. It is younger, more working-class, more linguistically diverse, and more politically connected to the homeland than the professional wave that preceded it. It is also more visible — and more vulnerable to the backlash that visibility invites.

II. The Canadian Story

Canada is the laboratory of the Indian diaspora’s future, and the experiment is not entirely comfortable.

People of Indian origin — including those from Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka who share South Asian heritage — constitute roughly 7 percent of Canada’s population. Indo-Canadians are the country’s largest visible minority group. They are concentrated in the Greater Toronto Area, the Greater Vancouver Area, and the agricultural regions of British Columbia and Alberta. They are in Parliament, in provincial legislatures, in city councils, in the RCMP, in the judiciary, and in the Cabinet. The leader of one of Canada’s major federal parties, Jagmeet Singh, is a turbaned Sikh — a fact that would have been unthinkable a generation ago and that is now unremarkable.

The economic contribution is immense. Indo-Canadians operate businesses, staff hospitals, drive trucks, build houses, write software, and teach in schools at every level. The Punjabi agricultural community in British Columbia has transformed the province’s farming economy. The IT professionals in Toronto and Waterloo — Rikesh’s cohort — are embedded in Canada’s technology sector. The international students pay tuition fees that subsidize Canadian universities.

But the relationship is strained, and it is strained in ways that connect directly to the main series.

The Khalistan question is the most visible tension. The Sikh diaspora in Canada — roughly 770,000 people, the largest Sikh community outside Punjab — includes a politically active minority that supports the creation of an independent Sikh homeland, Khalistan. The movement was suppressed within India after the 1984 crisis but has persisted in the diaspora, particularly in British Columbia and Ontario. India regards the Khalistan movement as a terrorist threat. Canada regards it as a matter of free speech and political expression — at least until the Nijjar assassination forced a reckoning with the possibility that Indian state actors were conducting targeted killings on Canadian soil.

The Nijjar affair, which I covered in Part XI of this series, has not been resolved. It has been managed — diplomatically, carefully, with the awkward pragmatism of two countries that need each other economically and cannot afford a permanent rupture. This week, Indian Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal led the largest Indian trade delegation ever sent abroad to Canada, meeting with Prime Minister Mark Carney. The diplomatic signal is clear: the relationship is too important to abandon. It is also too damaged to pretend is healthy.

Meanwhile, Hindu nationalism has extended into the Canadian diaspora. Hindu-Canadian organizations aligned with the Sangh Parivar operate in Canadian cities, organizing cultural events, temple activities, and — critics argue — political mobilization that imports the BJP’s communal framework into Canadian multiculturalism. Caste discrimination has been documented in Canadian workplaces and community organizations. The politics of the homeland do not stay in the homeland.

III. The Gulf

The largest concentration of Indian workers outside the subcontinent is not in North America or the UK. It is in the Persian Gulf.

Approximately 8.5 million Indians work in the Gulf Cooperation Council countries — Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Oman, and Bahrain. They are the construction workers who built Dubai’s skyline. They are the domestic workers who staff the homes of Gulf elites. They are the engineers, the accountants, the teachers, the shopkeepers who make the Gulf economies function. Without Indian labour, the Gulf states would stop.

The conditions under which many of these workers labour are, by any reasonable standard, exploitative. The kafala (sponsorship) system ties a worker’s legal status to their employer, creating a power imbalance that facilitates wage theft, passport confiscation, and working conditions that international human rights organizations have repeatedly condemned. Indian construction workers in Qatar died building the stadiums for the 2022 World Cup. The numbers are disputed. The deaths are not.

India’s government has been reluctant to confront the Gulf states about labour conditions, for the same reason India is reluctant to confront Russia about Ukraine: the relationship is too valuable. Gulf remittances — money sent home by Indian workers — totalled over $110 billion in 2022, making India the largest recipient of remittances in the world. That money sustains families, funds education, builds houses, and powers local economies across Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Uttar Pradesh, and Bihar. The workers suffer. The families survive. The government calculates.

IV. What the Diaspora Carries

The Indian diaspora carries India’s contradictions into every country it enters.

It carries caste. Studies in the United States, the UK, and Canada have documented caste-based discrimination in diaspora communities — in hiring, in social exclusion, in marriage practices, in temple governance. The California Department of Fair Employment and Housing added caste to its list of protected categories. The debate over whether caste discrimination exists in the West is, for Dalits in the diaspora, not a debate. It is their daily experience.

It carries religion. Hindu temples, Sikh gurdwaras, and Muslim mosques in diaspora cities replicate the devotional and community structures of the homeland — and, in some cases, the tensions. The communal polarization that the BJP has intensified within India does not stop at the border. WhatsApp groups, YouTube channels, and social media platforms circulate the same divisive content in Toronto and New Jersey that they circulate in Varanasi and Ahmedabad.

It carries language. The five languages in my kitchen are a miniature of a phenomenon that plays out in every major diaspora city. Hindi film, Punjabi music, Tamil television, Gujarati business networks, Malayalam church services — the linguistic diversity of India is reproduced in the diaspora with remarkable fidelity.

And it carries aspiration. The Indian who emigrates — whether it is Rikesh coming to Toronto for IT opportunities, or Gurneet coming through Punjabi family networks, or a Keralite nurse going to Riyadh — carries a bet. The bet is that somewhere outside India, the talent and work ethic that India produced but could not fully employ will find its reward. The bet has paid off spectacularly for some. It has exploited others. It has changed every country it has touched.

V. The Return Question

The great unresolved question of the Indian diaspora is whether it will come home.

China has benefited enormously from return migration — Chinese professionals who trained in the West and returned with capital, connections, and expertise. India has not, or not yet. The brain drain that began in the 1960s has slowed but not reversed. India produces the talent. The West employs it. The remittances flow back, but the people — mostly — do not.

Modi’s government has courted the diaspora aggressively, hosting Howdy Modi rallies in Houston and Namaste Trump events in Ahmedabad, treating the diaspora as both a political constituency and an economic resource. The appeal works: many diaspora Indians are proud of Modi’s projection of Indian power and receptive to his civilizational narrative. The BJP has won the diaspora’s hearts, or a large portion of them, more successfully than any previous Indian government.

But winning hearts is not the same as bringing people home. The India that produces world-class engineers also produces the conditions that drive them to leave — bureaucratic sclerosis, infrastructure gaps, pollution, the grind of a system that, for all its improvements, still makes daily life harder than it needs to be. Rikesh has been in my house for ten years. He is not going back. Gurneet has been here for two. The trajectory is clear.

The diaspora is India’s greatest export and India’s greatest loss. It is proof of what Indian civilization produces and evidence of what the Indian state cannot yet retain. It is, like everything else in this series, a contradiction that India contains rather than resolves.

With thanks to every Indian man who has passed through my kitchen and taught me, over chai and argument, what no textbook could.
