Elon Musk wielding a chainsaw in front of the White House — the defining image of DOGE’s performative approach to government efficiency.

SpaceX works because Elon Musk hired the best aerospace engineers, gave them targets the industry considered impossible, and let the engineering determine the solution. The engineers iterated through failure — three failed launches before the fourth succeeded — but the iteration was disciplined, data-driven, and constrained by physics. When a Falcon 1 failed because of a corroded nut, the team identified the nut, replaced it, and launched again. When the second flight experienced roll control malfunction, the team diagnosed the software, corrected it, and launched again. The process was rigorous because rockets do not forgive sloppy thinking. The physics provides immediate, unambiguous feedback. Build it wrong and it explodes on live television.

Government does not work like this.

Government is not a rocket. It is not a machine that fails visibly when a component is defective. It is a system of interlocking obligations — legal, constitutional, contractual, procedural — that evolved over centuries to protect citizens from the arbitrary exercise of power. The inefficiencies Musk identified are real. Federal procurement is often absurd. Regulatory overlap is genuine. Bureaucratic inertia wastes money. These are legitimate problems that serious people have studied for decades.

But the inefficiencies are not corrosion on a fuel line. They are not software bugs that can be patched with a firmware update. Many of them exist because the alternative — a government that moves fast and breaks things — breaks veterans’ benefits, Social Security payments, food safety inspections, air traffic control, and the regulatory frameworks that keep Musk’s own rockets from falling on populated areas.

DOGE — the Department of Government Efficiency — was Musk’s attempt to apply first principles thinking to government. It was, by the standard this series has established, the most spectacular failure of first principles thinking in his career. And unlike the first three Falcon 1 failures, this one did not produce useful data. It produced chaos, human suffering, and a concentrated demonstration of what happens when an engineer mistakes a complex adaptive system for a machine.

A Falcon 1 explosion — dramatic, visible, and immediately instructive. Rockets provide unforgiving feedback. Government systems do not

Musk entered the Trump administration in an advisory capacity following Trump’s inauguration in January 2025. The arrangement was structurally unprecedented: a private citizen with massive financial interests across multiple regulated industries — electric vehicles, autonomous driving, rocket launches, satellite telecommunications, AI — accepting a formal role inside the government that regulates those industries. The conflict-of-interest questions were obvious and were raised immediately. They were not resolved. They were ignored, in the manner of the Trump administration’s approach to institutional norms generally.

The specific conflicts are worth enumerating because they illustrate the structural impossibility of a man with Musk’s portfolio operating inside the government that regulates it. Tesla’s autonomous vehicles require NHTSA approval. SpaceX’s launches require FAA licensing. Starlink’s satellite deployment requires FCC approval. xAI’s data practices will eventually face FTC scrutiny. Tesla’s factory emissions are regulated by the EPA. Each of these agencies falls within the executive branch where Musk held his advisory role. A man whose companies are collectively worth over $2 trillion, and whose personal wealth exceeds $800 billion, was advising the government on how to restructure the agencies that determine whether his products reach the market.

The standard remedy for conflicts of interest is divestiture or recusal. Musk divested nothing and recused from nothing. The argument, advanced by his defenders, was that DOGE’s mandate — cutting government spending broadly — did not involve specific regulatory decisions about Musk’s companies. The counter-argument, advanced by everyone who understands how government works, was that an advisor with the ear of the President and the ability to shape agency budgets, personnel decisions, and organisational structures has influence that extends far beyond his formal mandate. You do not need to write the regulation to influence the regulator. You need to determine the regulator’s budget, staff, and institutional priorities — which is precisely what DOGE proposed to do.

DOGE’s stated mission was to identify and eliminate wasteful government spending. The approach was to bring in teams of young technologists — many of them from Musk’s companies or the broader Silicon Valley network — to audit federal agencies, identify redundancies, and recommend cuts. The model was explicitly imported from the technology industry: move fast, question everything, accept that disruption creates short-term pain for long-term gain.

The model does not translate. Technology companies can move fast because the worst consequence of a software bug is a service outage. The worst consequence of a government “bug” is that a veteran does not receive their disability payment, a federal employee loses their health insurance, or a safety inspection does not happen and someone dies. The feedback loops are different. In technology, you ship a broken feature, measure the failure, and iterate. In government, you cut a programme, and the consequences may not be visible for months or years — and the people who suffer the consequences have no “undo” button, no product manager to file a ticket with, no version rollback.

Bureaucracy as red tape and sludge — real inefficiencies exist, but they often serve as safeguards. Cutting without understanding the trade-offs creates new problems

The results were predictable and predicted. DOGE teams, armed with access to federal systems and an ideological conviction that government is inherently wasteful, began identifying programmes for elimination. The criteria were not transparent. The process was not subject to the standard administrative procedures — notice-and-comment rulemaking, congressional oversight, judicial review — that constrain federal agencies. The speed Musk demanded was incompatible with the deliberative processes that exist precisely to prevent rapid, uninformed decisions about programmes affecting millions of people.

Federal employees were fired or placed on leave. Contracts were frozen. Programmes that served vulnerable populations were disrupted. In some cases, the disruptions were reversed by courts — federal judges issued injunctions ordering the reinstatement of employees and the restoration of funding that had been cut without proper administrative procedure. In other cases, they were reversed by the administration itself when the political consequences became apparent. In most cases, the stated savings were either illusory (double-counted, already planned, or based on misunderstandings of how federal budgets work) or achieved by transferring costs rather than eliminating them.

The human cost was not abstract. Federal workers — many of them mid-career professionals with families, mortgages, and children in school — received termination notices generated by processes that did not follow standard civil service procedures. Some were rehired after courts intervened. Others were not. The uncertainty itself was damaging: agencies responsible for food safety, air traffic control, veterans’ health care, and environmental monitoring found their operations disrupted not by policy decisions subjected to debate and review but by rapid cuts driven by headlines and engagement metrics.

Musk’s public posture throughout was performative rather than substantive. He posted chainsaw emojis. He tweeted about eliminating waste. He shared screenshots of government spending line items stripped of context, inviting his followers to express outrage at numbers whose meaning required understanding the programmes they funded. The discourse was designed for X — for the algorithmic amplification of simplified outrage — not for the deliberative process that governance requires.

The comparison to SpaceX is instructive. When SpaceX built a valve in-house rather than purchasing it from an aerospace supplier, the in-house valve performed the same function at lower cost. The physics was the same. The function was preserved. The cost was reduced. This is engineering.

When DOGE eliminated a government programme, the function it served did not disappear. The need — for veterans’ care, food inspection, environmental monitoring, workplace safety enforcement — persisted. The programme was the government’s mechanism for meeting that need. Eliminating the programme without an alternative mechanism does not save money. It transfers the cost from the federal budget to the individuals who no longer receive the service — a transfer that does not show up in DOGE’s efficiency calculations but shows up with devastating clarity in the lives of the people affected.

The attention cost to the Musk stack was substantial.

During the months Musk devoted significant time to DOGE in early and mid-2025, Tesla was navigating a period that demanded executive focus. The refreshed Model Y — codenamed Juniper — was launching globally, the most important product refresh since the Model 3 Highland. FSD v12 and v13 were in critical development, representing the transition to the end-to-end neural network architecture that would underpin the entire autonomy strategy. The Austin robotaxi pilot was being prepared for its June 2025 launch — Tesla’s first public deployment of unsupervised rides, the proving ground for everything that followed. Optimus Gen 2 was being deployed inside Tesla factories. The Cybercab was moving from prototype to production planning. The January 2026 earnings call — where Musk would announce the Model S and X discontinuation, the $20+ billion CapEx commitment, and the transformation thesis — was being prepared in the background.

Every one of these initiatives required the kind of obsessive, detail-oriented engineering leadership that made SpaceX and Tesla successful. Instead, Musk was in Washington — tweeting about government waste, feuding with federal employees, posting chainsaw emojis, and spending time on a vanity project that consumed the attention a $1.3 trillion company needed directed at engineering, not politics.

Tesla sales performance diverging sharply in key markets like Europe — measurable brand and business damage during Musk’s DOGE period.

The brand damage was measurable. In Europe, where Musk’s association with the Trump administration and far-right politics was particularly toxic, Tesla’s market share declined sharply. European consumers who had been willing to overlook Musk’s Twitter provocations were not willing to overlook a direct role in an administration whose trade policies, immigration stance, and withdrawal from climate agreements were deeply unpopular across the continent. Tesla lost nearly half its European market share in 2025. The Musk brand, which had once been an asset that made Teslas cool, became a liability that made Teslas political.

In the United States, the damage was subtler but real. Institutional investors — pension funds, index funds, ESG-screened portfolios — began asking whether Musk’s political activities constituted a material risk. The UBS upgrade in April 2026, documented in my Tesla series, cited “sentiment and narrative” as primary drivers of Tesla’s stock volatility. When a Wall Street analyst identifies the CEO’s personal behaviour as a stock-moving variable, the CEO’s personal behaviour has become a governance problem.

Here is the first principles test.

First principles thinking asks: what is government inefficiency actually made of, and what is the most efficient solution?

The answer, from decades of public administration research, is that government inefficiency arises from overlapping mandates, procurement regulations designed to prevent corruption (which also prevent speed), civil service protections designed to prevent politicisation (which also prevent flexibility), and the fundamental challenge of running organisations that must serve every citizen rather than a profitable subset.

These are real problems. They are also structural problems with structural causes. Addressing them requires understanding the trade-offs embedded in the structures — the reason civil service protections exist, the reason procurement rules are complex, the reason administrative procedures take time. An engineer who understands these trade-offs can propose reforms that preserve the protective functions while improving efficiency. This is difficult, unglamorous work that requires years of engagement with the details of administrative law and organisational design.

Musk did not do this work. He arrived with the Silicon Valley conviction that complexity is always the enemy and that any system can be simplified by someone smart enough. He was smart enough. He was not knowledgeable enough. And the gap between intelligence and knowledge — a gap that does not exist in physics, where the equations are available to anyone smart enough to solve them — is the gap where DOGE failed.

SpaceX works because Musk understood rocket engineering deeply enough to ask the right first principles questions. DOGE failed because Musk did not understand government deeply enough to ask any first principles questions at all. He asked Silicon Valley questions — why does this take so long? why are there so many people? why can’t we just cut this? — and received answers that a first principles thinker would have recognised as superficial. Why does procurement take so long? Because the alternative is corruption. Why are there so many people? Because the services require them. Why can’t we just cut this? Because people depend on it.

These are not the answers an engineer wants. They are the answers the system provides. And a first principles thinker who ignores the system’s answers because they are inconvenient is not thinking from first principles. He is thinking from arrogance.

DOGE is over. Musk’s formal advisory role has concluded. The damage to specific programmes is being assessed and, in some cases, repaired by courts and congressional action. The lasting impact on government efficiency is approximately zero — the programmes that needed reform still need reform, and the political toxicity of the DOGE brand has made reform harder, not easier, because any future efficiency initiative will be compared to the chaos Musk created.

Musk in the White House with Trump — the unprecedented blending of private empire and public power that defined DOGE.

The lasting impact on the Musk stack is the demonstration it provides. DOGE proves that first principles thinking has a domain of applicability. In physics and engineering — rockets, cars, chips, satellites — first principles analysis produces transformative results because the substrate is governed by equations that do not have opinions. In government, economics, social systems, and human behaviour — domains where the substrate is people, institutions, laws, and history — first principles analysis produces arrogant oversimplification unless combined with deep domain knowledge that Musk does not have and did not acquire.

The engineer who asked “what does a rocket actually cost?” and changed the aerospace industry asked “what does government actually cost?” and changed nothing except the lives of the people whose programmes were disrupted. The difference is not intelligence. The difference is that rockets are machines and governments are not.

There is a deeper irony that the first principles framework of this series exposes. Musk’s entire career demonstrates that the most consequential insights come from asking questions that the establishment considers naive or impertinent. Why are rockets expendable? No one in aerospace asked this because the answer seemed obvious. Musk asked it and changed the industry. Why are electric cars impractical? No one in automotive asked this because the answer seemed settled. Musk asked it and created a market.

But the question why is government inefficient? is not a question no one has asked. It is a question that has been studied by thousands of scholars, practitioners, and reformers over decades. The literature on government efficiency fills libraries. The answers are known: structural incentives, legal constraints, democratic accountability requirements, the inherent complexity of serving 330 million people with conflicting needs and preferences. Musk’s failure with DOGE was not that he asked the question. It was that he did not bother to learn that the question had already been asked, extensively answered, and that the answers were more complicated than a spreadsheet and a chainsaw emoji could address.

In rocketry, the naive question was the right question because no one had asked it. In government, the naive question was the wrong approach because everyone had asked it, and Musk’s contribution was not insight but arrogance. The first principles thinker who succeeds is the one who asks questions no one else has asked. The first principles thinker who fails is the one who asks questions everyone has asked and assumes the previous answers were wrong because they were inconvenient.
