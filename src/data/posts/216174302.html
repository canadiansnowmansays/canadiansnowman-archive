Stephen Gordon

Part VIII of this series dealt with the single most consequential exchange of the September 15 Senate Judiciary Committee hearing — Kash Patel’s ambiguous, arguably reversed position on whether FBI personnel might appear at polling places in November. That exchange deserved its own essay. But it was only one strand of a two-hour hearing that, taken as a whole, confirms something this series has been tracking since Part VII: four months on from his last appearance before Congress, nothing about the underlying pattern has changed. If anything, Tuesday supplied the clearest evidence yet that Patel’s problem is not a bad month. It is a director who cannot get through a routine hearing without the hearing itself becoming the story.

The Lifeline He Wouldn’t Take

The hearing’s most-covered exchange, ironically, was not about elections at all. It was about the FBI’s hiring standards — and it produced the odd spectacle of a Republican senator visibly trying to help Patel out of a hole, only to watch him keep digging.

The trouble began with reporting, first surfaced by CBS News, that the Bureau had quietly loosened its hiring bar. Under the revised standards, the FBI would now reportedly consider applicants who had hired prostitutes fewer than three times, stolen from an employer more than three years earlier, or engaged in sexual activity with animals before the age of 18. Senator Dick Durbin of Illinois, the committee’s ranking Democrat, raised the changes first. Patel’s explanation was that the bestiality provision existed to protect trafficking victims — people who had been coerced into such acts and should not, in his telling, be disqualified from Bureau service as a result. “We did not want to punish victims of bestiality,” he said. “We are not going to target victims.” It is a sympathetic-sounding rationale. It is also, according to CBS’s reporting on the underlying memo, not what the policy actually says: the standards reportedly draw no distinction between an applicant who was coerced and one who was not.

That should have been the end of it, or close to it. Instead, Senator John Kennedy of Louisiana — a Republican, and no reliable Patel critic — used his own questioning time to try to walk the director back from the ledge. “I’m really trying to understand,” Kennedy said, rubbing his face. “’Cause I listened to the exchange between you and Senator Durbin, and I’ve read a few news accounts, and I’m confused.” He gave Patel the chance to simply affirm that the Bureau still maintained real standards. Patel took the opening to defend the changes as necessary for faster hiring of qualified agents — and then, unprompted, returned to the bestiality question and repeated the trafficking-victim rationale, even though nothing in Kennedy’s question required him to.

Kennedy could not resist the obvious joke — “So you disqualified the animal?” — but the senator’s real message was closer to exasperation than mockery. “I understand the game,” he told Patel. “People try to make you look bad. We do the same thing when the other party’s in office.” That is about as candid a description of modern hearing dynamics as a sitting senator is likely to offer on the record, and it was, in context, an offer: acknowledge that this was overblown, give a tighter answer, and move on. Patel’s response was to explain, again, that human traffickers sometimes force victims into bestiality and that the Bureau did not want to punish those victims — the same rationale, unconnected to the actual text of the policy, that had started the controversy in the first place. Kennedy’s exasperation was audible: “Someone came to you and recommended this? And when you saw bestiality — I say this with respect — the person who recommended it, why didn’t you just say, ‘What planet did you parachute in from? Why do I wanna lead with my chin and get into bestiality?’”

As The Atlantic’s David A. Graham observed, this is the detail that ought to worry Patel’s allies more than his enemies: it was not Democrats who did the damage on this story. A Republican senator spent his allotted time trying to hand the director an exit, and the director could not recognise it as one. Whatever else can be said about Patel’s political instincts, Tuesday’s hearing supplied strong evidence that they are not, in fact, especially sharp — and that a man appointed largely for his willingness to fight cannot always tell the difference between a fight and a favour.

“Thug,” “Lackey,” and the Reporter Question

Senator Cory Booker of New Jersey used his time to revisit a familiar thread: the Bureau’s use of investigative tools against journalists. He accused Patel of acting “like a thug” in using subpoenas to target reporters. Patel’s denial was flat and unadorned — “We are not targeting reporters at any point in time” — offered without elaboration or example, in the same register he has used throughout this series to answer questions about conduct with assertions rather than evidence.

Booker did not let the exchange end there. “We don’t have an F.B.I. director, we have a lackey here,” he said, “and frankly not even a good lackey, he’s an incompetent lackey. This is a mockery and a sham.” It fell to committee chairman Senator Charles Grassley of Iowa to try to lower the temperature — not, notably, by addressing Patel, but by turning to his own side’s counterpart in the shouting match: “Are you done now? Are you ready to shut up?” he growled at Booker. The exchange is worth flagging for what it says about the hearing’s texture as much as its substance: by Tuesday, even the committee’s own management of decorum had become a matter of picking which side to shush, rather than reining in the person giving non-answers under oath.

The Wedding, the Oligarch, and “Whatever Information Comes Our Way”

Senator Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut used his time to press Patel on whether counterintelligence agents had opened an inquiry into reports that a wealthy Russian oligarch had helped underwrite part of the wedding celebrations for Donald Trump Jr. “It may involve some intelligence degradation,” Blumenthal said, “and I would like to know what steps you are taking to investigate... I think there’s a potential for compromise in intelligence and also the possibility of improper influence.” Patel’s answer was the softest kind of non-commitment available to a witness under oath: “We will look at whatever information comes our way.” It is not a denial. It is not a confirmation of an active inquiry. It commits the Bureau to nothing beyond the passive receipt of whatever lands on its desk — a standard so low that it would be met even by a director doing nothing at all.

The Olympics Callback

Blumenthal’s questioning also revisited the recreational-travel pattern this series first documented in Part VII: Patel’s habit of combining official travel with high-profile leisure, most memorably the filmed beer-chugging celebration with the U.S. men’s hockey team in the Olympic locker room in Milan. Patel’s response on Tuesday was unapologetic, framed as a reward for performance rather than a lapse in judgment: “I go where I want to go, after delivering the most prolific crime reduction in U.S. history. What you’ll never understand is the players who are my friends invited me to the locker room.” It is worth noting what that sentence does: it converts a question about judgment into a question about accomplishment, which is precisely the Obfuscate move this series catalogued at length in Part VII — and precisely the move Patel deployed again and again on Tuesday, whether the subject was hiring standards, reporters, or an oligarch’s wedding gift.

The Crack That Never Widened

Perhaps the most quietly important piece of reporting to emerge from Tuesday’s coverage did not come from the hearing room at all. Writing for The Atlantic, David Graham noted that this was Patel’s first Capitol Hill appearance since May — and, crucially, filled in what happened after the moment Part VII flagged as a genuine crack in Patel’s defensive posture: his on-the-record agreement with Senator Chris Van Hollen that each would take a self-administered test for alcohol use disorders. Van Hollen, Graham reports, posted his results the following day. According to his office, Van Hollen’s office never heard back from Patel’s.

This matters for how this series should read Part VII’s closing framework in hindsight. At the time, the test agreement looked like the first visible movement toward Capitulate in the TACO cycle — a concession that would have been unthinkable in Parts I through VI. Four months of silence answers that question definitively: it was not capitulation. It was Accommodate doing exactly what Accommodate is designed to do — buying a news cycle’s worth of goodwill with a promise that costs nothing if it is simply never followed through on. The wall did not develop a crack in May. It developed the appearance of one, which closed again the moment the cameras did.

The Numbers Behind the Turnover

Graham’s piece also supplies figures worth setting alongside Patel’s laminated stats sheet from Part VII, which highlighted arrests, recoveries, and fugitives captured. According to Office of Personnel Management data cited in his reporting, more than 1,100 special agents — with an average tenure approaching 22 years — left the Bureau in 2025 alone. Durbin has separately alleged that more than 5,000 nonpartisan civilian staff departed the same year. Some of that exodus was voluntary. Some of it, by multiple accounts, was not: career personnel involved in investigations Patel and the administration found unwelcome, including inquiries tied to the January 6 riot, have been shown the door.

Set against that backdrop, the loosened hiring standards start to look less like a reform and more like triage — an attempt to refill an agency haemorrhaging experienced personnel by widening the aperture of who qualifies to walk through the door. Twenty-two years of institutional knowledge does not walk out one entrance and get replaced through a wider one at the other without a cost. Whether that cost shows up in the crime statistics Patel is so fond of displaying remains, for now, an open question — but it is not one the laminated sheet is built to answer.

One further figure from Graham’s reporting belongs on the record here: Patel is currently suing The Atlantic and its reporter Sarah Fitzpatrick for defamation over this spring’s account of his conduct — excessive drinking, repeated absences, emotional outbursts, impulsive behaviour — seeking $250 million in damages. He denies the allegations. The lawsuit is, in its own way, consistent with everything this series has documented: when the subject is his own conduct, Patel’s instinct is not to explain, but to escalate.

Where This Leaves the Series Part VII closed by asking whether Kash Patel is a man who should be running the Federal Bureau of Investigation. Tuesday’s hearing, stripped of the election-interference question addressed separately in Part VIII, does not complicate that question so much as answer it with mounting redundancy. A Republican senator tried to hand him an exit on a genuinely strange controversy and watched him decline it. A Democratic senator’s accusation of thuggishness was met with a denial and nothing more. A question about a wedding gift from a Russian oligarch was met with the lowest conceivable standard of institutional commitment. And a pledge to take a test he was never obligated to take turned out, four months later, to be exactly what sceptical readers of Part VII suspected it was: a gesture, not a change.

The FBI’s rank and file, as this series has said before, continue to do difficult and often admirable work under enormous strain, and under a director whose conduct increasingly dominates coverage of an agency that would rather be talked about for the work itself. That gap — between the institution’s output and its leadership’s temperament — is not narrowing. If anything, after Tuesday, it is on more vivid display than at any point since this series began.
