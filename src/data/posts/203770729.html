Stephen Gordon June 26, 2026

Across twenty-five parts of this series, I have made a single argument, refined and restated as the evidence accumulated: that the prosecutions documented here are not designed to win. They are designed to punish. The indictment is the sentence. The process is the punishment. The law, in Todd Blanche’s Justice Department, has become optional.

I stand by every word of it. And precisely because I do, I am obliged this morning to tell you about the one case that does not fit.

This morning, in a federal courtroom in Greenbelt, Maryland, John Bolton pleaded guilty.

Not to a manufactured charge. Not to a theory about seashells. Not to a perjury allegation that two juries and two appellate panels had already refused to entertain. He pleaded guilty to a single count of illegal retention of classified information, and when Judge Theodore Chuang asked him about it, he said four words that none of the other names on this list have had occasion to say: “I’m sorry for it.”

The honest analyst does not bury that. The honest analyst leads with it.

What Actually Happened

Bolton admitted to retaining classified national-defense information — notes he compiled while serving as Trump’s national security adviser, material that became the spine of his 2020 memoir, The Room Where It Happened. Under the plea deal described in court, as the New York Times has reported, he faces up to five years in prison and a fine of $2.25 million. Sentencing is set for October. The original indictment ran to eighteen counts; had he gone to trial and lost, he faced decades.

This is the first conviction this series has had to record. Across twenty-five installments I have documented dismissals, no-bills, quashed subpoenas, collapsed theories, and prosecutions that judges threw out for the simple reason that there was no crime. Today, for the first time, there is a defendant who stood before a judge and conceded that there was.

So let me do the thing the administration never does when one of its cases falls apart. Let me account for the evidence that cuts against my own argument.

Why Bolton Is Different

John R. Bolton’s indictment accused him of mishandling classified information when he compiled notes for a book that was critical of President Trump. Credit...Doug Mills/The New York Times

The case against Bolton was not built the way the others were built. This is the part that matters, and it is the part a careless reader — or a White House communications office — will want to flatten.

The Comey seashells indictment was manufactured in a year, in a borrowed jurisdiction, out of a deleted Instagram post. The Letitia James case was rejected by two grand juries. The Powell probe was abandoned the moment a single senator made it inconvenient, with Pirro leaving the weapon loaded on the table. The E. Jean Carroll investigation was handed to a Chicago prosecutor whose office was, that same week, caught committing grand-jury misconduct. Sarah Fitzpatrick was investigated by an insider-threats unit for the crime of being a reporter.

The Bolton matter has a different provenance entirely. The investigation into his notes did not spring from a Truth Social post. It began during the first Trump administration — when the government tried, and failed, to stop the book’s publication — and it gathered evidence through the Biden years. By the time it reached a courtroom it was a genuine classified-information case, with a genuine paper trail. Bolton’s own notes, according to the indictment, recorded that he understood what he was documenting: one entry began “The intel briefer said,” another “While in the Situation Room, I learned.” His emails were later hacked by someone tied to the government of Iran. His own lawyer, Abbe Lowell — who, in one of this saga’s small ironies, also represents Lisa Cook and Letitia James — did not stand on the courthouse steps and cry political persecution. He drew a contrast instead: Bolton, he said, did what real leaders do and took responsibility for a mistake, while Trump took actual classified documents to a Florida mansion, obstructed the investigation, and accepted no accountability whatsoever. Bolton kept a record to preserve history; Trump kept secrets to serve himself.

It is a good line. It also happens to be a retreat from the persecution defense Bolton himself raised when he was first indicted — and a knowing one. The strongest version of my own argument has to concede the point. A guilty plea to retaining national-defense information is not the same artifact as a $50-a-month mortgage charge or a beach photograph. The machine that has spent a year flailing at seashells and an eighty-two-year-old woman occasionally closes its hand around something real.

The Trap, and How Not to Fall Into It

John R. Bolton, who became one of President Trump’s most outspoken critics, arriving at Federal District Court in Greenbelt, Md., this morning.Credit...Allison Robbert for The New York Times

Here is where it gets difficult, and where I have to ask you to hold two true things at once, because the administration is counting on you being unable to.

The White House will use Bolton. Of course it will. By this afternoon, or tomorrow, the obfuscation account will be open — the same account Kush Desai opened on the morning the Powell probe collapsed, when he praised the administration for moving to “restore competence and confidence” in the institution it had spent a year trying to criminalize. Watch for it here too. See? The cases are real. Bolton confessed. The critics said it was all political, and look — a conviction. One genuine case, deployed retroactively to launder a dozen fraudulent ones.

That is the trap. The answer to it is not to pretend Bolton’s case was fraudulent. It wasn’t. The answer is to insist on the distinction the administration needs you to lose.

A justice system can prosecute a real classified-information offense and manufacture a perjury case against a sexual-assault survivor. A justice system can secure a legitimate guilty plea from a former national security adviser and assign the investigation of a reporter to an insider-threats unit. These are not contradictory. They are simultaneous. The existence of the first does not cleanse the second — it is the camouflage for the second. The whole danger of an enemies list is not that every name on it is innocent. It is that the list mixes the guilty and the innocent together, processes them through the same machinery, and dares you to tell the difference.

Bolton may well have committed the crime he pleaded to. Kyle Boynton’s warning from Part XXII still holds, unchanged and undisturbed by this morning’s plea: a Justice Department willing to stretch both the facts and the law to mislead a grand jury “could literally charge any American with a crime.” A department can do that and also, in a separate case, charge a guilty man with a real one. The warning was never that they would only pursue the innocent. It was that they had acquired the power to pursue anyone — and a power that can reach the innocent is not redeemed by occasionally reaching the guilty.

What Distinguishes the Real From the Manufactured

If Bolton’s plea teaches anything worth keeping, it is a test — a way to tell the genuine cases from the performances, now that we have one of each to compare.

The real case predated the grievance. It was investigated by career prosecutors across two administrations of opposing parties. It rested on the defendant’s own contemporaneous words. It produced evidence solid enough that an experienced lawyer advised his client to plead rather than gamble. And it ended with the defendant’s own admission.

The manufactured cases invert every one of those features. They postdate the grievance — often by minutes, in the case of a Truth Social post. They are routed to handpicked prosecutors in borrowed jurisdictions. They rest not on the defendant’s words but on the prosecutor’s insinuations, written, as Boynton put it, to encourage the reader to find more than the indictment states outright. They collapse on contact with a grand jury, or a judge, or the First Amendment. And they end not in admissions but in dismissals.

By that test, Bolton belongs in one column and Comey’s seashells, James’s mortgage, Powell’s renovations, Fitzpatrick’s reporting, and Carroll’s deposition belong in the other. The administration would like all six in the same column — the Bolton one. The entire purpose of this morning’s careful analysis is to refuse them that.

The List Does Not Close

So no — the Bolton plea does not vindicate the enemies list. It clarifies it.

It tells us that the machinery is not only a fraud. It is something more dangerous than a fraud: a real instrument of justice that has been pointed, selectively and vindictively, at people the president dislikes — some of whom are guilty, most of whom are not, all of whom were chosen for who they crossed rather than what they did. A purely fraudulent justice system is easy to condemn. A justice system that occasionally convicts a genuinely guilty man, and uses that conviction to justify everything else it does, is the harder and more corrosive thing. It asks you to accept the whole list because one name on it was earned.

Do not accept the whole list.

John Bolton retained classified information and pleaded guilty to it, and a court will sentence him in October, and that is how the system is supposed to work. E. Jean Carroll told the truth about an assault, won twice, and is being investigated for it, and that is how the system is not supposed to work. Both things happened. The skill the next several years will demand of all of us is the refusal to let the first erase the second.

The marble was always a lie, I wrote in the spring. The TACO was the ending of the Powell story. The guilty plea is the beginning of a harder chapter — the one where the administration finally has a real conviction to wave, and the rest of us have to keep insisting, against the noise, that a real conviction in one case does not make the other twenty-five real too.

The Enemies List continues. It just acquired its most useful piece of misdirection.
