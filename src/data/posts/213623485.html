There is a particular kind of persistence that, in most walks of life, we would call diligence. A researcher who reruns a failed experiment, adjusting one variable at a time, is practising the scientific method. A prosecutor who reopens a case after new evidence surfaces is doing their job. But there is another kind of persistence, less flattering, that consists of asking the same question of the same witness a third time not because the answer might change, but because you are hoping the venue will.

On 31 August 2026, the New York Times reported that federal prosecutors in the Southern District of Florida had issued a grand jury subpoena to Daniel C. Richman, a Columbia University law professor, former federal prosecutor, and longtime friend of James B. Comey. This is not Mr. Richman’s first subpoena. It is not his second. It is his third — a fact so remarkable that even the paper of record felt obliged to note the running tally as though scoring a boxing match nobody expected to go this many rounds.

For readers who have been following The Enemies List from the beginning, Mr. Richman is not a new name. He surfaced first as a peripheral figure in the original leak investigation into whether Mr. Comey used him as a conduit to feed sensitive F.B.I. material to reporters — an inquiry that, notably, closed without charges. He surfaced again, more centrally, in the first indictment against Mr. Comey, filed in the Eastern District of Virginia, which leaned heavily on a cache of text messages and emails between the two men to argue that Mr. Richman had served as Mr. Comey’s back channel to the press. That case did not survive contact with the judiciary; it was dismissed in its entirety in November 2025 after a federal judge found that the prosecutor who brought it, installed specifically for the purpose by the administration, had never been lawfully appointed to the position in the first place. Mr. Richman, for his part, did not merely survive that skirmish — he went on offence, persuading a federal judge that investigators had obtained his communications with Mr. Comey improperly to begin with, a ruling that complicated the Department’s efforts to rebuild the case on the same foundation.

Five months after the Virginia case collapsed, the Department tried again, this time indicting Mr. Comey in North Carolina on the theory that a photograph of seashells arranged on a beach — spelling out, depending on how charitably one squints, either “86 47” or nothing intelligible at all — constituted a threat to kill the President of the United States. Prosecutors argued that “86” is underworld slang for murder and “47” a reference to Mr. Trump’s ordinal place in the presidency. Mr. Comey’s lawyers, with the patience of people who have been asked to take a seashell arrangement seriously as a matter of federal criminal law, pointed out that the government had produced no evidence whatsoever that Mr. Comey intended the photograph as a threat, because — one is tempted to add — there was none to produce.

Which brings us to round three. This time the venue is the Southern District of Florida, the grand jury sits in Fort Pierce, the case before it is presided over by Judge Aileen M. Cannon — an appointee whose friendliness to this administration’s legal theories has by now become less a talking point than an established datum — and the theory of the case has grown considerably more ambitious than either of its predecessors. Under the direction of Joseph diGenova, a Trump loyalist, prosecutors are attempting to weld together the 2016 Russia investigation and the later inquiry into the President’s mishandling of classified documents into a single “grand conspiracy” to deprive Mr. Trump of his rights. It is, as the Times dryly notes, arguably the most far-fetched of the Department’s serial efforts to pursue the President’s political opponents — which, on this list, is a genuinely competitive category.

What Mr. Richman is actually expected to testify to this time remains unclear even to the reporters covering it. That vagueness is itself worth sitting with. In the Virginia case, the theory was specific and testable: Richman as conduit, texts as evidence, leak as crime. It was wrong, but it was a theory. In Fort Pierce, the ask appears to be broader and blurrier — less “tell us about this specific exchange” and more “help us find something, anything, that connects two entirely separate federal investigations conducted years apart by different agencies under different legal authorities into a single scheme.” That is not how criminal conspiracies are normally proven. It is, however, exactly how a fishing expedition is conducted when you already know what fish you want to catch and are simply waiting for the right net.

Readers of Part XXVI, “The One That Was Real,” will recall the test this series has used all along to sort genuine prosecutions from manufactured ones: does the inquiry predate the grievance or follow it? Was it built by career prosecutors across administrations, or handpicked specifically for this defendant? Does it rest on the defendant’s own documented words, or on inference stacked on inference? And critically — does it survive contact with an actual grand jury or an actual judge, or does it dissolve the moment someone with no political stake in the outcome looks closely at the paperwork?

The Comey prosecutions have now failed that last test twice. Virginia collapsed on an appointments-clause technicality that should never have been necessary to litigate, because the underlying case was never going to hold regardless. North Carolina rests on a beach photograph that a first-year law student could dismantle in a moot court exercise. And now Fort Pierce — a third venue, a third theory, the same witness summoned a third time — suggests not a Department building a case, but a Department that has decided the case will exist because it has been ordered to exist, and is simply cycling through jurisdictions and prosecutors until one of them produces an indictment that survives past arraignment.

There is a term for this in clinical settings, though I want to be careful about how I use it, because this series has always distinguished between naming a pattern and diagnosing a person. The behaviour itself — repeating an action that has failed twice, without modification, in the apparent belief that the third attempt will simply work through persistence alone — is a pattern this administration has exhibited well beyond the Comey matter. It appeared in the tariff litigation. It appeared in the Lisa Cook removal fight chronicled in Part XXVII, where a Supreme Court loss on process grounds was treated not as a signal to build a better case but as an invitation to try the identical manoeuvre again with slightly different paperwork. It is, in short, the house style. Lose on the law, do not revise the theory, change the room.

It is worth pausing on the particular pair of names doing the work in Fort Pierce, because neither arrived there by accident. Joseph diGenova has been a fixture of Trump-adjacent legal circles for the better part of a decade, a Reagan-era U.S. attorney turned cable-news advocate who spent the years between administrations arguing, loudly and often, that the very investigations he is now empowered to reopen were themselves the product of a “deep state” conspiracy. Handing him the authority to build a “grand conspiracy” case against the people who ran those investigations is not the appointment of a neutral prosecutor assigned by rotation; it is the appointment of a man to litigate a theory he has been rehearsing on television for years, now with subpoena power attached.

And Judge Cannon’s presence over the docket is its own kind of throughline. She is best known, of course, for her handling of the classified-documents case against Mr. Trump himself in 2023 and 2024, rulings that were repeatedly criticized by legal observers across the ideological spectrum and, in at least one instance, reversed by the Eleventh Circuit for departing sharply from ordinary procedure. That the same judge now sits over a grand jury investigating the people who investigated that very case is not, strictly speaking, evidence of anything improper — judges are assigned by district, and Fort Pierce is her courtroom. But it does mean that a defendant unhappy with the outcome of the documents case has managed, through the ordinary rotation of a federal docket, to end up litigating his grievance about that case in front of the same judge who handled it favourably the first time. If you were designing a system to maximize the odds of the President’s preferred outcome while maintaining the appearance of judicial randomness, it would look very much like this.

None of which is to say the theory is likely to survive scrutiny. Grand conspiracy cases spanning multiple administrations, multiple agencies, and multiple independently predicated investigations are notoriously difficult to prove, for the simple reason that conspiracies require an agreement, and an agreement requires that the alleged co-conspirators actually have been in contact with a shared unlawful purpose — not merely that they each, independently, investigated the same man for unrelated reasons over the course of a decade. The 2016 Russia investigation was opened by career F.B.I. counterintelligence officials responding to a specific tip from an Australian diplomat about a Trump campaign adviser’s boasts; the classified-documents investigation began after the National Archives, not the F.B.I., noticed that boxes of government records had gone missing from a Florida beach club. Connecting those two threads into a single scheme “to deprive the President of his rights” requires treating institutional continuity — the fact that some of the same officials touched both matters in the ordinary course of their jobs — as proof of coordinated malice. That is not a legal theory so much as a resemblance, dressed up in an indictment’s clothing.

Mr. Richman’s position in all this deserves a moment of its own attention, because it is easy, amid the parade of indictments and subpoenas, to lose sight of the fact that he is not a public official, not an elected combatant in anyone’s political war, and not — so far as three separate inquiries have managed to establish — guilty of anything. He is a law professor who once worked for a man the President does not like. That is the entirety of his offence. He has now been hauled before federal investigators three times over matters spanning nearly a decade, has had his own private communications seized in a manner a federal judge found improper, and is being asked, once again, to explain a relationship that two prior grand juries and two dismissed indictments have already failed to turn into a crime. If there is a definition of prosecutorial harassment that does not describe this pattern, I have not encountered it.

None of this means Mr. Richman’s third appearance before a grand jury will produce nothing. Grand juries are not designed to fail; a sufficiently motivated prosecutor can, as the old line goes, indict a ham sandwich, and Judge Cannon’s docket has not exactly been a graveyard for this administration’s ambitions. The point of tracking this pattern is not to predict that round three will collapse like rounds one and two — it may not. The point is to notice that the Department’s evident strategy, at least where Mr. Comey is concerned, has stopped resembling prosecution and started resembling attrition: enough subpoenas, enough grand juries, enough legal fees, enough venues, until either the theory sticks somewhere or the target simply runs out of road first. Attrition is not a legal argument. It does not require probable cause. It requires only time, budget, and a Department willing to keep asking.

The scorecard, updated: Comey, three prosecutions across three jurisdictions, two dismissed outright, one theory built on a beach photograph, and now a third attempt whose actual accusation remains, as of this writing, undisclosed even to the witness being compelled to answer it. Richman, three subpoenas, zero charges, one successful motion establishing that his own communications were seized unlawfully the first time around. Whatever else this is, it is not a case gathering strength. It is a case being relocated.
