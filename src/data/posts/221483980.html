Tomorrow on October 27, 2026, Israelis will vote in an election that many of them describe as the most important in their country’s history. They may be right. But the people whose daily lives depend most directly on the result will not be voting, because they are not allowed to.

Some five million Palestinians live in Gaza and the West Bank under Israeli military control without Israeli citizenship. They cannot vote for the government that decides how much food crosses into Gaza, which olive groves are safe to harvest, whose tax money is released, and which hilltop gets the next outpost. The roughly one-fifth of Israel’s citizens who are Palestinian Arabs can vote, and many will. Their cousins in Jabaliya and Hebron will watch the returns on their phones, if they can charge them.

As Adam Rasgon and Aaron Boxerman reported for the New York Times on October 21, 2026, from Hebron and Tel Aviv, those non-voters are paying closer attention to this Israeli election than to any before it. That alone tells you something about the ledger this series has been keeping.

Bad and Worse

Palestinians have not always cared much who governs Israel. For most of the past three decades, the standard view in Ramallah and Gaza City was that an Israeli election offered a choice between bad and worse, and that the difference was mostly one of manners.

There was evidence for that view. On the eve of the March 2015 election, Benjamin Netanyahu promised that no Palestinian state would be created while he remained in office. Some Palestinians reacted with something close to relief, reasoning that an honest opponent was at least easier to campaign against abroad than a polite one. During the 2019 campaigns, Palestinian analysts told Arab News that the main Israeli parties differed only in how hard they intended to press. Each round, the Palestinian Authority quietly hoped for a Netanyahu defeat, and each round it officially stayed out of things.

This time is different, and Palestinians say so openly. Nidal Kuhail, 32, lives in a tent measuring six metres by four in Gaza with his parents and three sisters, after being displaced by the war. He described the election to the Times as existential, and then reached for Hamlet’s question about being and not being. In Gaza in October 2026, that is not a figure of speech.

What changed was not the Palestinians’ opinion of Israeli politicians. It was the government they now have to live under: the most right-wing coalition since Israel’s founding in 1948, with Bezalel Smotrich at the Finance Ministry and a settler movement that has spent four years treating the cabinet table as a planning office.

The Ledger in Gaza

The ceasefire with Hamas took effect in October 2025. It was supposed to end the war. It has instead converted the war into something with a lower daily rate and no end date.

Since the ceasefire, according to local health officials, Israeli strikes have killed more than 1,400 people in Gaza, and Israel has enlarged the share of the territory it controls. The Israeli military says it is targeting Hamas militants and protecting Israeli communities near the border. That may well be true of any given strike. It does not change the total, and it does not change the fact that Gazans still live among the ruins of their buildings and in tents they increasingly expect to grow old in.

Mr. Netanyahu has largely declined to cooperate with President Donald Trump’s Board of Peace for Gaza, whose plan trades the disarmament of Hamas for an Israeli withdrawal. Senior members of his coalition, meanwhile, have said that a re-elected government would encourage Gazans to “voluntarily emigrate” in large numbers. In September 2026, Mr. Smotrich went further and argued that Israel should keep conditions in Gaza as wretched as possible, precisely so that Gazans would stop hoping for anything better.

It is worth pausing on that. Governments at war have often said cruel things about their enemies. It is rarer for a finance minister to name despair itself as a policy objective, and rarer still for him to say so during an election campaign, on the assumption that it will help.

Saed Abu Aita, 46, lives in a displacement camp in Jabaliya with a piece of shrapnel still lodged in his chest. He told the Times that the Israeli attack that put it there also killed his father, Hamed, and his two daughters, Mira, 8, and Tala, 13. Their graves lie in the part of Gaza that Israel now holds, so he and his wife cannot visit them. He described the past three years as a long ordeal of torment and loss.

He does not expect the election to solve Gaza. His hopes are smaller than that, and therefore more telling. If the extremists leave government, he said, perhaps families could go home, get better medical care, and receive more aid. That is the entire platform of a man who has lost three generations of his family in one strike: a visit to a grave and a working clinic.

Jaser Abu Mousa, a political analyst in Gaza, told the Times that ordinary life in the strip has effectively stopped while people wait for the Israeli result. A society of two million people is holding its breath for a vote it cannot cast.

The Ledger in the West Bank

Part XIV of this series described the financial chokehold on the West Bank. Since then the grip has not loosened.

Since October 7, 2023, Israel has largely barred West Bank Palestinians from working inside Israel, cutting off what was for many families the main source of cash. It has also withheld billions of dollars in tax revenue that it collects on the Palestinian Authority’s behalf, accusing the Authority of funding terrorism, which Palestinian officials deny. The result is an Authority close to bankruptcy, unable to pay its own creditors.

Nasser Osaily, a Hebron entrepreneur in real estate and importing, told the Times that the Authority owes him millions in reimbursements for work done with foreign donors, and that the tenants in his shops cannot pay rent because their customers have no money. He called the policy one of strangulation and compared it to a hand around the throat. Economists would describe the same thing as a liquidity crisis. Mr. Osaily’s version is more accurate.

On the ground, the settlers have done the rest. The annual olive harvest is both an economic lifeline and a cultural ritual for West Bank families, and in autumn 2026 it has become a season of assaults. Haitham Abdel Jaber, 47, an American citizen, was picking olives with relatives near his home in Burqa, close to Ramallah, in mid-October 2026 when masked men began hurling stones. He said he was struck, fell, and was then beaten and kicked before his attackers fled. He was hospitalized with a head injury. The Israeli military’s account referred to “friction” between Israelis and Palestinians, a word that does a remarkable amount of work.

On October 19, 2026, Israeli police said they had detained at least 10 Israelis after settlers attacked Palestinians during the harvest, along with an American press photographer and European diplomats. Analysts and rights groups say such arrests remain the exception. Mr. Netanyahu has occasionally condemned the attacks, and the attacks have continued.

The settlement project itself has never been busier. Mr. Netanyahu recently celebrated the founding of more than 100 new settlements and promised more. At a settlers’ conference in August 2026, he credited his government with a huge effort to fulfil what he called a dream generations old. New settlements have appeared in parts of the northern West Bank where Israelis have not lived for years, alongside a scattering of outposts that are illegal even under Israeli law, which is a fairly low bar.

And Mr. Smotrich, in a podcast interview in October 2026, proposed that the next government do in the West Bank what it did in Gaza. He was referring to a military campaign that levelled much of the strip. A reader in Toronto can treat that as campaign rhetoric. A farmer in Burqa, watching the hillside above his grove, does not have that luxury.

What Eisenkot Offers

The man the Palestinians are pinning their modest hopes on is not a peace candidate, and he does not pretend to be.

Gadi Eisenkot was chief of staff of the Israel Defense Forces and served for a time in the war cabinet after October 7, 2023. He lost a son in Gaza that December. He rejects a Palestinian state. He broadly supports the settlements. Many of his voters, like most Israelis, want tight limits kept on goods entering Gaza and continued strikes against Hamas, and nothing suggests he disagrees.

What separates him from Mr. Netanyahu is narrower but real. He has promised to rein in settler violence. He has said he would dismantle settlements built in the parts of the West Bank assigned to Palestinian Authority administration under the Oslo interim agreements of the 1990s. He says he would work with the Board of Peace rather than around it. Yoram Cohen, a former head of the Shin Bet security service and a senior figure in Mr. Eisenkot’s party, told a Tel Aviv party event in October 2026 that their government would crack down on Israeli extremists who attack Palestinians and ease the economic pressure on the West Bank, since Israel has no interest in opening another front.

That is not a peace plan. It is a stability plan, made by security men who have concluded that a West Bank on fire is bad for Israel. But for a Palestinian living through this harvest, the difference between a government that tolerates arson and one that arrests arsonists is not academic. It is the difference between finishing the harvest and losing it.

The Shrinking Ask

Palestinian officials usually avoid endorsing Israeli candidates, on the sensible grounds that an endorsement from Ramallah is not a gift most Israeli politicians want. This year they have dropped the pretence. Ahmed Majdalani, a senior Palestinian official, told the Times there was a vast difference between the two men and that he hoped Mr. Eisenkot would release the confiscated funds.

Then he said the sentence that belongs at the centre of this series. “We’re not even saying ‘let’s establish a state’ at this point.” What the Palestinians want now, he explained, is for the immediate problems to be solved, so that some trust between the two peoples can be rebuilt. If Mr. Netanyahu returns, he warned, it would be a catastrophe for Palestinians and Israelis alike.

Set that sentence against the history.

In 1993, the Oslo Accords set out a five-year interim period that was supposed to end in a final settlement and, in Palestinian expectations, a state. In 2000, at Camp David, the negotiation was over borders and Jerusalem. In 2008, Ehud Olmert offered the Palestinians more than 90 per cent of the West Bank, and Mahmoud Abbas did not accept it. Palestinian negotiators at the time insisted they would never settle for less than what they had already turned down.

In October 2026, a senior Palestinian official is asking for the Palestinians’ own tax money back, and for farmers to be able to pick olives without being stoned.

That is what compound interest looks like from the debtor’s side. Every default, every failed round, every war, every new settlement has been added to the principal, and each time the Palestinians have come back to the table asking for less. They asked for a state, then for a freeze, then for a ceasefire, and now for the release of funds and some protection from arsonists. The demand has fallen as the debt has grown. That is not moderation. It is exhaustion, priced in.

The Asymmetry

There is one more line in the Times report that deserves attention, because it is easy to miss. For all the Palestinian attention on this election, the Palestinians themselves are not a major campaign issue among Israeli voters.

Israelis will argue about security, about Iran, about the hostages and the war’s conduct, about the judiciary and the cost of living, and about whether Mr. Netanyahu should be allowed to keep governing while his corruption cases continue. The people in the tents and the olive groves will appear mostly as a security category: a threat to be managed, a front to be kept quiet. Mr. Eisenkot’s camp proposes to manage it more competently. Mr. Netanyahu’s coalition proposes to make it go away.

That asymmetry is the oldest entry in the ledger. Since 1967, Israel has governed the West Bank and Gaza, directly or by siege, for 59 years. Millions of people have been born, grown up and died under a government they never had any say in choosing. Every Israeli election in that period has been, among other things, a decision about them made without them. What is new in 2026 is only that the stakes have become impossible to ignore, even for the people who cannot vote.

Part XVIII of this series ended by asking whether Israelis, on October 27, would take stock of what nearly five decades of the Iranian “Death to Israel” campaign had bought and for whom. The Palestinians have already taken stock of their own position. Their conclusion, as these interviews make clear, is that they are no longer hoping for justice from this election, or even for a state. They are hoping for less shelling, fewer attackers in the groves, some tax money, a working hospital, and a visit to their daughters’ graves.

It is a very small list. It would be a considerable mistake for anyone, in Jerusalem, Washington or here in Canada, to read its smallness as contentment. A people whose demands have shrunk this far has not given up its claim. It has simply stopped expecting the other side to honour it. The interest is still accruing.

On October 28, 2026, we will know which government the Palestinians will be living under. They will not have chosen it. They never have.
