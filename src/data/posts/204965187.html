The duration is fixed. The number of durations is flexible.

The Spokesperson took the podium at 11:04, four minutes late, which by the standards of the engagement counted as punctual.

“Good morning. I’m pleased to announce that the parties have agreed to extend the seventy-two-hour humanitarian pause.”

A pen scratched. Somewhere a phone was set to silent too late and played three notes of a song before dying.

“This will be the eleventh extension of the seventy-two-hour pause.”

It is worth sitting with the arithmetic, because the Spokesperson did not. Eleven extensions of seventy-two hours, plus the original seventy-two, is seven hundred and ninety-two hours. Seven hundred and ninety-two hours is thirty-three days. We were, that morning, thirty-three days into a seventy-two-hour pause, and the Spokesperson delivered this the way you would announce that the cafeteria had switched to a new supplier of yogurt — as housekeeping, as a small administrative grace, as something that need not detain serious people.

A hand went up in the second row. The Spokesperson, with the air of a man who had hoped to get further, recognized it.

“You said seventy-two-hour pause. It’s been a month.”

“The pause is seventy-two hours. It has been extended.”

“Eleven times.”

“That’s correct.”

“So it’s not seventy-two hours.”

“It is a seventy-two-hour pause,” the Spokesperson said, “that is renewed on a rolling basis.” And here he produced the phrase that I believe will outlive every one of us, the phrase I have had carved, in my mind, above the lintel of the whole enterprise: “The duration is fixed. The number of durations is flexible.”

A seventy-two-hour pause. Thirty-three days and counting.

I want to be clear that I am not making fun of peace. Peace is the only thing worth being serious about, and a pause that holds is a pause in which someone’s child sleeps a night through, and that is not a small thing, it is the only thing. I am making fun of the word, which is a different target, and a fair one, because the word is being asked to do work that the world is plainly not doing.

When a thing is called a ceasefire, the ordinary citizen, possessed of a dictionary and a memory, assumes two facts are in evidence: that there was a fire, and that it has ceased. The genius of the rolling seventy-two-hour pause is that it commits to neither. It does not concede that the fire is ongoing — that would be defeatist — but it declines, with great delicacy, to claim that it has stopped, because the scorecard would embarrass the claim by suppertime. So we get the pause. The pause is brilliant. The pause is the diplomatic equivalent of holding your breath and announcing that you have quit breathing.

The questions resumed, as they do, in ascending order of fatigue.

“Have hostilities stopped during the pause?”

“There have been incidents inconsistent with the spirit of the pause.”

“How many incidents?”

“I would refer you to the daily figures.”

The daily figures, for those keeping the daily figures, were not the figures of a pause. The daily figures were the figures of a war with better paperwork. But the Spokesperson had a genuine point, in his way, the terrible point at the centre of the whole grey morning: every incident inconsistent with the spirit of the pause was, by the very fact of being called an incident, a tribute to the pause. You only get to call it an incident if you have first agreed there is a pause to violate. The pause survives precisely by being broken. Each violation reaffirms it. This is the only war I know of that is sustained by its own failure to occur.

A pause is a silence with good lawyers.

A woman near the front, who had clearly been at this for eleven extensions and possibly several previous wars, lowered her pen.

“Has anyone,” she asked, “consulted the dictionary?”

The Spokesperson paused. It was, I will grant him, a real pause — seventy-two hours of expression compressed into a second and a half — and for that moment I thought we might get somewhere honest, that he might set down the folder and say no, of course not, no one has consulted the dictionary, the dictionary lost its seat at this table in the first week and has been waiting in the hall ever since with the other words we no longer let in the room.

He did not say that. What he said was: “The pause speaks for itself.”

The pause, of course, says nothing. That is its whole function. A pause is a silence with good lawyers.

I have spent some weeks now watching language do this — watching the words sent out each morning to stand at the podium and absorb fire so the policy behind them need not. And I have come to think that the rolling seventy-two-hour pause is not a failure of diplomacy but its purest current expression. It is conflict that has learned to describe itself as the absence of conflict, indefinitely, in tidy renewable increments, so that no one need ever stand up and say the older, shorter, deadlier word, which is war, and no one need ever stand up and say the better, harder, almost-impossible word, which is peace. The pause splits the difference and bills by the hour.

Up north, where I write from, we have a long tradition of being so reasonable that nothing gets decided, and I recognize a cousin when I see one. But our paralysis is at least honest about being paralysis. We do not extend a seventy-two-hour committee meeting thirty-three days and call it adjourned.

The briefing ended at 11:31. The Spokesperson gathered his folder. The last question, called out as he turned, came from the back, from someone too junior to have learned not to.

“When does the pause end?”

He could have said many things. He could have said we are working toward a durable resolution. He could have said that is up to the parties. Instead he said the truest thing said in the room all morning, and I do not think he meant to, and I have not been able to put it down since.

“The pause,” he said, “does not end. It is extended.”

And he was right. That is exactly what it does. That is all it does. Somewhere a scorecard ticked over to a number nobody would read aloud, and a woman in a prison cell three time zones away — there is always a woman in a prison cell — counted another morning, and the pause refreshed, fixed in duration, flexible in number, holding its breath, and calling it peace.
