In January 1992, an 87-year-old man who held no formal office stood beside the Shenzhen River in a cold wind and looked across at Hong Kong. A local official named Chen Xitian, recalling the moment to China Daily decades later, remembered that Deng Xiaoping simply gazed at the other side.

Deng had come south by special train, a journey of nearly two days from Beijing. Three years after the Tiananmen massacre, conservatives in the party were rolling back his economic reforms, and he had come to the place where those reforms began to tell the country not to retreat. Jonathan Chatwin’s account of the trip, excerpted in the South China Morning Post, takes its title from his message: “Do not turn back!”

It was not his first visit. In January 1984 he had toured the young zone and left behind an inscription declaring that Shenzhen’s development and experience proved the policy of special economic zones correct. By 1992 the town he had seen eight years earlier was unrecognisable, and he said so. The visit was a reminder to the rest of the party that the experiment had his name on it.

The Southern Tour, as it became known, settled the argument. Within a few years foreign money was pouring into the coastal provinces, and Shenzhen became the proof that Deng had been right. On November 18, 2026, it becomes the stage for the third act of the Opera Cycle.

The official story

Beijing tells Shenzhen’s story often and well, and the numbers are genuinely remarkable even allowing for the telling. Chinese state media describe a border town of a little over 30,000 people in 1980, the year it became China’s first special economic zone; CGTN put the population at 18.25 million this year. The city’s output rose from 270 million yuan in 1980 to 3.68 trillion yuan in 2024, according to figures published by Shenzhen’s own government, and it is now China’s largest foreign-trade city.

The reason the experiment was put there is visible on any map. Shenzhen sits directly across the river from Hong Kong, and in the late 1970s the gap between the two sides was a standing embarrassment. A Thai account of the city’s history in Manager Online cites figures from that time: villagers in Luofang, on the Chinese side, earned 134 yuan a year per person, while people who had fled across the river to a settlement of the same name earned 13,000 Hong Kong dollars. Young people were swimming the river to escape. The special economic zone was, among other things, an attempt to stop them by bringing Hong Kong’s prosperity across the border instead.

It worked on a scale nobody predicted. In 2018 Shenzhen’s economy overtook Hong Kong’s. The river Deng gazed across in 1992 now separates a Chinese technology capital from a former British colony whose freedoms were curtailed by Beijing’s national security law in 2020. The view has reversed.

When Xi Jinping announced at the close of the Gyeongju summit in November 2025 that Shenzhen would host APEC, he called the city’s rise from fishing village to metropolis “a miracle in world history created by the Chinese people,” as Macao News reported. Chinese commentators told The Korea Times that the choice was meant to demonstrate China’s commitment to openness and to put the city’s innovation on display.

Xi’s pilgrimages

Xi has his own history with the city, and it is a history of deliberate echoes. In December 2012, a few weeks after becoming party leader, he made his first trip outside Beijing to Shenzhen and laid a basket of flowers at the bronze statue of Deng in Lianhuashan Park. The gesture was read at the time as a signal that the new leader would keep Deng’s reforms going. In October 2020, for the special economic zone’s 40th anniversary, he returned to unveil a plan to make Shenzhen a trade, finance and technology hub, a speech the Japan Times likened to Deng’s Southern Tour.

There is a second echo, and it runs the other way. In the early 1990s, after Tiananmen and the collapse of the Soviet Union, Deng gave China a foreign-policy maxim usually translated as “hide your strength and bide your time.” For two decades Chinese leaders followed it. They played down China’s power, avoided confrontation with the United States and let the factories of the Pearl River Delta speak quietly for themselves.

Xi abandoned that caution early in his leadership, and Shenzhen in November is the clearest possible statement that the time for hiding is over. Deng’s city was built to catch up with Hong Kong. Xi’s summit is staged to show that China has caught up with America, and in some industries passed it.

Bringing the American president to Shenzhen completes the sequence. Deng used the city to tell his own party that reform would continue. Xi will use it to tell 20 other leaders, and one in particular, that the reform produced a rival.

Dressing the set

A production this important is not left to chance. In the first week of November, construction crews were working late into the night in downtown Shenzhen, and the city was rolling out traffic restrictions and planting trees along the routes the delegations will travel, as the South China Morning Post reported. Shenzhen’s mayor, Qin Weizhong, has promised a professional, orderly and secure summit. The city has been recruiting summit volunteers from a pool of more than 4.2 million registered volunteers, according to Macao News, which is roughly one adult in four.

The leaders’ meeting is only part of the bill. APEC’s business advisory council meets in the city from November 14 to 16, and a CEO summit run by China’s main trade promotion body follows on November 17 and 18, with more than 2,000 participants expected from government, business and the media, according to the organisers’ announcement.

There will also be a delegation from Taiwan, though it will not be called that. Taiwan takes part in APEC as “Chinese Taipei,” and its president does not attend the leaders’ meeting in person; an envoy goes instead. The last time China hosted, in Shanghai in 2001, Taiwan withdrew after Beijing rejected the envoy it had chosen. In Shenzhen, Taiwan’s representative will sit at the same table as the American president who has been holding back a $14 billion arms package for the island as a bargaining chip with Xi. The seating plan alone is a small drama.

What the set is built to show

Beijing is pressing for an outcome document covering digital trade, artificial intelligence, connectivity, energy and reform of the World Trade Organization, according to an analysis by Lawrence Greenwood for Bower Group Asia.

The city’s corporate residents make the argument without speeches. Huawei, BYD and Tencent are all based in Shenzhen, as is DJI, the drone maker that McMaster singled out in his 2020 essay for driving American competitors out of the market. BYD has overtaken Tesla as the world’s biggest seller of battery-electric cars. Tencent runs WeChat, the messaging app on which much of Chinese daily life depends. And Huawei is the company that the United States spent the better part of a decade trying to cut off from the world.

A word about Huawei

I have been to Huawei’s campus. Some years before the American ban, I was invited to a presentation in Toronto where Huawei was showing off its new hardware, and afterwards I got in touch with the company’s Toronto office. I was already planning a trip to Shenzhen to see friends, and Huawei arranged a visit to fit around it. A driver and a guide collected me in Shenzhen and took me to its campus in Dongguan, about an hour away, for a day that included a walk along a mobile-phone production line and, naturally, lunch. It was a gracious day. It was also, I understood even then, a production. The hospitality was real, and so was the staging. Looking back, it was the Opera Cycle in miniature, years before the cycle began.

I spent 18 years at Rogers, in the office that handled the complaints that came up through every other channel, so I make no claim to be a network engineer. But nobody who worked in Canadian telecommunications in those years could avoid the Huawei question, and Canada has paid more for it than most countries.

Huawei was founded in Shenzhen in 1987 by Ren Zhengfei, a former engineer in the People’s Liberation Army. By the 2010s it was the largest maker of telecommunications equipment in the world, and its radio gear was inside the 4G networks of two of Canada’s three national carriers. In December 2018, at the request of the United States, Canadian police arrested Ren’s daughter, Huawei’s chief financial officer Meng Wanzhou, at Vancouver’s airport. Nine days later China detained two Canadians, Michael Kovrig and Michael Spavor. They spent more than 1,000 days in Chinese custody and were released in September 2021, on the same day that Meng was allowed to fly home.

In 2020, Bell and Telus chose European suppliers for their 5G networks. In May 2022, Ottawa formally banned Huawei and ZTE equipment from Canada’s 5G networks, years after the United States had placed Huawei on its trade blacklist. That listing came in May 2019, during Trump’s first term, and it cut Huawei’s new phones off from Google’s apps and services, which crippled its handset business outside China.

McMaster’s 2020 essay described Huawei as part of China’s security apparatus and urged free countries to build trusted 5G networks without it. Whatever one thinks of that judgment, it became American and Canadian policy. On November 18 the president who signed the first restrictions will be a guest in the company’s home city.

Beijing has a habit of timing its technology to American visitors. In August 2023, while the American commerce secretary, Gina Raimondo, was in China, Huawei launched a smartphone powered by an advanced Chinese-made chip that American export controls were supposed to make impossible. It is reasonable to expect that something will be launched, unveiled or demonstrated within sight of the Shenzhen summit. The production values of this cycle have been consistent.

What the set leaves out

McMaster’s lesson from the Forbidden City was that the tour mattered as much for what it skipped as for what it showed. Shenzhen has its own unmentionables.

China Evergrande, the property developer whose collapse came to symbolise the end of China’s housing boom, had its headquarters in Shenzhen. A Hong Kong court ordered it liquidated in January 2024. The housing slump it helped trigger is still weighing on household wealth across the country, and it is one of the reasons Xi wants a calm year with Washington ahead of the party congress in 2027.

The river is another. The view Deng admired in 1992 was of a free city. Hong Kong’s protest movement was broken in 2020, and McMaster’s essay was written while those protests were still being blamed on foreigners. Visitors to Shenzhen in November will see the skyline across the water. They will not be invited to discuss what happened there.

And then there is the barbed wire. For most of its history the special economic zone was sealed off from the rest of China by a fence known as the Second Line, which Chatwin describes Deng’s train passing through in 1992. Chinese citizens needed a permit to cross it. The checkpoints were finally dismantled in 2018, and the zone was by then the whole city. Shenzhen was built as an exception, a place where rules that applied everywhere else were suspended. The miracle is real. It is also the product of a political system that can declare a city an exception, and can withdraw the exception whenever it chooses.

The audience of one

Every production in this cycle has been designed for a single member of the audience. In 2017 he was given a palace, an opera and a private tour. In May 2026 he was given the Great Hall of the People. In September he gave his guest a construction site and a second eagle.

In Shenzhen he will be given a city that was barely a town when Deng took power, built on the edge of the British empire’s last great Asian possession, crowded with companies his own government has tried to restrain. Nobody will need to say that China no longer needs the United States. The skyline will say it for them.

Whether the visitor hears the message is the subject of Part III, which opens when he lands from Manila on the afternoon of November 18.
