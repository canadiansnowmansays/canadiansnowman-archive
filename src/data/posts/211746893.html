On 3 July 2026, the eve of America’s 250th birthday, Donald Trump posted an image to his social media platform of a gleaming gold bas-relief: Mount Rushmore, recast in bullion, its four familiar granite faces — Washington, Jefferson, Roosevelt, Lincoln — joined by a fifth. His own. The sculpture drew over five thousand reposts and twenty thousand likes within hours. Nobody at the National Park Service commissioned it. No act of Congress authorized it. It exists because a man wanted to see his own face carved into the mountain that carries the nation’s founders, and technology has made wanting a sufficient condition for seeing.

This series has spent twelve parts tracing the inscription of Trump’s name onto buildings, bridges, and departments — a real and escalating phenomenon, but one that still operates within a recognizable genre: politicians have always wanted things named after them. What this instalment catalogues is different in kind. It is not naming. It is replacement — the self-insertion of one man into the nation’s visual pantheon, its currency, and even its government financial products, in a register that has less to do with vanity than with the iconography of veneration.

The pantheon, uninvited

The gold Rushmore image is the starkest example, but it is not isolated. A painting titled “The Tariff Men” places Trump’s face at the centre of a triptych between William McKinley and Abraham Lincoln — an odd historical pairing on its face, since McKinley’s protective tariffs and Lincoln’s Civil War have little to do with each other beyond both men having, at different moments, raised import duties. The connecting tissue is not policy; it is succession. The image argues, wordlessly, that Trump belongs in a lineage of great presidents specifically through the one policy area — tariffs — he has made his own.

A second painting, in a private office or reception room, places Trump between Ronald Reagan and Lincoln beneath a full American flag, the three men rendered in the same soft, illustrative style usually reserved for retrospective tributes to presidents long dead. Reagan is smiling; Lincoln is solemn; Trump, positioned in the foreground and slightly forward of both, stares directly out of the frame. The painting does not argue that Trump belongs in this company. It simply assumes it, the way a family photo assumes everyone in it belongs to the family.

Elsewhere, a wall of official presidential portraits places two black-and-white photographs of Trump — framed identically, labelled “Donald J. Trump, 2025–” — flanking a third frame containing not a portrait but a facsimile of Joe Biden’s signature, captured mid-stroke on what appears to be an autopen device. The juxtaposition invites a reading Trump’s allies have pushed aggressively in public: that Biden’s presidency should be regarded as procedurally suspect, its authority reducible to a mechanical signing arm, while Trump’s two photographed likenesses bracket that suspicion on either side like sentries.

The wound as icon

The most recurring image in this batch is not a building or a banner. It is Trump’s raised fist after the July 2021 assassination attempt in Butler, Pennsylvania — blood on his cheek, flag behind him, Secret Service agents crouched around him. That photograph, already one of the most reproduced images of his political career, has now been repainted as fine art and hung in at least two identifiable interior spaces: once as a large canvas beside a red settee near a marble hallway, and again in a smaller sitting room beside an unrelated 19th-century still life of fruit, as though the two paintings were simply decor chosen for their gold-and-blue palette rather than for what one of them depicts.

The image has also been rendered as a small bronze statuette — the raised fist, the crouching aides, the flag — sized for a desk or a shelf display, the kind of object usually reserved for war memorials or retired generals’ retirement gifts, now available as a keepsake. And it appears a third time as a large circular emblem stamped onto the hull of the fictionalized “USS Defiant,” discussed in Part XII’s treatment of military branding — the wound folded into a naval insignia, worn into battle like a coat of arms.

There is a legitimate instinct behind the original photograph’s fame: it captured a genuine, frightening moment, and its status as an iconic image of resilience is not manufactured. What this catalogue documents is the deliberate multiplication of that moment into devotional object — painting, statuette, insignia — the visual grammar normally reserved for martyrs and saints, applied to a living president while he is still in office to approve the next reproduction.

The framed and the foretold

Two further portraits round out the gallery. One, a soft, formally lit oil painting signed by artist Sarah A. Boudreau, shows Trump in a dark suit and red tie against a warm brown background — the conventional register of an official presidential portrait, unremarkable except for where it hangs, in a private residential-style room beside ornate candelabra sconces rather than in any public gallery. The second, painted in a harsher, more gilded style and signed only with initials, carries a small brass plaque beneath it: “DONALD TRUMP, 45TH AND 47TH PRESIDENT, 2017–2021 AND 2025–2029.” The plaque does not hedge. It does not say “2025–,” open-ended, the way the earlier photographic portraits did. It states an end date for a term not yet two years old, as though the remaining walk of the presidency were already historical fact rather than an outcome still subject to whatever the next three years bring.

Beside these hangs a framed New York Post cover from the president’s 2023 Fulton County booking — the mugshot itself, glowering and lit like a mangled campaign poster, framed in ornate gilt and displayed under glass as though it were a war medal rather than an arrest record. The reframing of a mugshot as trophy is its own small act of alchemy: the same image that was designed by the booking process to be a document of accountability becomes, once gilded and hung, a document of defiance instead. Nothing about the image changes. Only its frame, and the room it hangs in, tell the viewer how to read it.

The currency itself

If the paintings insert Trump into the nation’s visual memory, the currency material inserts him into its literal medium of exchange. A mock-up hundred-dollar bill circulated this year shows Trump’s signature — not Benjamin Franklin’s, not a Treasury Secretary’s, but the president’s own looping autograph — printed where the Treasury Secretary’s signature ordinarily appears on genuine currency, “President of the United States” printed beneath it. A separate and more consequential controversy, reported by The Washington Post, concerns a proposed $250 bill bearing Trump’s actual portrait: internal Bureau of Engraving and Printing mock-ups show his face where Franklin’s currently sits, marking what would be the first depiction of a living person on U.S. paper currency in the nation’s history — a threshold federal law and a century and a half of practice have both quietly held, until an administration official pushed the printing office’s staff to design around it.

A rendering of a commemorative dollar coin goes further still: “LIBERTY” arcs over Trump’s profile on the obverse, “IN GOD WE TRUST” beside his cheek, “1776 ~ 2026” beneath his chin — the same design grammar as every circulating Liberty coin since the nation’s founding, simply with his face substituted for the allegorical figure of Liberty herself. And a “Trump Gold Card” — styled like ornamental gilt currency, complete with an eagle, the Statue of Liberty, and a facsimile signature — markets itself as “45th & 47th President of the United States” merchandise, blurring the line between numismatic tribute and personal-brand collectible so completely that it is difficult to tell, from the image alone, whether it is a government artifact or a product for sale.

The government’s own products

The most consequential material in this instalment may be the least visually dramatic: not paintings or coins, but ordinary-looking government websites. TrumpAccounts.gov offers tax-advantaged investment accounts for American children, branded with the same stagecraft as a Silicon Valley product launch — Trump himself photographed at a podium beneath a banner reading “TRUMP ACCOUNTS: JUMPSTART THE AMERICAN DREAM,” the website’s own icon a small flag-and-bar-chart glyph beside the tagline “The American Dream starts now.” A second site, TrumpIRA.gov, describes itself as “an official initiative of the United States Department of the Treasury,” offering retirement accounts to independent contractors and part-time workers with federal matching funds, its launch dated for January 2027 and its founding traced to an April 2026 executive order. A third product, “TrumpRx,” presents itself as a prescription-drug price comparison tool, its homepage declaring “$400,000,000+ Saved for Americans” beneath the tagline “TrumpRx the price.”

Each of these is, ostensibly, a legitimate government service: child savings accounts, retirement accounts, and drug price transparency are all defensible policy goals, and previous administrations have built similar tools without incident. What distinguishes these three is the branding decision, made deliberately and at the level of the product name itself, to fuse the service with the man rather than the office. A future administration cannot easily rename “TrumpAccounts” back to something neutral without appearing to erase a benefit already in use by families who signed up for it — which is, per this series’ recurring argument, precisely the design logic behind carving a name in stone rather than printing it on a temporary banner. A URL is not stone. But a URL that millions of people have bookmarked, used, and shared with their accountants functions, in practice, almost as permanently.

A note on precedent

It is worth pausing on how unusual all of this is by the standard of the office itself. Presidential portraits have always existed, but by convention they are painted after a presidency ends, commissioned by a library foundation or a museum, and reviewed by historians and the subject together — a slow, somewhat bureaucratic process designed to produce something closer to a considered likeness than a campaign asset. Coins bearing a president’s image have, since a 1866 law prohibiting the depiction of living people on currency, waited for death as a precondition — a rule aimed specifically at preventing exactly the kind of premature self-monumentalizing this instalment catalogues. Mount Rushmore itself took fourteen years to carve and required an act of Congress, a National Memorial Commission, and the sculptor Gutzon Borglum’s judgment about which four presidents had earned inclusion by the verdict of history rather than the preference of any one of them.

Every safeguard this section describes exists for the same reason: because the individuals being honoured are, by design, not the ones doing the choosing. What this instalment documents is the systematic bypassing of that separation — not through any single illegal act, but through a hundred small substitutions of self-selection for institutional judgment, each individually defensible as a rendering, a mock-up, a social media post, or a private commission, and each one, taken together, amounting to a man appointing himself to a pantheon that has always depended on his absence from the room where the appointing happens.

What Rushmore actually reveals

Return, to close, to the gold sculpture. Its significance is not that it will ever be built — no one seriously expects the National Park Service to add a fifth face to South Dakota’s granite. Its significance is that a sitting president chose to manufacture and distribute, to millions of followers, an image of himself joining the four men history has already agreed founded, expanded, and preserved the union, without irony, apology, or any sense that the comparison required justification. Twelve parts ago, this series began with a psychological question: why does this man need his name on everything he touches? This instalment answers a harder version of the same question. It is no longer only about being remembered. It is about being enshrined — placed, by his own hand and while he still holds office, in the company of the dead men whose judgment history has already rendered, before history has had the chance to render his.

Postscript

When Part XIII went to press, the Liberty dollar coin was a rendering — a mock-up circulating online, filed alongside the mock $100 bill and the proposed $250 note as one more item in Trump’s gallery of self-issued currency. On September 2, 2026, it stopped being a rendering.

The U.S. Mint announced that day that the 2026 Semiquincentennial President Donald J. Trump $1 Coin had entered circulation and was available for purchase on its website, as ABC News reported. The design matches the mock-up almost exactly: Trump's portrait on the obverse, ringed by "LIBERTY," "IN GOD WE TRUST," and the dual date "1776 ~ 2026." The reverse carries the Presidential Seal, with "250" worked into the eagle's shield to mark the nation's Semiquincentennial. It was struck at the Philadelphia Mint, sculpted by Chief Engraver Joseph Menna from an official White House photograph, and it makes (King) Trump the first sitting president to appear on circulating American currency — a milestone that carries an unavoidable echo of King Charles III, whose profile has appeared on British coinage since 2022. The comparison is instructive precisely because it doesn't hold: a monarch's face on the coin of the realm is the constitutional norm in Britain, unbroken since the seventeenth century and utterly divorced from policy or election; an elected president's face on the currency of a republic that has spent 250 years deliberately keeping the two apart is something else entirely, dressed in the same metal.

The Mint’s own language, though, is worth sitting with. Its press materials and its X account both insist the coins are “in circulation” and urge Americans to check their pocket change. Coin-industry trade press, covering the same release, notes the rolls and bags sold to collectors — priced at $61 for twenty-five coins, $154.50 for a hundred — “have never been placed into circulation,” despite carrying a circulating finish. Two claims about the same object, issued by the same institution, on the same day. Call it the Mint’s own small contribution to the genre this series has been tracking: the inscription that describes itself into existence.

There is a chase element, because there always is. Of the coins struck, 250,000 carry a “JULY 4th” privy mark, minted on Independence Day and scattered at random through the rolls and bags rather than sold separately — a scarcity mechanic borrowed straight from the collectibles market, applied to legal tender.

And there is the legal question this series flagged as unresolved back in Part XIII. The authorizing statute — the Circulating Collectible Coin Redesign Act of 2020 — bars “any head and shoulders portrait or bust of any person, living or dead” from the reverse of a Semiquincentennial coin. It says nothing about the obverse. Menna’s portrait sits on the front. Legal observers who reviewed the draft design last October called this the loophole by which the ban would be honoured in the letter and set aside in the spirit; the Mint appears to have taken the note.

The Rushmore problem, in other words, did not stay a problem for very long. It became a coin, then a headline, then — the Mint hopes — a fixture of pocket change from Seattle to Sarasota. Whether it becomes a fixture of the collection or of the change tray remains to be seen; dollar coins have a long history in America of being minted in earnest and hoarded in a drawer.
