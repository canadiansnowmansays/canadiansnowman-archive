Deng Xiaoping was five feet tall.

This is not irrelevant. He operated in a political culture organized around Mao Zedong — a man of commanding physical presence, rhetorical power, and absolute certainty — and Deng survived forty years in that culture by being the opposite of everything Mao was. Where Mao was grand and theoretical, Deng was practical and specific. Where Mao operated through charisma and mass mobilisation, Deng operated through consensus and coalition. Where Mao demanded ideological purity, Deng offered the most consequential phrase in the history of Chinese economic policy: “It doesn’t matter if the cat is black or white, as long as it catches mice.”

The cat caught mice. By the time Deng’s reforms had run their course, they had produced the largest sustained reduction in poverty in recorded human history, transformed China from an isolated agricultural economy into the world’s largest manufacturer, and set in motion the chain of developments that produced the 141 pages. There is no Five-Year Plan without Deng. There is no quantum network without Deng. There is no Long Return without Deng — because without Deng, there is no China capable of attempting one.

He was also the man who ordered the tanks into Tiananmen Square.

Both things are true, and understanding Deng requires holding them simultaneously. The reformer and the authoritarian were not two different people alternating in the same body. They were the same person operating from the same set of convictions about what China needed and what the party could permit. The economic opening was not a concession to liberalism. It was a calculated bet that prosperity was the most durable foundation for party authority. Tiananmen was not a lapse. It was the enforcement of the bet’s terms.

Deng’s path to power was longer and more interrupted than almost any comparable figure in modern political history.

He had joined the Chinese Communist Party in Paris in 1922, as a young worker at a Renault factory who had come to France on a work-study programme and found politics more compelling than automotive assembly. He returned to China in 1926, worked as a party organiser through the warlord period, survived the Long March, served as a political commissar during the Civil War, and rose to become General Secretary of the Communist Party by 1956 — one of the most powerful positions in the country.

The Cultural Revolution removed him twice. The first purge came in 1966, when he was stripped of his party positions and sent, with his family, to work in a tractor factory in Jiangxi province. He spent three years there — a man in his mid-sixties who had devoted his life to building the party, operating a lathe and writing self-criticisms under the supervision of local party officials young enough to be his grandchildren. He was rehabilitated in 1973, purged again in 1976 after Zhou Enlai’s death — the radical faction accused him of being behind student protests at Tiananmen Square — and rehabilitated a final time in 1977 after Mao’s death and the arrest of the Gang of Four.

He was seventy-three years old when he consolidated power in 1978. He would govern China, formally and informally, for the next twenty years.

The Third Plenary Session of the Eleventh Central Committee, held in December 1978, is the hinge point of modern Chinese history.

It sounds bureaucratic because it was bureaucratic — a party meeting, one of hundreds, governed by the internal procedures of a Leninist organisation. What it decided was that China’s future lay in economic reform rather than class struggle, in pragmatic policy rather than ideological mobilisation, in opening to the world rather than revolutionary isolation. In the language of the communiqué, the party was “shifting the focus of work to socialist modernization.” In plain language: we are done with the Cultural Revolution, done with the Great Leap, done with Mao’s experiments. We are going to make China rich.

The agricultural reforms came first, because agriculture was where the most people lived and where the damage from collectivisation was most visible. The commune system was dismantled and replaced with the household responsibility system: farming families were given long-term leases on land and could sell their surplus production on the market after meeting a state quota. The incentive was simple and immediate — you benefit directly from your own work. Agricultural output increased sharply within a few years. The peasants who had starved under communal farming were now farming with the efficiency of people who owned the consequences of their decisions.

The Special Economic Zones came next — designated areas where foreign investment was permitted, market mechanisms operated, and the normal rules of the socialist planned economy were suspended. Shenzhen, which in 1979 was a small fishing village of 30,000 people across the border from Hong Kong, was the first. It became the test case for everything that followed: a city built almost from scratch on market principles, attracting foreign capital and technology and manufacturing expertise, growing at a pace that had no precedent in urban history. By 1990, Shenzhen had more than a million people. By 2020, it had fifteen million. It had been built in forty years from a fishing village to one of the world’s major technology centres. It is where my friend lives — the one who stopped replying to my essays.

Deng was not naive about what he was doing.

The economic reforms were not accompanied by political reforms, and this was not an oversight. Deng had thought carefully about the relationship between economic liberalisation and political freedom, and he had concluded — partly through observation of the Soviet experience and partly through his own reading of Chinese history — that political liberalisation before economic consolidation would produce instability rather than progress. The party would direct the economy. The economy would grow. Growth would create prosperity. Prosperity would legitimate the party. Political rights were not part of the exchange.

This is the bargain that Part III of the Formosa series examined from the Taiwan angle and that The Price of the Bargain examined from the urban loneliness angle. Deng constructed the architecture of it with clear eyes. He was not promising democracy later and delivering it. He was explicitly not promising democracy — ever — and delivering prosperity instead. The citizens who accepted the deal understood its terms, at least implicitly.

The Four Cardinal Principles — socialism, the people’s democratic dictatorship, the leadership of the Communist Party, and Marxism-Leninism and Mao Zedong Thought — were constitutionally enshrined precisely to make clear that the economic reforms did not extend to the political system. You could start a business. You could make money. You could travel more freely. You could buy things. You could not organise politically, advocate for multiparty elections, or challenge the party’s monopoly on power.

The students who gathered in Tiananmen Square in the spring of 1989 were asking, in effect, whether the bargain could be renegotiated. The answer was the tanks.

The Tiananmen Square protests of April to June 1989 require their own extended treatment — and will receive it in the next part of this series. But their place in the story of Deng requires a paragraph here, because the decision to suppress them was Deng’s, and it defines him as fully as the economic reforms do.

The protests began as a memorial gathering for Hu Yaobang, the reform-minded General Secretary who had been forced to resign in 1987 for being too sympathetic to student demonstrators. They grew into a movement of hundreds of thousands of students, intellectuals, and workers calling for greater political freedom, press freedom, and accountability for corruption. The images that circulated worldwide — the Goddess of Democracy statue facing Mao’s portrait across the square, the hunger strikers, the Tank Man — captured a moment when it seemed, to many observers, that China was on the edge of democratic transformation.

Deng declared martial law on May 20. On the night of June 3rd to 4th, armoured columns moved into Beijing. The death toll — which the Chinese government has never disclosed — is estimated at several hundred to perhaps several thousand, with the higher estimates including deaths in surrounding streets and neighborhoods beyond the square itself.

The world’s reaction was swift and severe: sanctions, international isolation, the suspension of high-level diplomatic contacts. Deng moved as swiftly to contain the damage. He dismissed Zhao Ziyang, the General Secretary who had opposed the crackdown, and replaced him with Jiang Zemin. He sat with the damage for three years, allowing the international reaction to cool, and then in January 1992 made his famous “southern tour” — a journey through the Special Economic Zones of Guangdong province in which the eighty-seven-year-old retired patriarch of Chinese politics gave speeches endorsing accelerated reform and signalled, through the tour itself, that Tiananmen had not ended the economic opening.

The southern tour is one of the more remarkable episodes in the history of political communication. Deng held no formal office. He was a retired man of eighty-seven, travelling by train through southern China and making speeches that were not carried by the official media — at least not initially, because the party conservatives who controlled Beijing’s media were opposed to the accelerated reform he was advocating. The speeches leaked through provincial channels, through Hong Kong newspapers, through word of mouth. The market responded before the official press caught up: when Deng’s position became clear, investment inflows into the Special Economic Zones accelerated sharply. He had communicated a policy direction by travelling to where the policy was working and talking about it to local officials, and the communication worked precisely because it bypassed the institutional resistance in Beijing.

It had not. The growth continued. The investment returned. The bargain held, because prosperity continued to be delivered.

What Deng built in the decade after Tiananmen is the economic foundation of contemporary China.

The 1990s saw the full integration of China into the global trading system: the opening of additional sectors to foreign investment, the reform of state-owned enterprises, the development of a corporate legal framework, and the sustained negotiations for WTO membership that culminated in 2001. The manufacturing capacity that made China the factory floor of the world was assembled during this period — not through state direction alone, but through the intersection of state policy, foreign investment, domestic entrepreneurship, and the extraordinary energy of a population newly permitted to pursue economic self-interest.

The WTO accession deserves a particular note, because the assumptions embedded in the Western decision to support it were wrong in ways that have reshaped global politics. The prevailing view in Washington, Brussels, and Tokyo in 2001 was that integrating China into the rules-based international trading system would make China more like the system — that exposure to market competition, contract law, and international commercial norms would gradually produce political liberalisation. Bill Clinton, who negotiated the terms of China’s accession, said explicitly that the agreement would accelerate the process of democratic reform.

The theory was not absurd. It had partial precedent in the democratic transitions of South Korea and Taiwan, both of which had liberalised politically as they developed economically. What it missed was that those transitions had occurred in countries that were American security allies with no alternative model of development available. China was neither. The party had watched the Soviet collapse and drawn a precise lesson: economic stagnation, not economic openness, was what destroyed one-party states. The WTO accession was, in the party’s reading, a tool for economic development that would strengthen rather than undermine party rule. Twenty years of evidence supports their reading.

The GDP numbers are the most cited measure and also the least humanly legible. Between 1978 and 2000, China’s GDP grew at an average of roughly ten percent per year. In 1978, GDP per capita was approximately $200. By 2000, it was approaching $1,000. By 2020, it was approaching $12,000. Hundreds of millions of people moved from subsistence farming to urban wage labour to middle-class consumption in a single generation — a compression of economic history that has no parallel.

The less cited measure is life expectancy. In 1978, average life expectancy in China was 66 years — already an improvement over the Mao era, when famine and political violence had taken their toll. By 2020, it was 77 years. People were living longer, healthier lives, in cities, with purchasing power their parents could not have imagined. The bargain, measured this way, was delivering.

Deng died in February 1997, five months before the handover of Hong Kong — which he had negotiated with Margaret Thatcher in 1984 under the “one country, two systems” formula that would govern Hong Kong for fifty years. He did not live to see the WTO accession, or the Beijing Olympics, or the 141 pages. He did not live to see what his successors would do with what he built.

The question that Deng’s legacy poses for the Long Return is this: was Deng’s system designed to produce Xi Jinping, or to prevent him?

The answer is uncomfortably: both. Deng deliberately maintained the party’s monopoly on power, which is the precondition for Xi’s consolidation. But Deng also established informal norms — collective leadership, term limits, retirement ages, the separation of party and government functions — that were intended to prevent the recurrence of one-man rule. The norms were informal because Deng could not constitutionally entrench them without challenging the party’s authority in ways he was not prepared to do. Informal norms, as China demonstrated between 2012 and 2018, can be abolished by a sufficiently determined successor.

Xi abolished them. He did so within the system Deng built, using the tools Deng had not locked away. Whether Deng, who had survived two Cultural Revolution purges and spent years in a tractor factory, would recognise what has been built with his legacy is a question Chinese citizens are not free to ask aloud.

That inability — the silence where the question should be — is the Cultural Revolution’s most durable inheritance, and Deng’s most consequential failure to address.
