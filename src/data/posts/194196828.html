Charles Keating, chairman of Lincoln Savings and Loan. “The transaction did not need to be explicit

Stephen Gordon for CanadianSnowmanSays June 6, 2026

Charles Keating understood something about money and politics that most people prefer not to say out loud.

He understood that the transaction did not need to be explicit — no handshake in a back room, no envelope passed under a table. It needed only proximity, regularity, and the shared understanding that people who give large amounts of money to politicians expect, in some general and deniable way, to receive consideration in return. The consideration need not be named. The expectation need not be stated. Everyone in the room already knows.

Keating was the chairman of Lincoln Savings and Loan, an Arizona thrift institution that he had transformed from a modest regional lender into an aggressive real estate speculation vehicle operating well outside the boundaries that federal savings and loan regulations were designed to enforce. He was also, between 1984 and 1987, one of the most generous political donors in the United States. He gave $1.3 million to the political campaigns and causes of five United States senators: Alan Cranston of California, Dennis DeConcini of Arizona, John Glenn of Ohio, Donald Riegle of Michigan, and John McCain of Arizona.

In return — and the word “return” is doing careful work here, because Keating was careful, because careful men don’t end up in back rooms with envelopes — in return, those five senators met with federal regulators who were investigating Lincoln Savings and Loan and suggested, in terms that ranged from the pointed to the merely persistent, that the investigation might be reconsidered.

The Keating Five. Senators Cranston, DeConcini, Glenn, Riegle, and McCain. $1.3 million in contributions. One Senate Ethics investigation. Four careers ended. One career transformed.

Lincoln Savings and Loan collapsed in 1989. It cost the federal government approximately $3.4 billion to clean up. More than twenty thousand depositors lost their savings. Charles Keating went to prison. And five United States senators found themselves the subject of a Senate Ethics Committee investigation that would, in the case of four of them, effectively end their careers.

The fifth was John McCain. And his story is the one that matters for this series, because it is the story of a man who looked directly at what the soft money system had made of him and decided, at considerable personal and political cost, that he could not leave it unchanged.

The Gap the Parties Found

To understand what McCain was caught up in, you need to understand the system that had grown up around the limits that Buckley v. Valeo had left standing.

The 1974 campaign finance reforms, as we saw in Part I, survived Buckley in partial form. Contribution limits remained. Disclosure requirements remained. Public financing for presidential elections remained. What Buckley had destroyed was the limit on independent expenditures — money spent to support candidates without direct coordination with their campaigns.

But the parties, and the lawyers who served them, had identified another gap. The contribution limits applied to money given directly to candidates. They applied, with some complexity, to money given to party committees for the purpose of influencing federal elections. What they did not clearly cover — or what the parties successfully argued they did not clearly cover — was money given to party organizations for activities that were technically separate from federal campaign activity. Voter registration drives. Party building. Generic advertising that promoted the party without explicitly advocating for specific candidates. Get-out-the-vote operations.

This money was called soft money, in contrast to the hard money of regulated direct contributions. It was soft in the sense of being unregulated. It was soft in the sense of having no legally enforceable limit on its amount. It was soft in the sense that, like water finding its level, it moved wherever gravity — meaning political necessity — directed it, regardless of the channels the law had tried to build.

The Federal Election Commission, in a series of advisory opinions during the late 1970s and early 1980s, effectively blessed the soft money system by declining to regulate it comprehensively. The reasoning was technical and the consequences were enormous. Once the FEC had indicated that party soft money was not clearly prohibited, the parties began raising it in earnest. Both parties. This is important to establish at the outset: soft money was a bipartisan enterprise. The Republicans were better at it for most of the 1980s, because they had better access to corporate donors and wealthy individuals who were enthusiastic about the Reagan agenda. The Democrats caught up in the 1990s, partly out of competitive necessity and partly because Bill Clinton turned out to be a prodigious fundraiser who understood intuitively that the rules were softer than they appeared.

The Numbers

In 1984, the two parties raised a combined total of approximately $19 million in soft money. By 1988, that figure had risen to $45 million. By 1992, it was $86 million. By 1996 — the election that would eventually provoke the most serious reform effort since Watergate — the two parties raised a combined $263 million in soft money. By 2000, it was $495 million. By 2002, the last election cycle before McCain-Feingold took effect, it had reached $496 million.

Those numbers represent the aggregate. The individual contributions that composed them are, in many ways, more instructive. In the 1996 cycle, the Lippo Group, an Indonesian conglomerate with extensive interests in American business and American policy, funneled approximately $450,000 into Democratic Party soft money accounts. A Taiwan-connected businessman named Johnny Chung gave $366,000 to the Democratic National Committee and later told investigators he understood the money bought him access to the Clinton White House. A Buddhist temple in Hacienda Heights, California hosted a fundraiser for Vice President Al Gore that generated $140,000 in contributions, many of which turned out to have been illegally reimbursed by the temple itself to its monks and nuns, who had taken vows of poverty and were not supposed to be making political donations.

None of this was, technically, a direct contribution to a candidate. All of it was, functionally, exactly that.

The soft money machine had one central operating principle: the form of the restriction was maintained while the substance was hollowed out. The hard money limits told donors they could not give more than a certain amount directly to a candidate. The soft money system told donors they could give unlimited amounts to the party, which would then spend the money in ways that benefited the candidate, and this was different. Everyone in the room understood it was not different. The lawyers said it was different. The FEC, deadlocked along partisan lines, declined to say it wasn’t.

The Senator Who Had Been Inside the Machine

John McCain arrived in the Senate in 1987, one year before the Keating Five meetings that would define and nearly destroy his political career. He was a war hero — five and a half years as a prisoner of war in Hanoi, torture, solitary confinement, the refusal of early release that became the foundational story of his public identity. He was a conservative Republican from Arizona with a reliable voting record and a genuine belief, forged in circumstances most politicians never face, that personal honor was not merely a campaign slogan.

He was also, in the ordinary way of Senate politics, raising money from the people who needed things from the Senate. Charles Keating was one of those people. Keating was from Arizona. He was wealthy. He was generous. He gave approximately $112,000 to causes connected to McCain. McCain met with the regulators. McCain later said he had done nothing improper, that his participation in the meetings was limited and reluctant, and that he had quickly removed himself when he sensed they were heading somewhere he did not want to go.

The Senate Ethics Committee found that McCain had exercised “poor judgment” but had not acted improperly. He was the only one of the five senators to receive that relatively mild finding. The other four received findings ranging from poor judgment to having substantially and improperly interfered with federal regulators.

For three of the five — Cranston, Riegle, DeConcini — the Keating affair effectively ended their Senate careers. Glenn and McCain survived politically but not without cost. Glenn spent years rehabilitating his reputation. McCain spent years doing something more unusual: trying to understand how the system that had produced the Keating Five had been built, and whether it could be dismantled.

The answer he arrived at, slowly and with considerable resistance from his own party, was that it could not be dismantled without confronting soft money directly. The Keating contributions had been hard money — regulated, disclosed, within the technical limits. But the culture that made those contributions routine, that made the meetings that followed them unremarkable, that made the entire transaction feel like ordinary politics rather than corruption — that culture was soft money’s gift to American political life. When there is no limit on how much money can flow through the system, and no clear line between legal generosity and illegal favor-seeking, the line between a donor and a purchaser becomes negotiable. McCain had been on the wrong side of that negotiation and he knew it.

The Arms Race

The soft money arms race of the 1990s had a quality that distinguished it from the more familiar forms of campaign finance corruption: it was completely legal, extensively documented, and widely understood by everyone involved to be corroding the institution it was nominally supporting.

This is not a small thing. The senators and party officials who were raising soft money in seven- and eight-figure amounts were not, for the most part, under any illusion about what the money was for or what it implied. Bob Dole, who ran for president in 1996 on a platform that included mild gestures toward campaign finance reform, was simultaneously raising soft money at industrial scale. Newt Gingrich, who led the Republican revolution of 1994 in part by attacking Democratic corruption, presided over a party apparatus that was raising soft money from corporations with business before Congress with the efficiency of a well-run collection agency. The Democrats were doing the same thing, with the added entertainment value of the Buddhist temple and the Indonesian conglomerate.

The shared understanding was: this is how it works. The individual understanding was: I did not create this system and I will be punished if I unilaterally disarm within it. The collective outcome was: the system got worse every cycle because the competitive logic required each party to raise more than the other, which required both parties to make themselves available to larger and larger donors, which required the parties to be more and more responsive to those donors’ expectations.

This is not a conspiracy. It is an arms race. Arms races do not require anyone to be evil. They require everyone to be rational, in the narrow sense of responding to incentives. The incentives were: raise more money or lose. Everyone raised more money. The system got worse.

The Partnership

Feingold and McCain pressing the case for reform — the long, lonely fight.

McCain found his partner in Russ Feingold, a Democratic senator from Wisconsin who had been elected in 1992 on a platform that included a voluntary spending limit agreement — he would limit his own campaign spending if his opponent agreed to do the same. His opponent did not agree. Feingold limited his spending anyway. He won anyway. This established Feingold’s political identity as a man who meant what he said about campaign finance, which was unusual enough to be genuinely distinctive.

The McCain-Feingold bill — formally the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act, a name chosen specifically to prevent it from being dismissed as partisan — was introduced in 1995 and proceeded to fail repeatedly, in ways that would have been comic if the underlying subject were less serious. It was filibustered. It was amended into incoherence and then restored. It was passed by one chamber and stalled in the other. It was the subject of procedural maneuvers so baroque that following them required a specialist.

McCain kept bringing it back. He brought it back because the Keating affair had given him something that most politicians who survive a scandal do not acquire: a genuine understanding of the system’s mechanics and a genuine motivation to change them that was not purely rhetorical. He had been inside the machine. He knew what it did to you. He was not interested in pretending otherwise.

His own party was largely hostile. The Republican establishment understood that soft money was currently flowing more generously in their direction and saw no particular reason to turn off the tap. Mitch McConnell of Kentucky, who had identified campaign spending as speech with the enthusiasm of a man who had thought carefully about which constitutional principles were most convenient, became the legislation’s most persistent and creative opponent. McConnell’s position was principled in the way that positions tend to be principled when they align precisely with the interests of the people holding them.

Seven Years, Then Seven More

Senators John McCain and Russ Feingold. Seven years. One bill. Dismantled in seven years more.

The Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act passed in 2002, after seven years of effort, because several things converged simultaneously. Enron had collapsed, taking with it the retirement savings of thousands of employees and the credibility of a Republican Party that had taken millions in Enron money. September 11 had created a brief window of national unity in which obstruction felt politically untenable. And McCain had become, through his 2000 presidential primary campaign — in which he ran explicitly on campaign finance reform and won several states before losing to George W. Bush — a figure with sufficient popular support that blocking him indefinitely had become expensive.

The Act banned soft money contributions to national party committees. It restricted the use of corporate and union funds for “electioneering communications” — advertising that mentioned a federal candidate within sixty days of a general election or thirty days of a primary. It was the most significant campaign finance reform since the post-Watergate legislation of 1974.

It was immediately challenged in court. The Supreme Court upheld its core provisions in McConnell v. FEC in 2003, in a decision that suggested the soft money ban would survive.

It survived for seven years.

In 2010, Citizens United arrived. We will get to Citizens United in Part V, because it deserves its own reckoning. What matters here is the pattern: reform passed, reform challenged, reform partially upheld, reform eventually dismantled. The maze resets. The dots reappear.

McCain lived until 2018. He watched Citizens United hollow out the legislation he had spent seven years of his Senate career building. He said, publicly, that the decision was wrong and that its consequences would be severe. He had earned the right to say it. He had seen the system from the inside — as a beneficiary, as a near-casualty, as a reformer, and finally as a man watching his reform be undone by a court that had decided the problem he had spent his career trying to solve was actually a constitutional right.

There is something almost classical about that arc. The hero’s journey, in American campaign finance, ends not with the monster slain but with the monster declared protected by the First Amendment and therefore immune to slaying.

The soft money machine is gone, technically. What replaced it is larger, faster, and considerably harder to see.
