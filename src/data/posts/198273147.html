On Saturday evening, the official White House X account did something that would have made even the most reckless Bond villain pause and reconsider. It posted an AI-generated image of the 79-year-old President of the United States styled as James Bond — tuxedo, silenced pistol, gun-barrel silhouette, the works — with “Make America Great Again” stamped across it like a tagline and the official 007 logo tucked into the corner. The caption was a single eyes emoji. The context was that Amazon MGM Studios had just announced the search for Daniel Craig’s successor, with casting director Nina Gold leading the auditions.

The internet did what the internet does. Within twenty-four hours, the post had three million views and approximately zero positive comments. One user noted that Trump would be better cast as Auric Goldfinger — the Bond villain literally obsessed with gold. Another, referencing the late Jeffrey Epstein, posted Licence to Groom. And a commenter offered what may be the most surgically precise review of the Trump foreign policy doctrine ever written in a single sentence: “Walks into a villain lair, immediately announces ‘great conversation, very productive,’ gives the villain a 10-day extension, a 2nd extension, and probably a golf invite, and by the end credits he’s still very close to a deal.”

But satire, like intelligence work, requires structure. So let us do what the White House failed to do: take the premise seriously. If Donald Trump is James Bond, then there must be a film. Let us review it.

The Spy Who Loved Himself

Directed by: The Algorithm Produced by: Amazon MGM Studios (a subsidiary of the Bezos empire, which is definitely not currying favour with anyone) Casting Director: Nina Gold (the universe is not subtle) Runtime: Four years, or until the sequels run out of constitutional amendments to violate

Act I: From Russia with Love

Every Bond film needs a geopolitical backdrop, and our protagonist has provided one so generous it barely requires embellishment. The original From Russia with Love featured a British agent navigating Cold War duplicity, playing East against West with lethal precision. Our version features a protagonist who navigates the same terrain by publicly praising the Eastern adversary, accepting his intelligence assessments over those of his own agencies, and meeting him privately with no note-takers present.

Bond carried a concealed weapon. Our protagonist carries a concealed admiration.

In the Fleming novels, Bond’s loyalty to queen and country was the fixed point around which every mission turned. He could be seduced, poisoned, tortured, and left for dead, but the allegiance never wavered. It was the one thing that made the martinis and the murder palatable — the knowledge that beneath the tailored exterior was a man who served something larger than himself.

The casting director for the new film, we are told, is looking for someone who can “ooze sex appeal” and commit to at least three films. The White House appears to have interpreted this as a job posting.

Act II: Goldfinger

Of all the Bond villains the internet might have reached for, the one that stuck was Auric Goldfinger — and not merely because his name contains the word “gold.” Goldfinger was a man consumed by a single metal. He cheated at cards. He painted women in gold as trophies. He hatched an elaborate scheme not to steal Fort Knox but to irradiate it, rendering America’s gold reserves worthless so that his own holdings would skyrocket in value. He didn’t want to serve the system. He wanted to corrupt it so thoroughly that he became the system.

The casting director’s name is Nina Gold. The universe, as noted, is not subtle.

Goldfinger surrounded himself with people whose loyalty was purchased, not earned. He employed Oddjob — a silent enforcer who never questioned orders, whose hat was literally a weapon, and whose entire function was to eliminate problems before they became public. Goldfinger did not tolerate dissent. He did not invite debate. He delivered a famous lecture to the assembled mob bosses explaining his plan in exquisite detail, and then killed them all anyway, because the lecture was never about persuasion. It was about performance.

One searches the Trump cabinet table and wonders which of them is Oddjob and which is merely waiting to be gold-painted.

Act III: Casino Bankrupt

Bond was a gambler, but a disciplined one — a man who understood odds, read opponents, and knew when to fold. The poker scene in Casino Royale is a masterclass in controlled risk: Bond loses everything, gets poisoned, flatlines, is defibrillated back to life, returns to the table, and wins with a hand that was never about the cards. It was about nerve.

Our protagonist also gambles. He has operated casinos — several, in fact, in Atlantic City, where the house is supposed to always win. The casinos went bankrupt. Not once. Not twice. Multiple times. This is the mathematical equivalent of losing a fight with gravity. The house edge in a casino is built into the architecture. You have to work at losing.

And yet here he is, at the biggest table in the world, playing with chips denominated in tariff percentages and aircraft carrier deployments, and the tell is always the same: the bluff is announced in advance, on social media, in capital letters. Bond never told Blofeld what he was planning. Our protagonist posts it at 3 a.m. with a spelling error and a threat.

The Iran war — which has cost American lives, shattered what remained of Middle Eastern stability, and prompted the president to publicly declare “I don’t think about Americans’ financial situation” — is the raise that nobody at the table asked for. Bond played poker to fund the mission. This one plays poker as the mission, and the chips belong to other people.

Act IV: Die Another Day (But Not Today, Because We Defunded the CDC)

Bond was perpetually at risk of death. It was the engine of every plot. But Bond also had Q — the genius quartermaster who provided the tools, the intelligence, and the technological edge that kept 007 alive. Q was the institutional infrastructure behind the myth. Without Q, Bond was just a man in a nice suit with a drinking problem.

In our version, Q has been replaced by Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who has suggested that the gadgets might actually be causing the problems, that the bulletproof vest is untested, and that the exploding pen might be linked to autism. The laboratories have been defunded. The scientists have been reassigned or fired. The institutional memory that kept the mission running has been systematically dismantled in favour of a man who once sued a vaccine manufacturer and now oversees the nation’s public health infrastructure.

Bond relied on MI6. MI6 was the deep state that made the spy state functional. Our protagonist has declared war on the deep state — which, in Bond terms, means the spy has turned on the agency, destroyed the files, and is now operating freelance while still collecting the government salary and using the government jet.

M would have had him killed by the end of the first act.

Act V: The World Is Not Enough

The title of the nineteenth Bond film was borrowed from the Bond family motto — Orbis non sufficit, the world is not enough. It was meant as aspiration: a man whose reach exceeded the ordinary, whose appetites were larger than life, whose commitment to the mission transcended borders.

Apply it to the current administration and the meaning inverts. The world is not enough — not enough to tariff, not enough to threaten, not enough to reshape in one’s own image. The tariff regime has turned allies into adversaries and adversaries into leverage opportunities. Canada, the country that shares the longest undefended border in human history with the United States — that fought alongside America in every major conflict of the twentieth century, that is America’s largest trading partner — has been subjected to tariffs justified by the invocation of “national security.” Bond protected the Western alliance. Our protagonist has decided the alliance is the enemy.

And then there is the Bezos question. Amazon MGM Studios now controls the Bond franchise, having struck a deal in February 2025 with longtime producers Michael G. Wilson and Barbara Broccoli. Jeff Bezos, the founder of Amazon, owns the Washington Post — the same Washington Post that spiked its presidential endorsement in 2024 under circumstances that suggested something other than editorial independence. Bezos has attended Trump functions. He has navigated the administration with the careful deference of a man who understands that his regulatory environment is controlled by someone who governs by grievance.

So when the White House posts an AI image of the president as Bond, and the Bond franchise is owned by Amazon, and Amazon is owned by a man who has been accused of currying favour with the administration — the question is not whether the image is absurd. The question is whether it is an audition tape or a loyalty test.

Act VI: No Time to Read

James Bond, for all his vices, was a man who did his homework. He read the dossier. He studied the target. He knew the floor plan of the villain’s lair before he walked in. The novels are full of Bond absorbing briefings, memorizing details, learning languages, and mastering the local customs of wherever the mission took him. He was a professional.

The current occupant of the role has been credibly reported to skip intelligence briefings, to require information in bullet points with his name mentioned frequently to maintain engagement, and to derive his understanding of world events primarily from cable television and social media. Bond went to the casino having memorized the odds. Our protagonist goes to the summit having memorized the applause lines.

When the White House posted that image — the gun-barrel pose, the tuxedo, the MAGA slogan — they were not making a joke. That is the unnerving part. The AI-generated pope image was not a joke. The Christ-like healing image was not a joke. These are not acts of humour. They are acts of mythmaking, deployed with the regularity and strategic purpose of a propaganda ministry that has learned to speak in memes.

Bond was fiction that knew it was fiction. The martini was shaken because Ian Fleming thought it sounded better, not because it was operationally superior. The Aston Martin was a fantasy. The women were a fantasy. The entire enterprise was a fantasy — but it was a fantasy that never mistook itself for reality.

The White House image mistakes itself for reality. It says: this is who we are. A man of action. A man of style. A man with a gun and a mission and a slogan. And if you squint — if you ignore the bankrupt casinos and the classified documents in the bathroom and the war that nobody voted for and the cost of groceries and the pardoned insurrectionists — you can almost see it.

Almost.

The Final Scene

In every Bond film, the last shot resolves everything. The villain is dead. The world is saved. The girl is in the lifeboat. The music swells. The credits roll. Bond will return.

But this is not a Bond film. This is a White House social media post made by a government account using AI-generated imagery to insert the president of the United States into a fictional British spy franchise owned by a tech oligarch who may or may not be angling for regulatory relief. And the response — three million views, universal mockery, parody images involving Big Macs and Epstein — tells you everything you need to know about the distance between the image and the reality.

James Bond served queen and country. He was flawed, violent, alcoholic, and morally compromised — but he served. The mission came first. The ego came second. The suit was camouflage, not costume.

The Spy Who Loved Himself has no mission. He has no country — only an audience. The suit is the point. The gun is a prop. And the only thing shaken is the republic.

Bond will return. The question is whether democracy will.
