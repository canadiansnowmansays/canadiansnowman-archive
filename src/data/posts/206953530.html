Postmark: An operating room, UC San Diego. A gallbladder, a pig, two machines nicknamed Surgie. The hands that moved were human. The human was somewhere else.

Part V of this series was called The Hands That Never Shake, and it was about the machines that had already quietly conquered the operating room — the fixed-arm surgical systems, the da Vinci and its kin, that filter a surgeon’s tremor and let a scalpel move with a steadiness no human wrist can manage. That postcard ended on a frontier it could not yet cross: those machines were magnificent but immovable, 1,800-pound installations bolted into retrofitted rooms, available only where a hospital could afford the space and the specialized team. The surgical robot had arrived, but it had arrived in the wealthiest operating rooms on Earth and stayed there.

This postcard is the sequel, and its occasion is a threshold crossed on July 8, when a team of engineers and surgeons at the University of California, San Diego published a paper in Nature describing something that had never appeared in the medical literature: two general-purpose humanoid robots, controlled from a distance by human operators, completing live surgical procedures on a living patient. In one operation, a humanoid worked alongside a human surgeon acting as assistant to remove a gallbladder. In the other, two humanoids performed the procedure together, with no human hands anywhere near the table. The patient was a pig — this is a preclinical study, and no human has yet gone under a robot’s instruments — but the milestone is real and dated: for the first time, general-purpose humanoid machines have operated on a living body.

And the detail that makes this a Tomorrow postcard rather than a press release is the one hiding in the phrase “controlled from a distance.” The hands that removed that gallbladder moved with human intention, human judgment, human skill. They were simply not attached to a human. The surgeon was in the room in every sense that matters and absent in the one sense we have always taken for granted. This is the story of the surgeon who wasn’t there — and of why that absence may be the most hopeful thing this series has reported.

The machine you have already met

Here is the fact that should stop a regular reader of these postcards cold: the robots that performed this surgery are machines you already know.

They are Unitree G1s. The same platform — five feet tall, roughly sixty pounds, off the shelf, purchasable for under twenty thousand dollars — that danced the Drunken Fist at the Spring Festival Gala in Part IX, that hauls cargo on the tarmac at Haneda in Part XIII, that sits in university robotics labs the world over. The UC San Diego team did not custom-build a surgical robot. They bought two of the cheap, mass-produced humanoids that this entire series has been tracking, fitted them with custom adapters so they could hold standard laparoscopic tools, nicknamed them “Surgie,” and taught them to operate. The dexterous three-fingered hands, the extra wrist and waist articulation, the onboard lidar and depth camera — the same features that let the G1 dance and fetch and sort — turn out to be the features that let it hold tissue with one hand while cutting with the other, coordinating both arms the way a human surgeon does.

I have argued across fourteen postcards that the Unitree G1 is the universal substrate of the humanoid century — the Model T, the Raspberry Pi, the one cheap machine endlessly re-tasked. This is the proof, and it is more vivid than any I expected to find. The same object, unchanged in its bones, is a dancer in Beijing, a baggage handler in Tokyo, and now a surgeon in La Jolla. That is not three technologies. It is one technology, wearing three costumes, and the costume that removes your gallbladder costs less than a used car. The economic argument that runs through this whole series — that the Chinese supply chain has made the humanoid body cheap enough to point at any problem — reaches its most consequential form here. When the machine that can save your life is the same machine that danced on television, the price of saving your life has just collapsed.

The case for the surgeon who wasn’t there

Why does this matter enough to publish in Nature? The answer is a single word the researchers return to again and again: access.

The surgical robots of Part V are extraordinary and almost entirely confined to the rich world. As the UC San Diego team explained, a conventional system weighs some 1,800 pounds, arrives with three or four arms and proprietary software, needs a large trained team to operate, and usually requires the operating room itself to be rebuilt around it. That is why robotic surgery lives in major-city hospitals and nowhere else. The humanoid inverts every one of those constraints. It is mobile, compact, and light enough to move around a normal room. It uses standard tools. It fits, as one of the surgeons put it, into the space a bedside human assistant would occupy, because it is shaped like the human assistant it stands in for. And it can be driven from anywhere there is a network connection.

Follow that last point to where the researchers point it, because it is genuinely moving. As reported, one of the study’s senior authors — a surgeon who teleoperated a robot during the trial — described the deployment vision in a single sentence: it is a fraction of the cost and takes a fraction of the space, so it is easy to deploy anywhere, from rural areas to the battlefield to space. A colleague imagined the device on a ship, in a village, in an austere search-and-rescue scenario where field medicine has to arrive fast and small. This is the promise: a specialist surgeon in a major hospital, driving a twenty-thousand-dollar humanoid in a village clinic a thousand miles away, removing an appendix from a patient who would otherwise have died for want of a surgeon within reach. The surgeon who wasn’t there is the surgeon who could not otherwise have been there at all.

And notice how this reframes the anxiety that has shadowed the second wave of these postcards. Part XV worried about the stranger behind the home robot’s eyes — the remote operator with a view into your living room. Part XIV traced the same teleoperation structure onto the battlefield, where the human behind the machine directs lethal force from miles away. Here is the identical structure — a human mind piped through a distant machine — turned to the oldest and best of human purposes: healing a body that no local hands can reach. The teleoperated humanoid is neither good nor evil in itself. It is a lever, and the moral weight is entirely in what it lifts. On the battlefield it lifts a gun. In the living room it lifts your laundry and, sometimes, your privacy. In the operating room it lifts a scalpel toward a life that would otherwise be lost. Same lever. The difference is the hand on it, and the intent behind the hand.

The honest limits, and the line that holds

This series does not sell fantasies, and the surgical postcard is no exception — least of all here, where the stakes are a living body.

The trial was a proof of concept, and it wore its rough edges openly. As the researchers acknowledged, the robots required recalibration multiple times during the procedures, which slowed the work considerably, and network latency — the lag between the surgeon’s motion and the robot’s response — remains a real obstacle to anything approaching routine clinical use. This is not a technology ready for your local hospital; it is a first demonstration that the thing is possible at all, with a long road of engineering between here and a human patient. The team is candid that recalibration must become automatic and latency must fall through better networks and edge computing before this leaves the research setting. A gallbladder came out of a pig. That is the achievement, stated exactly: no more, and no less.

But here is the line that holds, and it is the same line this series found on the battlefield in Part XIV, arrived at from the opposite moral direction. The autonomy is deferred — deliberately, explicitly, by choice. These robots did not decide anything. Every motion traced a human surgeon’s motion; every judgment was a human judgment relayed through steel. The team frames fuller autonomy — a robot that anticipates the surgeon’s next move, or eventually performs selected steps under remote supervision — as a future goal, a horizon, not a present capability and not a present intention. For now, and by design, the human remains in the loop, hands on the controls, mind on the patient.

Contrast this with the other path the field is walking. A different group, at Johns Hopkins, has trained a non-humanoid system on surgical videos to perform a phase of a gallbladder operation without human help — the autonomous approach, the machine making its own moves. Both paths are being walked at once, and the contrast is the whole ethical landscape of robotic medicine in miniature: the teleoperated humanoid keeps the surgeon in command and extends their reach; the autonomous system removes the surgeon and replaces their judgment. UC San Diego chose reach over replacement. That choice — the human amplified rather than the human erased — is the one this series has argued for, gently but consistently, across every territory it has visited. It is worth noticing when a field, at its frontier, chooses it too.

The questions the scalpel leaves behind

No honest postcard about a machine cutting into a living body can stop at the wonder, and the surgical humanoid raises a cluster of questions the operating-room lights throw into sharp relief.

Start with the one a hundred internet commenters asked within hours of the news, in the blunt way the internet asks things: who do you sue when the robot takes out the wrong organ? It is a crude phrasing of a real problem. Robotic surgery already sits inside a tangle of liability — surgeon, hospital, manufacturer — and the teleoperated humanoid stretches that tangle across distance and across the seam between the human driving and the machine executing. If a specialist in Toronto drives a robot in a northern clinic and a network lag causes a slip, where does the fault land: on the surgeon, the hospital that bought the robot, the company that made it, or the telecom whose network stuttered at the wrong instant? These are not reasons to stop — every surgical advance in history arrived with an unsettled liability question — but they are the questions that will occupy the ethicists and administrators and, eventually, the courts, long before Surgie touches a human patient.

Then there is the deskilling question, which this series’ parent publication has tracked across every profession AI has touched. If humanoid robots handle the routine portions of surgery, what happens to the surgical skill that comes only from doing the routine portions ten thousand times? The pilot who never hand-flies forgets how; the radiologist who defers to the algorithm loses the eye. Medicine will have to decide, deliberately, how much of the craft it is willing to relocate into machines, and how it will train the surgeons of a generation that learned partly by watching a robot do what they will one day be asked to do without one. The empty chair beside the operating table saves a life today. Whether it hollows out the profession that fills it tomorrow is a question the Nature paper cannot answer and the medical schools will have to.

And place all of this inside the industrial story this series has been telling, because it explains why the surgical humanoid arrived now and not in ten years. The global surgical-robotics market is projected to reach into the billions within the decade, and the reason a research lab could suddenly attempt this is precisely the price collapse Part VIII documented: when the humanoid body costs twenty thousand dollars instead of a million, a university team can buy two of them and try something audacious. The same Morgan Stanley trajectory that has China building hundreds of thousands of humanoids a year by 2030 is what puts a surgical humanoid within a research budget’s reach today. The breakthrough in La Jolla is not separate from the dancing robots and the cargo handlers and the marathon runner. It is the same collapsing price curve, arriving at last in the one room where the stakes are a human life — which is exactly why it deserved the cover of Nature rather than a viral clip.

What the empty chair means

From the balcony, the surgeon who wasn’t there leaves us with the question this whole series has been circling, now posed in an operating room where the stakes could not be higher.

We have spent seventeen postcards watching humans recede from the frame — the dancer replaced by the machine, the baggage handler, the soldier, the housekeeper, and now the surgeon, each stepping back as a humanoid steps forward. And the reflexive reading of that recession is loss: the human displaced, made redundant, hidden behind the machine that takes the credit. But the operating room offers a different and truer reading. The surgeon who wasn’t there did not vanish. She was relocated — her skill extended across a thousand miles to a patient she could never have reached in the flesh, her hands multiplied into places her body could never go. The empty chair at the operating table is not a human subtracted. It is a human distributed — one specialist’s judgment made available to ten villages, a hundred ships, a forward base, a future colony on the Moon that Part X reminds us Canada is already building the arms for.

A Canadian feels the force of this with particular sharpness, because access to care across vast and empty distance is not an abstraction to us — it is the central problem of Canadian medicine, the reason a person in a northern community can be a five-hour flight from a surgeon who exists in abundance in Toronto. We have spent a century trying to close that gap with bush planes and telehealth and the sheer stubbornness of rural doctors. A twenty-thousand-dollar humanoid that a specialist in a teaching hospital can drive from her own operating room is not a threat to that project. It is the most promising tool it has ever been offered. The surgeon who wasn’t there is, for the patient in the medical desert, the surgeon who finally was.

That is the hope, held honestly, with the recalibration delays and the latency and the long road to a human patient all kept in view. The machine that danced on television removed a gallbladder in La Jolla, guided by a human mind it could not see and did not question. The hands never shook, because they were, at the root, human hands — merely stretched, by twenty thousand dollars of Chinese steel and a good network connection, farther than human hands have ever reached. If the humanoid century has a case for itself, its best case is not on the stage or the battlefield or the tarmac. It is here, in the empty chair beside the operating table, where a surgeon is present and absent at once, and a life that distance had written off gets saved by hands that were never in the room.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one was signed by a surgeon who wasn’t there.
