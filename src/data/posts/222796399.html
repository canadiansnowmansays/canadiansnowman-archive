Every election office in America keeps a binder for the bad day.

It is a dull document by design. It tells a county clerk what to do when the power fails at a polling place, when a river comes over its banks the night before the vote, when a tabulator jams or a server goes down or a fire alarm empties a school gymnasium full of voters. Election administrators are, by temperament and training, the country’s most patient pessimists. They plan for the things that almost never happen so that, when one of them does, nobody notices.

This year the binder has a new chapter.

As Richard Fausset, Zolan Kanno-Youngs and David W. Chen reported for The New York Times on October 27, 2026, state and local officials across the country are now training poll workers on what to do if immigration enforcement officers appear at a polling place. They are drilling staff on the federal statute that keeps armed agents away from the vote. They are walking through, line by line, what a federal election monitor may and may not do on November 3. The overarching worry, distilled, is that the administration will try to interfere with the voting, the count, or both. The difficult part, the officials told the Times, is not knowing what shape the interference will take, or whether it will come at all.

Maine’s secretary of state, Shenna Bellows, said this is the first time her office has ever had to plan for interference from Washington, and that the federal government now looks adversarial to the states. That is a remarkable sentence for a state official to say on the record. It is more remarkable that, days before a national election, nobody found it surprising.

The slide nobody wanted to write

Start with the small thing, because the small things tell you most.

Bellows’s office added a new slide this year to its training presentation for law enforcement. It contains the text of the federal law barring armed federal agents from polling places, the same statute this series examined in Part LII, the one that carries up to five years in prison. Nobody in Maine believes a slide will stop an agent. The slide exists so that a sheriff’s deputy standing outside a church hall in Aroostook County knows, on the morning itself, that the law is on the side of the people inside. Bellows called the need for it sad, and said it simply reflects where the administration’s rhetoric and actions have left things in 2026.

Tina Barton, co-chair of the Committee for Safe and Secure Elections, gave the Times a number that says the same thing at scale. Her group has helped run about 140 training sessions this year for local officials and law enforcement. In 2024, a presidential year, it ran 67. The election is smaller. The training load has more than doubled. One of the scenarios her team ran involved ICE enforcement near a polling location, after congressional staff raised the possibility, despite the homeland security secretary’s assurance that ICE will not patrol polling stations.

The Government Counsel and Election Officials Network has gone further, posting samples of what a federal grand jury subpoena and a search warrant actually look like, so a county clerk will recognise one if it is handed across the counter. Its guidance on what to do when agents arrive with a warrant begins, sensibly, with an instruction to stay calm. It is good advice. It is also advice that, until this cycle, no one thought election clerks would need.

The clerk who used to worry about tornadoes

Barb Byrum has been the clerk of Ingham County, Michigan, for nearly fourteen years. The county includes Lansing, the state capital. For most of that time, by her own account, the threat she planned for was weather.

In August, the Justice Department deployed federal election monitors to her county for the state primary, the first such deployment she had seen in her tenure. Afterwards, federal officials suggested there had been long lines, that voters had been denied provisional ballots, and that precinct workers had failed to check voters against the state’s qualified voter file. Byrum told the Times that none of it was true.

Her response was not a press conference. It was more training. Her election workers now know precisely what Michigan law permits a federal monitor to do: watch. Not touch, not question, not handle anything. Watch. Byrum said she found it disheartening that the federal government had become, in her planning, a potential threat to the election she runs.

Michigan’s attorney general, Dana Nessel, took the opposite public tack in the same week. After the Justice Department’s civil rights division notified seven Michigan municipalities that monitors would attend their polling places on November 3, Nessel reminded residents that federal monitors are a standard feature of American elections and that she expects them to follow state and federal law. Both women are Democrats. Both are right. Monitors are routine. A monitoring report that alleges failures the county says never happened is not, and it is the second thing, not the first, that sent Byrum back to the training room.

That is the friction thesis this series has been building since the summer, seen from the receiving end. A monitor who files an inaccurate report has broken no law. The report need not be acted on. It only needs to exist, so that a clerk spends September retraining staff and October wondering what the report will be used for in November.

A guessing game with three doors

What are the officials actually preparing for? The Times laid out the menu, and every item on it has a precedent in this series.

The first door is a seizure. In January 2026, the FBI took some 650 to 700 boxes of 2020 ballots from Fulton County, Georgia, as Part LI recorded, in an investigation that, nine months on, has produced no charges. Officials now ask whether the same could happen to 2026 ballots or machines, during the count rather than five years after it. Trump has said he regrets not seizing voting machines after he lost in 2020. Clerks have read that sentence too.

The second door is the polling place itself. Could ICE or other federal agents appear at voting sites? The homeland security secretary says ICE will go near a polling station only to investigate a specific threat. Kash Patel, as Part LII documented, could not quite bring himself to give the Senate a plain no about FBI agents. Officials are not planning around the reassurance. They are planning around the gap between the reassurance and the evasion.

The third door is the one nobody wants to name. The President has refused to rule out invoking the Insurrection Act in connection with the election. No one the Times spoke to thinks troops at polling places are likely. Several are planning for it anyway, because the cost of being unprepared is unthinkable and the cost of the drill is an afternoon.

Minnesota’s secretary of state, Steve Simon, put the problem in a single image: “We really are shadowboxing here.” Officials cannot see the blow, cannot know whether it is coming, and cannot know what it will look like if it does. So they practise against every shape at once.

It is not only Democrats

The temptation is to read all this as one party bracing against the other. The record does not allow it.

Utah’s lieutenant governor, Deidre Henderson, who oversees elections in one of the most Republican states in the country, said recently that while she does not necessarily expect federal interference, her state is planning for it all the same. A Republican election chief in a state Trump carried comfortably has looked at the same evidence as a Democrat in Maine and reached the same operational conclusion: write the chapter, run the drill, hope it stays in the binder.

The White House response was delivered by spokeswoman Lauren Bis, who told the Times that the only worries about election security come from radical Democrats who refuse to enact voter ID and citizenship verification, and that the President has done more than any of his predecessors to ensure free and fair elections. It is an answer to a question nobody asked. The officials were not worried about voter ID. They were worried about subpoenas, monitors, agents and soldiers, all of which come from the executive branch the spokeswoman works for. And the answer did not address the Republican in Salt Lake City at all.

Readers of this series will recognise the shape. Threaten (prosecution for counting ballots, as California heard in Part LVIII). Obfuscate (the problem is voter ID). Accommodate (federal officials dismiss the idea of agents or soldiers at the polls). What the officials are bracing for is the gap between those three, because in that gap anything remains possible and nothing has been promised.

The subpoena on the counter

On October 30, the scenario the Government Counsel and Election Officials Network had illustrated with sample documents stopped being a sample. The Justice Department issued federal grand jury subpoenas to local election officials in California, widening its effort to prove an unsubstantiated claim that thousands of noncitizens are on the state’s voter rolls, as NPR reported. The union representing California’s election workers went straight to court. Its lawsuit accuses the department of “unprecedented and unlawful threats” that risk interfering with an election already under way, and with the counting of its ballots.

The same day brought the other half of the pattern: a federal appeals panel refused, two to one, to make California set aside about 20,000 mail-in ballots from people the administration calls likely noncitizens, the list at the centre of Part LVIII. Two days earlier, Judge David O. Carter had refused the same demand, warning that it threatened to “wreak havoc” on the state’s elections. On appeal, one judge appointed by Trump and one appointed by Biden wrote that granting it would mean “undermining the Constitution’s plain text,” and that a government wanting different rules for mail ballots should take the idea to Congress, not the courts. The courts held, as they have held all year. The subpoenas went out anyway. That is the friction strategy compressed into a single day: a loss on the merits, and a fresh demand on the time and nerve of the people counting the votes.

Somewhere in California this weekend, a clerk who read that sample guidance in September is handing a real subpoena to the county lawyer. The chapter was written for exactly this. It did not stay in the binder.

The cost of the drill

Here is the part that is easy to miss.

David Becker of the Center for Election Innovation and Research, who also featured in Part LVIII, told the Times that the most chilling scenarios are unlikely to happen, because the courts would almost certainly block them. He is probably right. The courts have blocked most of the administration’s attempts to insert itself into state election administration this year, often within days. But Becker added that election officials feel more comfortable knowing they have thought the scenarios through. That is a reasonable thing to feel. It is also a measure of what the scenarios have already taken from them.

Every training session about ICE is a session not spent on provisional ballots. Every hour a county lawyer spends rehearsing a search-warrant response is an hour not spent on the signature-matching appeals that decide close races. Every slide about the armed-agents statute is a slide that did not exist in 2022. The friction strategy, as this series has described it, needs no courtroom win. It works by making the defenders spend. And the evidence of these final weeks is that it can work even before the administration acts. The threat of a raid generates the drill. The drill costs time, money and attention, in the last weeks before a vote, when officials have least of all three.

There is a second cost, and the officials know it. Some told the Times they are reluctant to share the details of their plans, for fear of giving Washington a tactical advantage if the fight goes to court. Democrats among them face a sharper version of the dilemma. Talking openly about preparation shows constituents the threat is taken seriously. It may also convince some of those constituents that the polling place is no longer safe, and that staying home is the prudent choice. An official who reassures too loudly frightens; an official who stays quiet looks complacent. The administration benefits either way.

That is why Becker’s reassurance and Bellows’s alarm are not contradictory. They are two halves of the same job. The courts will probably hold. The voters need to believe they will. And the people who run elections are, days out, trying to prepare for the worst without talking anyone into expecting it.

A seven out of ten

The Times asked Keith Ellison, Minnesota’s attorney general, to rate his level of concern about threats to the November 3 vote on a scale of one to ten. He said seven, and added that it is as high as it has ever been for him. Ellison has been Minnesota’s attorney general since 2019 and spent twelve years in Congress before that. He has seen contested counts, a pandemic election and the long winter of 2020. Seven is his ceiling.

It is worth sitting with what a seven means. It is not panic. It is the number a careful person gives when he thinks the bad thing is less likely than not, but likely enough that a responsible official cannot treat it as a fantasy. That is the place American election administration has reached, days out: a bipartisan collection of clerks, secretaries and attorneys general, from Ingham County to Augusta to Salt Lake City, quietly agreeing that the chance their own government will interfere in the vote is high enough to plan for, and not high enough to say so too loudly.

There is something faintly Leacockian about the picture, as there was in Los Angeles with the thumb drive nobody would plug in. Somewhere in Michigan last week, Lisa Lyons, the clerk of Kent County, told an online forum that her office would follow the law, follow the Constitution and protect its election equipment. Those are the three things an election clerk has always done. That she felt the need to say them aloud, in that order, as a statement of resolve, is the story.

Part LVIII ended with the question of whether one judge could end a war that thirty courtrooms had only interrupted. This part has a simpler answer to a simpler question. Whatever Judge Boasberg decides, the war has already reached the binder. The clerks have written the chapter. In California, they have already had to open it. On November 3 we will find out how many others need it.
