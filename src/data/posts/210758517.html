NOW SHIPPING TO A STREETLIGHT NEAR YOU

FLOCK SAFETY HOME EDITION™ “You are not a license plate. You are a free citizen. We just need to log which free citizen, and when, and where, and how often, and with whom.”

THE PROBLEM

You’ve been driving around America this whole time as if you were a private individual with a reasonable expectation that your movements are your own business. That was, we regret to inform you, a design flaw in the previous version of your life. FLOCK SAFETY HOME EDITION™ corrects it.

THE SOLUTION

A small black camera, solar-powered, mounted on a pole near you, that photographs your car — make, model, color, dents, bumper stickers, the angle of your windshield, the coffee cup you forgot in the passenger seat — every single time you pass it. One camera does this. Now imagine over 5,000 police departments’ worth of them, all talking to each other, all day, forever.

FEATURES

  • Warrantless by design. No judge required. No probable cause required. Just a subscription, a login, and a search bar.

  • 526 logged locations in four months — this is not a hypothetical example. This is an actual customer, in Norfolk, Virginia, a retired veteran, who found out exactly how many times he’d been tracked only by suing to get the number. We include it here as a feature, since he did not have the option of declining it as one.

  • Now with facial and pedestrian tracking. The newest model, the Condor, doesn’t stop at your license plate. It tracks you. Not your car. You. The company describes this, without apparent irony, as an upgrade.

  • Distress detection. A companion product listens for screaming. We are not going to make a joke about this one. It’s in the actual spec sheet.

WHO’S USING IT

Local police departments, mostly, who describe it in terms like “you can’t have a cop on every corner” — which is true, and also not what anyone asked, since the alternative to a cop on every corner was previously understood to be “no cop on every corner,” not “a corporate camera on every corner instead, logging everyone equally, guilty or not.”

A WORD ABOUT PRIVACY

We take your privacy seriously, in the sense that we have thought about it, priced it, and decided to proceed anyway. Flock’s own CEO has said, in public, that “we can have safety and privacy” — a sentence with the grammatical structure of a promise and the actual content of an open question, which is presumably why it remains, per Mr. Langley, “an ongoing debate probably forever” rather than a settled one.

A WORD FOR THE FOLKS WHO USED TO MAKE FUN OF CHINA

For years, a great many Americans have enjoyed a small, comfortable feeling of superiority regarding the surveillance state, the sort that comes from watching footage of Beijing’s camera networks and thinking, with real conviction, thank God we don’t live like that. We are not here to relitigate that comparison. We are simply going to lay a few numbers next to each other and let you do it yourself: over 5,000 American police departments, one company, warrantless access, real-time vehicle tracking, and — as of the newest camera model — pedestrian tracking too, all growing quietly enough that most of the country only found out it existed because a retired veteran in Norfolk got curious and sued. Nobody voted for this network directly. Most city councils that approved it were pitched a stolen-car finder, not a nationwide movement ledger. If you’ve ever driven past one of these cameras, glanced up, and had a brief, uncomfortable flicker of huh, this feels a bit like the Middle Kingdom — that flicker was accurate. The only meaningful difference is who’s billing you for the privilege.

THE OPT-OUT

There isn’t one, technically. There is, however, an app called DeFlock, built by citizens rather than corporations, that maps where these cameras are so you can at least know you’re being logged, if not prevent it.

COMPLIANCE NOTE

Some jurisdictions have begun removing their cameras, citing cost. Greenfield, Massachusetts pulled its four. This should not be read as a trend so much as an early data point in what a village — camera by camera, subscription by subscription — eventually decides it is and isn’t willing to become.

EXPANDING USE CASES

Originally marketed as a tool for finding stolen cars, FLOCK SAFETY HOME EDITION™ has, like most successful products, grown beyond its original mission. Journalists and privacy researchers have reported the network’s data being queried in connection with immigration enforcement — a use case not advertised at launch, not mentioned in the original pitch to city councils, and not something most of the towns who bought in were specifically asked about before their approval. We include this not as a criticism, but as an example of what the industry calls “platform growth” and what everyone else calls “the thing they didn’t tell you it would eventually do.”

A WORD ON OVERSIGHT

Access to the data logged by these cameras generally requires no warrant, no judge, and in many jurisdictions, no meaningful audit of who searched what, when, or why. Police in multiple states have already been caught running searches unrelated to any investigation — tracking an ex-partner, a colleague, a personal acquaintance — because the technical capacity to do so exists and the guardrails preventing it, where they exist at all, rely largely on the good judgment of whoever is logged in. We are told this is being addressed. We are also told, by the same people, that it is “an ongoing debate probably forever,” which are not, strictly speaking, compatible statements.

FOR CITIZENS WHO WOULD LIKE ONE FEWER CAMERA

Removing a camera you do not own, that is mounted on public infrastructure, monitoring a public street, is generally understood to fall outside the category of things private citizens are permitted to do to corporate surveillance equipment, however strongly they may feel about it. This office is not encouraging that. This office is simply reporting that several hundred cameras have nonetheless been disabled nationwide by persons unknown, and that the volume of persons remaining unknown appears, so far, to be a feature of the situation rather than a bug the company has managed to solve.
