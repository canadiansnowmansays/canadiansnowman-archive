🖋 Wry — a lighter one, a breather, though it happens to be the hinge on which the whole series’ lighting changes. We have reached the year humanity stopped making its own light and started buying it, by a number printed on a bill, from a man downtown with a machine you were not invited to inspect.

I count my essays, and I spent eighteen years of my working life in an industry whose entire business model was charging people for quantities they could not see, hold, or independently verify — so you will forgive me if I approach essay 1,882 with the tender recognition of a man meeting his own profession’s grandfather. Because 1882 is the year the metered utility was born, and with it the strangest transaction in the history of commerce: the sale of a thing with no thing in it.

Pearl Street, the fourth of September

On September 4, 1882, Thomas Edison threw a switch at a generating station on Pearl Street in Lower Manhattan, and the lights came on in a district of offices around Wall Street. The first day’s customers numbered fifty-nine. By the standards of this series — which has watched fleets of a hundred and thirty ships and armies of hundreds of thousands — fifty-nine is a rounding error. It is also one of the most consequential fifty-nine of the entire nineteenth century, because of what those fifty-nine had agreed to.

They had agreed to be billed for electricity.

Stop and feel how odd that is. Every commodity traded in the whole prior history of the species had been a thing. You bought a pound of coal and you could heft it, burn it, watch it blacken your hands. You bought a gallon of whale oil and you could spill it. You bought a candle — and here is the point — and you could count the candles, and light them yourself, in your own room, with a flame you owned outright from the moment of purchase to the moment it guttered. Light, for the entire run of this series up to now, from the monks’ scriptoria of 1066 to the counting-houses of 1707, had been something a human being made, personally, from a thing they held.

After Pearl Street, light became something you subscribed to. It arrived through a wire from a machine you would never see, in a quantity measured by an instrument you did not own and could not read, and at the end of the month a bill arrived stating a number, and you paid it. You had no way on earth to confirm the number was right. You took it on faith. Fifty-nine customers took it on faith on the fourth of September, and roughly eight billion of us have taken it on faith every month since.

The birth of the unseeable unit

This is the number-idea, and it is one of the deepest in the series, so let me state it plainly. Electricity was the first product ever sold by an abstract quantity that the buyer could not perceive. Not the thing, but a reading of the thing. The kilowatt-hour — an amount of a phenomenon no human sense can detect, accumulating invisibly in a box on the wall, totted up by the seller, and presented as a figure to be settled in cash.

Edison understood from the start that this was the whole game. The bulb was the romance; the meter was the business. He knew that to sell light by the unit he first had to invent a way to count the unit, and his earliest electricity meters were crude almost to comedy — electrolytic cells in which current deposited metal on a zinc plate, and once a month a man came and weighed the plate and worked backward to your bill. Your electricity consumption was established by weighing a piece of metal in a jar. You were invited to trust the jar. The entire modern utility economy — every power bill, water bill, gas bill, every gigabyte of data a certain narrator once helped charge people for — rests on the moment a customer first accepted that a number arrived at by someone else, measuring something nobody can see, was a debt they genuinely owed.

The whole of this series has been about who keeps the ledger. Pearl Street is the episode where the ledger became the product. The reading isn’t the record of the sale. The reading is the sale.

The man who booked the wrong certainty

Now the honest ledger, because Edison does not get to be only the hero of this one.

Edison was the greatest self-counter of his age — a man who understood, as few inventors have, that the credit goes to whoever gets his number into the newspaper first. He ran a laboratory of brilliant people and signed the results; he was generous with his own fame and stingy with everyone else’s. Essay 1876 gave you Bell, who booked a certainty in advance — patented the telephone before it worked — and had the extraordinary luck to be right inside the week. Edison is Bell’s dark twin. He also booked a certainty in advance. He bet the entire enterprise, his reputation and his capital, on direct current — DC, the kind Pearl Street generated, which could not travel more than about a mile from the station before it faded to nothing.

And he was wrong. The future belonged to alternating current, which could be stepped up to high voltage, sent for a hundred miles, and stepped down again — the system that would, thirteen essays from now at 1895, light a city from a waterfall. When Edison grasped that AC threatened his DC empire, he did not concede the figures. He ran a propaganda campaign of genuine ugliness, publicly electrocuting animals to paint his rival’s current as a killer, and lent his laboratory’s expertise to the development of the electric chair specifically so that execution by AC would tar the technology he opposed. The father of metered light tried to win a dispute about which kind of number to count in by making the other number frightening.

He lost anyway. The man who taught the world to trust the meter could not, himself, read the one reading that mattered — the direction the current was flowing. There is a lesson in that which I will not belabour, except to note that it is the oldest one in this series: the person who controls the count is never as in control as he believes, and the fleet is never the fleet it books itself to be.

The wire that bills you

Permit the professional his one indulgence. I spent the better part of two decades inside an industry descended directly from Pearl Street — a business whose product is likewise invisible, likewise metered, likewise billed in units the customer must simply believe in. I will name no regulator and relitigate no hearing. I will only say that I have sat in rooms where very serious people argued for very long hours about the correct price of a quantity that no participant in the argument could see, touch, or hold, and that every one of those rooms was a descendant of a man weighing a zinc plate in a jar in Lower Manhattan in 1882. We never got away from the jar. We only made it smaller and called it a modem.

Dior is not on the grid

The dog, I am pleased to report, remains the last fully off-grid member of this household — the one consumer who has never once accepted a metered bill. Her warmth is generated locally and consumed on-site; her light is whatever falls on her through the window, taken on no one’s terms but her own and paid for with nothing. When the power failed last winter and the furnace died and the house went dark and cold, I lit — I will tell you, because it is the whole essay — a candle, the first open flame in that room in years, and read by it, and the dog came and lay in its small circle of heat as if nothing in the world had changed since 1066. For one evening the oldest light in this series was back on the job, making no reading, sending no bill, owed to no one. Then the grid came back, and the meter resumed its silent counting, and we rejoined the fifty-nine and their eight billion heirs. But I kept the candle on the desk. A man should know where his light used to come from.

The closer

So 1882 is the hinge. Before it, every light in this series is a flame — tallow, wax, wick, oil, the little mortal fire a person makes and owns. After it, light is a current, bought by the unseeable unit from a central authority, and the candle that lit a thousand years of this account is retired to the drawer. Humanity gained the most reliable light it had ever known and gave up, in the same transaction, the last thing it made entirely for itself. I think that is a fair trade. I am not certain it is a free one. Nothing in this series ever is.

The count moves on, and it comes home to Canada for one of the proudest and most shameful entries on the whole map — a single year that drove the last spike of a railway binding a country coast to coast, and in the same autumn dropped a trapdoor beneath a man who spoke for the people that railway was built across. The next landmark is essay 1885, and it is two ledgers kept in one year: a nation finally added up, and a people struck out of the sum to make the addition balance. The metered wire of 1882 wired the cities. The steel of 1885 wired the country. Both bills, it turns out, had names on them that the headline never printed.

See you at 1,885, where the country is finished and a man is hanged to finish it.
