OFFICIAL TRAINING FILM No. 8-647 WAR DEPARTMENT — RESTRICTED Produced by the United States Department of War Pete Hegseth, Secretary of War

TESTOSTERONE: GOD’S FAVORITE MAN JUICE

REEL ONE: THE MODERN WARFIGHTER

NARRATOR (V.O.): Soldier. Sailor. Airman. Marine. You are the tip of the spear. But even the sharpest spear grows dull without proper maintenance. This week, the Department of War, under the leadership of Secretary Pete Hegseth, announced a bold new initiative: annual testosterone screening for all service members age 30 and older, with weekly injections offered to any warfighter found lacking.

NARRATOR (V.O.): The Secretary calls this the individual warfighter’s “most decisive advantage.” Not radar. Not logistics. Not air superiority. The advantage, this week, is glandular.

REEL TWO: THE SCIENCE, AS UNDERSTOOD BY THE SECRETARY

NARRATOR (V.O.): It is well established science, the Secretary notes, that as men age, testosterone levels naturally decline. This is true, and uncontroversial, and has been known to endocrinologists for the better part of a century, largely without requiring a Cabinet-level department to act on it.

NARRATOR (V.O.): The Secretary describes the goal as restoring and optimizing the warfighter’s “natural capabilities,” which is a phrase that could describe testosterone therapy, protein powder, a good night’s sleep, or, in this Department’s telling, apparently all three at once, at a cost, and mandatory for men over 30 whether or not any capability was found lacking to begin with.

NARRATOR (V.O.): Outside experts — urologists, endocrinologists, several people who attended medical school for reasons other than making training films — noted, this same week, that healthy men without symptoms are not typically tested for low testosterone in ordinary clinical practice, let alone offered weekly injections as a matter of departmental policy. One physician, writing in the Washington Post, described the whole framework charitably as “testosterone-seeking behavior... triggered by what they’re seeing online.” The Department was not deterred by this. The Department is rarely deterred by outside experts.

REEL THREE: A NOTE ON WHO THIS DOES NOT APPLY TO

NARRATOR (V.O.): The screening, mandatory for service members 30 and over, applies, by its own terms, to a portion of the force that is not entirely composed of one biological sex. This detail did not appear to receive extensive discussion in the initial announcement. Questions regarding female service members’ role in a testosterone-optimization initiative remain, as of this filming, unanswered — filed, perhaps, alongside the Department’s other unaddressed paperwork.

NARRATOR (V.O.): The Secretary was careful to note that treatment remains voluntary — a soldier may decline his weekly injection freely, provided he is comfortable being the one man in the unit who declined the Secretary’s signature readiness initiative.

REEL FOUR: DISSENTING VOICES FROM THE HOME FRONT

NARRATOR (V.O.): Not every American received the news with the reverence the Department intended. Representative Becca Balint, addressing the policy directly, described the screening requirement as indicative of what she termed “homoeroticism” — a characterization the Department has not, as of this filming, incorporated into future training materials, though the Secretary himself has spent a portion of his tenure litigating exactly this category of accusation against others.

NARRATOR (V.O.): One columnist, writing for Slate, confessed to assuming the entire policy was satire — an item from a fictional newspaper, or a scene misplaced from a Kubrick film about a general with strong feelings regarding bodily fluids and national purity. The Department has issued no correction.

REEL FIVE: THE WARFIGHTER OF TOMORROW

NARRATOR (V.O.): What does this mean for you, the American service member? It means an annual blood draw. It means a needle, weekly, should your levels fall short of the Secretary’s standard. It means a Department of War that has looked out across a volatile world — a live conflict in the Persian Gulf, a Strait held hostage by a policy that changed twice in two days, alliances tested and re-tested — and identified, as its most urgent readiness gap, a hormone.

NARRATOR (V.O.): As with any Departmental initiative, a full accounting of possible side effects is provided, in the interest of transparency and unit cohesion: reduced natural production. Enlarged breast tissue. Acne, of the sort you have not experienced since basic. Mood swings, unrelated to combat. And, in some documented cases, infertility — none of which, the Secretary notes, should discourage a warfighter from optimizing his natural capabilities on a weekly basis.

REEL SIX: A WORD BEFORE YOU ENLIST IN THE PROGRAM

NARRATOR (V.O.): The Department of War is not, itself, a licensed medical provider, however enthusiastically it may present as one. Any service member with genuine concerns about hormone levels, fatigue, or general readiness is encouraged — by this film, if not always by the Department — to consult his own physician, request an annual physical, and treat the results of a blood test as a matter between himself and a doctor, rather than a matter of departmental morale. This has always been sound medical guidance. It remains so, regardless of who is standing at the podium announcing otherwise.
