The Security Council’s veto, the subject of the essay that follows this one, gets described as a design flaw so often that it is worth pausing, before we get there, on the system it was built to fix. That system was the League of Nations, and its founding failure was not that its members disagreed too often. It was that the man who designed it could not persuade his own country to join it.

A general association of nations

Woodrow Wilson laid out his vision for the postwar world in an address to a joint session of Congress on 8 January 1918, ten months before the fighting stopped. The speech became known for its numbered list — the Fourteen Points — and most of those points dealt with specific, practical grievances of the war: Belgian sovereignty restored, Alsace-Lorraine returned to France, an independent Poland with access to the sea. But the fourteenth point was different in kind from the other thirteen. It called for “a general association of nations” to be formed “under specific covenants for the purpose of affording mutual guarantees of political independence and territorial integrity to great and small states alike.” It was one sentence, tacked on at the end of a list otherwise concerned with borders and reparations, and it turned out to be the sentence that mattered most to Wilson personally, and the one that would consume the rest of his presidency.

Wilson sailed for France on 4 December 1918 and arrived in Paris on 13 December, the first sitting American president to travel to Europe while in office. He was greeted, by most contemporary accounts, with a warmth bordering on adulation — cheering crowds lined the streets of Paris, Rome, and London, treating the American president less as a foreign head of state than as the man who might have just saved the continent from repeating itself. It is worth sitting with that image for a moment, because it makes what followed so much stranger: within eighteen months, the man crowds were showering with flowers in the streets of Europe would be a paralysed invalid, hidden from public view by his own wife and doctor, watching from a sickbed as the United States Senate killed the one thing he had gone to Paris to build.

At the Paris Peace Conference, Wilson insisted on something that seems, in retrospect, like a tactical error so large it is almost hard to credit: rather than negotiate the League of Nations Covenant as a separate instrument, to be taken up once the harder business of the peace settlement was finished, he wove it into the body of the Treaty of Versailles itself, presenting the completed draft Covenant to the full conference on 14 February 1919. His reasoning was defensible on its own terms — a League left to be negotiated later, once the war-weary urgency of 1919 had passed, might never be negotiated at all. But the decision meant that anyone in the American Senate who objected to the League now had to vote against the entire peace treaty to register that objection, and it meant that Wilson’s most consequential diplomatic legacy would rise or fall as a rider on a document three hundred pages long, covering everything from German coal shipments to the borders of Poland.

The Treaty, Covenant included, was signed in the Hall of Mirrors at the Palace of Versailles on 28 June 1919 — five years to the day after the assassination in Sarajevo that had started the war the League was meant to prevent from ever happening again. France’s negotiators at Paris, led by Georges Clemenceau, had wanted something considerably harder-edged than what Wilson was offering: a standing Allied military force, or at minimum an ironclad guarantee of American and British military support should Germany ever again threaten French territory. Clemenceau got a separate bilateral guarantee treaty from both Britain and the United States as the price of accepting Wilson’s softer, more aspirational League — and that guarantee treaty, like the League itself, would die in the same Senate that killed the Covenant, leaving France to face the 1930s with neither the hard security Clemenceau had wanted nor much faith in the soft security Wilson had built instead. The point is worth pausing on, because it recurs throughout this series in different costumes: an institution’s design compromises are frequently made on the assumption that some other party will backstop what the institution itself cannot guarantee, and the backstop has a way of not showing up when it is needed.

A structural weakness nobody advertised

The Covenant that emerged from Paris built a League with two main organs: an Assembly, in which every member state had a voice, and a Council, initially composed of the war’s principal Allied powers as permanent members alongside a rotating set of non-permanent members — the direct ancestor, twenty-six years early, of the UN Security Council this series exists to examine. But the League’s Council operated under a rule that the later UN Charter would deliberately abandon: with a small number of procedural exceptions, decisions of the Council required the unanimous agreement of every member state represented at the meeting, not merely of the great powers among them. This is worth dwelling on, because it is the single structural fact that connects everything else in this series. The UN’s founders in 1945, sitting down to design the Security Council, looked back at the League’s unanimity rule and concluded, correctly, that requiring everyone to agree had made it functionally impossible for the League to act against anyone who mattered. Their fix was to formalise the inequality already present in practice — five great powers would get an explicit veto, and the other members would not — on the theory that a system honest about which states actually held power might function better than one that pretended, on paper, that Luxembourg and France carried equal weight in a crisis. Whether that fix solved the problem or merely relocated it is the argument this entire series is built to trace. But it began here, in Geneva, with a Council that could be blocked by nearly anyone and enforced by almost no one.

Fourteen reservations for fourteen points

Wilson returned home to a Senate that the 1918 midterm elections had handed to the Republican Party, and to the chairmanship of the Senate Foreign Relations Committee, a man who had loathed Wilson personally for the better part of two decades: Henry Cabot Lodge of Massachusetts. Lodge was not, whatever Wilson’s supporters said of him at the time and historians have sometimes repeated since, a simple isolationist. He had supported American entry into the war, believed in an American role in world affairs, and was not opposed in principle to some form of League. What Lodge opposed, specifically and with a lawyer’s precision, was Article X of the Covenant — the clause committing member states “to respect and preserve as against external aggression the territorial integrity and existing political independence of all Members of the League.” Lodge and his allies read Article X as a standing promise to go to war on the League’s say-so, without Congress ever getting to exercise the war power the Constitution reserved to it. It was, in their reading, an automatic commitment substituting for the judgment Congress was supposed to make case by case.

In committee, Lodge attached fourteen reservations to the Treaty — the number an irony almost too pointed to require comment, arriving as a direct rebuttal to Wilson’s own Fourteen Points eighteen months earlier — most of them aimed at preserving Congress’s power to decide, independently, whether the United States would actually go to war under Article X or accept any League mandate. One reservation went further still, requiring that any American withdrawal from the League be achievable by a simple concurrent resolution of Congress, without needing the president’s signature at all — a direct shot at the presidency’s ordinary control over foreign affairs, and a sign of just how far the fight had moved from the merits of the League itself into a raw institutional turf war between two branches of government that neither man was prepared to lose. Ranged against Lodge’s reservationists, who wanted the League with conditions attached, sat a much smaller bloc of senators nicknamed the “irreconcilables” — led by William Borah of Idaho and Hiram Johnson of California — who wanted no League at all, reservations or otherwise, on the grounds that any binding international commitment compromised American sovereignty; it is worth noting that neither Borah nor Johnson was a simple isolationist crank in the caricatured sense either, both being prominent progressive internationalists on other questions who genuinely believed the Covenant, as written, violated principles of international law and self-determination they had spent careers defending. And ranged against both of them sat Wilson himself, who regarded any reservation, however modest, as a betrayal of the Covenant’s spirit, and who instructed loyal Senate Democrats to vote down the Lodge version even though doing so meant killing the only version of the Treaty with any realistic chance of the two-thirds support ratification required.

The tour and the stroke

In September 1919, with the Senate deadlocked and his own health already fraying under the strain of the Paris negotiations, Wilson set out to do what he had always been better at than backroom vote-counting: take his case directly to the American public. Over three weeks he travelled more than eight thousand miles by rail and delivered some forty speeches, arguing for the League in city after city across the Midwest and West, telling audiences that a Senate which added reservations to the Covenant would be choosing, in effect, to let the next war happen. On 25 September, after a speech in Pueblo, Colorado, Wilson was struck by severe headaches and nausea severe enough that his physician cancelled the remainder of the tour and rushed him back to Washington. A week later, on 2 October, he suffered a major stroke that left him partially paralysed. For months afterward, the severity of his condition was concealed from Congress, the cabinet, and the public alike, managed by his wife Edith and his physician, Dr Cary Grayson, in an arrangement that several historians have since described as the closest thing to a constitutional crisis the office of the presidency produced in the entire twentieth century short of outright removal. Wilson, immobilised and largely inaccessible even to his own staff, remained implacably opposed to any compromise with Lodge, communicating his refusal through his wife to a Democratic caucus that might otherwise have found the votes for a reservationist version of the Treaty.

Two votes, two defeats

The Treaty came to its first Senate vote on 19 November 1919. Voting first on the version carrying Lodge’s fourteen reservations, the Senate rejected it 39 to 55 — Wilson’s loyal Democrats and the irreconcilables voting against it for opposite reasons, one bloc because the reservations gutted the Covenant, the other because no Covenant was acceptable regardless of its wording. A second vote, on the Treaty without any reservations at all, fared barely better, failing 38 to 53. Four months of further negotiation, public pressure, and private manoeuvring produced no meaningful movement, and on 19 March 1920 the Senate tried once more, voting on Lodge’s reservationist version a final time. It fell 49 in favour to 35 opposed — seven votes short of the two-thirds majority the Constitution required, doomed in the end by the same arithmetic as before: half of Wilson’s own party, following his instructions from the sickroom, joined the irreconcilables to keep the total below the threshold that would have bound the United States to the organisation its own president had designed.

A League missing its architect

The League of Nations came into legal existence anyway. The Covenant entered into force on 10 January 1920, once the required number of ratifications had been deposited, and the League’s first Assembly convened in Geneva on 15 November 1920 — a body of more than forty member states meeting to organise the peace of the postwar world, absent the one government whose head of state had insisted, over the objections of his own diplomatic advisers, on making it the centrepiece of the settlement. The formal state of war between the United States and Germany did not end through the League’s mechanisms at all; it ended through the separate Knox–Porter Resolution, passed by Congress in 1921, a peace concluded entirely outside the institution Wilson had spent the last productive years of his presidency building. Wilson himself, broken in health and increasingly marginal to a Democratic Party that had already begun looking past him, would never see the United States join the organisation that bore his intellectual fingerprints on every clause. He died in February 1924, four years after the second Senate rejection, having watched the League take its first, halting steps into a world it was never structurally equipped to keep at peace — because the peacekeeping mechanism, at bottom, still needed everyone in the room to agree, and the one power capable of making that agreement mean something had excused itself from the room entirely.

That is the shape of the orphan this series is named for: an institution built on a genuinely radical idea — that great powers might bind themselves, in advance, to a shared process rather than to the balance-of-power diplomacy that had produced 1914 — designed by a president who could not translate his own conviction into thirteen more votes in a chamber three miles from the White House. The next essay in this series follows what that orphaned League actually did with the authority it had been given, and what happened, in Manchuria in 1931 and Abyssinia in 1935, when a Council built on unanimous consent met a great power that had simply decided not to consent.
