I don’t suppose one person in a hundred, outside of Perth County itself, has ever attended the Elsinore Easter Egg Hunt. Which, if you mention it to anyone in Elsinore, is less a problem to be solved than a condition of the event’s particular dignity, which depends, in the way that certain dignities depend, on not being oversold.

It is held every year in Confederation Park on the Saturday before Easter, beginning at ten o’clock sharp, or thereabouts, depending on whether Gerald Fitch — who serves as volunteer timekeeper, official egg counter, and the man responsible for laying the trail of eggs from the municipal works yard to the bandshell — has managed to finish before the children arrive. Gerald is conscientious. He simply moves at his own pace. The children have learned, over several years of collective experience, to wait at the far end of the park near the Avon with their backs turned, because honour requires it and because Mrs. Kowalski from the organizing committee stands at that end of the park and tells them it does and they believe her, as children believe people who speak with sufficient conviction about what honour requires.

Gerald had been up since six-thirty. He had his basket, his list, his high-visibility vest, and his cardigan — the lighter grey one, it being April — with both pockets full, the left containing the egg placement record on a folded sheet of paper and the right containing a small flashlight he had brought in case the bandshell’s eastern shadow was deeper than anticipated. He had passed McKelvie’s on his way to the park. The bakery had been open since five-thirty and the cinnamon smell reached him from half a block away, which it does on still April mornings when the air is cool enough to hold a smell and Ontario Street is quiet enough to let it travel. He noted this, as he notes most things, without particular comment.

Confederation Park, Easter Saturday. Gerald has placed the butter tart coupon where a child of average speed and moderate observational skill will find it. The swans are proceeding. Ten o’clock approaches.

He laid sixty-two eggs. Each one contained a small chocolate and, since 2019, a coupon from a participating local business. Fourteen businesses had participated this year. The coupons offered things like ten percent off a Tuesday haircut at Avon Clips, and a complimentary butter tart with any purchase of eight dollars or more at the Perth County Pantry. Gerald considered the butter tart coupon the strongest in the collection and had quietly placed it where a child of average speed and moderate observational skill would find it, because fairness, in Gerald’s view, was not random.

He was done by nine forty-seven. He radioed Mrs. Kowalski. He went to the Tim Hortons and sat at the window table on the right with a large coffee and two sugars and read the news on his phone until it was time to go back. Through the window he could read the Festival marquee six blocks south. THIS ROUGH MAGIC — 2026 SEASON. He had read it every Tuesday and Thursday morning since April. He read it again. He finished his coffee. He went back to the park.

Jim had been through Confederation Park at seven-fifteen with the labrador, the vizsla, and the corgi from Waterloo Street, before the eggs were laid and before the high-visibility vests appeared and before the morning acquired its official quality. The park at seven-fifteen on an April Saturday is the park at its most straightforwardly itself: the grass still wet, the light low and specific, the river visible between the willows, and nobody in it except Jim and three dogs and occasionally the Swan Warden, who passes through on her way from the south bank to the Elizabethan Gardens without appearing to notice the dogs, though she notices everything.

Jim had observed the municipal works van in the parking lot, the stacked crates visible in the back, and had noted that it was the Saturday before Easter and had drawn the obvious conclusion. He walked the dogs their usual route. He left the park before Gerald arrived.

He mentioned the egg hunt to Maggie at Sunday dinner.

Maggie said she’d had two Easter-adjacent appointments that morning — a rabbit, which was seasonal, and a very small tortoise belonging to a family on Festival Park Heights whose son had received it as a Christmas present and who had named it Hamlet, which Maggie considered an appropriate name for a tortoise in Elsinore and which she had not said aloud because the family was earnest and the tortoise was fine.

Jim passed the potatoes. He said the park had looked good. He said this without elaboration, because the park had looked good and that was sufficient.

Carly Meadows covered the Easter Egg Hunt for the Elsinore Beacon because Norman had told her in February that she would be covering the Easter Egg Hunt for the Beacon, in the tone he used for assignments he considered straightforward and which she had learned, over eight months, sometimes concealed things that were not straightforward at all, and sometimes were exactly as straightforward as they appeared, and that the skill was in knowing which.

She arrived at nine fifty-five with her notebook and her phone and the specific alertness of a journalist at a public event who has not yet established which detail is the detail. She introduced herself to Mrs. Kowalski. She found Gerald at the bandshell, vest on, egg placement record folded in his left pocket, and asked him how the hunt worked. Gerald explained it to her thoroughly and without abbreviation. It took six minutes. She wrote it all down, including the detail about the butter tart coupon and where he had placed it, which she later cut from the story but which she kept in her notes because you keep the details that are specific and true even when there is no immediate place for them.

She photographed the children waiting at the far end of the park. She photographed the bandshell. She photographed Gerald in his high-visibility vest consulting his placement record with the focused attention of a man confirming that the operation has proceeded according to plan, which it had. She sent this photograph to the Beacon’s Instagram account with the caption: “All sixty-two eggs accounted for. Results pending.” Norman texted her: Good. This was, in Norman’s vocabulary, enthusiasm.

Mayor Doug arrived at ten o’clock in his good coat.

He wore the good coat to all civic events, including the Easter Egg Hunt, including when the Easter Egg Hunt was held in weather that did not require a coat, because his wife had told him in 2009 that it was the one that made him look like he meant it, and he had always meant the Easter Egg Hunt. He stood at the bandshell and said a few words. He said Easter was a time for community, and that Elsinore was a community that understood this, and that he was very proud of the volunteers who had made this morning possible, and that the butter tart coupon from the Perth County Pantry was, personally speaking, not something he had ever been disappointed by. He said this last part with the air of a man offering testimony rather than an endorsement.

The children applauded. Several of them were not listening, being at the age where an adult at a microphone is an obstacle between them and the eggs rather than a source of information worth attending to.

Reg Plimpton stood slightly to the Mayor’s left during the remarks, close enough to be in the photograph that Carly took and far enough to have options. He had attended the Easter Egg Hunt for nine consecutive years. He had positioned himself near whoever was speaking for nine consecutive years. He shook hands warmly with seven people in the first twelve minutes, his right arm extending and landing with the practiced ease of a man who considers physical contact a form of civic infrastructure. He told Gerald that the egg trail was excellently done. Gerald thanked him. Gerald did not ask how Reg knew the egg trail was excellently done, having not seen it, because Gerald does not ask questions he already knows the answers to.

The hunt itself lasted eleven minutes, which is approximately how long these things last when you have sixty-two eggs and a park of Confederation Park’s dimensions and children operating at the velocity that children operate at when the prize is chocolate. The finding was done efficiently and without controversy, except for a brief moment near the willows when two children converged on the same egg simultaneously and Mrs. Kowalski intervened with the authority of a woman who has been intervening in these moments for eleven consecutive years and has a protocol.

The butter tart coupon egg was found by a girl of about seven in a yellow rain jacket who held it up with the satisfaction of someone who had been moving in approximately the right direction all along and had simply needed to keep going. Gerald observed this from near the bandshell. He made a small tick on his placement record.

The Swan Warden was not present at the hunt itself, having been on the south bank since five forty-five with her notebook. She passed through the park at eleven-fifteen, after the children had gone, walking from the river toward the Elizabethan Gardens with her dark green notebook under her arm. The park was in the aftermath state of a successful public event: a few forgotten chocolate wrappers, the echo of the morning’s noise in the empty bandshell, Gerald collecting the event signs with the methodical patience of a man who sets things up and takes them down and considers both equally part of the work.

She stopped. She looked at the two municipal benches near the river, where the Easter morning light was doing something specific to the water.

She wrote something in her notebook. She walked on.

Whether she was noting the light or the bench positions or something about the south bank that the morning had revealed, nobody asked. Gerald was the only other person present. Gerald does not ask the Swan Warden what she is writing. He has his own notebooks.

That afternoon I happened to read, as I read most things, about a rather different kind of Easter celebration being held to the south.

To the considerable south, as it turned out.

The event in question was held at a private golf club in Florida. The eggs were gold-coloured. Or bronze. The precise metallurgical distribution was not entirely clear from the coverage, though the coverage itself was considerable — more coverage, I am fairly certain, than the Elsinore Confederation Park Hunt has received in its thirty-two-year history, including the year Gerald Fitch inadvertently hid eleven eggs in the same tree and had to conduct a quiet recovery operation while the children were having their post-hunt hot chocolate.

The prize structure was tiered. There were Gold Eggs and Bronze Eggs, and a significance attached to which one received that the reporting made clear without fully explaining, in the way that the significance of certain things is understood to be self-evident by those for whom it is self-evident. The host circulated among the guests with the bearing of a man who has personally invented Easter and is generously sharing it with the assembled company. A child on the far fairway waved a Bronze Egg in the Florida sun. It caught the light. It gleamed.

It was, everyone agreed, the greatest egg hunt in history. Possibly in the history of eggs.

The host said a few words. He said Easter was a time for winners. He said this country was going to be incredible. He said the eggs were beautiful. He did not mention the butter tart coupon, though I have no way of knowing whether one was available.

Jim read about it on his phone that evening, between the afternoon walk and his first poker session of the week. He considered it for a moment in the way he considers most things: noting what was present, noting what was absent, forming a view.

He said nothing about it to anyone. He was not asked.

Gerald read about it on his phone that same evening, in the fixed sequence in which he reads everything, which meant he encountered it between the municipal water quality report and the Festival’s ticket sales update for the first week of the season. He noted it. He filed it, mentally, in the category of information he monitors rather than the category of information he acts upon, because the category distinction matters and Gerald maintains it carefully.

Carly read about it and thought about it for a long time and wrote three paragraphs in her notebook and then crossed them out because she couldn’t find the right register — not angry enough to be a column, not detached enough to be news, something in between that she didn’t have a name for yet. Norman, if asked, could have given her the name. She hadn’t asked yet. She was getting there.

Reverend Faye read about it on Saturday night and made a note for the sermon she had already been preparing for two weeks, which was about the difference between celebration and spectacle, and which she had not yet found the right way into. She found the right way in on Sunday morning. The congregation of St. Andrew’s-on-the-Avon felt, leaving the service, specifically understood. They could not have said exactly what she said.

The Swan Warden was shown a photograph of the Florida event by Gerald on his phone the following Monday. They were in the municipal parking lot behind the Tim Hortons, both arriving for different meetings at the same moment.

She looked at the photograph. She looked at it for a moment longer than she usually looks at things.

“Gold eggs,” she said.

She handed the phone back. She went into the building. She raised the drainage issue she had come to raise with her customary precision. She did not refer to the egg hunt again.

Whether the swans have opinions on the matter is not known. They were on the river, as usual, proceeding with their business. They have never, in Darlene Potts’ twenty-two years of observation, shown the slightest interest in gold.

They have, however, clear views about which bank they use in April, and those views have held for twenty-two years without revision, and without anyone suggesting they should be tiered.

A Note for the Record

Canada holds its Easter egg hunts in municipal parks. The eggs are plastic. Each contains a small chocolate and, since 2019, a coupon of varying but genuine usefulness. The children are told there is no hierarchy except speed, and mostly they believe this, and the girl in the yellow rain jacket found the butter tart coupon because she had been moving in approximately the right direction all along.

Gerald lays the trail. The Mayor wears his good coat. Mrs. Kowalski manages the protocol. Reg Plimpton attends and extends his arm. Carly covers it for the Beacon. The Swan Warden passes through on her way back from the river and writes something in her notebook that nobody asks about.

To the south, the eggs were gold and the prize structure was tiered and the host had personally invented Easter.

The swans were on the river.

The Avon ran through all of it, at the depth it has always run, indifferent to the occasion and entirely unimpressed by the metallurgy.

Happy Easter. Mind where you step near the south bank.
