On the evening of January 5, 2021, Burt Jones went to dinner at the vice president’s residence. He brought a letter asking Mike Pence to delay the Electoral College certification for twelve days.

He did not deliver it. He read Pence’s “body language,” he later said, and “saw the writing on the wall.”

The next afternoon, as a mob stormed the Capitol, Jones — back in Georgia — sent a text message: “Well everything has officially gone off the rail.”

Twenty months from now, Burt Jones may be the governor of Georgia.

The Record

The New York Times published a detailed investigation of Jones’s role in the effort to overturn the 2020 election on May 26, drawing on thousands of files from state and federal investigations, including leaked discovery material from the failed Georgia criminal case against Trump and his allies.

What the record shows is not a man swept up in a moment. It is a man who worked systematically, over weeks, to reverse an election result.

Jones was an early Trump supporter — he backed him in 2015, when most Republican officials were keeping their distance. After the 2020 election, he moved quickly. In texts the day after the vote, he wrote to David Shafer, chairman of the state Republican Party, about Secretary of State Brad Raffensperger, who was overseeing the count: “What in the hell is SOS doing????”

As Trump escalated his claims of a stolen election, Jones escalated with him. When Lieutenant Governor Geoff Duncan asserted — accurately — that there was no credible evidence of widespread fraud, Jones texted a Trump campaign official: “Unleash the POTUS on him. His ass needs to be brought down a notch or 4 spots.”

Geoff Duncan, former lieutenant governor of Georgia, was attacked by Mr. Trump.Credit...Kenny Holston/The New York Times

Trump obliged. He attacked Duncan publicly, calling him a “RINO Never Trumper.” Duncan is now a Democrat. He ran for governor this year and lost in the primary.

Jones tried to organise a special session of the state legislature to override the election results — part of a Trump campaign contingency plan to have swing-state legislatures overturn their states’ votes. In a call on December 7, 2020, Trump told the Georgia House Speaker that “Burt Jones is very much on our side.” A Trump campaign lawyer emailed the campaign’s leadership that Jones “wanted the campaign to know that there is a group of legislators” who “will do whatever it can to make this productive for the President.”

Jones helped arrange State Senate hearings where Rudolph Giuliani aired false claims about the election. Giuliani later acknowledged, in a successful defamation lawsuit brought by two Atlanta election workers, that claims he had made about them were false — but said in court documents that he had relied on information Jones had provided.

Jones filed an amicus brief supporting Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton’s attempt to have the Supreme Court prevent Georgia, Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin from casting their legitimate Electoral College votes. The brief claimed that at least 66,247 underage people in Georgia had illegally voted. The claim dissolved under scrutiny.

And Jones served as a fake elector. Text messages show him working through the logistics with Shafer. At one point he hesitated: “I am not there yet..... I need to weigh everything out.” He asked Shafer to consult his father, a lawyer. Ultimately, he participated in the fake Electoral College contingent that sent false votes to Washington.

On January 3, 2021, White House phone logs show Trump had a three-minute conversation with Jones at 11:07 p.m. On the morning of January 5, Jones texted someone asking where they were. The reply: the Willard Intercontinental Hotel — working headquarters for the election-denial movement, where Alex Jones and Roger Stone were operating.

“Be there in 20,” Jones replied.

That evening, he went to Pence’s dinner with his letter. The next day, the Capitol fell.

Trump would later say, at a 2020 rally, that Jones was “in my pocket.”

Jones was asked about the Times investigation. He chuckled and asked if it would be “a little fluff piece about what a wonderful person I am.” He added: “I don’t have anything to hide.”

The Candidacy

Rick Jackson, a pro-Trump billionaire, will face Mr. Jones in a runoff. Credit...Dustin Chambers for The New York Times

Last week, Jones was the top vote-getter in the first round of Georgia’s Republican gubernatorial primary. He carried a Trump endorsement. He faces a June 16 runoff against Rick Jackson, a pro-Trump billionaire.

On June 17 — one day after that runoff — Governor Kemp will convene a special session of the Georgia legislature to redraw the state’s congressional and legislative maps. The session was called in response to Callais. The maps drawn in that session will determine who represents Georgia’s four million Black residents through 2028.

The timing is not coincidental. Kemp scheduled the session for the day after the primary runoffs so that the current Republican majority can lock in new maps before November’s elections potentially change the composition of the legislature. The session will also address a QR-code ballot tabulation crisis — the current system becomes unlawful on July 1 — which means that the same special session will determine both who gets represented and how the votes are counted.

If Jones wins the runoff, he will not take office until January 2027. Kemp will preside over the special session. But the governor’s race will cast its shadow over every decision made in that chamber — because the man who may next occupy the office is a man who has already demonstrated, in exhaustive documented detail, what he is willing to do with power.

The Inversion

This series documented Brad Raffensperger’s defeat in Part XXX. Raffensperger was the Georgia secretary of state who certified the 2020 election results over Trump’s direct pressure — including the infamous phone call in which Trump asked him to “find 11,780 votes.” On May 20, Georgia Republicans voted him out.

The inversion is now complete.

The man who defended the election lost his primary. The man who tried to overturn it is the front-runner for governor.

Raffensperger did his job. He certified an accurate count. He testified before Congress. He resisted the most powerful person in the country asking him to fabricate votes. Georgia Republicans thanked him by ending his career.

Jones did the opposite. He coordinated with the Trump campaign. He arranged hearings for conspiracy theorists. He served as a fake elector. He went to the Willard Hotel. He brought a letter to the vice president asking him to delay the certification. And when the Capitol was stormed, he texted that everything had “gone off the rail” — as though he had been standing on the platform, not helping to load the train.

Georgia Republicans are making him their candidate for governor.

The Loyalty Trials — the companion series tracking primary challenges against Republicans who crossed Trump — has documented this pattern across multiple states. Cassidy lost for voting to convict. Massie lost for voting against the party. Raffensperger lost for counting the votes accurately. The punishment is consistent: any Republican who chose the Constitution over the president has been removed by the Republican electorate.

Jones’s candidacy is the mirror image. The reward for trying to steal an election is a promotion.

Barbara Shoaf’s Mystery

There is a detail near the end of the Times investigation that deserves attention.

Barbara Shoaf, a retired Delta employee, was one of roughly fifty people at a Jones campaign event inside a gun store in suburban Atlanta. She believed the debunked video of ballot counting at State Farm Arena showed something illegal.

“I like watching a lot of mysteries and things,” she said.

This is not a voter who has been deceived in some sophisticated way. This is a voter who has absorbed a conspiracy theory and processed it through the grammar of entertainment. The stolen election is a mystery to be solved. The evidence has been debunked — Giuliani himself acknowledged his claims were false — but the narrative persists because it satisfies the same cognitive appetite as a detective show. There are clues. There is a cover-up. There is a sense of being in on something others have missed.

Fifty people in a gun store. One of them will tell a reporter she believes the conspiracy because she likes mysteries.

And the man standing at the front of the room, asking for their votes, was at the Willard Hotel on January 5, 2021. He does not need to believe the conspiracy. He helped build it.

Where Democracy Fights Back

Every instalment of this series asks the same question.

In Part XXIX, democracy fought back in Alabama and South Carolina — through a federal court that blocked a discriminatory map and a state senate that refused to act on one. Those were Tuesday’s victories.

Georgia is Tuesday’s warning.

The special session begins on June 17. The maps drawn in that session will shape Black political representation in Georgia for years. The man who will likely be the state’s next governor is a documented participant in a multistate conspiracy to overturn a presidential election. The man who defended that election has been voted out.

The redistricting machinery and the election-denial machinery are converging in the same state, in the same month, around the same set of power structures. The people who tried to steal the last election are positioning themselves to draw the maps for the next one.

Shane Massey asked the question in Part XXVIII: what do you do with power when you’ve attained it?

Burt Jones answered that question in 2020. He used it to try to keep a president in office who had lost. He arranged fake electors. He went to the Willard Hotel. He stood in a gun store six years later and laughed when a reporter asked about it.

The question is no longer what he would do with power. He has already shown us. The question is whether Georgia gives it to him.

The runoff is June 16. The special session is June 17. The machinery does not pause.
