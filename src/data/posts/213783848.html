Six parts into this series I have been circling a word without using it. I have written about coalition math, about vocabulary reclamation, about venue-crossing, about the stacking problem. What I have not written, until an economist did it for me, is the word for what all of that adds up to: populism. Not the pitchfork kind. The kind with a tent big enough to hold a small-business owner, a union electrician, and a Fortune 500 middle manager under the same canvas without anyone having to apologise for existing.

As Daron Acemoglu wrote in The New York Times on 1 September 2026, the Democratic Party’s turn toward economic populism is both understandable and dangerous — understandable because the affordability crisis is real and workers without a college degree have been left behind by decades of growth they never shared in, dangerous because populism has two settings, and only one of them builds a majority capable of governing. Acemoglu calls the useful setting “big-tent populism.” He credits it to Jon Ossoff’s line about a wealthy country in which no one should go without care for lack of money, and to a lineage running from Franklin Roosevelt’s improbable coalition of Southern conservatives, labour, and educated progressives, through the Swedish Social Democrats’ 1938 Saltsjöbaden accord with business, which built the high-wage, high-investment postwar Swedish economy on a foundation of trust rather than suspicion.

I want to sit with that word “trust” for a moment, because it is doing more work in Acemoglu’s essay than he perhaps lets on, and it is the missing ingredient in five of the last six parts of this series.

Part I opened with Abdul El-Sayed’s one-point primary win over Haley Stevens and the warning that mistaking a bare-knuckle primary victory for a governing mandate is how the Tea Party ate the Republican Party alive. Part VI extended the thesis: it isn’t the substance of a position that draws fire, it’s the sequencing and venue in which it’s aired. Ro Khanna can hold nearly the same view on Israel that cost other candidates dearly, and escape largely unscathed, because he chose Fox News as his venue and framed it as a fourteen-point plan rather than a purity test. Acemoglu’s essay gives that intuition its theoretical spine. Big-tent populism is not about softening your positions. Roosevelt did not soften anything when he told the country in 1936 that he welcomed the hatred of “organised money.” He escalated. But he escalated in a way designed to draw people into the tent rather than sort them by their proximity to sin. The rhetoric was adversarial; the coalition behind it was not narrowed by the rhetoric. That is the trick Democrats in 2026 keep almost, but not quite, pulling off.

Consider what Acemoglu identifies as the failure mode: what he calls, with the deliberate provocation of an academic who knows exactly what he’s doing, “Woke 1.0” — a politics that saw the world entirely through an oppressor-oppressed lens and then, crucially, extended that lens even to people who had no business being cast as oppressors, including a great many non-college-educated white voters who were, materially, in roughly the same boat as everyone else scrambling to make rent. Acemoglu’s point is not that inequality doesn’t exist or that anger about it is misplaced. It’s that a politics organised entirely around identifying villains eventually runs out of allies, because everyone not currently holding the pitchfork starts wondering when their turn comes.

This is where the series’ recurring cast re-enters, because Acemoglu’s essay was published the same week that two of them supplied living illustrations of both settings.

Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, whom Acemoglu cites for once suggesting a person cannot “earn” a billion dollars — that anyone who has one must have broken rules or underpaid workers to get there — represents the adversarial setting he warns against, not because the underlying critique of concentrated wealth is baseless, but because “you can’t earn that” forecloses the conversation rather than opening it. It tells the small-business owner watching at home that the tent has a velvet rope, and Acemoglu suspects, probably correctly, that a fair number of them will decline the invitation to stand behind it.

Zohran Mamdani, now installed as mayor of New York City, gets a more interesting treatment. Acemoglu doesn’t dismiss him. He notes that Mamdani built a winning coalition on affordability, and that as mayor he is now navigating exactly the choice this series has been tracking since Part I: double down on the campaign promises of city-run grocery stores and subsidised transit, policies that make for excellent rally lines and uncertain long-term economics, or pursue the harder work of a genuinely broad coalition, which Mamdani has at least gestured toward by convening a business advisory council. Acemoglu reports, fairly, that the business leaders on that council remain sceptical. Gestures toward a big tent are not the same as having built one. But the gesture itself is the point Part V of this series made about governors: Andy Beshear’s advice to “talk like a normal human being” and Rob Sand’s Iowa singalong ritual were never really about charm. They were about signalling, credibly, that the tent has room.

Here is where I think Acemoglu’s framework does something this series hadn’t quite articulated on its own: it explains why the “stacking problem” from Part VI isn’t primarily about media strategy or debate discipline. It’s about which populism a candidate is practising. El-Sayed’s Green New Deal and defund-the-police comments from years past aren’t dangerous to him because they’re extreme; they’re dangerous because they signal, rightly or wrongly, an oppressor-oppressed framework in which police officers and fossil-fuel workers land on the wrong side of the rope. Khanna’s fourteen-point Fox News plan survives scrutiny not because it’s more moderate — by Acemoglu’s own account it may not be — but because its architecture is inclusive rather than accusatory. It reads as an invitation. The El-Sayed record, fairly or not, reads as a verdict already rendered.

I want to be careful here, in the spirit this series has tried to maintain since the “Wake Up, Dems” piece opened it. This is not an argument that the Democratic establishment was right and the insurgents were wrong, any more than Part IV’s account of Chuck Schumer’s authority problems in Michigan, Maine, and Florida was an argument that Schumer deserved to be listened to. Acemoglu is explicit that centrists are right about the necessity of governance and feasible policy, and progressives are right that new ideas are needed to constrain corporate power and expand opportunity. His synthesis isn’t “move to the centre.” It’s “stop sorting the electorate into the saved and the damned,” which is a different and considerably harder instruction, because it requires holding a genuinely adversarial economic message — Roosevelt’s “I welcome their hatred” — without letting that adversarial energy metastasise into a loyalty test applied to ordinary voters who happen to work at, invest in, or aspire to run a business.

There is a Canadian angle here that I don’t think American commentators reach for, because they don’t have the comparison sitting next door. The Westminster “loyal opposition” tradition that gives this series its name presumes, structurally, that the opposition is trying to become the government, not simply to be right. A Canadian opposition party that spent its time in the wilderness sorting voters into oppressor and oppressed camps would be laughed out of the next election, not because Canadians lack appetite for redistribution — we built a health system on the appetite — but because the entire parliamentary culture assumes you eventually have to govern the people you were just calling villains. Roosevelt understood this instinctively; Acemoglu is arguing the Democratic Party of 2026 needs to relearn it deliberately.

It’s worth noting, too, that Acemoglu’s list of problems a big-tent populism could actually solve — the monopoly problem driving up prices, the underperforming health and education systems, crumbling infrastructure, and artificial intelligence “running amok as an automation technology displacing millions” — is not a list of culture-war grievances. It’s a list of kitchen-table problems, which brings the argument back to the affordability framing this series has watched Democrats circle for months without quite landing on a single sentence for it. The party’s advantage on cost-of-living polling, which shows Democrats ahead by eight points when voters are asked which party has a better plan for the price of groceries and gas, is only useful if the coalition delivering that message doesn’t simultaneously alienate the small-business owners and middle managers who are also feeling squeezed and who would, in a big-tent frame, be allies rather than suspects. Monopoly power is a big-tent villain in a way that “billionaires” as a category is not: everyone downstream of a monopolist’s pricing power, from the family running a hardware store to the family shopping at one, has a stake in breaking it up. That is precisely Acemoglu’s point about Roosevelt targeting “organised money” rather than wealth as such — the target has to be a structure people recognise as unfair, not a class of people they might someday hope to join.

Ossoff’s Georgia is a useful data point here, and one this series flagged as an outlier as far back as Part IV. Georgia never developed the establishment-versus-base rift that swallowed Michigan, Maine, and Florida this cycle. Part of that is candidate quality and part of it is simple electoral math in a state Democrats need to hold rather than flip. But part of it, read through Acemoglu’s lens, is that Ossoff has spent his Senate term speaking in exactly the register the big-tent thesis calls for — adversarial toward concentrated power, inclusive toward the people caught underneath it. Nobody has needed to stage an intervention over Ossoff’s vocabulary, because he never gave anyone cause to sort him into a camp in the first place.

The test, over the next nine weeks, is whether the party’s most compelling messengers can hold both halves of Acemoglu’s synthesis at once. Ossoff’s line — no one should go without care for lack of money, in the wealthiest country in the world — is adversarial in its implication and universal in its address. It doesn’t require anyone to confess to being an oppressor to be moved by it. That is the sentence-level test for every candidate this series has tracked: does the line invite, or does it sort? Sand’s Iowa optimism invites. Whitmer’s “too obtuse” NPR moment, from Part V, was criticised precisely because it read as sorting the audience into people sophisticated enough to follow her and people who weren’t. Khanna’s fourteen points invite, even from a Fox News set. El-Sayed’s record, however sincerely he now minimises it, was assembled in a period when a great deal of Democratic rhetoric was built to sort rather than invite, and he is now paying the interest on that loan in real time, a subject this series will return to directly in the next instalment.

Acemoglu ends his essay by insisting that populism “can and should be a major part” of the answer to the problems he lists — the monopoly problem, the underperforming health and education systems, the AI displacement risk — but only if it speaks for ordinary people in their own language rather than hunting for oppressors, real or imaginary. It is, in its way, the same warning this series opened with in “Wake Up, Dems”: that mistaking the satisfaction of naming an enemy for the harder work of building a coalition is how movements win primaries and lose majorities. The tent, as Roosevelt and the Swedish Social Democrats both understood, has to be big enough to hold the people you’re angry at, provided they’re willing to come inside and negotiate the terms. Whether the Democratic Party of 2026 can build one before 3 November remains the central question this series exists to answer.
