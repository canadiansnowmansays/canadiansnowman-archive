I. The Crash-Out

David Wallace-Wells, writing in the Times on July 8, 2026, opened with the Palantir CEO Alex Karp’s now-notorious CNBC appearance — described online as a “crash out” — in which Karp declared that “something has gone completely wrong” with the entire structure of the AI industry. His target was the business model underneath Anthropic and OpenAI themselves: closed, proprietary models sold at a premium, built, in his telling, on hoarding value from clients rather than empowering them. Open-source and open-weight alternatives, Karp argued, were the obviously better path for nearly everyone. “The jig is up,” he said.

It would be easy to dismiss this as noise from what Wallace-Wells calls “an irresistibly noxious figure” — and it is worth saying plainly that Karp is not a disinterested observer. He was on television promoting a new Palantir–Nvidia partnership built to compete directly with the frontier labs he was criticizing, timed a day after Palantir published its own manifesto on “AI sovereignty.” France’s intelligence service has reportedly cut ties with Palantir over sovereignty and privacy concerns of its own, and the firm’s NHS partnership in Britain looks shaky. Karp was, in Wallace-Wells’s phrase, “talking his own book.”

None of that makes the underlying question less real, and this series has been asking a version of it since Part 1: can any single lab actually win this race outright, or is the entire premise of a monopolistic AI future — the one that has justified hundreds of billions in capital expenditure — simply wrong?

II. The Bet Underneath the Bet

The AI investment boom was built on a specific wager, and Wallace-Wells states it cleanly: that a sufficiently powerful model could recursively improve itself fast enough to create an exponential liftoff toward “artificial superintelligence,” at which point even a small early lead would compound into something like a natural monopoly on machine intelligence — sellable to the world at almost any price the leader chose to name.

That bet is looking shakier by the month. No lab has held a durable advantage. Open-source alternatives — the pattern this series traced through DeepSeek in January 2025 and again through GLM-5.2 in Parts 51 and 60, and updated with the Ramp and OpenRouter adoption data in Part 61 — keep closing the gap almost as fast as it opens. When the frontier labs raised prices to bring them closer to the actual cost of running these models, corporate customers balked, and uptake of premium frontier models flatlined while cheap open-source use kept climbing.

This is the same appeal-to-Congress dynamic this series documented in the distillation fight: labs that built their valuations on the assumption of a durable technical moat are now asking lawmakers to build them a legal one instead, arguing that “lesser” competitors — many of them foreign — are stealing what the market itself is failing to protect. Wallace-Wells notes that a draft internal Treasury Department analysis has reportedly warned that the sheer size of the major AI companies now represents a systemic risk to the broader financial system — a warning that, tellingly, higher-ups at Treasury have publicly pushed back on rather than embraced. That is a governance gap in miniature: an internal warning about systemic financial risk, produced inside the government itself, and swatted down before it could become policy.

Peter Thiel once framed the split cleanly, if in his own vocabulary — crypto as a libertarian technology, decentralizing power away from any single authority, AI as a “communist” one, concentrating godlike planning capacity into a single machine whose judgment would eventually supersede the market’s. What Karp’s rant and the diffusion data both suggest is that the convergence Thiel predicted for AI hasn’t happened. The landscape is more competitive and more fragmented than the monopoly thesis assumed, which means the leading labs may end up mattering less to the actual shape of the AI future than three years of headlines have suggested — even as the capital keeps flowing as though the opposite were true.

III. The Gap Between the Question and the Answer

If Section II is about who controls the intelligence, the second piece of this installment is about what that intelligence is quietly doing to the humans using it — a thread this series opened in Part 37 (The Dependency Problem) and that gets a sharper, more measurable account in a companion Times essay published the same day.

Anne-Laure Le Cunff, a neuroscientist writing in the Times on July 8, 2026, reports that more than 60 percent of Google searches in the United States now end without a single click — the user reads an AI-generated summary and leaves. Claude, ChatGPT, and Perplexity all do a version of the same thing: compress what used to be a meandering search into an immediate arrival at an answer. Le Cunff’s argument is not the familiar one about declining web traffic or content monetization. It’s about what neuroscience says curiosity actually does. Researchers have found that people in a state of curiosity — waiting for an answer to an intriguing question — retain unrelated information encountered during that wait far better than they otherwise would; brain scans show the waiting period itself activates reward circuits and primes the hippocampus for new memory formation. Curiosity, in other words, opens a window for learning that extends well past the specific fact being sought. Close the gap between question and answer fast enough, and the window closes before it can do that work.

Le Cunff’s example is the discovery of cosmic microwave background radiation: Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson could have written off a persistent hiss in their antenna as equipment noise in 1964. Instead they kept asking what it might be, and found the afterglow of the Big Bang. Her point translates directly into the Asimov Gap’s central argument: the apprenticeship pipeline documented in Part 47 is not the only kind of training ground that AI has quietly closed off. Incidental learning — the tangents, the wrong turns, the unplanned adjacent article — is itself a training ground for exactly the kind of judgment this series has argued the next generation of senior workers will need and won’t have had the chance to build.

Le Cunff is careful to note that this isn’t the kind of harm that produces a class-action lawsuit or draws a regulator’s attention, the way addictive infinite-scroll design eventually did. Nobody is forced to use an AI summary instead of clicking through. But the default architecture nudges everyone toward the fast answer, and Le Cunff’s prescription — keep sources visible, show competing explanations instead of one smoothed paragraph, build search modes that reward exploration — is a design choice, not a law. Which means, in this series’ terms, it is exactly the kind of decision nobody is required to make well.

IV. Two Gaps, Same Country

Put the Karp critique and the curiosity finding next to each other and a pattern from this series’ spine sharpens further. Section II describes a market structure question nobody has settled: whether AI power concentrates into a handful of firms or diffuses outward faster than anyone building a business plan four years ago expected. Section III describes a cognitive question nobody has settled either: whether the tools built to answer any question instantly are quietly closing off the kind of undirected exploration that produces the discoveries — and the workers — capable of asking the next one.

Neither gap required malice. The AI labs didn’t set out to make the world less curious; they set out to build a better answer engine, and the erosion of incidental learning is a side effect nobody priced in. Karp doesn’t need frontier labs to fail out of ill will; he needs Palantir’s bet on sovereignty and openness to be the one that pays off, and he is happy to say so on television. Nobody decided any of this. That has been the thesis of this series since Part 1, and it holds here in a register this series hasn’t used before: even the market itself may not know yet who’s actually winning.
