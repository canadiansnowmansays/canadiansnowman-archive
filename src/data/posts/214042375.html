Third of five essays Stephen Gordon Sept 9, 2026

Part I of this series closed on a specific claim: that every mechanism Donald Trump accused of stealing the 2020 election — the machines, the mail ballots, the state voter rolls, the count itself — was tested against evidence and found, in courtroom after courtroom, department after department, not to have failed him at all. This essay does not revisit that finding. It asks what a man does with a system he has spent four years insisting cannot be trusted, once he holds the power to redesign it. The answer, laid out state by state and agency by agency since early 2025, is that he has not tried to make the system more trustworthy in any neutral sense. He has tried to make it answerable to him specifically, in advance, through four mechanisms aimed at the same underlying goal — controlling the outcome before the count happens, rather than contesting it after — even though the four are not equally novel. Three are federal actions with little precedent. The fourth, redistricting, is an old and ordinary tool of American politics pushed toward a new extreme; it belongs in this essay for what it is being used to accomplish, not because it is unusual in itself.

The first mechanism is the district map itself, and it began, characteristically, with a direct request rather than a study or a commission. In the summer of 2025, Trump personally pressed Texas Republicans to redraw the state’s congressional lines mid-decade — outside the normal once-a-decade redistricting cycle that follows the census — specifically to add Republican-leaning seats ahead of the 2026 midterms. Texas complied, adding five GOP-friendly districts. What followed was not contained to Texas. Missouri, North Carolina, and Ohio enacted new maps of their own; Indiana debated one before its state Senate initially found the votes lacking; Florida, Louisiana, and South Carolina joined the wave through 2026. Trump personally celebrated each addition on Truth Social as states “signed up,” writing of Missouri specifically that the state was “now IN” and that Republicans would “win the Midterms in Missouri again, bigger and better than ever before.” By the count of nonpartisan trackers, nine or more states had redrawn maps by mid-2026, with Republican gains estimated at seven to fourteen seats depending on how ongoing court challenges resolve — a mid-decade redistricting race unlike anything the country has seen since the 1960s, initiated not by population shifts the census revealed, but by a president who wanted different numbers on a scoreboard he does not control by winning votes alone. Fairness requires noting the race has not run in only one direction: California voters approved a Democratic countermap in direct response to Texas, adding an estimated five Democratic-leaning seats of its own, and Indiana’s state Senate initially rejected the map Vice President Vance traveled there personally to request. The redistricting fight is genuinely two-sided in the sense that both parties are now drawing maps to their own advantage wherever they hold the legislature to do it. It is not two-sided in its origin: nonpartisan trackers agree the current cycle began with Trump’s request to Texas in the summer of 2025, not with any Democratic-led state moving first, which makes the countermaps a response to a race he started rather than evidence the race was already underway.

The second mechanism runs through the Department of Justice, and it targets the same voter rolls Trump spent 2020 insisting were full of the dead and the ineligible. In July 2026, the DOJ’s Civil Rights Division — now led by Harmeet Dhillon, who has spent her tenure redirecting the division that historically protected voting rights toward investigating the officials who administer it — sent letters to secretaries of state in all fifty states, warning that any election officer who “knowingly retains noncitizens” on a state’s voter rolls could face criminal prosecution. The letters lean on a repurposed federal tool called SAVE — the Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements system, built originally to check immigration status for benefits eligibility, now cross-referenced against state voter files to flag potential noncitizens. The tool’s own track record undercuts the urgency behind it, and nowhere more starkly than in Nevada, one of four states Trump named by name in a prime-time address as harboring thousands of noncitizen voters. As Nick Corasaniti and Alan Feuer reported for The New York Times, the administration’s public claim was 15,903 noncitizens on Nevada’s rolls specifically, part of a 278,000 figure across four states. In a subsequent closed-door meeting with state election administrators, Homeland Security officials could not substantiate that number and admitted, on recordings later obtained by reporters, that they had so far identified 185 potential cases — a figure one DHS official called “the ceiling,” not a floor. Idaho’s own review told the same story at smaller scale: 760 initial flags, roughly three dozen that survived investigation. Even the conservative Heritage Foundation, in a study spanning twenty-five years, found only about 100 cases of noncitizen voting nationwide. Nationally, a ProPublica analysis of Justice Department data found that between January 2025 and May 2026, fewer than 150 alleged noncitizen voters were referred for prosecution and only 41 were actually charged — out of an electorate of more than 150 million people. A prosecution threat that produces a public figure ninety-nine percent larger than what the same officials privately admit they can support is not a response to a documented crime wave. It is a lever aimed at officials who might otherwise decline to purge names a flawed federal database has flagged, timed to land in the months and weeks before an election rather than the “quiet period” election-integrity advocates across both parties have traditionally called necessary to let wrongly flagged voters correct the record. The same rolls campaign runs through a separate legal front, detailed fully in Part III: a December 2025 lawsuit demanding unredacted voter data from thirty states, twenty-three of which have already had the suit against them dismissed.

The second mechanism has a second front, and it targets the one voter-roll tool with a genuine bipartisan pedigree rather than a partisan one. Per reporting from Alan Feuer, Nick Corasaniti, and Alexandra Berzon in The New York Times, Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin asked the Justice Department in a letter this month to investigate the Electronic Registration Information Center, or ERIC — a multistate data-sharing consortium, once considered a wonky and uncontroversial way for states to catch voters registered in more than one place, that nine Republican-led states withdrew from in 2022 after a report claiming it was a front for partisan voter-registration drives. That report was written by Heather Honey, then an outside activist — and now a senior official at the same Department of Homeland Security issuing the new investigation demand, working alongside Cleta Mitchell, a lawyer who helped organize Trump’s own attempt to overturn the 2020 election. Nearly thirty states still use ERIC, which election officials across both parties credit as the only reliable way to catch double-voting and clean dead registrants from the rolls — the same function the DOJ letters above claim to want strengthened. The mechanism is not being improved. The people who spent 2020 and 2021 building the theory that it couldn’t be trusted are, in 2026, the ones now empowered to investigate it.

The third mechanism is the one Trump has pushed hardest and, notably, not yet gotten: the SAVE America Act, which shares its acronym with the database above, though the two are legally distinct instruments. Where the SAVE database is an administrative tool DOJ and DHS use against existing law, the SAVE America Act is legislation Trump has spent 2026 demanding Congress pass outright — a bill that would require documentary proof of citizenship at voter registration, photo identification at the polls stricter than any state currently imposes, and, in its House-passed form, a mandate that every state submit its voter registration list to the Department of Homeland Security within thirty days of enactment, with no restrictions specified on what DHS may do with that data once received. Trump has been unusually direct about what the bill is for. He has told reporters and posted, without qualification, that passing it would “guarantee the midterms” for Republicans — not secure them, not protect them from fraud, guarantee them, a word that assumes an outcome rather than an honest process for reaching one. He has pushed Senate Majority Leader John Thune to eliminate the filibuster to force it through, refused to sign unrelated bipartisan housing legislation as leverage, and backed an attempt to attach it to the must-pass National Defense Authorization Act after the direct route stalled. Thune has held the line, telling reporters the Senate GOP conference is “not even close” to the sixty votes required, and as of this writing the bill remains unpassed — a genuine limit on this mechanism worth stating plainly, since a series built on testing consistency does not get to only report the levers that worked. Even the bill’s own supporters have struggled to justify its urgency with evidence: a review of Utah’s voter rolls, conducted specifically to find the fraud the Act is meant to prevent, turned up a single potential noncitizen registration out of more than two million voters, and no confirmed instance of that person actually casting a ballot.

There is a detail in how the SAVE America Act has been sold that echoes Part I more directly than any redistricting map or DOJ letter does — and the same speech that sold it also revived the single 2020 claim this series has most carefully avoided overstating. In July 2026, in the prime-time address urging Congress to pass the bill, Trump cited documents he said showed Chinese interference with voter data and cyber-vulnerabilities in U.S. election infrastructure — a foreign-adversary framing his own administration’s intelligence findings do not support. Senator Mark Warner, the ranking Democrat on the Intelligence Committee, responded that U.S. intelligence agencies had unanimously concluded China did not attempt to alter a single vote in the 2020 election, and that the voter-file access the administration was now calling a security breach was, in fact, information publicly available for purchase by any candidate. In the same address, Trump went further and revived the original 2020 machine-fraud claim almost verbatim, telling the country that voting systems reliant on bar codes and QR codes were “vulnerable” and “easily compromised,” and pointing to a trove of declassified intelligence documents about a company called Smartmatic — the same company at the center of the 2020 conspiracy theory, tied in his telling to the Venezuelan government. The documents he cited do not support the claim he built on them. One of them, a CIA analysis, states that while the Venezuelan government had “some capability in manipulating electronic voting systems,” neither Venezuela nor Smartmatic could “manipulate the outcome of an election outside Venezuela.” Trump’s own selectively declassified evidence, in other words, rules out the thing he used it to allege — the same gap between claim and record Barr documented in December 2020, except this time the record contradicting the president is a document his own administration chose to release. Homeland Security Secretary Mullin’s own September letter to the Justice Department, seeking a fresh federal examination of election machinery, references a parallel winter effort by senior Trump intelligence officials to probe the vulnerabilities of Dominion Voting Systems — now rebranded as Liberty Vote — the exact company Fox News paid $787.5 million to stop being accused of the same thing five years earlier. Blame a foreign adversary for compromising an election, and use that claim to justify new domestic control over how the next one is run: this was the Dominion theory’s own structure in 2020, and in 2026 it has been revived twice in a single administration, against the same company under a new name, with a government’s own declassified intelligence undercutting it both times.

The fourth mechanism is the one this publication has already tracked in real time across the companion series When Democracy Fights Back, and it does not need re-litigating here in full: the Postal Service rule directing mail-ballot eligibility through a federal citizenship-data portal, with election-mail service withheld entirely from states that decline to comply. What matters for this essay is only where that mechanism fits alongside the other three. Redistricting reshapes which votes count toward which seat. The DOJ letters and the SAVE database determine who stays on the rolls to vote at all. The SAVE America Act would write federal voter-data control into permanent law. The mail rule determines whether the ballot reaches the voter, and the voter's ballot reaches the count, in the states that rely on mail voting most. Four different agencies, four different legal instruments, one convergent target: the machinery Trump named as broken in 2020 is, mechanism by mechanism, becoming machinery his administration holds a hand on directly.

None of these four mechanisms, examined individually, proves the claim this series exists to test. A redistricting push is not illegal; states have redrawn maps mid-decade before, if rarely — it is, as this essay said at the outset, the legal cousin of the other three rather than their equal in novelty. A DOJ letter warning of prosecution is not, by itself, a purge. A bill that has not passed is not a law. A postal rule currently blocked by a federal judge is not yet in effect. Fairness requires saying so, the same way Part I said plainly that the Fulton County case against Trump remains procedurally unresolved. What changes the reading of these four mechanisms is not any one of them alone. It is what they share: a common target in exactly the machinery Trump spent 2020 calling untrustworthy, pursued through methods that consistently place a federal lever — his administration’s lever — over decisions that, in every one of the sixty-plus 2020 lawsuits, courts held belonged to the states. In 2020, the claim was that state-run election administration had failed him and needed federal correction. In 2026, faced with the same state-run election administration, the remedy on offer is not correction. It is federal control, sought through whichever of four different instruments will move first.

The next essay in this series turns from building tomorrow’s machinery to something stranger: federal power spent reopening the settled election of 2020 itself, and dismantling the bodies built to referee elections at all. The concluding essay after that will do what these three will have deliberately avoided doing along the way: place the specific 2020 claims and the specific 2026 record side by side, pair by pair, and ask whether a consistent standard connects them. If it does not — if the pattern that emerges is one standard when Trump loses and a different, more convenient standard once he holds the power to prevent losing — this series will say so, using the word it has held back since the Introduction. If it does, this series will say that too. The record built across these three essays is what that judgment will have to answer to.
