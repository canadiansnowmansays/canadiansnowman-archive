On September 1, 2026, Prime Minister Mark Carney told reporters in Ottawa that Canada would not return to the negotiating table until Washington stopped “throwing shade.” As CBC’s Peter Zimonjic and David Cummings reported, Carney was responding to a run of insults and mockery from the Trump administration since the August 21 collapse — Lake Ontario renamed “Lake America,” Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth mocking Canadian military cadets online, an AI-generated White House video of Canada geese with Trump-styled hair marching with rifles. Carney called the comments “beneath their office” and said the door to talks would open again “when the Americans are ready” — his own terms, no timeline attached, no concession volunteered.

Five days later, on September 6, the administration answered — not at the table, but on Truth Social, in the form of a Grok-animated cartoon of the president standing over a fallen Carney on a hockey rink, Carney in a Team Canada jersey sprawled on the ice, Trump in a Team USA jersey looming over him with his stick still raised. The caption read: “GET UP, GOVERNOR.” Not “Prime Minister.” Governor — the same 51st-state mockery this series has tracked since Part XXIII, now rendered as a taunt aimed at a man still on the ground. It drew 1.8 thousand retruths and 8.24 thousand likes by the time anyone in Ottawa could formally respond to it, which is to say: this is roughly what “seriously engaging,” in this administration’s vocabulary, currently looks like.

Two nights later, the actual policy answer arrived, and it was not a hockey stick. Tonight, September 8, 2026, President Trump signed five proclamations that did something his administration had, until now, only threatened. Tariffs are a tax. A ban is a lock on the door. Canadian alcohol — most beer, wine, cider and spirits — along with certain dairy products and motorcycles, will simply no longer be permitted into the United States, effective September 29. Alongside the ban, Washington reshuffled its existing tariff schedule: duties on cement, road salt, toilet paper, hospital pads and fishing rod parts were quietly lifted, while new 50 percent tariffs landed on ATVs, some cheeses, motor boats, and an expanded list of steel, aluminum, furniture and paper goods. As Axios reported, the White House invoked Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 — a 1930 statute so rarely used that no president in the ninety-six years since its passage appears to have ever issued tariffs under it before this administration discovered it this year.

Regular readers of this series will recognize the significance of that statute number before I explain it, because we have been here before. In Part XXVI, covering the Section 338 legal exposure as reported by PBS, I noted a detail that seemed almost theoretical at the time: the statute contains an escalation clause. If a tariffed country “maintained or increased its said discriminations” in response to the initial 338 tariffs, the president may exclude those imports from the United States altogether. An embargo power. Unused, as of late August. Peter Harrell of Georgetown had warned that if the administration won its looming court fights over the statute’s legality, “you have to expect we’re going to see a lot of 338 use.” He was talking about the future. It is now the present. Canada’s own retaliatory tariffs — announced August 25, effective September 8 — are precisely the “maintained or increased discrimination” the clause requires, and Washington used the escalation clause less than twenty-four hours after Ottawa’s countermeasures took effect. The series flagged the loaded gun three weeks ago. Someone has now fired it.

I. The Ledger, Updated

Set the alcohol ban alongside everything else that happened the same night, because none of it happened in isolation. A separate Truth Social post accused Canada and its provinces of banning American small businesses from government procurement markets, with the President writing “NO RECIPROCITY – NO ACCESS” and threatening to lock Canadian firms out of U.S. government contracts in turn — a new front, procurement, that this series has not previously had to track. A senior administration official confirmed on the same evening’s press call that the long-threatened 50 percent tariff on cars and trucks “remains on the table,” unresolved, dangling since Part XXV. And earlier the same week, Bombardier — the Montreal-based aerospace manufacturer — was told it would be banned from the American market entirely unless it relocated production to the United States, with the President writing that the company “lives off American Buyers, American Companies, American Airports, and American Service” while Canada “blocks our GREAT American Banks, and Companies.”

Notice the shape of the pattern, because it is the same shape this series has documented since Part XXIII. Threaten. The other side does not fold. Escalate to a formal instrument — tariff, ban, procurement lock — that requires an executive signature rather than a social media post. Then find a new front to threaten on while the last one is still being litigated, diplomatically or otherwise. TACO, in other words, minus the Capitulate stage, because Canada keeps declining to supply it.

II. Last Call for Molson

Here is where I want to make an argument that, as far as I can tell, nobody in Washington has bothered to run the numbers on: this ban is going to be unpopular with the people who actually drink the product being banned, and those people vote.

Canadian beer holds a specific, durable reputation in the American market that has nothing to do with trade policy and everything to do with brewing regulation. Canadian beer is typically brewed to a higher standard alcohol content than the American mainstream — a 5 percent Canadian lager is common where the historically dominant American lagers sat closer to 4.2 percent before the craft revolution narrowed the gap — and it has spent decades being marketed, not inaccurately, on exactly that difference. Border-state drinkers in Michigan, New York, Washington and Maine did not need an import ban explained to them; they have been driving across the Ambassador Bridge or the Peace Bridge for a case of Moosehead or Labatt 50 for longer than most trade negotiators currently in the room have been alive. Molson, Labatt, Moosehead, Alexander Keith’s, and a long tail of craft exports are not niche imports confined to Canadian restaurants in American cities — they are shelf staples in exactly the kind of border and Rust Belt states that decide close elections.

This is not a claim that a beer ban will swing any house or senate race by itself. It is a narrower and more interesting claim: that this administration has, in the space of one Tuesday night proclamation, converted an abstract argument about steel tariffs and auto content rules — genuinely hard for most voters to feel in their daily life — into something a voter feels the next time they stand in a liquor store aisle and the familiar green bottle simply is not there. Tariffs raise a price. A ban removes a choice. Voters forgive the former far more easily than the latter, and the midterms are less than 2 months away, well inside the memory of anyone who liked their Tuesday-night Moosehead and can no longer buy it.

There is an additional irony worth sitting with, because the series has already documented it once from the other direction. Nearly every Canadian province pulled American beer, wine and spirits from government-run liquor store shelves back in early 2025, in direct response to the administration’s first round of tariffs — a boycott the White House itself has repeatedly cited as a grievance, and which the administration’s own fact sheet blamed for an 81 percent collapse in American alcohol sales into Canada. Washington spent a year and a half treating a consumer boycott as an unfair trade practice worth escalating over. It has now responded not by matching the boycott with one of its own choosing, but by legislating an identical outcome onto its own drinkers by executive order — the very thing it called discriminatory when Canadian provinces did it voluntarily, it has now done to Americans involuntarily. If the boycott was worth a Section 338 proclamation when Canadians chose it for themselves, it is difficult to see the argument for why an American drinker should feel differently about having the same choice removed from him by Washington rather than by Queen’s Park or Victoria. If the administration’s own polling has not flagged this, someone in the West Wing should be fired before the next liquor store run does the flagging for them.

III. The Rest of the Cellar

The second point follows from the same instinct — namely, that this administration keeps confusing “products we’ve decided to ban” with “products we can afford to lose” — and it cuts the other way entirely once you look at what this round of measures pointedly does not touch.

Every tariff and ban imposed on Canada since this trade war began has carved out the same short list: oil, gas, electricity, potash. As the New York Times’ Ian Austen reported back in Part XXV, the new US tariffs exclude these categories specifically because the administration understands, correctly, that Canada is not a discretionary supplier of them. Canada supplies roughly 60 percent of American potash imports — the fertilizer base for a meaningful share of U.S. corn and soybean production, with no comparably-priced substitute available at scale on short notice. Canada supplies a significant share of U.S. uranium imports for civilian nuclear power. Canadian electricity keeps the lights on, literally, in parts of New York, Michigan, Minnesota and New England through cross-border grid interconnections that were built for reliability, not politics. Canadian crude, mostly heavy oil from Alberta, feeds Midwest refineries specifically engineered to process it — refineries that cannot simply swap to a different crude grade the way a consumer swaps a beer brand.

Doug Ford has been saying some version of this since Part XXV — “you won’t get a grain of sand out of Ontario” — and Carney himself has resisted weaponizing energy exports even as Ford pushes him toward it, telling reporters back in August “I don’t think they want us to stop sending it.” That resistance has so far held. But it is worth stating plainly, in a week when Washington escalated to an outright product ban over beer and cheese, that the list of things Canada has so far declined to touch is considerably longer and considerably more consequential than the list of things it has touched.

Consider the asymmetry in concrete terms rather than abstract ones. A Canadian who cannot buy an imported American bourbon has dozens of domestic and international substitutes on the same shelf within the hour. An American electric utility that suddenly cannot draw power across a cross-border interconnection built decades ago for exactly this kind of reliability has no equivalent hour-scale substitute; new generation and transmission capacity is measured in years, not liquor store runs. A Midwest refinery engineered around Alberta’s heavy crude cannot simply retool for a lighter grade without capital expenditure most operators have no appetite for absorbing voluntarily. And American farmers dependent on Canadian potash for fertilizer are, if anything, more exposed than the automakers and steelmakers this trade war has spent a year fighting over, because there is no comparably-priced alternative supplier capable of meeting American demand at anything like current volume. None of this is a call for Ottawa to reach for those levers — Carney’s own caution here has been, on the evidence of this series, more strategically sound than Ford’s appetite for escalation. It is simply an observation that an administration confident enough to embargo Molson on a Tuesday night might want to remember, before it reaches for the next escalation, that the reason potash and uranium and heavy crude are still flowing freely is a choice Ottawa is making, not a limitation Washington imposed. Choices, unlike tariff schedules, can be revisited — and the country making them has spent a year demonstrating, part after part in this series, that it is more willing to test that proposition than Washington seems to have priced in.

IV. What Ottawa Said Back

The response inside Canada arrived faster than Washington’s own follow-through usually does. Quebec’s government held its own press conference the same day — Premier Christine Fréchette, flanked by Economy Minister Bernard Drainville and Municipal Affairs Minister Samuel Poulin — addressing the tariff response directly rather than waiting for Ottawa to speak first, a pattern of provincial leaders getting ahead of the federal government’s own messaging that this series has tracked since Ford’s interventions in Part XXV. Lana Payne, president of Unifor, Canada’s largest private-sector union, publicly backed the federal retaliation the same day, saying, in essence, that Ottawa had done everything possible as a country to resolve this at the negotiating table before reaching for countermeasures. Carney himself had not issued a formal statement as of this writing — consistent with his pattern since Part XXIII of letting cabinet ministers absorb the first news cycle before he speaks.

V. The Ledger Continues

Add this to the running tally the series has kept since the Section 338 tranche first took effect: an average effective U.S. tariff rate on Canadian goods that Trevor Tombe and Frances Donald have placed somewhere in the 6 to 6.5 percent range, an outright ban now layered on top of that for the first time, a new procurement front opened without warning, an auto tariff still hanging fire from Part XXV, and a Section 338 escalation clause used for the first time in the statute’s ninety-six-year history — used, specifically, in direct response to the retaliation this series predicted three weeks ago Canada’s own countermeasures would provoke.

The pattern holds. What has changed is the instrument. Tariffs can be absorbed, budgeted around, passed partly onto consumers, litigated over years in the Court of International Trade. A ban cannot be absorbed; it can only be obeyed or reversed. Washington has now shown it is willing to use the more absolute tool over a product category — beer — where the political cost of doing so falls closest to home. Whether that turns out to be strategic confidence or a genuine miscalculation about what American voters will tolerate losing is, as with so much else in this series, a question the next few months will answer better than any proclamation can.

Correction and Update, September 24, 2026

Sixteen days after this essay ran, and five days before the ban takes effect, the fine print has surfaced — and it undercuts the central claim of Section II. I would rather say so here than leave it standing.

As Clay Risen reported for the New York Times on September 24, the proclamation carries a carve-out that most of the coverage, this essay included, missed entirely: whisky brought from Canada in bulk and bottled in the United States is excluded not only from the ban but from the 50 percent tariff as well. That exemption covers Crown Royal, the highest-selling Canadian whisky in the American market, which Diageo moved from Ontario bottling to Illinois within a few months last year. Stock already sitting in the United States awaiting distribution also remains legal to sell.

The consequence is close to the opposite of what this essay predicted. Large distillers with existing American bottling capacity can plausibly dodge both instruments at once and end up better positioned than they were before the proclamation was signed. Risen’s conclusion is worth quoting flat: “What looks like a draconian move may not result in very much Canadian whisky disappearing from American store shelves at all.” John Boscariol, a trade lawyer at McCarthy Tétrault, put the general principle more tartly: “When they describe these things, they describe them in very broad terms, and they have a political objective or agenda in saying, ‘We’re going to block Canadian products from the U.S. market.’ But when it comes down to it, as always, the devil is in the details.”

So the sentence under the photograph in Section II — a tariff raises the price, a ban empties the shelf — is, on the evidence now available, wrong about the second half. A ban with a bulk-bottling exemption does not empty the shelf. It relabels it.

Three honest caveats in both directions. Risen’s reporting concerns whisky and spirits specifically; whether an equivalent carve-out applies to beer, which was the actual subject of Section II, is not addressed, and the parallel is not automatic — though Molson Coors already brews some Canadian-branded product in American facilities, which suggests the beer position may be softer than this essay assumed too. Second, the exemption’s precise scope remains genuinely unclear: the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative did not respond to Risen’s request for clarification, and neither Diageo nor Heaven Hill would comment. Third, none of this touches the argument in Section III, which concerned potash, uranium, electricity and heavy crude, and which the intervening weeks have if anything strengthened.

There is a second correction owed, and it runs against Canada rather than Washington. Section II treated the provincial boycott as the ironic mirror of the American ban. Risen’s reporting complicates that too. The boycott did cut American alcohol exports to Canada by roughly 70 percent, costing U.S. distillers something like $150 million a year — but it also damaged Canadian importers, distributors and sales teams, and the expected boom in domestic Canadian spirits sales never arrived. Davin de Kergommeaux, author of Canadian Whisky: The Essential Portable Expert, was blunt about it: “In a way I think this backfired on us. I don’t think any of these politicians have thought this through.” A fair reading is that both sides reached for the same symbolic weapon and both drew some of their own blood.

Which leaves the interesting question, and it is one this series has now met in four separate forms. Why alcohol at all? Whiskey is normally under a third of one percent of the roughly $870 billion in goods and services crossing that border every year. The answer, per Risen, is that a shopper cannot see a softwood lumber tariff but can see an empty space on a shelf — and that whiskey is governed by strict rules of national origin, bourbon only American and Canadian whisky only Canadian, so that restricting it “means taking a shot at a country’s very sense of identity.” Cal Bricker of Spirits Canada gave the cynic’s version: “In terms of the whole economy, you can send a signal without doing that much damage to yourself.”

That is the pattern this series keeps running into, in a fourth costume. The Grok citation buried in a White House fact sheet. The lead claim with no source. The twenty-three-point gap between a President’s figure and his own trade agency’s. And now a ban announced as a lock on the door, with a side entrance written into the same document. In each case the announcement was built to be read at speed, and the details were built for whoever bothered to read them slowly.

I got this one wrong in the direction of taking the proclamation at its word. It is a useful reminder that in this administration the proclamation and the policy are frequently two different documents, and only one of them is meant to be read.
