The Workshop, OpenAI’s lobbying and policy headquarters, opened on May 14, 2026 — blocks from the White House.

On May 14, 2026, OpenAI held the grand opening of its first lobbying office in Washington, D.C. The company named it the Workshop. It occupies the Gallup Building — a former Masonic temple — blocks from the White House. It is part lab, part showroom, part event space. The inaugural programming includes training local high schoolers and senior citizens on how to use AI. After the community events, the company will begin hosting policy discussions with lawmakers and Trump administration officials.

Chris Lehane, OpenAI’s chief global affairs officer, described the Workshop to the New York Times using a line from the musical Hamilton: “It’ll be the room where it happens.”

He said this without irony. He may not have intended the full force of the reference. In the musical, “The Room Where It Happens” is Aaron Burr’s song — the anthem of a man who realizes that power is exercised not in public deliberation but in private negotiation, and who will do anything to be inside the room rather than outside it. The song’s lesson is not that being in the room is admirable. The lesson is that being in the room is what matters, and that the room is always closed.

OpenAI opened its room on the same day its lawyers delivered closing arguments in an Oakland courthouse, defending the company against the charge that it had betrayed a public promise to build AI for humanity’s benefit. The timing was not coordinated. It did not need to be. The room and the courtroom are the same story. One is the public reckoning for promises made. The other is where the next set of promises will be negotiated.

The Numbers

Meta, Nvidia, and Alphabet spent a combined $47.8 million on federal lobbying in 2025 — a 22% increase in a single year

The scale of AI lobbying in Washington has no precedent in the technology industry’s history with government.

A quarter of all 13,000 federal lobbyists in the United States are now involved in AI issues. In 2023, that figure was 11 percent. The shift happened in two years. It happened before Congress passed a single piece of comprehensive AI legislation.

Meta, Nvidia, and Alphabet spent a combined $47.8 million on federal lobbying in 2025, a 22 percent increase from the previous year. Meta and Alphabet were the top corporate spenders. OpenAI doubled its lobbying spend to $1 million in the first quarter of 2026 alone. Anthropic increased its Washington spending tenfold to $3 million last year and hired six lobbying firms, including the Trump-connected Ballard Partners to manage its Pentagon dispute.

These figures were compiled by Public Citizen and reported in congressional disclosures. They do not include state-level spending, industry-funded think tanks, conferences, media campaigns, or the cost of operating Washington offices like the Workshop. The disclosed numbers are the visible fraction of the influence operation. The visible fraction is already unprecedented.

Isabel Sunderland of Issue One, a nonprofit government accountability group, described it plainly: “We’re seeing an unprecedented deluge of money being poured by AI companies into lobbying in order to protect their bottom lines and their images at a time when Americans are very anxious about the technology.”

The anxiety is documented. In a recent NBC News poll, 57 percent of registered voters said AI’s risks outweighed its benefits. Thirty-four percent said the opposite. Parents’ groups have sounded alarms about children’s interactions with chatbots following cases in which teenagers took their own lives. Voters in communities near proposed data centers have expressed concern about electricity costs and environmental impact. The midterm elections are in November. The public is not asking for AI to be unleashed. The public is asking for AI to be governed.

The companies are in Washington to make sure they are part of that conversation. The question this essay asks is whether being part of the conversation is the same thing as having the conversation governed democratically.

The Two Stories

The Kang report identifies a division within the industry that maps precisely onto the division this series has been tracking since Part 1.

OpenAI, Meta, and Google have pushed for little to no regulation, arguing that restrictions would harm American competitiveness against China. Anthropic and others have supported new laws, pointing to the technology’s potential dangers. This is the divide between the commercial maximalists and the safety advocates — the divide that produced Anthropic’s founding, that produced the Musk v. Altman trial, that produced the Mythos standoff with the Pentagon.

But the divide is narrower than it appears. Both sides are in Washington. Both sides are spending. Both sides are opening offices and hosting events and meeting with the same lawmakers. The question is not whether AI should be regulated — the 20-to-80 percent swing among AI insiders has settled that. The question is what regulation looks like when the people writing it are also the people spending $47.8 million a year to shape it.

Lehane’s framing is instructive. He described AI as “a general-purpose technology on the scale of the wheel, printing press, combustion engine, electricity.” The analogy is designed to make regulation seem small — to make the people asking whether chatbots should be allowed to talk to children unsupervised seem like they are standing in front of the printing press asking Gutenberg for a permit.

But the printing press analogy contains its own rebuttal. The printing press produced the Reformation, the Counter-Reformation, the wars of religion, the pamphleteering that preceded every revolution in Europe for three centuries, and a censorship apparatus that lasted longer than most of the governments that built it. The printing press was a general-purpose technology. It was also a technology whose governance failures produced catastrophic consequences that lasted centuries. The argument that a technology is powerful is not an argument against governing it. It is the argument for governing it before the consequences are irreversible.

The companies know this. That is why they are in Washington. They are not there because they oppose governance. They are there because governance is coming, and they intend to be the ones shaping it.

The Alliance and the Counterweight

Not all of the lobbying runs in the same direction.

The Alliance for Secure AI, funded by Facebook co-founder Dustin Moskovitz’s philanthropy, is pushing for strong chatbot regulations to protect children, safety oversight of AI models, and opposition to federal pre-emption of state AI laws. Its chief executive, Brendan Steinhauser — a former Tea Party leader, which tells you something about the political realignment AI has produced — has lobbied Congress, met with Texas legislators, and appeared on media ranging from Steve Bannon’s podcast to NPR.

The existence of a counter-lobbying operation is a sign of democratic health. The question is scale. Moskovitz’s philanthropy is funding one organization. Meta, Nvidia, and Alphabet are spending $47.8 million a year on federal lobbying alone. Anthropic has hired six firms. OpenAI has a showroom in a former Masonic temple. The Alliance for Secure AI is outspent by orders of magnitude, and its chief executive’s media strategy — appearing everywhere from Bannon to NPR — is the strategy of an organization that cannot buy influence and must therefore earn attention.

States have introduced dozens of AI bills in 2026. None of this adds up to comprehensive federal AI legislation.

The vacuum remains.

The Canadian Observation

From the outside — which is where this series has always been written — the Washington AI lobbying surge looks like a familiar pattern in an unfamiliar context.

Democracies have faced industry lobbying before. Tobacco. Pharmaceuticals. Banking. Oil. In each case, the pattern is the same: the industry grows faster than the regulatory framework, the industry funds the conversations about how to regulate it, the regulation that emerges reflects the industry’s preferences more than the public’s concerns, and the democratic correction — when it comes — arrives after the damage is done.

AI is following the pattern. The difference is speed. Tobacco took decades to regulate. Banking deregulation took a generation to produce its crisis. AI has gone from a research curiosity to an $852 billion company in three years, and the lobbying infrastructure has scaled to match. A quarter of all federal lobbyists in two years. The room is being built faster than the democratic institutions that are supposed to govern what happens inside it.

The 57 percent of voters who say AI’s risks outweigh its benefits do not have a showroom blocks from the White House. They do not have six lobbying firms. They do not have $47.8 million in annual federal lobbying spend. They have their vote in November, and whatever their state legislators manage to pass before the federal pre-emption bills — which the industry is also lobbying for — take effect.

Asimov understood this. His Three Laws were not negotiated between the robots and the government. They were imposed before the robots were built. The laws came first. The technology came second. The entire premise of the Three Laws was that you do not let the technology into the room where the laws are written.

The Workshop opened on May 14. The room where it happens is a former Masonic temple, blocks from the White House, operated by a company currently defending itself in federal court against the charge that it broke its founding promise to humanity. As this essay goes to press, those lobbying efforts have produced a first result — and it is worth examining carefully what the room has produced.

Part lab, part showroom, part the room where it happens.

What the Room Produced

On June 2, 2026, President Trump signed an executive order asking technology companies to give the government a 30-day window to review new AI models before public release. The order also creates an AI “cybersecurity clearinghouse” at the Treasury Department. The White House framed it as a shift to a more hands-on stance. “Advanced AI capabilities make our nation stronger,” the order reads, “but also introduce new national security considerations that require coordinated action.”

The reality is more instructive than the framing. Daniel Remler, an AI expert at the Center for a New American Security, told The Atlantic that the order “effectively formalizes what has already been happening between the U.S. government and the leading AI companies.” The major AI firms already had voluntary agreements in place to share models with government safety testers. The order codifies a practice that was already occurring. The preamble of the order itself celebrates that Trump “unleashed tremendous technological growth” by removing Biden-era constraints — and then reproduces the core of those same constraints as its operative provisions.

The process that produced this document deserves the record. As the New York Times reported, Trump was scheduled to sign a more substantial order — one that would have created a window of up to 90 days — just two weeks earlier. On the morning of the signing, Trump began making calls: to Mark Zuckerberg, to Marc Andreessen, and most consequentially to David Sacks, the former White House AI czar. Sacks told him directly: don’t sign this. Within hours, Trump cancelled. The signing was dead.

What revived it was Susie Wiles, the White House chief of staff, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent — both of whom worried that a hands-off administration would be blamed if Mythos or a successor model were used to attack a hospital or take down the power grid. They kept pushing. A confidential Monday meeting at the White House brought together Sacks, Bessent, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, and other principals. The result: the window was cut from 90 days to 30, and a line was added to the order explicitly forbidding “a mandatory government licensing, preclearance, or permitting process.” That language — the specific prohibition on the one mechanism that would have given governance real teeth — was Sacks’s price for blessing the deal. Sacks then declared the provisions he had neutered a “game changer” on X. The room produced exactly what it was designed to produce: a governance document with the governance removed.

There is one further detail that deserves the room it requires. The executive order tasks the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency — CISA — with a central role in implementation. CISA is the federal agency created to protect the nation against hackers. It is also one of the agencies the Trump administration has gutted. The agency tasked with implementing the AI cybersecurity order is the agency that the order’s own administration has systematically weakened. The room where it happens has produced a governance document that depends for its execution on an institution it has been dismantling.

The gap between a 30-day voluntary review and a comprehensive democratic governance framework is the Asimov Gap, stated as the distance between what the public needs and what the room produces.

The gap is the room.
