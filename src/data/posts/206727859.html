Part XIII followed a general who crowned himself out of the wreckage of a revolution. This part turns to the monarchy that revolution destroyed, and to a question Part XIII could only gesture at: how the French crown, which in the seventeenth century was the most powerful and most admired throne in Europe, arrived by 1789 at a position so brittle that a single fiscal crisis could bring the entire structure down. The answer runs through one magnificent and ultimately ruinous idea, that a king’s authority answered to God alone, and that no parliament, no court, no estate, and no subject had any standing to limit it. France built that idea into the most imposing royal machinery in European history. It then spent a century and a half discovering that a throne designed never to bend has no way of bending when it finally must.

The idea found its fullest expression in Louis XIV, who inherited the throne in 1643 at the age of four and reigned for seventy-two years, the longest reign of any European monarch in recorded history. His childhood gave him a lasting lesson in what happens when a crown’s authority is shared. Between 1648 and 1653, a series of revolts known collectively as the Fronde, led first by the Parlement of Paris, France’s senior royal court, and later by great nobles resentful of the crown’s ministers, twice drove the young king and his mother out of Paris, on one occasion forcing them to flee the capital in the middle of the night. Louis never forgot the humiliation. When his chief minister, Cardinal Mazarin, died in 1661, the twenty-two-year-old king informed his startled council that he would henceforth govern without a chief minister at all, taking personal charge of the state in a manner no French king had attempted in living memory.

The theory behind this personal rule was supplied most eloquently by Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet, the bishop and court preacher who served as tutor to Louis’s son, and who argued in a treatise drawn explicitly from scripture that royal authority was sacred, paternal, and absolute, that the king was accountable to God alone, and that rebellion against him was rebellion against God’s own order. It is the same divine-right theology this series traced through Charles I in Part VIII, and Louis’s reign was barely five years old when Charles was executed across the Channel in 1649, a coincidence that was not lost on the French court, which drew from the English example the lesson that a king who allowed a parliament any real purchase on his authority was a king in mortal danger. The line most often attributed to Louis, L’État, c’est moi, I am the state, is almost certainly apocryphal, but it has survived for three centuries because it described his actual system of government with uncomfortable accuracy.

Versailles was that system made into architecture. Beginning in the 1660s, Louis transformed his father’s modest hunting lodge southwest of Paris into the largest palace in Europe, and in 1682 moved the court and the machinery of government there permanently. The move was political rather than merely aesthetic. By requiring France’s great nobles, the same class that had led the Fronde against him, to live at Versailles in close attendance on the king, competing for the privilege of handing him his shirt at his morning rising or holding a candle at his evening retirement, Louis converted a dangerous and independent aristocracy with regional power bases into a gilded court absorbed in ceremony, gossip, and debt, too busy seeking the king’s favor to organize against him. It was an extraordinarily effective short-term solution to the problem of noble power, similar in purpose to the Ottoman Janissary system this series will examine in Part XXIII, and it carried a cost that would only become fully visible a century later: a nobility stripped of real governing responsibility kept every one of its tax privileges, and a crown that had concentrated all authority in itself had also concentrated all blame.

Louis’s reign ended the way Aurangzeb’s had ended in Part IX, at the peak of territorial power and at the edge of exhaustion. In 1685 he revoked the Edict of Nantes, the 1598 settlement that had granted France’s Protestant Huguenot minority a measure of religious toleration, and somewhere between one hundred and fifty thousand and two hundred thousand Huguenots, many of them skilled artisans and merchants, fled to England, the Netherlands, Prussia, and the Americas, taking their skills and their capital with them. And he fought almost continually, in a succession of wars culminating in the War of the Spanish Succession, which ended in 1714, a year before his death, leaving the treasury deeply indebted and the population exhausted by taxation and famine. On his deathbed, Louis is reported to have told his five-year-old great-grandson and heir that he had loved war too much, and urged the boy not to imitate him. Whether or not the words were exactly his, the warning went unheeded.

Louis XV, reigning from 1715 to 1774, inherited the full apparatus of absolutism and considerably less of his great-grandfather’s energy for operating it. His reign’s most consequential defeat will be familiar to any Canadian reader: the Seven Years’ War, which ended with the Treaty of Paris in 1763 and the surrender of New France to Britain, costing France nearly its entire North American empire and adding still further to a royal debt that no one in the government had a politically viable plan to repay. The remark attributed to Louis XV or to his mistress, Madame de Pompadour, après moi, le déluge, after me, the flood, may be as apocryphal as his great-grandfather’s, but it captured with unsettling precision a court that could see the fiscal crisis approaching and chose, reign after reign, to leave it for the throne’s next occupant. His grandson, Louis XVI, who acceded in 1774 at the age of nineteen, four years after his marriage to the Habsburg archduchess Marie Antoinette, inherited that flood in full.

France’s crisis had been building for decades before 1789, and it’s worth tracing the specific financial mechanics that finally forced the issue, because they show a monarchy trapped by exactly the kind of structural rigidity this series’ earlier parts have identified as fatal. The French monarchy’s tax system fell disproportionately on commoners while the nobility and clergy, who together owned something close to half the country’s land, retained extensive traditional exemptions from direct taxation, an arrangement that had made a certain rough sense under the feudal logic examined in Part V but had become an increasingly unsustainable drag on royal finances as France’s population and administrative costs grew. The treasury was further drained by a series of expensive eighteenth-century wars, most consequentially France’s own costly financial support for the American Revolution against Britain, a policy decision that succeeded brilliantly in weakening France’s imperial rival but left the crown burdened with debt it had no politically viable way to repay given the existing tax exemptions protecting its wealthiest subjects from contributing their proportional share. By the 1780s, a succession of finance ministers, including Jacques Necker and Charles Alexandre de Calonne, had exhausted every available maneuver short of confronting the nobility’s tax privileges directly, and Louis XVI, facing outright bankruptcy, was finally forced to convene the Estates-General in 1789, a consultative body representing the clergy, the nobility, and the common Third Estate, which had not been called into session at all since 1614, a hundred and seventy-five years earlier.

A pamphlet published earlier that year by the clergyman Emmanuel Joseph Sieyès, titled What Is the Third Estate, captured the growing sense of grievance in terms that would echo through the following months: Sieyès argued that the Third Estate, comprising the overwhelming majority of the French population, was in fact everything the nation actually consisted of, while the privileged orders contributed nothing productive and yet controlled every lever of political power, a rhetorical framing that reframed the entire crisis from a dispute over specific tax exemptions into a far more fundamental question about who the nation itself actually was and on whose behalf it ought to be governed. The Estates-General’s traditional voting structure, in which each of the three estates cast a single collective vote regardless of how many individuals it actually represented, guaranteed that the privileged clergy and nobility could always outvote the considerably more populous Third Estate on any matter touching their own privileges, a structural imbalance the Third Estate’s representatives recognized almost immediately as designed to prevent exactly the kind of tax reform the crisis actually required. In June of 1789, the Third Estate’s delegates, joined by sympathetic clergy and nobles, declared themselves the National Assembly, asserting that they alone represented the genuine will of the French nation, and when locked out of their usual meeting hall by royal guards on the twentieth of June, they gathered instead in a nearby indoor tennis court and swore what became known as the Tennis Court Oath, pledging not to disband until France had a written constitution limiting royal power.

Louis’s response to this challenge proved catastrophically indecisive, wavering between grudging accommodation of the Assembly’s demands and periodic attempts to reassert royal authority by force, a pattern that satisfied neither his own conservative supporters nor the increasingly radicalized revolutionary leadership, and convinced a growing share of politically engaged French society that the monarchy itself, rather than merely its specific policies, was the underlying obstacle to reform. The storming of the Bastille fortress on the fourteenth of July, 1789, seized by a Parisian crowd searching for weapons and gunpowder, became an instant and enduring symbol of popular resistance to royal authority even though the fortress itself held only seven prisoners at the time. The following month, the National Assembly abolished the entire feudal privilege system in a single dramatic overnight session on the fourth of August, and issued the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, a document explicitly asserting principles of popular sovereignty and legal equality that left essentially no theoretical room remaining for the kind of divine-right monarchy Bossuet had preached to Louis XIV’s court barely a century earlier. In October, a crowd of Parisian women, angered by bread shortages and rumors of royal excess, marched to Versailles itself and forced the royal family’s relocation to Paris, a return to the capital that reversed, in a single day, the retreat from Paris that Louis XIV had engineered after the Fronde, and placed Louis XVI under a form of close and increasingly hostile public supervision that stripped away much of his remaining independent authority.

Louis’s own attempted flight from Paris in June of 1791, seeking to escape to the eastern border and rally royalist forces and foreign support against the revolution, ended in his recognition and capture at the town of Varennes, and this failed escape did more damage to his remaining legitimacy than perhaps any single event preceding it, since it revealed unambiguously that Louis himself regarded the revolutionary settlement as illegitimate and was actively working to overturn it by force, destroying whatever remaining trust existed between the king and the increasingly radical revolutionary government. Following the abolition of the monarchy entirely in September of 1792, Louis was tried and convicted of treason by the National Convention and executed by guillotine in the Place de la Révolution on the twenty-first of January, 1793, followed nine months later, in October, by his queen, Marie Antoinette, this series’ own reminder from Part V of how a marriage arranged for one dynasty’s advantage could become a fatal liability once the crown it married into had lost its underlying legitimacy entirely.

The revolutionary government did not stop with the two executions themselves. The Reign of Terror that followed through 1793 and 1794, overseen by the Committee of Public Safety under Maximilien Robespierre, sent thousands of additional aristocrats, clergy, and eventually revolutionaries themselves to the guillotine on often flimsy charges of counter-revolutionary sympathy, consuming the revolution’s own leadership before Robespierre himself was executed in July of 1794 in the reaction that followed his own excesses. The couple’s surviving son, the young Louis-Charles, technically Louis XVII to royalists who never accepted the republic’s legitimacy, died in prison in 1795 at the age of ten under circumstances that remained genuinely disputed for two centuries. His disappearance produced France’s own small crop of pretenders, the most persistent of them a Prussian clockmaker named Karl Wilhelm Naundorff, who spent decades insisting he was the lost dauphin smuggled out of prison and whose gravestone in the Dutch town of Delft still names him as Louis XVII, until DNA testing on the boy’s preserved heart finally confirmed in 2000 that Louis-Charles had in fact died in captivity.

France’s political trajectory in the decades following Louis’s execution shows just how unsettled the question of what should replace monarchy actually remained, even after the guillotine had answered the question of the existing king so decisively. The republic that executed Louis gave way, as this series traced in Part XIII, to Napoleon’s self-made empire by 1804, which collapsed in 1815, at which point the victorious European powers, determined to restore a stable and legitimate order to the continent, reinstalled the Bourbon line Louis himself had belonged to, placing his younger brother on the throne as Louis XVIII and then, after his death, another brother as Charles X. This restored monarchy proved no more durable than the original had been, toppled by a further popular uprising in 1830 that replaced Charles X with a more liberal cousin, Louis Philippe, reigning as a considerably more constrained constitutional monarch under the title King of the French rather than King of France, a small but symbolically loaded distinction meant to signal that his authority derived from the nation rather than from divine hereditary right. Even this more accommodating arrangement lasted only until 1848, when another revolution swept Europe and established France’s Second Republic, which itself gave way to Napoleon’s nephew’s Second Empire within a few years, meaning that between 1789 and the permanent establishment of France’s Third Republic in 1870, the country cycled through two kingdoms, two empires, and two republics in less than a century, a demonstration that removing one monarch by force settles remarkably little about what durable alternative should actually take his place.

What France’s case demonstrates, set against the absolutist ambition with which this part began, is the hidden cost of a throne engineered never to bend. Louis XIV built a monarchy that had deliberately hollowed out every institution capable of sharing its authority: the Estates-General went uncalled for a hundred and seventy-five years, the nobility was domesticated at Versailles, the Parlements were periodically humbled, and the Protestant minority was driven abroad. The result was magnificent for as long as the crown could carry the load alone, and catastrophic the moment it could not, because there was no intermediate body left with the standing and the habit to negotiate a compromise before the crisis became a revolution. When Louis XVI finally summoned the Estates-General in 1789, he was reaching for a consultative tool his own dynasty had spent nearly two centuries allowing to rust, and it broke in his hands.

Part XV turns east, to a monarchy that absorbed France’s lesson in reverse. Russia’s tsars watched the French crown fall and concluded that the answer was more autocracy, not less, and for well over another century they were, by their own lights, proven right, until the bill came due in a single night in a basement in Yekaterinburg.
