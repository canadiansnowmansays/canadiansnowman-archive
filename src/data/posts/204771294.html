For most of the time I have been writing these essays — not just in this series, but across the whole sprawling project these pages have become — the prevailing wind has blown one way. America does a thing, and Canada breathes it in. The tariffs, the politics, the cultural exports, the late-night anxieties about the elephant we share a bed with — the metaphor of my entire body of work has been a smaller country downwind of a larger one, watching what blows across the border and bracing for it.

So I want to spend an essay on the one phenomenon where the wind, quite literally, reverses. Where it is our emissions, our burning forests, our mismanaged or unmanageable North, that crosses the border and settles into American lungs. Because nothing clarifies a relationship like role reversal, and nothing clarifies climate change quite like the moment your own country becomes the thing fouling someone else’s air.

You will remember June of 2023, even if you have tried to forget it. That was the month the smoke from Canadian wildfires poured south and turned the New York skyline a color for which we did not previously have a word — a sort of apocalyptic amber, the sun reduced to a dull coin, the air thick enough to taste. On June 7 of that year, New York City had, by one widely cited measure, the worst air quality of any major city on the planet, with an index reading above 350 — more than double the level recorded that day in Dubai. Photographs of a sepia Manhattan went around the world. For a few days the most powerful city in the most powerful country had been handed, by the prevailing winds, a small involuntary lesson in atmospheric humility, and the return address on the lesson was Canada.

It was not a one-time event, which is the whole point. The summer of 2025 brought it back, and worse in its persistence if not its single-day peak. By late July, more than 550 wildfires were burning across Canada, many of them in Manitoba and Saskatchewan and northern Ontario, with something on the order of fifteen million acres already scorched for the season. The smoke moved on the same clockwise circulation that always carries it, down into New York and the Hudson Valley and New England, prompting a now-familiar rhythm of air quality advisories. By early August, New York found itself once again among the ten most polluted major cities in the world, ranked by the same drifting Canadian haze. The governor’s office issued the kind of statement that has become a summer ritual: check the air quality index, be “air quality aware,” keep the children and the elderly and the asthmatic indoors. New York State now runs a program to give free air conditioners to eligible residents, partly so that the windows can stay shut against the smoke. The houses, once again, were not built for this.

For a long time it was possible to treat all this as a nuisance — a ruined weekend, a hazy skyline, an inconvenience with good sunsets. It is not a nuisance. In September of 2025 a team of international researchers published a study in Nature attempting, for the first time at this scale, to count the dead. Their estimate for the smoke from Canada’s record-breaking 2023 fire season alone: roughly 82,100 premature deaths worldwide, including some 33,000 in the United States and 8,300 in Canada. These are not people who burned. These are people whose hearts and lungs, already burdened, were pushed past their limits by months of breathing fine particulate matter — the microscopic soot, smaller than a human cell, that slips past every defense the body has and lodges in the blood. The same study found that 354 million people across North America and Europe lived through at least one “Canada smoke day” in 2023 — a day when our fires were the dominant poison in their air. Three hundred and fifty-four million people. That is a number larger than the population of the United States, breathing, for at least one day, the exhalation of a burning Canada.

And here is a fact that ought to change how we think about wildfire smoke, because it upends the reassuring assumption that smoke is just smoke, a temporary irritant that clears when the wind shifts. The particulate matter in wildfire smoke appears to be more dangerous, particle for particle, than the ordinary pollution that pours from tailpipes and smokestacks. When you burn a living forest — the wood, the resins, the soil, and increasingly the houses and cars and plastics of the towns the fire consumes — you produce particles that are on average smaller than the soot of ordinary combustion, and freighted with a heavier load of the oxidative, pro-inflammatory compounds that do the body’s cells the most harm. Smaller particles penetrate deeper. The smallest of them, the so-called ultrafine particles, are tiny enough to pass from the lungs directly into the bloodstream, and there is emerging evidence that some can travel from the nose along the olfactory nerve directly into the brain, bypassing the body’s defenses entirely. Researchers are now investigating links between wildfire-smoke exposure and outcomes far beyond the obvious asthma attack and heart attack — to cardiovascular disease, to adverse birth outcomes, and, in studies still being refined and debated, to the long slow degradations of the aging brain. The science here is young and I will not overstate it; some of the early findings have been revised as the data were re-examined, which is exactly how honest science is supposed to work. But the direction of the evidence is not comforting. The smoke is not merely unpleasant. It may be quietly reaching into organs we do not associate with breathing at all, in millions of people who thought they had simply endured a hazy week.

This matters enormously for the argument of this series, because it means the true cost of a burning Canada cannot be tallied in the days the sky was orange or even in the funerals held that summer. A portion of the harm is deferred — lodged in a coronary artery, seeded in a developing fetus, perhaps set slowly working in a brain that will not show the consequence for a decade or two. The 82,100 deaths counted in that single season are the visible, immediate toll. The full reckoning, like so much else in this series, has been quietly loaded onto a future that has not yet arrived to collect it.

Here is what I find genuinely clarifying about the smoke, beyond its grim capacity to kill at a distance. It collapses, completely and visibly, a fiction that both of our countries find convenient: the fiction that emissions and consequences can be kept in separate jurisdictions. The smoke does not clear customs. It does not recognize the 49th parallel, which is, viewed from the altitude at which smoke travels, an entirely imaginary line that humans take very seriously and the atmosphere does not take at all. Carbon released in Fort McMurray warms a planet that does not file the carbon under “Canada.” A forest that burns in the Northwest Territories sends its particulates into a child’s lungs in Buffalo, and into the bloodstream of a pensioner in Ohio, and across the ocean into the air of a city in western Europe, and no amount of national sentiment on either side changes the chemistry.

This is, of course, the entire logic of climate change in miniature, which is why the smoke is such a useful teacher. The whole problem is that the thing is global and the responses are national. Every country emits into a common sky and then negotiates, separately and jealously, about who should stop. The atmosphere is the one true commons — a single shared volume of air over the whole planet, into which everyone exhales and from which everyone breathes — and we have built a political order that pretends it can be partitioned, that my smoke is my business and your smoke is yours. The wildfire smoke just makes the absurdity of that arrangement visible for a few days each summer, painting the sky a color that says, plainly, there is only one atmosphere, and you are all standing under it.

And it puts Canadians, specifically, in an uncomfortable and clarifying position. We are accustomed, in our climate conversations, to a certain comfortable victimhood — the melting Arctic, the warming twice as fast, the foundation going, all of which I wrote about in the last essay and all of which is true. But the smoke is the other ledger. The smoke is us as the source, not the victim. Those fires are larger and more frequent because the country is hotter and drier than it used to be — Canada warming at twice the global rate, the Arctic nearly three times, the boreal forest turning from carbon sink to carbon torch. We are, in the smoke, both the burned and the burning. Trace the chain: the North melts because the world warms; the forests dry and burn because the North melts and the summers lengthen; the smoke crosses the border because the forests burn; and somewhere in Manhattan a man who has never thought about Saskatchewan in his life coughs, and checks an app, and curses a country he probably could not find on a map. He is, without knowing it, breathing the consequences of a warming that we did our share to cause and now cannot control.

I do not raise any of this to flagellate my own country, of which I remain stubbornly fond. I raise it because the smoke is the one part of this story where the usual Canadian posture — earnest, aggrieved, downwind — does not survive contact with the facts. When it comes to wildfire smoke, we are upwind. We are the larger weather, for once, and someone else is bracing for what crosses the border. It is a strange place for a Canadian to stand, and I think it is a healthy one, because it forecloses the easy move of treating climate change as a thing that is done to us by others.

There is a version of climate politics, on both sides of the border, that runs entirely on grievance — the sense that someone else’s emissions are the problem, that the real culprits are over there, that one’s own country is a relatively innocent party caught in a global crossfire. China is the favorite villain in this telling, and I noticed, when the smoke was at its worst, how quickly some American commentators reached for Canada as the new culprit — as though our forests were burning out of negligence, as though a hotter, drier continent were a Canadian policy choice. The temptation to find a foreigner to blame is bipartisan and bottomless. But the smoke is the rebuke to all such tidy moral geography. The smoke says: you, too, are upwind of someone. Your country, too, is the larger weather in some direction you prefer not to look. The American who blames Canada for the smoke is breathing, in the same lungful, the carbon his own country has poured into the sky for a century and a half — the carbon that helped dry the forests that are now burning. There is no seat at this table reserved for the merely aggrieved. We are all, in some direction, the wind.

I will get to China later in the series, because the China argument deserves an honest hearing rather than a slogan, and because the question of who owes what to whom — historically, per capita, at the point of production versus the point of consumption — is genuinely hard and genuinely important. But the smoke settles the moral preliminaries. Before we argue about who should cut what, the smoke reminds us that the sky keeps no borders, that every nation is both a polluter and a victim of pollution, and that the posture of pure innocence is available to no one who breathes.

Dior hates the smoke days worse than the heat days, if it is possible to rank a husky’s grievances. On the bad-air mornings she will not go further than the end of the block, and she gives me a look on the way back that I have come to read as a question: you did this? It is, I think, the correct question, addressed to the correct species, and I have not yet found an answer for her that I am proud of. She is, after all, breathing the same smoke as the pensioner in Ohio, drifting down from the same burning North her ancestors were built for, and she has even less idea than he does why the air has turned against her.

Next in the series we turn from the symptoms to the system — to the question of who profits from the delay, and to a framework I have been sharpening in these pages for a year now. After that, a long and unsparing look at the most powerful man on the continent and his insistence that the road forward runs through a coal mine. The smoke, it turns out, has a great deal of company on the list of things we have chosen not to look at directly.

Degrees

82,100 — the estimated number of premature deaths worldwide attributed, in a 2025 study published in Nature, to the smoke from Canada’s 2023 wildfire season alone. Some 33,000 of them in the United States; 8,300 in Canada. The figure for June 7, 2023 — the day Manhattan turned amber — was 350 on the air quality index, the number everyone remembers. Postscript — July 15, 2026

I wrote the sentence about Manhattan losing its skyline to Canadian smoke as a piece of recent history, something that happened to someone else’s city while I watched from the comfortable remove of an essayist across a border. This week the essay came home. Standing on my own balcony on July 15, I could not find the downtown core — not obscured, not hazy, simply gone, the way a word disappears when you stare at it too long. As the New York Times has reported, a heat dome parked over the middle of the continent has been doing double duty: baking tens of millions of people from the Dakotas to New England under major or extreme heat warnings, and at the same time trapping smoke from fires in Canada and the Great Lakes low enough to the ground that it stops behaving like weather and starts behaving like a wall. Toronto spent Wednesday under an orange sky and, by Environment Canada’s own ten-point scale, a flat 10 for risk to air quality — the worst number the scale has. New York was bracing for the same air I couldn’t see through, forecasters warning of a night that could get worse before the wind changes.

The fires doing the damage this time are not even the ones I was writing about a week ago. At least seventeen are burning across Minnesota and into Canada, several thousand acres apiece, none of them contained as of this writing, two of the largest — the Bear Trap and the Thumb — having crossed the border the same way the smoke does: without paperwork, without asking. It is a small, tidy irony that the essay’s whole argument was the atmosphere’s indifference to the 49th parallel, and the illustration arrived, unbidden, in time for the postscript.

Dior would not go further than the driveway on Wednesday. She has, I think, worked out that the bad-air days and the bad-heat days are cousins, and that neither one improves with argument. I stood with her at the end of the block, looking in the direction downtown is supposed to be, and found I had nothing to add to the essay that the missing skyline hadn’t already said better.
