The most important legal document in Taiwan’s contemporary situation is not the Taiwan Relations Act of 1979, though that is what American politicians invoke most often. It is not the Cairo Declaration of 1943, though Beijing cites that constantly. It is not even the Shanghai Communiqué of 1972, though that is where the famous American “acknowledgment” of the one-China position was first articulated in language so carefully hedged that it has supported three contradictory interpretations simultaneously for more than fifty years.

The most important document is Article 2(b) of the San Francisco Peace Treaty of 1951, which reads, in its entirety: “Japan renounces all right, title and claim to Formosa and the Pescadores.”

That is all it says. Japan renounces. No recipient is named. No transfer of sovereignty is specified. The island is put down and no one is designated to pick it up. This was not an oversight. It was, as one legal scholar has put it with admirable precision, a deliberate ambiguity under Cold War politics — and it became the starting point for every subsequent argument about Taiwan’s legal status.

Understanding why the ambiguity was deliberate requires understanding what the world looked like in September 1951, when the San Francisco conference convened.

Japan surrendered on August 15, 1945. In the immediate aftermath, the Allied powers had certain understandings about what would happen to the territories Japan had held. The Cairo Declaration of 1943, issued jointly by the United States, Britain, and the Republic of China, had stated that “all the territories Japan has stolen from the Chinese, such as Manchuria, Formosa, and Pescadores, shall be restored to the Republic of China.” The Potsdam Proclamation of 1945 had reaffirmed this. When the Japanese Governor-General of Taiwan surrendered in Taipei in October 1945, he surrendered to representatives of the Republic of China government under Chiang Kai-shek, and the ROC began administering the island.

For a brief moment, the path seemed clear. Taiwan would go to China.

Then history intervened, as history has a habit of doing in this part of the world.

The Chinese Civil War, which had been suspended during the Japanese occupation, resumed immediately after the Japanese surrender. By 1949, Mao Zedong’s Communist forces had won it. The Republic of China government under Chiang Kai-shek fled to Taiwan with roughly two million mainland Chinese — soldiers, officials, intellectuals, and their families — and declared that it remained the legitimate government of all of China, that the mainland would be reconquered, and that the Communist usurpers in Beijing were a temporary anomaly. The People’s Republic of China was proclaimed on the mainland on October 1, 1949.

There were now two governments claiming to be the government of China, and Taiwan was being administered by the one that had just lost a civil war and controlled no territory on the mainland whatsoever.

When the San Francisco Peace Conference convened in 1951 to formally end the Second World War with Japan, it faced an immediate and apparently insoluble problem: which Chinese government to invite. The United States recognised the ROC. The United Kingdom had recognised the PRC in 1950 and was not about to reverse that. Neither government was invited. Japan was required to sign a separate bilateral peace treaty with the ROC in Taipei in 1952 — but that treaty also conspicuously declined to specify that sovereignty over Taiwan was being transferred to the ROC. It merely noted, as the San Francisco treaty had, that Japan renounced its claims.

As a British minister of state noted at the time, and was quoted in subsequent legal proceedings: “Japan has renounced all sovereignty and title to Formosa, but the question of sovereignty remains in abeyance.”

The void was built in. The ambiguity was the policy.

Why? Because in 1951, with the Korean War underway and the Cold War hardening into its most dangerous early configurations, no major power could agree on who legitimately spoke for China, and making a definitive legal determination about Taiwan’s sovereignty would require taking a position on that question that someone important would find intolerable. The Americans could not say Taiwan belonged to the PRC. The British could not say it belonged to the ROC. The solution, reached by exhausted diplomats who had a larger peace treaty to finish, was to say nothing definitive and hope the problem resolved itself.

It did not resolve itself. It calcified.

Republic of China representatives at the United Nations, c. 1950. The ROC held China's Security Council seat until 1971, representing the world's most populous nation from an island it had controlled for five years after losing a civil war. This was only possible because of American political support — and it could not last.

For twenty years after 1951, the ROC sat in China’s seat at the United Nations, claiming to represent the world’s most populous nation from an island it had administered for a few years before losing the mainland entirely. This position was simultaneously absurd and, given American support, tenable. Then Nixon went to China in February 1972, and everything shifted.

The Shanghai Communiqué, issued at the end of Nixon’s visit, is a document of extraordinary verbal precision deployed in the service of maximum ambiguity. On the Taiwan question, it reads: “The United States acknowledges that all Chinese on either side of the Taiwan Strait maintain there is but one China and that Taiwan is a part of China. The United States Government does not challenge that position.”

Henry Kissinger meets Mao Zedong in Beijing, 1975. Kissinger, who drafted the Shanghai Communiqué's Taiwan language in 1972, understood precisely what the phrase "does not challenge" meant — and what it did not mean. The distinction has governed American Taiwan policy for more than half a century.

Parse that carefully. The United States acknowledges — not accepts, not endorses, not agrees — that Chinese people on both sides of the strait hold a view. The United States does not challenge that view. Does not challenge is not the same as accepts. It is the diplomatic equivalent of a nod, the kind of nod you give when you understand what someone has said without committing to believe it. Henry Kissinger, who drafted the communiqué, understood exactly what he was doing. He was maintaining the ambiguity that the San Francisco treaty had built in, now in updated language designed to serve the new strategic reality of US-China rapprochement without technically abandoning the ROC.

President Jimmy Carter signs the Taiwan Relations Act, April 10, 1979. The Act committed the United States to providing Taiwan with defensive arms without formally recognising the island as a sovereign state — a commitment deliberately worded to sustain the ambiguity that every administration since has inherited.

In 1979, the United States formally switched diplomatic recognition from Taipei to Beijing. The Taiwan Relations Act, passed by Congress in the same year, established the framework under which the US has operated ever since: it committed to selling Taiwan defensive arms and maintaining the capacity to resist any forcible change to Taiwan’s status, without formally recognising Taiwan as a sovereign state or explicitly committing to its defence.

Strategic ambiguity, the doctrine that emerged from this architecture, holds that the United States deliberately refuses to say whether it would militarily defend Taiwan in the event of a Chinese attack. The theory is that this uncertainty deters both Beijing — which cannot be confident that the US would stay out — and Taipei — which cannot be confident that the US would intervene, and therefore cannot feel free to declare independence without calculating the consequences. Both sides are kept uncertain. The uncertainty keeps the peace.

It is an elegant theory. It has kept the peace for forty-seven years. It has also meant that Taiwan has spent forty-seven years in legal limbo, its status deliberately unresolved, its people’s political future held hostage to a calculated ambiguity that was designed to serve American strategic interests rather than Taiwanese ones.

Legal scholars debate Taiwan’s status with an intensity that would be impressive if the stakes were not so high. The independence camp argues that because Japan renounced sovereignty without specifying a recipient, and because the ROC’s subsequent administration of Taiwan was never legally recognised as a transfer of sovereignty, Taiwan’s status remains genuinely undetermined — and that undetermined status creates the space for self-determination, for the Taiwanese people to choose their own future free from the legal shadow of the 1895 cession. The San Francisco treaty, on this reading, did not give Taiwan to China in 1952. It left Taiwan in a legal void, and voids can be filled by the people living in them.

Beijing’s position is that this is sophistry. The Cairo Declaration and the Potsdam Proclamation constitute a commitment by the Allied powers to return Taiwan to China. Japan accepted those terms in its surrender. The question was settled in 1945, before the San Francisco treaty was even drafted. The treaty’s omission of a named recipient does not create a new legal status; it simply reflects the political dysfunction of the Cold War period. China’s sovereignty over Taiwan was established by the wartime agreements and has never legitimately been in question.

Both arguments have real legal weight. Neither is obviously correct. The world’s leading international law scholars have been unable to reach consensus for seven decades. This is not because the scholars are incompetent. It is because the ambiguity was engineered by people who understood exactly what they were doing, and who left no clean answer because a clean answer would have required taking sides in a conflict they could not resolve.

What the deliberate void has produced, over seventy years, is a Taiwan that is functionally a state — with its own government, military, currency, judicial system, foreign relations conducted through unofficial channels, and a democratic political culture that has undergone three peaceful transfers of power between rival parties — that legally does not quite exist.

Taiwan issues passports that most of the world accepts. It competes in the Olympics under the name “Chinese Taipei.” Its airlines land at international airports. Its companies manufacture the chips that run the global economy. Its citizens travel the world. And in international law, the status of the territory they call home remains, in the careful phrase of the British minister who observed the San Francisco conference in 1951, in abeyance.

It is the most successful non-country in the history of international relations. It has outlasted the strategic calculations that were supposed to resolve it. It has built a democracy in the space that the deliberate void created. And it now faces a moment when the power that engineered the ambiguity may be preparing to trade it away, in the name of a trade deal, to a leader who has said, of the most dangerous territorial question on earth: “That’s up to him.”

The architects of San Francisco 1951 built a void where an answer should have been. They believed the problem would resolve itself in time, or that time would make the question moot, or that the strategic logic of the Cold War would eventually produce a settlement that everyone could live with.

They were wrong. The void remains. Taiwan lives in it.
