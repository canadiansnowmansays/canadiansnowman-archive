Shanghai’s Pudong financial district. In 1945, when Hugh Keenleyside helped build the post-war order, China was emerging from war and poverty. Today it is the world’s largest economy by purchasing power parity and the central force shaping the century ahead.

Predictions about the distant future are the historian’s most dangerous temptation and the analyst’s most necessary obligation. Dangerous because the record of confident long-range forecasting is almost uniformly embarrassing — in 1900 no one predicted two world wars, the Holocaust, the atom bomb, the Cold War, or the internet, and the people who claimed to see them coming did not see them correctly. Necessary because the alternative — refusing to project forward from present trends — is not caution but abdication. The decisions being made today about education, infrastructure, alliance, and institutions will shape the world of 2100 whether we think about that world deliberately or not.

So let me be deliberately unfashionable and say plainly what the evidence of this series suggests about the century ahead.

The American century is over. The question is what comes next.

The Three Scenarios

Serious analysts of international order generally offer three scenarios for the post-American world. I will describe all three honestly before telling you which one I think is most likely.

The first scenario is American renewal — the argument that American decline is cyclical rather than structural, that the United States has been written off before and recovered, that its demographic dynamism, institutional resilience, and technological leadership will reassert themselves once the Trump disruption passes. Proponents of this view point to the 1970s — stagflation, Vietnam humiliation, Watergate, the apparent Soviet ascendancy — as a period that looked terminal and wasn’t. Ronald Reagan’s presidency, in this reading, was the renewal that followed the Carter malaise. A similar renewal, perhaps in the late 2020s or 2030s, could restore American leadership on something approaching its post-war model.

I want this scenario to be true. I was raised in the shadow of American power at its most constructive, in the generation that Hugh Keenleyside served. The America of the Marshall Plan, the America that rebuilt its defeated enemies and turned them into prosperous democracies, the America that created the institutional architecture of the post-war order — that America deserves renewal rather than epitaph.

But I do not think it is the most likely scenario. The damage being done in 2026 is not merely policy damage. It is credibility damage, alliance damage, institutional damage of a kind that takes generations to repair. Nations that have been threatened, tariffed, abandoned, and humiliated do not quickly forget. The European allies who watched Trump simultaneously bomb Iran without consultation and threaten to seize Danish territory will spend the next two decades building strategic autonomy — not because they want to but because they have learned they must. That autonomy, once built, does not dissolve when an American election produces a more cooperative administration.

The second scenario is multipolar fragmentation — a world of regional powers, competing blocs, and no single hegemon. In this scenario, no country replaces America as the organizing principle of international order. China dominates East Asia. Russia dominates parts of the former Soviet space. Europe manages its own affairs with varying degrees of coherence. The United States retains hemispheric dominance and significant global military reach but no longer sets the rules. International institutions — the UN, the WTO, the IMF — persist in degraded form, providing forums for negotiation without enforcing norms. The result is a world that is messier, more dangerous, and more expensive for everyone — because the public goods that American hegemony provided, however imperfectly, now have no provider.

This is a plausible scenario and possibly a transitional one. It describes the world of the 2030s and 2040s more accurately than the world of 2100. The question is what emerges from that transitional period — a new equilibrium or a new hegemon.

The third scenario is Chinese primacy — the scenario that makes Western analysts most uncomfortable and that the evidence most stubbornly supports. In this scenario, China’s combination of demographic scale, manufacturing capacity, technological investment, and long-term strategic patience produces, over the course of this century, a world order organized around Chinese power rather than American power. Not necessarily a world order imposed by force — though force remains an option — but one shaped by Chinese economic relationships, Chinese infrastructure investment, Chinese technological standards, and Chinese institutional frameworks.

This is the scenario I think is most likely by 2100. Let me explain why, and then let me say what I think that means for those of us raising children and grandchildren in North America today.

Why China

China’s path to primacy is not inevitable. It faces serious structural challenges — an aging population, an over-leveraged property sector, a political system that has historically struggled to sustain innovation, and a neighborhood full of nations with long memories of Chinese imperial power. Any serious analysis of Chinese primacy must account for these constraints.

But the constraints on American primacy are more severe. The United States is actively dismantling the architecture of its own influence. It is alienating its allies, withdrawing from its institutions, and demonstrating — through the Iran war — that it can no longer be trusted to exercise power in ways that benefit its partners rather than destabilizing them. Robert Kagan’s epitaph for the American order is accurate: nations that once bandwagoned with the United States will now remain aloof or align against it, not because they want to but because they have no choice.

China, by contrast, is playing a long game with considerable skill. The Belt and Road Initiative — whatever its debt-trap dimensions — has built infrastructure across Africa, Asia, and parts of Europe that creates lasting economic relationships. Chinese universities are graduating more engineers than any other country on earth. Chinese companies dominate the supply chains for the clean energy technologies that will power the next century — solar panels, batteries, rare earth processing. And China is learning, slowly and with setbacks, how to build the kind of soft power and institutional legitimacy that American primacy required.

The Iran war has accelerated this trajectory. Japan gets 95 percent of its oil from the Middle East. China has ensured its oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz while American and Israeli bombs fall. Pakistan is shuttling between Washington and Beijing as the war’s primary mediator. Gulf states that spent eighty years under American security guarantees are quietly hedging, building relationships with Beijing and Moscow, and noting that Chinese oil tankers are moving through the strait while American ones wait. The world is watching who can deliver stability — and in the spring of 2026, the answer is not the United States.

By mid-century, the most likely world order is one in which China has consolidated its position as the dominant power in Asia, has extended its economic reach into Africa and the Middle East through infrastructure investment and trade relationships, and has built institutional alternatives to the American-designed frameworks that are now hollowing out. The Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation, the growing network of bilateral currency swap agreements that bypass the dollar — these are the embryonic architecture of a Chinese-led order, built slowly and deliberately over decades while American attention was elsewhere.

The Mombasa–Nairobi Standard Gauge Railway in Kenya, one of the flagship Belt and Road Initiative projects. China has committed over $1 trillion to infrastructure across the developing world since 2013 — building not just tracks and ports, but the economic relationships and dependencies that form the physical backbone of an emerging Chinese-led order. Infrastructure is how hegemons embed themselves for generations.

By 2100, if current trends continue — and trends of this magnitude move slowly enough that current trajectories are meaningful guides — the global order will be organized around Chinese economic and institutional frameworks in much the way it was organized around American ones for the second half of the twentieth century. Not a Chinese empire in the nineteenth-century sense. Something more like what America built after 1945: a system that benefits its architect disproportionately but provides enough stability and public goods that others participate voluntarily.

The lingua franca of that order will be Mandarin.

The Language of Power

Language follows power. This is one of the most consistent patterns in the history of international order.

Latin was the language of the Roman Empire and remained the language of European scholarship and diplomacy for a thousand years after Rome fell — because the institutional architecture Rome built was so durable that its language outlasted its legions. French was the language of European diplomacy from the seventeenth century through the First World War — because France was the continent’s dominant cultural and military power during that period. English became the global lingua franca not because it is a particularly elegant or logical language but because the British Empire and then American hegemony made it the language of commerce, technology, aviation, diplomacy, and popular culture for two centuries.

The language of the dominant power becomes the language of aspiration. Parents teach their children the language they believe will open the most doors — which means the language of whoever holds the most keys.

For the last century, that language was English. For the next century, the evidence increasingly suggests it will be Mandarin.

I say this not with satisfaction but with the analytical coldness that this series has tried to maintain throughout. I am an anglophone Canadian. I was educated in English, I think in English, I write in English. The displacement of English as the global language of aspiration would be a profound cultural disruption for everyone in this hemisphere. I understand why the suggestion provokes resistance.

But resistance to a fact does not change the fact. And the fact is this: the 1.4 billion people of China, plus the hundreds of millions across Southeast Asia and the Chinese diaspora who already speak Mandarin as a first or second language, already constitute the world’s largest linguistic community. As Chinese economic power extends through Belt and Road infrastructure, through trade relationships, through the technological supply chains that China is coming to dominate — Mandarin follows. A Kenyan engineer working on a Chinese-financed railway learns Mandarin. An Indonesian businessman trading with Shenzhen learns Mandarin. A Saudi official negotiating an oil contract with CNPC learns Mandarin.

This is how Latin spread. This is how French spread. This is how English spread. Power creates the incentive structure, and language follows.

The question for North American parents and educators is not whether this is happening. It is whether we will prepare our children for it or pretend it is not happening until it is too late.

Mandarin immersion students in a North American classroom. Language follows power: Latin after Rome, French after Versailles, English after the British Empire and Pax Americana. The children learning Mandarin today will enter the workforce in the 2040s–2050s equipped for a world in which it is increasingly the language of commerce, technology, diplomacy, and opportunity.

My position is direct: Mandarin should be taught in North American primary and secondary schools, beginning now, as a standard part of the curriculum rather than an exotic elective. Not because we should celebrate Chinese primacy or accept it as inevitable. But because the children entering school today will be working professionals in the 2040s and 2050s, navigating a world in which Mandarin is increasingly the language of commerce, technology, and institutional power. Equipping them only with English is equipping them for the world of 1990 rather than the world they will actually inhabit.

This is not a betrayal of our values. It is how intelligent nations have always responded to shifting power realities. The British aristocracy of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries educated their children in French — not because they admired Louis XIV but because French was the language of European diplomacy and culture, and they intended their children to navigate that world effectively. The generation that builds North American Mandarin literacy will give its children options. The generation that refuses to will give its children ideology.

What the 2100 World Order Might Actually Look Like

Let me be specific, knowing I will be wrong about the details and possibly right about the direction.

By 2100, the most likely world order has four defining characteristics.

First: Chinese institutional primacy, not Chinese empire. China will not rule the world in the way that the British Empire ruled it — through direct colonial administration and military occupation. The costs of that model were demonstrated definitively in the twentieth century. What China is building is closer to what America built: an order organized around Chinese-designed institutions, Chinese-denominated financial systems, and Chinese technological standards, in which other nations participate because the benefits of participation outweigh the costs of resistance. This is hegemony through architecture rather than hegemony through force — exactly the model that the post-war generation built for America and that is now being abandoned.

Second: A fragmented but surviving West. Europe will not disappear. North America will not disappear. The democratic values and institutional traditions of the Western liberal order will persist — not as global organizing principles but as regional ones. Europe, forced by American abandonment to build genuine strategic autonomy, will emerge as a coherent geopolitical actor in its own right — neither American vassal nor Chinese client, but a third pole with real but limited global reach. North America, absent the corrupting influence of imperial overreach, may rediscover the domestic virtues — social investment, institutional integrity, democratic accountability — that made it genuinely admirable before the excesses of hegemony began to hollow them out.

Third: Climate as the wildcard. Everything in this forecast is conditional on the trajectory of climate change, which introduces uncertainties that dwarf anything in the geopolitical analysis. A world in which two or three meters of sea level rise has inundated coastal megacities, in which chronic drought has destabilized agricultural systems across the Global South, in which mass migration on a scale that makes the current refugee crisis look modest has reshaped the demographics of every continent — that world has a political economy that no current analytical framework adequately captures. The optimistic reading is that the climate crisis forces the kind of genuinely multilateral cooperation that produces new institutional architecture — a Green Bretton Woods that neither American nor Chinese primacy alone could deliver. The pessimistic reading is that resource competition accelerates the fragmentation already underway and produces conflicts that make the Iran war look like a regional skirmish. I do not know which reading is correct. I suspect the truth is somewhere between them and worse than either comfortable version.

Jakarta, Indonesia — one of the fastest-sinking major cities on Earth, with parts already regularly inundated. Sea-level rise, agricultural disruption, and mass climate migration will be the defining wildcard of the 21st century. Whatever geopolitical order emerges by 2100 will be shaped far more by environmental realities than by any single power’s ambitions.

Fourth: The return of the city-state. The nation-state as the primary unit of political organization may itself be in question by 2100. The megacities of the twenty-first century — Shanghai, Mumbai, Lagos, São Paulo, Jakarta — have economic outputs that exceed those of most nations and populations that create their own cultural and political dynamics. The Hanseatic League of the medieval period, in which powerful city-states organized trade and security across national boundaries, may be a more useful model for the 2100 world than the Westphalian nation-state system that the post-war order tried to stabilize. Hugh Keenleyside worked within a world of sovereign states. The children being born today may live to see that sovereignty increasingly hollowed out from above — by Chinese institutional frameworks and climate-driven migration — and from below — by the political economies of megacities that have more in common with each other than with their national hinterlands.

What Hugh Keenleyside Would Say

Hugh Keenleyside in Victoria BC, C.C., 1984

I have been thinking, throughout this series, about what Hugh Keenleyside would make of the world his generation’s work has become.

He was not an idealist in the naive sense. He worked in the real world of state power, economic interest, and diplomatic compromise. He understood that international order was not built on goodwill alone but on the carefully calibrated alignment of national interests with collective frameworks. He knew that the American power he was helping to channel and constrain was real power — sometimes brutal, often self-interested, occasionally magnificent — and that managing it required intelligence, patience, and a clear-eyed understanding of what it could and could not do.

What he built — what his generation built — was a framework designed to make American power survivable for everyone else, including America. The genius of it was that it gave the United States enough of what it wanted — security, markets, deference, influence — that it had a stake in maintaining the system rather than simply exploiting it. The rest of the world got enough in return — stability, development assistance, freedom of navigation, the rule of international law — to make participation rational.

That bargain is now broken. Not because the rest of the world walked away from it but because America did. And the breaking of it is producing exactly the consequences that Keenleyside’s generation feared: a world of competing powers, eroding institutions, degraded norms, and the slow return of the logic of force that the post-war order was designed to supersede.

I think he would be angry. Not despairing — he was too much of a pragmatist for despair — but angry, with the focused anger of someone who spent his career building something that is being destroyed by people who do not understand what it was or why it mattered.

He would also, I think, be clear-eyed about what comes next. He built for his moment. His successors must build for theirs. If the American-led order is ending, the question is not how to restore it but what to build in its place. And that building — like all serious institution-building — requires exactly the qualities he embodied: the willingness to work with power as it is rather than as you wish it were, the patience to construct frameworks that outlast the individuals who design them, and the understanding that legitimacy — the voluntary consent of those living under a system — is the only foundation durable enough to build on.

The next order, whatever it is, will require people like Hugh Keenleyside. People who can sit across a table from a rising power and negotiate the terms on which it will exercise its dominance in ways that the rest of the world can live with. People who understand that the alternative to a legitimate order is not a better order but no order at all.

Whether those people will be Canadian, I do not know. Whether they will be able to do what needs to be done before the climate crisis forecloses the options, I do not know. Whether the Chinese leadership that inherits primacy will have the wisdom to accept the constraints that legitimate power requires — as the American leadership of the 1940s, at its best, accepted them — I genuinely do not know.

What I know is that the work Keenleyside did mattered. That the order his generation built, for all its failures and hypocrisies, was the closest approximation to a humane international system that this species has yet managed to construct. And that its loss — which is what we are witnessing, in the spring of 2026, from the shores of the Persian Gulf — is not an abstraction.

It is a catastrophe in slow motion. And it deserves to be named as such.

A Note on Language

I began this conclusion with the observation that language follows power. I want to end it there too.

My grandchildren will grow up in a world that is already different from the one I grew up in, and will grow old in a world I cannot fully imagine. The most useful thing I can tell their parents is this: teach them Mandarin.

Not instead of English. In addition to it. Not as a gesture of submission to Chinese power but as a tool for navigating the world that is coming. The children who speak Mandarin in 2050 will have options that the children who only speak English will not. That is not a political statement. It is a demographic and economic projection, as cold and clear as any in this series.

Hugh Keenleyside learned Japanese in the 1930s when Japan was the rising power in Asia. He did not do it because he admired Japanese imperialism. He did it because he understood that a diplomat who cannot speak to the people he is negotiating with is not a diplomat — he is a tourist. The lesson holds for the children of the twenty-first century.

Teach them Mandarin. Teach them the history of how the world order was built and how it is being unmade. Teach them that power without consent is power on borrowed time — that the most durable forms of influence are those that benefit their recipients enough that the recipients defend them as their own. Teach them that the frameworks of international cooperation their great-grandparents built were not naive idealism but hard-headed realism about what makes power last.

And teach them that it is possible to build again. That Hugh Keenleyside’s generation built from the rubble of the worst catastrophe in human history, with the full knowledge of what unconstrained power produces, and managed — imperfectly, partially, but genuinely — to construct something better.

The rubble of the American century is not yet complete. But it is accumulating. The builders of the next order are already being born.

The question is whether we will give them the tools they need.

A diverse North American classroom in the 2020s. These children will live most of their adult lives in whatever order replaces the American century. Whether they inherit fragmentation, Chinese primacy tempered by wise institutions, or something better will depend in large part on what we teach them now — including history without illusion, languages without ideology, and the hard lessons of how power is built, maintained, and lost.
