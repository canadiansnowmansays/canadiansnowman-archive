Let us begin where the erasing is easiest to see, because it involves a building whose entire purpose is to be a memory.

The John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts is not merely a concert hall. It is a memorial — a living one, built by act of Congress to honour a president murdered in office, a place where the remembering was supposed to take the form of music and theatre and dance carrying on, year after year, in his name. That was the idea. You keep the memory of a man alive by keeping the thing he loved alive inside a building that bears his name.

This administration has spent the past year methodically dismantling every part of that sentence.

First it took the leadership, installing a handpicked board with the president as its chair. Then it took the name, or tried to — the board voted to drill Trump’s own name onto a building Congress had dedicated to Kennedy, until a federal judge, reading a 1964 statute that says only Congress may rename it, ordered the name removed last month. Then it took the audience: the center has lost, by recent accounting, roughly ninety percent of its staff and, for the moment, one hundred percent of its paying audience. The Opera House no longer has an opera. The orchestra pit, a longtime volunteer told the New York Times, sits empty and scattered with trash.

And then, this week, it took the last thing left. It sent home the people who remembered.

The Memory in the Room

Her name is Sandy Weiswasser. She is eighty-seven years old, a retired schoolteacher, and for eighteen years she has volunteered as a tour guide at the Kennedy Center — one of a corps that once numbered a hundred and forty, offering tours in ten languages to as many as four hundred thousand visitors a year. She kept showing up this summer even as the crowds vanished, out of nothing but love for the place. On a recent Monday, only five of more than a dozen scheduled guides bothered to appear, and there were three visitors for them to guide.

I want to be precise about what a person like Sandy Weiswasser is, in the life of an institution, because it is the whole point of this essay. She is its memory. Eighteen years of it. She is the human being who can stand in a corridor and tell a stranger what this room was, what happened on that stage, how full these seats used to be, what the place meant before it meant whatever it means now. An institution’s memory does not live only in its archives and its plaques. It lives in the people who have been there long enough to hold the before and the after in the same head, and who will answer honestly when a visitor asks what changed.

That is a dangerous thing to have on the premises of an institution being hollowed out. Because when a visitor asks Sandy Weiswasser what happened here, she tells them. “This place used to be really thriving and busy and crowded,” she told the Times. “Now it’s a black hole.”

That sentence — eight words, spoken by an eighty-seven-year-old volunteer — appeared in a New York Times article on a Monday. By Thursday, a memo had gone out. The Kennedy Center was placing a “temporary pause” on its entire public tour program, effective Friday. The guides were told to stop reporting for duty “until further notice.” The memo, the Times reported, gave no reason. When a reporter reached the director of the volunteer program to confirm it, she did not offer one either. She asked, “How did you find out?” and declined to say more.

The program had run continuously since 1971, pausing only once, for the pandemic. It was ended two days after one of its guides described, accurately, what had become of the place.

The Cover Story

The Kennedy Center did eventually produce a rationale, and it is worth preserving, because this series will hear a great many like it. “We are conducting a review of campuswide tour routes,” a spokesman said, “to ensure they provide the best possible experience for our visitors.”

A fifty-five-year-old program, suspended entirely and indefinitely, so that management may study the routes. The hallways. As though the crisis at the Kennedy Center were a matter of visitors being led inefficiently past the coat check, and not a matter of there being almost no visitors and almost no guides and, soon, no memory of what the building was for.

This is the characteristic voice of a Ministry of Memory: the bland, procedural noun laid gently over the act of erasure. Not “we are silencing the woman who called us a black hole,” but “we are reviewing the routes.” Not “we removed the plaque about slavery,” but — in the actual words of the Interior Department when it stripped historical signage from the national parks this year — we are ensuring “accuracy, honesty and alignment with shared national values.” The euphemism is never meant to be believed. It is meant to be available — a phrase the credulous can repeat and the compliant can hide behind, so that anyone describing what actually happened is made to sound shrill.

The tell, this time, was the timing. As the Times reported, before the memo even circulated, several volunteers had told the paper they feared the tour program would be targeted in retribution for the article. They predicted the punishment. It arrived on schedule, two days later. When the people about to be erased can set their watches by it, the route review is not fooling anyone who is paying attention.

Why This Is a Memory Story, and Not Merely a Cruel One

It would be easy to file this under pettiness — a thin-skinned institution lashing out at an old woman over eight words — and the pettiness is real. But pettiness is not why it belongs at the head of this series. It belongs here because of what was destroyed, which was not a person’s feelings but an institution’s capacity to be remembered accurately.

Consider the sequence as a single motion. The name that tied the building to Kennedy: contested, nearly erased, saved only by a judge. The programming that carried the memory forward in living form: gutted. The audience that witnessed it: gone. And now the guides — the last remaining humans who could tell you, out loud, in the building, what it had been. Each removal is defensible on its own terms. A board reshuffle. A renaming. A budget. A route review. The aggregate is the deliberate hollowing of a memorial until nothing inside it can testify to what it was memorializing.

This is the same motion, in miniature, that this administration has run at every custodian of the national memory. At the Smithsonian — the institution whose literal charter is preserving American history — text was quietly stripped from a presidential portrait so that an inconvenient past would no longer greet the visitor. At Independence National Historical Park, workers pried down plaques about the slave trade. At Fort Sumter, a sign about climate change came off; at Acadia, a sign about the Indigenous people who were there first. A federal judge ordered the parks materials restored, calling it what it was: “a dangerous precedent of censorship and sanitization.” I will return to each of these in the essays that follow, because each deserves more than the passing mention it has received while serving as evidence for some other argument. They are not footnotes to a story about accommodation or vanity. They are the story. They are a government editing the past out of the physical surfaces of the country.

The Kennedy Center simply shows the motion reaching all the way down — past the plaque and the caption to the living witness, the volunteer, the memory that walks and talks and answers questions. You can remove a sign quietly. You cannot easily remove an eighty-seven-year-old who has loved a place for eighteen years and will tell the truth about it to anyone who asks. So you do the next best thing. You send her home, and you call it a review of the routes.

What Was Actually Lost

Let me end with what the route review actually took, since the memo will not say.

Since 1971, any person — a tourist, a schoolchild, a retiree, someone who spoke no English — could walk into a memorial to a murdered president and be shown around, for free, by a neighbour who had volunteered to do it. Four hundred thousand people a year, ten languages, no admission charge, no script. It was one of those small, unglamorous civic arrangements a confident country builds when it is willing to let strangers see its institutions up close and describe them however they find them.

That is the thing that has been paused “until further notice.” Not because the routes need reviewing, but because one of the guides, standing in the wreckage of a hollowed-out memorial, was asked what she saw and told the truth about it.

A Ministry of Memory cannot allow that. Its business is not remembering. Its business is deciding what may be remembered, and disposing of the rest — the plaque, the caption, the sign, and, when it must, the eighty-seven-year-old woman who remembers too much and says it out loud.

The black hole, it turns out, prefers not to be described.
