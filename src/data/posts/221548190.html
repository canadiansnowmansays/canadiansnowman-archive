The Exam Paper

Part 13 of this series ended with an exam paper. On July 2, 2026, Tesla had just posted the largest delivery beat in its history and watched its stock fall 7.49 percent, and the essay argued that the verdict would arrive with the full financial results on July 22. It set four questions. Did the European price cuts gut automotive margins? Could energy storage hold its margin near 40 percent at higher volume? How much of the quarter was SpaceX buying from Tesla? And what would the raised capital programme, north of $25 billion, do to free cash flow?

Tesla sat that exam on July 22 and failed it. On October 21, 2026, it sat the resit. This essay marks both papers.

The short version: the resit is a pass. Adjusted earnings of 48 cents a share beat the 43 cents analysts polled by LSEG expected, as CNBC reported, on revenue of $28.05 billion against $27.9 billion. Gross margin rebounded to 19.6 percent. Free cash flow came in at a positive $1.53 billion when, according to Transport Topics, the market had braced for a burn of nearly $5 billion. Shares, which had closed up 3.4 percent at $386.47 ahead of the report, rose about 1 percent on the release and then slipped about 2 percent below the close as the earnings call went on.

The longer version is that a resit is graded on a curve, and this one was graded on a very generous curve.

What Happened in July

The July paper deserves its own post-mortem, because it explains why October’s bar was set so low.

On July 22, 2026, Tesla reported record revenue of $28.24 billion, up 26 percent year over year, on the back of the 480,126 deliveries Part 13 described. Then everything below the top line went wrong. Adjusted earnings came in at 33 cents a share against a consensus near 50 cents, and operating income fell 57 percent to $398 million, a 1.4 percent margin, as Drive Tesla Canada reported. Energy gross margin collapsed to 20.4 percent after a warranty charge, and free cash flow went negative by $1.09 billion.

The stock fell about 12.7 percent the next day, removing roughly $140 billion of market value. Jon McNeill, Tesla’s former president, offered Bloomberg the most economical summary of the quarter, as relayed by Benzinga: “This is not an AI investment story.” Tesla was spending like an AI company while earning like a discounting carmaker.

Part 13 had warned that “a 74,000-unit surprise built on discounting is revenue borrowed from gross margin.” July 22 sent the bill.

So October’s resit came with expectations already marked down. Analysts went in looking for flat revenue, earnings down about 10 percent year over year, and a heavy cash burn. That is the curve Tesla beat.

Question One: The Automotive Margin

July grade: fail. October grade: pass, with an asterisk.

Automotive gross margin excluding regulatory credits, the number this series has watched since January, was 16.3 percent in the second quarter. In the third quarter it recovered to 18.1 percent, against 15.4 percent a year earlier. Tesla credits a better regional and product mix and more FSD subscription revenue, partly offset by lower average selling prices.

That last clause is the asterisk. Total automotive revenue fell 3 percent while deliveries fell 2 percent, so each car sold for less than it did a year ago. Tesla’s shareholder deck says the company ended the quarter with “the largest backlog for our passenger vehicles in four years,” and that battery availability, not demand, limited volumes. Companies with a four-year-high backlog and a supply constraint do not usually cut prices. As Electrek observed, falling selling prices are an odd companion to a claim of scarcity. One of the two statements will look better than the other by January.

There is good news inside the volume figures, and it closes a thread from Part 13. That essay noted that Tesla had built 50,363 more cars than it delivered in the first quarter, and that the second quarter cleared 28,368 of them. The third quarter cleared another 22,141. Across two quarters, Tesla has delivered 50,509 more cars than it built. The first-quarter overhang that haunted the stock through its April low is gone, almost to the car. Inventory now stands at 11 days of supply, down from 27 in March.

Regulatory credits, which once padded this line, fell 73 percent to $113 million and will not come back. The recovery to 18.1 percent was earned on the cars.

Question Two: The Energy Margin

July grade: fail. October grade: pass on margin, fail on revenue.

Energy gross margin recovered to about 33 percent in the third quarter, from 20.4 percent in July. That is a real recovery, although it remains below the 39.5 percent of the first quarter that Part 13 called “the quietest important line in the release.”

Revenue went the other way. Energy generation and storage revenue fell 16 percent year over year to $2.88 billion, even though deployments rose 10 percent to 13.7 GWh. Tesla attributes part of the drop to tariff-related customer refunds. Deployments also fell about 2 GWh short of the 15.9 GWh analysts expected. Energy gross profit, the figure that actually reaches the bottom line, slipped to $954 million from $1.07 billion a year earlier.

The longer-term case is intact and arguably stronger. Megafactory Texas began production with 50 GWh of planned capacity, Tesla shipped its first Megapacks built entirely with its own LFP cells from Nevada, and the deck ties storage demand directly to data-centre grid constraints. But for the quarter, the segment this series described as the strongest leg of the bull case shrank.

Question Three: The Related-Party Asterisk

July grade: incomplete. October grade: still incomplete.

Part 13 asked how much of Tesla’s business was SpaceX buying from Tesla, after SpaceX’s IPO filing revealed $269 million of Megapack purchases for xAI’s Memphis data centres. The third-quarter deck does not answer that question. It does, however, add new entries to the ledger of transactions between Musk companies.

Tesla’s $2 billion equity stake in SpaceX, bought in the first quarter, now moves Tesla’s reported profits. In July, a $1.005 billion unrealised gain on that stake flattered a quarter in which the core business struggled. In October, it produced a $142 million unrealised loss. The deck also notes that Grok, xAI’s assistant, now runs inside the Cybercab, so a SpaceX-owned product is part of Tesla’s robotaxi pitch. And Tesla’s Roadster was unveiled on October 15 at SpaceX’s facility in Waco, Texas, two weeks late, levitating on what Tesla presents as SpaceX cold-gas thrusters.

Then there is the merger. Barclays analysts led by Dan Levy wrote this month that a Tesla–SpaceX combination could come “as soon as this fall,” as relayed by Gotrade News, with an all-stock structure of roughly 0.7 SpaceX shares per Tesla share. Tesla’s annual meeting must be held by December 6. On SpaceX’s own earnings call on October 20, Musk confirmed that both boards have formed special committees of independent directors to consider a combination, and that he is excluded from their talks as “a conflicted party.” Asked whether a merger would be a good idea, he said “Yes,” while warning that treating it as a certainty “would be overstating it.” He also flagged the obstacle: approval needs 50 percent of all Tesla shares, not merely of those voted, which he called “quite a high hurdle.” On Tesla’s call the next day, analysts were told merger questions were off-limits, and the shareholder letter says nothing about it. When two companies trade products, investments, venues and engineers this freely, the question of what each one earns on its own becomes harder to answer every quarter. A merger would end the question by ending the distinction.

Question Four: Capex and Cash

July grade: fail. October grade: pass, on borrowed time.

This is the answer that moved the market, and the one that most needs reading twice.

Free cash flow of $1.53 billion beat expectations by more than $6 billion. But two things built that number, and neither is the business suddenly throwing off cash.

The first is that Tesla spent less than planned. Capex fell to $4.73 billion from $5.79 billion in the second quarter. Analysts polled by Bloomberg, as Yahoo Finance reported, had modelled $7.96 billion of third-quarter capex. Tesla spent $4.73 billion. Through nine months, it has spent $13.0 billion against a full-year guide of more than $25 billion, which would have required roughly $12 billion in the fourth quarter alone.

On the call, Tesla settled the question. Chief financial officer Vaibhav Taneja cut the 2026 guide to $20 billion, to be funded “primarily from our cash flows from operations.” That still implies about $7 billion in the fourth quarter, the biggest spending quarter of the year, but $5 billion of the programme has moved out of 2026. Analysts who modelled a $5 billion burn were modelling the old guide. Tesla was not spending to it.

The second is working capital, and the cash flow statement makes it plain. Of $6.26 billion in operating cash flow, about $2.6 billion came from timing rather than earnings. Accounts payable and accruals added $3.99 billion; deferred revenue, meaning customer money received in advance, added $1.26 billion; prepaid expenses and other items took back about $2.7 billion. The balance sheet shows the mechanism: days payable outstanding rose from 58 to 66. Tesla took about eight more days to pay its suppliers, and those eight days were worth roughly $1.9 billion.

Remove the working-capital help and free cash flow would have been about negative $1.1 billion, almost exactly July’s figure. The underlying run-rate did not change between the two exams. What changed was the timing of the cheques.

One balance-sheet line deserves attention in the 10-Q: other non-current assets rose $3.1 billion in a single quarter, which suggests long-term deposits or prepayments. Battery supply agreements are the likeliest explanation, given the deck’s talk of “securing future supply,” but the deck does not say.

What the 48 Cents Is Made Of

The reconciliation tables at the back of the deck are where the headline beat comes apart. GAAP earnings were 22 cents a share, below the roughly 26 cents GAAP consensus. Strip out a 6-cent bitcoin gain and a 3-cent loss on the SpaceX stake, and core earnings were about 20 cents. Almost all of the bridge to 48 cents is one line: 28 cents of stock-based compensation, added back.

Part 13 recalled Michael Burry’s “Lord of the Tragic Tier” jibe about Tesla’s stock compensation. The trailing-twelve-month figures now support it: $4.3 billion of stock compensation against $3.2 billion of net income. Meanwhile interest income of $445 million made up nearly half of pre-tax profit, earned on a $45.4 billion cash pile rather than on cars, batteries or software. None of this threatens the company, which carries only about $9.3 billion of debt. The question is not whether Tesla survives its transition. It is what the transition earns.

The Photo Album

A Tesla shareholder deck now runs 32 pages. Thirteen are photographs, and they are very good. The new Roadster hovers above a stage on columns of white vapour, billed as “the best of the last human-driven cars,” a striking slogan from a company whose valuation rests on the end of human driving. Gold Cybercabs roll through downtown Austin at dusk, past a mounted police officer. Cranes stand over the foundations of the Austin semiconductor fab.

The numbers behind the pictures are real. Robotaxi passed nearly 2 million cumulative unsupervised miles by the end of September, Texas registrations showed 319 Cybercabs by October 10, up from 45 at launch, and Optimus production has begun in Fremont.

The pictures also leave things out. NHTSA opened a probe into robotaxi behaviour in school zones on October 14, and has separately sent Tesla 21 questions about how it self-certified a car with no steering wheel or pedals; the answers are due October 30. The 1.74 million “active FSD subscriptions” are counted as Musk’s 2025 pay package defines them, upfront purchases included. And while the deck still says “Full Self-Driving (Supervised),” Tesla began renaming it “Tesla Assisted Driving” on its European websites on October 8.

What the Call Added

The call that followed the deck was, by Tesla’s standards, restrained. Musk opened his remarks on robots with a sentence this series has not heard from him before: “I do want to temper expectations around Optimus.” He then said it would take at least a year, possibly longer, to reach 20,000 robots a week, with deliveries to outside companies by the end of 2027.

The other dates followed the same pattern, each a little further out than the last promise. Musk said the Austin Cybercab fleet has grown tenfold since launch and that Dallas is next, but Cybercab will not be available to customers until around 2027, and mostly as a Tesla-owned service rather than a car people buy. Unsupervised FSD in customers’ own cars is now expected around the second quarter of 2027. Musk hopes for EU-wide FSD approval by the first quarter, while conceding the timing is not Tesla’s to control. The Roadster that levitated on October 15 goes into production in 2028, and the Terafab chip plant is aiming for useful chips at volume the same year.

Robotaxi revenue followed the same calendar. Management told Wolfe Research that unsupervised miles should become noticeable in the second quarter of 2027 and significant only by the third and fourth.

The most important answer came near the end, in response to Deutsche Bank. Terafab, the chip plant Tesla is building with SpaceX, will need “tens of billions” of dollars, and more than $100 billion over the long term, with real production expected in late 2028. Tesla and SpaceX have not yet settled how to split the cost. That is the largest related-party question this series has yet encountered, and it sits outside both the $20 billion capex guide and the shareholder deck.

The one firm number on the call was the capex cut. Nearly everything else moved to 2027 or later, and the biggest bill of all has no date or owner yet.

The Canadian in the Cheap Seats

Permit the usual observation from north of the border.

Across 2026, this series has watched the same company produce three opposite reactions. In April, bad numbers were rescued by narrative. In July, record deliveries were abandoned by narrative, and three weeks later record revenue was punished by margins. In October, a quarter in which operating income fell 56 percent, the car business shrank, and energy revenue dropped by a sixth was greeted with little more than a shrug, because it was less bad than feared and the cash flow looked healthy at a glance.

The common thread is that Tesla is graded against expectations, and the expectations are set by the last exam rather than by the business. A student who fails in July can pass in October by improving on July, without learning anything new. The resit is a pass. The underlying grade is unchanged: a car business that has not grown in three years, earning thin margins, funding an enormous bet on autonomy and robotics with a cash pile that currently earns nearly as much as the cars do.

That bet may well pay off. The Cybercab fleet is growing, the robotaxi miles are compounding, and the energy business has a structural tailwind from every data centre that cannot get a grid connection. Holding Tesla in 2026 means believing that. It does not require believing that October’s cash flow was the business turning the corner. The cash flow statement says it was suppliers waiting an extra eight days.

What January Must Answer

The fourth-quarter results, due in late January 2027, set the next paper. Five questions:

  1. Payables. Do days payable outstanding stay near 66, or reverse toward 58 and take $1.9 billion of cash back out?

  2. Capex. Does Tesla spend the roughly $7 billion needed to meet its reduced $20 billion guide, how much of the deferred $5 billion lands in 2027’s guide, and what share of Terafab’s tens of billions does Tesla carry?

  3. The $3.1 billion. What is the jump in other non-current assets, and is it battery supply prepayments?

  4. The backlog. If battery supply improves, do deliveries rise, and do selling prices stop falling? If not, the backlog claim weakens.

  5. The merger. Do the special committees produce a deal before the December 6 meeting deadline, and on what terms?

The exam has been resat and passed. The coursework is still due.
