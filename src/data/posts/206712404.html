I should confess the obvious problem first, because you will think of it before I finish this sentence: I am not a historian, I am not a monarchist, and I have never once been invited to a coronation. What I am is a subject. A small, quiet, thoroughly Canadian subject of King Charles III, who has never asked my opinion about anything and presumably never will. That fact — that I live inside a monarchy in the year 2026 and rarely think about it — is the seed of this whole series.

Because here is the thing nobody tells you growing up in Toronto: kingship isn’t a relic. It’s a technology. And like every technology humans have ever built, it was invented to solve a problem, it got adopted unevenly, it was patched and updated for thousands of years, and pieces of it are still running today — sometimes as ceremony, sometimes as absolute law, sometimes as pure appetite wearing a crown it was never issued.

That last category is the one that got me started. I’ve written before about the parallels between King John signing away power he never wanted to relinquish at Runnymede in 1215 and a certain twenty-first-century president who regards every check on his authority as a personal insult. The comparison stuck with me longer than I expected. And once you start looking for the wannabe kings — the men who wanted the throne’s power without inheriting its constraints — you find them everywhere. Napoleon crowning himself because no pope’s blessing could improve on his own ambition. Putin, blessed by a church and a rigged referendum instead of a bloodline, but chasing the identical thing: rule that answers to nothing. The comparison isn’t decoration. It’s the argument.

So here is the actual question this series is going to sit with, for as many parts as it takes: when did human beings first decide one person should rule over the rest, how did they get everyone else to agree to it — and when did people stop agreeing?

That question doesn’t have one answer, because it never had one origin. Kingship wasn’t invented once. It was arrived at, independently, by farmers and priests and soldiers in Mesopotamia and Egypt and China, all discovering roughly the same thing at roughly the same time: that a surplus of grain needs someone to defend it, that an irrigation canal needs someone to coordinate it, and that armies fight better under one commander than under a council that argues before every battle. Kingship’s founding insight wasn’t cruelty. It was efficiency. The cruelty came later, once efficiency had a crown on it and no one left who could take the crown back off.

What kept kingship in place for five thousand years wasn’t mainly the sword, either, though the sword was always in the room. It was the story — divine right in Europe, the Mandate of Heaven in China, pharaonic godhood in Egypt, the blessing of the Solomonic line in Ethiopia. Every monarchy that lasted more than a generation figured out the same trick: make the claim to rule sound like it comes from somewhere other than the ruler himself. And every monarchy that eventually fell did so at the exact moment enough people stopped believing the story — whether that unbelief arrived as a signature at Runnymede, a guillotine in Paris, a Marine landing party in Honolulu, or a king in the Himalayas who looked at his own absolute power and, entirely of his own will, gave most of it away.

That last one — Bhutan — is not in this series as a curiosity. My husband Ugyen is from Bhutan, and the Wangchuck dynasty’s decision to hand its own people a constitution nobody demanded is, I think, the single strangest and most admirable act in the entire history of monarchy. Every other chapter in this series is about power being seized, defended, inherited, or pried loose. Bhutan is the one chapter about a king giving it away on purpose. It will get the attention it deserves.

This series will travel further than most things I write. It has to. Kingship was never a European story with a few footnotes elsewhere — it was Mughal India’s Peacock Throne and five hundred Maharajas absorbed into one departing empire; it was Kamehameha unifying Hawaii and Liliʻuokalani watching it stolen from her by American businessmen with Marine support; it was Saul and David and Solomon, and a prophet warning the Israelites in exact and specific detail what a king would take from them before they demanded one anyway; it was the Golden Stool of the Ashanti and the Solomonic throne of Ethiopia and the unbroken, endlessly reinterpreted Chrysanthemum Throne of Japan.

I will not get to every kingdom that ever stood. No one could, and I’d rather say so now than have someone say it to me later. Near the end of this series there will be a piece called The Kingdoms We Haven’t Gotten To — a deliberate accounting of the thrones this series doesn’t reach, offered not as an apology but as evidence for the thesis itself: the fact that a survey of human monarchy could run to forty parts and still not be finished tells you something true about how deeply this particular idea took root in our species.

We’ll end where we started: in Canada, a country that fought a revolution’s neighbour without ever having one, and kept its king as a formality so mild most of us forget he’s there. That, too, is a strategy for how a monarchy survives — not divinity, not the sword, but simply making itself small enough that nobody bothers to object.

Part I begins before writing does, in the river valleys where the first kings were made. I’ll see you there.

This series runs twenty-seven parts plus a coda — roughly seventy thousand words tracing monarchy from ancient Mesopotamia to the twenty-six crowns still standing today. It won’t all arrive at once.
