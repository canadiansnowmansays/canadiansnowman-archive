The Elsinore Festival’s 2026 season includes, among its productions of Shakespeare, Beckett, Chekhov, and new Canadian work, a musical called Something Rotten!

Something Rotten! is a musical comedy about two brothers in Elizabethan England — Nick and Nigel Bottom — who, tired of living in the shadow of William Shakespeare, attempt to write a show that will outshine him once and for all. They fail. They fail with considerable energy, theatrical invention, and a song about eggs, which is not something that can be said of most serious artistic endeavours. The show has been a hit in New York, London, and regional theatres across the English-speaking world.

Alistair programmed it in January, in the particular mood of a man completing his last season and choosing to end it not only with tragedy and gravitas but also with something that makes people laugh out loud in the third row.

He had not, at the time of programming, fully examined the irony.

The show is about two brothers who want what Shakespeare has. It plays to full houses. The swan is on the river. The swan has no opinion on the matter

The irony was examined, in public, for the first time at the June meeting of the Elsinore Tourism and Culture Committee, by Wayne.

Wayne had not intended to raise it as a philosophical matter. He had intended to raise it as a marketing opportunity. He noted, in his quarterly tourism report, that the Something Rotten! booking figures were strong, that the show’s premise — two men trying and failing to outshine Shakespeare — was proving an effective draw, and that he had incorporated the irony into the Presidential Package materials, describing Something Rotten! as “a show about the impossibility of outshining Shakespeare, staged at the theatre that has been doing Shakespeare for seventy years, in a city named after Hamlet’s castle, which makes it either the most self-aware production in Canadian theatre history or the funniest.”

He had added, in the materials: “Both, probably.”

He looked up from his report.

He said he thought this was strong branding.

The room considered this.

Alistair was present. He had been present for the entire presentation. He had been looking at the ceiling for the past forty seconds.

Mayor Doug said it sounded like a very enjoyable production. He had been meaning to get tickets. He looked at his wife, who was not present, in the way of a man who has been meaning to get tickets and has not yet discussed this with the relevant domestic authority.

Gerald wrote something in his notes.

Alistair spoke after the meeting, in the Festival Theatre parking lot, which is where his most considered remarks have always been delivered.

He said he was aware of the irony of programming a show about the impossibility of outshining Shakespeare at a Shakespeare festival. He said he had been aware of it since February. He said he had thought about it at some length.

Wayne waited. He had learned, over eleven years in Elsinore, that when Alistair pauses in a parking lot the pause is intentional.

Alistair said: the whole point of Something Rotten! is that the Bottom brothers fail. Nick and Nigel try every trick, every shortcut, every appropriation of another person’s glory, and Shakespeare goes on being Shakespeare and the audience goes home having seen something true about ambition and its relationship to talent and the gap between what we claim and what we can actually do.

He said he had programmed it precisely because of the irony. He said the irony was the point.

Wayne said that was also good branding.

Alistair looked at him for a moment.

He went back inside. He had a rehearsal.

Wayne updated his phone. He had been considering adding a note about Alistair’s parking-lot remarks to the Presidential Package materials. He decided against it. Some things were better left to the experience itself.

Carly reviewed Something Rotten! for the Beacon in its second week of performances.

Norman had not assigned the review to the usual arts correspondent, who was on holiday. He had assigned it to Carly, who had been at the Beacon for fourteen months and had covered the Easter egg hunt and the paddle boat incident and the election and the heritage minutes and Wayne’s Presidential Package launch and had filed every piece accurately and on time and had not yet, in Norman’s assessment, written the thing she was capable of writing.

He told her she had eight hundred words and a deadline of Thursday.

She used nine hundred and twenty. He ran all of them.

The review noted that Something Rotten! was a comedy about men who wanted credit for something that belonged to someone else, and that it was funny in the way that things are funny when they are also true, and that the Elsinore company performed it with the particular precision of actors who understood that the joke in the third row depended on the detail in the first scene, and that watching it in a theatre named after a fictional place in a city named after a fictional castle made it feel less like a period comedy and more like a mirror, the angle of which the audience was free to direct as they chose.

Norman read it twice. He texted her: Good.

This was, in Norman’s vocabulary, considerably more than enthusiasm.

He did not tell her this. He filed the review under things he knew without needing to say.

Gerald read the review on Sunday morning. He cut it out of the paper. He filed it under Elsinore Beacon — Arts Coverage — Notable Pieces — Meadows, C.

The South Korean tourists booked Something Rotten! in groups of eight.

Wayne had not anticipated this specifically, though in retrospect it was consistent with the pattern. The paddle boat photograph had introduced Elsinore to a Korean travel market that responded, Wayne had observed, with particular enthusiasm to the combination of cultural prestige and gentle human comedy. Something Rotten! offered both. The Facebook group dedicated to Elsinore tourism had seventeen hundred members in Seoul. They had seen Carly’s review translated into Korean by a member who described herself as a retired English teacher with an interest in Canadian theatre.

Wayne called it the Bottom Brothers’ Butter Tart. It was a standard butter tart from the Perth County Pantry served on a slightly nicer plate. Alistair said nothing for four seconds.

Wayne prepared a package. He called it the Something Rotten! Experience. It included tickets, a pre-show Literary River Tour, and a curated dinner at the Avon Table featuring, at Wayne’s suggestion, a dessert the owner had agreed to call the Bottom Brothers’ Butter Tart, which was a standard butter tart from the Perth County Pantry served on a slightly nicer plate.

Alistair was informed of the Bottom Brothers’ Butter Tart. He said nothing for four seconds.

He said: “How are the bookings?”

Wayne said: very good.

Alistair said he saw.

He went back inside.

Constance Hay saw Something Rotten! on a Tuesday evening in July, in the seat she has occupied at the Festival for forty years, second row, left of centre, close enough to see the actors’ faces and far enough to see the whole stage.

She had seen it before, in Toronto, in 2019, before Alistair had programmed it here. She had thought, then, that it was a show that would play well in Elsinore and had said nothing, because she had not been asked, and because she had learned that the useful thing with Alistair was to wait for the question.

She watched the Bottom brothers try to outshine Shakespeare. She watched them fail, loudly and inventively and with considerable egg-related theatrical ambition. She watched the audience in the third row laugh in the places she had expected them to laugh.

In the second act, during a quiet moment between the comedy, she opened her small spiral notebook and wrote one sentence.

She closed it. She watched the rest of the show.

Afterward, in the car, she did not read the sentence back. She knew what it said.

Jim saw the show on a Thursday in August, on a ticket Maggie had given him because Debi had a work commitment and the ticket would otherwise go unused. He sat in the middle of the house and watched the Bottom brothers want what Shakespeare had, badly and completely and without any clear sense of what, exactly, they would do with it once they got it.

He mentioned it to Maggie at Sunday dinner. He said the brothers reminded him of something, and that he couldn’t quite say what, and that the show was funnier than he had expected.

Maggie said she’d read Carly’s review. She said the mirror remark had stayed with her.

Jim passed the bread.

The boys ate their dinner.

A Note for the Record

Something Rotten! is a show about wanting credit for something that belongs to someone else. The Bottom brothers look at Shakespeare and see not a playwright of extraordinary gifts but an obstacle between them and the glory they believe they are owed. They try every approach — imitation, appropriation, invention, claiming credit for things they have not written — and Shakespeare goes on being Shakespeare, performing before full houses, collecting the admiration that Nick and Nigel believe should be theirs.

The comedy, in Leacock’s tradition, is in the gap between aspiration and reality. The brothers’ belief in their own claim is entirely genuine. The gap between the claim and the talent is simply there, visible to everyone in the theatre except, for most of the show, to the brothers themselves.

To the south, a man who has wanted the Nobel Peace Prize for years lobbied for it openly at the United Nations General Assembly, told foreign leaders it would be “a big insult to the US” if he didn’t receive it, and accepted its 2025 winner’s physical medal when she offered it to him, calling it a “wonderful gesture of mutual respect.” The Nobel Committee clarified that the prize cannot be revoked, shared, or transferred. The committee said the prize “remains inseparably linked” to the original laureate, and that “even if the medal or diploma later comes into someone else’s possession, this does not alter who was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize.”

He has the medal. He does not have the prize. The Nobel Committee’s records show María Corina Machado as the 2025 laureate. They will continue to show this. He subsequently told Norway’s prime minister that because the Nobel Committee had not awarded him the prize, he no longer felt obligated to “think purely of Peace.”

Nick and Nigel Bottom, at the end of the show, are still not Shakespeare. Shakespeare is still Shakespeare. The audience goes home.

In Elsinore, the show plays to full houses. The Bottom Brothers’ Butter Tart is well received. The mirror, as Carly noted, can be directed at whatever angle the audience chooses.

The swans proceed on the river, indifferent to the question of credit and entirely unconcerned with who deserves what. They have been here since the 1970s. They will be here when the marquee changes.

They belong to no one and require no medal to confirm it.
