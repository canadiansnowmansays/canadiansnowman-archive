Robert F. Kennedy Jr. as Secretary of Health and Human Services. The man at the center of the Secretary Wars.

There is a useful military concept called “economy of force.” It describes the art of applying minimum resources to secondary objectives so that maximum resources can be concentrated where they matter most. It is the tactical logic of containing a problem rather than solving it.

The Trump administration has been applying economy of force to Robert F. Kennedy Jr. for several months now. He has not been fired. He has not been publicly rebuked. He has not been stripped of his title. He has simply been walled in — from the inside, from the outside, and now, as of this week, from two directions simultaneously.

The State Department is “going to be re-engaging on the issue of Gavi,” Secretary of State Marco Rubio said in a hearing on Tuesday. Credit...Haiyun Jiang/The New York Times

On Tuesday, June 2, Secretary of State Marco Rubio appeared before the Senate Foreign Relations Committee and quietly executed the international component of the containment. On April 16, the White House nominated Dr. Erica Schwartz to run the CDC — the domestic one. Neither action removed Kennedy. Together, they announced something more precise: the experiment is over, and other people will be cleaning up.

Welcome to The Secretary Wars.

The $600 Million Question

Gavi’s role in candidate vaccines and stockpiles became a flashpoint.

The international front first, because Rubio’s testimony deserves to be read slowly.

Congress had appropriated $600 million for Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance — a public-private partnership that has immunized more than one billion children in low-income countries since its founding in 2000, maintaining emergency stockpiles against Ebola, yellow fever, meningitis, and cholera, and saving an estimated 18.8 million lives in the process. The money was sitting, allocated, authorized, ready. It was not being spent because Robert F. Kennedy Jr. had decided it shouldn’t be.

Kennedy announced the United States would halt its Gavi relationship at a Brussels pledging conference in June 2025, sending a video address to assembled world leaders in which he declared that Gavi had “treated vaccine safety issues not as a patient health problem, but as a public relations problem.” He offered no evidence for this allegation. He did not need to. He was the Secretary of Health and Human Services, and President Trump had told him to play “a leading role.”

Gavi’s operations contracted. Its planned budget of $11.9 billion shrank to $10 billion. Stockpiles for cholera and other critical diseases were affected. And $600 million in American money, appropriated by the people’s representatives, sat frozen at the discretion of a man who once claimed a dead bear cub in Central Park was nothing to do with him.

On Tuesday, while an Ebola outbreak was spreading in central Africa — a strain for which there is currently no approved vaccine, and for which Gavi has committed up to $40 million toward candidate development — Senator Jeanne Shaheen of New Hampshire asked Rubio the direct question: how are you making sure Kennedy releases those funds?

Health worker during an Ebola response in Africa.

Rubio’s answer was a masterpiece of diplomatic circumnavigation. He did not criticize Kennedy. He did not say Kennedy was wrong. He said that the State Department had made a decision, “a few weeks ago,” to re-engage on the Gavi file — and that the State Department, which has historically managed the U.S. relationship with Gavi, was going to drive things “to an outcome.”

“I’m not here to tell you we’re going to yank this thing,” Rubio said, “and we’re not going to listen to his points of view.”

Translation: we are going to yank this thing, and we are done listening to his points of view.

Global health expert J. Stephen Morrison at the Center for Strategic and International Studies was more direct. There has been, Morrison told the Times, “a huge amount of ill will between State and HHS in this period that has not been repaired.” If Rubio wants to restore global confidence in American pandemic leadership, “he’s got to fix this.”

Gavi’s chief executive Dr. Sania Nishtar, upon hearing Rubio’s testimony, welcomed the news with the kind of careful diplomatic enthusiasm reserved for people who have learned not to celebrate too early. She noted that unlocking the frozen funds would allow Gavi to keep the world safe from infectious disease threats and accelerate the rollout of malaria, hexavalent, and meningitis vaccines across Africa.

The $600 million was never Kennedy’s to hold. Congress gave it to the State Department. He held it anyway, for months, while children went unvaccinated and an Ebola strain without a vaccine spread across central Africa. It took a secretary of state publicly reclaiming his own department’s turf to begin reversing that.

That is the international front.

“We Just Need Someone Who’s Not Crazy”

Now the domestic one.

When CNN reported on the White House’s decision to nominate Dr. Erica Schwartz as CDC director in April, a White House official summarized the entire search process in eleven words: “We just need someone who’s not crazy.”

This was not, apparently, as easy as it sounds.

Recall the CDC director timeline of the second Trump administration. Dr. David Weldon, a former Florida congressman, was the first nominee. His confirmation hearing was cancelled an hour before it was scheduled to begin when it became clear he lacked the votes. Susan Monarez followed, serving as CDC director for approximately four weeks before Kennedy had her removed. At her subsequent Senate testimony, Monarez alleged that Kennedy had pressured her to dismiss vaccine officials without cause and approve changes to immunization protocols without scientific evidence. The CDC had a confirmed director for twenty-nine days since Kennedy took office.

Following Monarez’s ouster, Dr. Jay Bhattacharya of the NIH served as acting CDC director — a title that expired under federal law after several months, leaving him in a legal grey zone. In a review of HHS that White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles ordered in January, the administration concluded it needed to stabilize what it had spent a year destabilizing.

Enter Chris Klomp, a Medicare executive installed as HHS’s chief counselor, whose primary job became finding a CDC director who could get through the Senate without immolating the administration’s midterm ambitions. The search criteria, according to CNN’s reporting, explicitly did not require sharing Kennedy’s vaccine skepticism. This was not a small caveat. It was the whole ballgame.

Schwartz is a retired rear admiral, a physician trained at Brown University, a former deputy surgeon general in Trump’s first administration, and a person with no discernible public record of vaccine opposition. When she described her credentials in a public health video, she said: “When I was a military physician, my job was all about readiness. It was all about public health: prevention, vaccines, early detection. If we get that right, we change lives before illness ever begins.”

Kennedy, according to two people familiar with the matter, was initially skeptical of Schwartz as a candidate. He grew more supportive after meeting with her and ultimately signed off on her recommendation to the White House.

Aaron Siri, a vaccine injury lawyer who has served as Kennedy’s personal attorney, told CNN his reading of the situation: “I believe that left to his own devices, Secretary Kennedy would not have chosen her.” He predicted Schwartz would restore the CDC to “business as usual.”

That is the accusation from the MAHA faithful: that the nominee their man signed off on will undo everything he built. It is also, from every other direction, the hope.

Dr. Amesh Adalja of the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security offered the mainstream public health community’s more guarded assessment: “She’s a good and well-qualified nominee, and would be in any administration. However, the issue is not her qualifications — it’s the environment that she’s being asked to work in. If this is supposed to be some kind of pivot away from what’s been going on for the last year, it will all be window dressing if RFK Jr. is still in place.”

That is the unresolved question. You can install a competent CDC director. You cannot install her competence into a department still run by the man whose tenure produced the headline “We just need someone who’s not crazy.”

The Background Witnesses

None of this arrives in isolation. The reader of this series will recall the material we have been holding.

Susan Monarez, in her Senate testimony, described being fired for refusing to rubber-stamp Kennedy’s vaccine agenda. She did not go quietly. She said so, on the record, before the Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor and Pensions.

Former CDC Director Susan Monarez gives her account of firing at a Senate Hearing

Dr. Daniel Jernigan, a senior CDC official who departed during the Kennedy-era upheaval, was asked what qualities the next CDC director would need to possess. His answer: acquiescing to Kennedy’s anti-vaccine views was “likely a necessary job skill.”

That quote has been sitting in this series’ files since April. It is worth deploying now, because Schwartz’s nomination is its direct answer. The White House looked at a CDC that had confirmed leadership for twenty-nine days in over a year, watched the Senate signal it would not confirm another vaccine skeptic, calculated its midterm exposure, and decided that the necessary job skill was, in fact, competence.

The midterm calculus is not incidental. Multiple sources confirm that the White House has been managing Kennedy’s public profile with increasing aggression as November approaches — restricting his vaccine commentary, redirecting his energy toward food dyes and seed oils and Twinkie regulations, keeping him away from the podium on anything that might frighten independent voters. Kennedy’s MAHA agenda has not been abandoned. It has been put on a “low-risk messaging diet,” in Bloomberg’s phrase, and handed a Grok account to generate content about.

Meanwhile, the agencies he spent fourteen months dismantling are being quietly reassembled around him, with personnel he did not choose, by people who answer to different principals.

The Containment Doctrine

Let us be precise about what is and is not happening here.

Kennedy has not been fired. He retains his title, his cabinet seat, and his ability to continue doing damage. Trust in federal health agencies has plummeted during his tenure, according to KFF polling conducted in February — across partisan lines, which is the detail that frightens Republican senators more than any Democrat ever could. The CDC’s morale, by Kennedy’s own admission to a House committee, reached “a nadir” last year. Vaccination rates have dropped. The leucovorin debacle — in which Kennedy and FDA Commissioner Marty Makary announced from the White House podium that a chemotherapy drug showed promise as an autism treatment, prescriptions surged 71% among children, and the supporting study was subsequently retracted — still hangs over the department like a weather system that won’t move.

What is happening is not accountability. It is management. The administration has concluded that Kennedy’s upside — the MAHA base, the name recognition, the symbolic gesture toward health-consciousness — is no longer worth his downside, which includes blocked congressional appropriations, Senate confirmation failures, and a mounting body of public health evidence that is becoming harder to finesse into a midterm message.

So Rubio reclaims the Gavi file. Klomp recruits a CDC director. Kennedy appears before appropriations committees and talks about how much better things are going. The picture being assembled is of a department that has turned the corner, with stability on the horizon and a new team ready to — as Kennedy himself put it — “revolutionize CDC and get it back on track.”

This is, to put it gently, an extraordinary thing for a man to say about an agency he spent a year methodically destroying.

What Containment Doesn’t Fix

Dr. Adalja’s warning deserves to be the closing note of this entry, not a footnote.

“If this is supposed to be some kind of pivot away from what’s been going on for the last year, it will all be window dressing if RFK Jr. is still in place.”

Erica Schwartz will walk into an agency where morale has been at a nadir, where experienced officials have departed in waves, where the vaccine advisory committee was purged and replaced with political loyalists (a decision a federal judge has since found likely violated administrative law), and where the institutional memory of decades of public health infrastructure has been compressed into whatever managed to survive. She will report to Robert F. Kennedy Jr.

Dr Erica Schwartz

Rubio’s Gavi re-engagement, however welcome, does not address the $600 million that sat frozen for months while children in low-income countries went unvaccinated and an Ebola strain spread without an approved countermeasure. It does not restore the global health surveillance networks the administration dismantled. It does not rebuild the trust that Gavi’s CEO is carefully welcoming back one cautious statement at a time.

What Tuesday’s testimony confirmed is that the State Department has decided it cannot afford to let Kennedy continue running a rogue foreign health policy from HHS. What April’s nomination confirmed is that the White House has decided it cannot afford to let him continue running a rogue domestic health policy from CDC. What neither action confirmed is that anyone in this administration intends to reckon honestly with what fourteen months of Kennedy’s leadership has cost.

The Secretary Wars are real. The containment is real. But containment is not the same as accountability, and management is not the same as repair.

Robert F. Kennedy Jr. remains America’s Medical Enemy No. 1. He is simply being managed, now, rather than celebrated.

The distinction matters. The damage does not change.
