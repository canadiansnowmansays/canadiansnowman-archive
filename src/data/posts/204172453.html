OpenAI’s CEO signed a 2023 letter acknowledging that AI might cause human extinction. Anthropic’s CEO has said AI will “test us as a species.” A March poll found a majority of American voters believe the technology’s risks outweigh its benefits. The industry’s own leaders have spent three years telling the public, in effect, to be afraid — and the public listened.

Now, with the midterms approaching, the industry is trying to buy its way out of the trust deficit its own rhetoric helped create. As Will Gottsegen reported for The Atlantic, the mechanics of this effort borrow their playbook from an industry this series has not previously covered, and that playbook is about to face its first real test in a single congressional district.

I. The Crypto Precedent

The model is Fairshake, the cryptocurrency industry’s 2024 super PAC. Crypto and venture capital firms poured hundreds of millions of dollars into the group, which backed pro-crypto candidates and worked to defeat anti-crypto ones across both parties. The playbook worked. Fairshake-backed candidates won. Congress grew measurably more receptive to crypto. The industry collected several major policy victories the following year.

AI’s super PACs are running the same play under different conditions, and the differences matter more than the similarities. Crypto, at its 2024 peak, had been used or invested in by under one-fifth of American adults — a niche financial product with a correspondingly niche political footprint. AI is used by roughly half of American adults through tools like ChatGPT, and is, per the polling Gottsegen cites, broadly distrusted by the people using it. No candidate in the current cycle is running on banning AI outright, the way some politicians ran against crypto. The fight is not existence versus prohibition. It is over what kind of regulatory regime an enormously powerful, already-ubiquitous technology will operate under — which means the money is not buying survival. It is buying the shape of the rules.

That distinction matters more than it first appears. A niche product with a small user base can be defended or attacked without most voters noticing either way; Fairshake’s money moved a policy outcome that the median American had no personal stake in and little occasion to resent. AI has no such insulation. Every one of the tens of millions of people who has used ChatGPT, been denied a loan by an algorithm, or watched a data center get approved two miles from their house has some basis for a personal opinion about what the rules should be — which means this fight, unlike crypto’s, is being waged in full view of an electorate that already has priors, and those priors, per the polling, run against the industry doing the spending.

II. Two Bets on the Same Technology

Two super PACs have emerged with opposite theories of what the industry needs from politics, and the split runs straight through the center of this series’ core argument about who gets to govern AI.

Leading the Future, co-founded by venture capital firm Andreessen Horowitz, has raised more than $140 million on a regulation-light platform explicitly devoted to “identifying, maintaining, and growing pro-AI candidates.” Contributors include the firm’s own founders and OpenAI President Greg Brockman — though OpenAI itself has issued a statement distancing the company from the PAC, a hedge worth noting given how closely the group’s stated priorities track the company’s public positions.

Anthropic, for its part, has donated $20 million to Public First Action, a nonprofit that works with super PACs to support candidates focused on AI safety, touting “comprehensive regulation” over what it calls the “move fast and break things” approach. Anthropic has stated the donation is reserved for the group’s AI-education initiatives and “cannot be used for federal election activity” — a careful legal distinction that does not change the basic fact that the company most associated, across this series, with seeking statutory AI governance is now also a financial backer of a political infrastructure built to advance that preference. One of Public First Action’s co-founders described the group bluntly, as “the anti-super PAC super PAC” — built specifically to counter Leading the Future and the Trump-aligned donor network behind it.

This is worth sitting with for a moment, because it complicates a clean reading of Anthropic as simply the responsible actor in this series’ running narrative. The company that asked for “a statutory process that is transparent, fair, clear, and grounded in technical facts” in Part 49, and that is currently suing the federal government over the supply chain risk designation referenced throughout Parts 18 through 51, is also now spending money to shape an electoral outcome in its preferred policy direction. That is not hypocrisy — democratic participation through lawful political spending is not the same thing as the unaccountable executive action this series has spent a dozen parts criticizing. But it is a reminder that “the side that wants regulation” and “the side spending money to win elections” are not opposing camps in this fight. They are the same camp, applying the same tool — concentrated capital — toward a different policy outcome than its rival. A voter trying to decide which super PAC represents the public interest has, in practice, a choice between two versions of the same industry deciding what the public interest should be.

III. The Final Exam, in New York’s Twelfth District

The clearest test case is a single House race. Alex Bores, a former Palantir employee who left the company after it renewed its contract with ICE, is running in New York’s Twelfth Congressional District as the candidate who understands Big Tech’s power well enough to regulate it.

Leading the Future’s ads call him a “hypocrite” who would stifle AI’s progress. Politico’s tally puts tech-industry-affiliated spending against Bores at $26 million; Public First Action and aligned groups have spent $18 million in his support. Political strategist Cooper Teboe told Gottsegen the race “will be viewed as the final exam” for the entire model of AI-backed political spending — if Leading the Future’s money defeats Bores, the playbook gets doubled down on nationally. If it doesn’t, the AI industry will have spent tens of millions of dollars discovering that Fairshake’s crypto-era formula does not transfer cleanly to a technology the public already associates with job loss, surveillance, and existential risk warnings issued by the technology’s own creators.

There is an early signal worth noting, and it cuts against the side spending more money. Leading the Future’s attack ads have, by Gottsegen’s account, arguably increased Bores’s profile and strengthened his appeal specifically among AI-skeptical voters — the opposite of the intended effect. Bores’s own campaign has leaned into this directly, running an ad that satirizes an “AI super PAC” with a menacing robot voice trying to destroy him, positioning Bores as the reasonable adult in the room. If a $26 million attack campaign is functioning as free advertising for the candidate it targets, that is not a minor tactical misstep. It suggests the entire premise — that AI-industry money can purchase the same kind of electoral outcome crypto money purchased in 2024 — may not hold for a technology whose public reputation problem the industry’s own leaders manufactured through years of extinction-risk rhetoric.

IV. The Anger Is Not Abstract

The backdrop against which this money is being spent is not generic skepticism. It has a documented edge, and Gottsegen’s reporting catalogs it without softening it.

Commencement speakers at the University of Central Florida, the University of Arizona, and Middle Tennessee State University were booed by graduating students this spring for praising AI’s importance to their futures. Seven in ten Americans, per the polling cited, do not want a data center built in their own area. Two months before Gottsegen’s reporting, an Indiana politician’s home was shot at thirteen times, with a handwritten note reading “No Data Centers” left at the scene. This is not the ambient grumbling this series has occasionally described as background skepticism. It is active, organized, and in at least one documented instance, violent opposition to the physical infrastructure the AI industry needs to keep operating at its current scale.

This context reframes what the super PAC spending is actually trying to accomplish. It is not simply trying to win a policy argument among informed voters. It is trying to overcome a documented, intensifying, occasionally violent public backlash using the same financial instrument — concentrated capital directed at electoral outcomes — that this series has identified throughout as part of the underlying problem. Gottsegen notes that the industry itself appears to recognize this: supersize personalities like Sam Altman and Dario Amodei, prone to discussing AI’s power and risk in extreme terms, are increasingly understood inside the industry as poorly suited to reassuring an already-distrustful public. The solution being attempted is not better public communication from those leaders. It is replacing the argument with spending.

V. TACO, Applied to a Single District

Run this through the framework this series has used since its earlier parts. Threaten shows up faintly in the industry’s own extinction-risk rhetoric — Altman’s signature, Amodei’s “test us as a species” framing — which functions less as a warning to regulators than as an implicit argument for why the people making it should be trusted to self-govern rather than be governed. Accommodate is Public First Action itself: real money, positioned as the safety-minded alternative, still operating through the same super PAC architecture it claims to be countering. Capitulate — either side abandoning electoral spending in favor of a transparent, statutory process neither PAC’s existence actually requires — is nowhere on the ballot. Obfuscate is Leading the Future’s own attack line against Bores, calling a former Palantir employee who left over an ICE contract a “hypocrite,” which asks voters to focus on his résumé rather than the substance of what he’s proposing to regulate.

VI. The Test This Series Has Been Waiting For

This series has spent fifty-four parts asking whether AI will be governed democratically or oligarchically, and the AI super PAC fight is the first place that question will be settled by an actual vote rather than a court filing, an executive order, or a Substack essay’s argument.

Both sides of this fight are, in the literal sense, playing by democratic rules — raising money lawfully, running ads, supporting candidates, abiding by the outcome of an election. That is meaningfully different from the Friday Night Order chronicled in Part 49, where one government agency disabled a company’s products with ninety minutes’ notice and no statutory basis. Electoral spending, however lopsided, is accountable in a way executive fiat is not: voters can punish a candidate for taking AI-industry money, the way Bores’s campaign is betting they will. The question the New York race will actually answer is narrower than “will democracy or oligarchy govern AI.” It is whether an electorate already primed by years of the industry’s own apocalyptic rhetoric, and now watching its representatives bought and sold by the same industry in real time, still has enough functioning democratic reflexes left to punish that behavior at the ballot box — or whether the concentration of capital this series has tracked since Part 48 has already proceeded far enough that $44 million combined spending in a single House race is simply how policy gets decided now, regardless of which side’s money wins.

Either answer will tell this series something it has not yet been able to measure directly: not what the industry wants, which has been documented exhaustively across fifty-four parts, but what the public will actually do about it, given the chance. If Bores wins despite being outspent, that is the first hard evidence this series has found that the democratic side of its founding question still has a pulse. If he loses, the honest conclusion is not that voters were persuaded — it’s that they were simply outspent, which is a different and more troubling verdict on the choice this whole series exists to track.
