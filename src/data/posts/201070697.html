I. The Statement

The president of the United States walked into the Oval Office on Wednesday afternoon and told reporters that the war he started ninety-nine days ago was “not a big thing.”

By this morning, Israeli jets were striking petrochemical plants in southern Iran, Iranian missiles were hitting central and southern Israel, the Houthis had announced a Red Sea naval blockade against Israel, and Trump was posting on social media that "both sides" were seeking "an immediate CEASEFIRE" — a claim that arrived while the strikes were still in progress. By Monday afternoon, Iran's Revolutionary Guards signaled they were standing down — for now — warning that any resumed Israeli strikes would bring "much harsher and more forceful actions than before." Netanyahu had still not commented publicly, eighteen hours into the crisis he started.

“We have the highest stock market in history with a military conflict going on, or a war — some people call it war, some people call it a military — it’s not a big thing for us,” he said. “We have a great military. It’s not a big thing for us.”

Thirteen American service members are dead. An estimated seventeen hundred Iranian civilians are dead. The global energy system is in its most serious disruption since the 1970s oil crisis. Regular gasoline at American pumps is approaching seven dollars a gallon. A civilian airport was on fire when he spoke. Four members of his own party had just voted with Democrats to demand that Congress authorize or end the war. And the secretary of state had told a Senate committee that same morning that the military operation “has concluded” — a claim so detached from observable reality that it had to share a news cycle with footage of a Kuwait airport terminal with a hole in its roof and flames pouring through it.

Not a big thing.

The remark was not a gaffe. It was not a slip. It was a strategy — the same strategy the administration has deployed since the first week of the war: if the president treats the war as minor, perhaps voters will agree. The difficulty, as this week demonstrated comprehensively, is that reality does not participate in the strategy.

By Sunday evening, Iran had fired ballistic missiles at Israel for the first time since the April cease-fire. The cease-fire that both sides insist remains in effect produced, in the span of five days, a burning airport, a dead House resolution, two collapsed Lebanon ceasefires, an Israeli strike on Beirut, and an Iranian missile barrage on northern Israel. The week ended with schools closed across Israel, the U.S. Embassy ordering staff to shelter in place, and Iran’s chief negotiator declaring American and Israeli military assets in the region “legitimate targets.”

Not a big thing.

II. The Airport

A photograph released by Kuwaiti state media showed officials inspecting damage in a terminal at Kuwait International Airport after an attack on Wednesday.Credit...Kuwait News Agency, via Reuters

The week’s worst morning began before dawn Kuwait time. Thirteen ballistic missiles and seventeen drones struck Kuwait International Airport’s Terminal 1 during the tail end of Eid al-Adha holiday travel. An Indian resident of Kuwait was killed. Sixty-three people were wounded — passengers, airport workers, ground crew. Videos verified by the New York Times showed the terminal interior filling with flames and smoke, a gaping hole torn in the roof, people running.

The terminal had reopened to travelers exactly two days earlier, after months of closure due to the conflict. Kuwait’s civil aviation authority suspended flights. Kuwaiti health officials said over twenty-five ambulances attended the scene and doctors performed at least seven emergency surgeries. By early afternoon, a different terminal had partially reopened. The Kuwaiti foreign ministry summoned the Iranian chargé d’affaires, lodged an official protest, and expelled two Iranian diplomats with twenty-four hours to leave the country.

Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps claimed its Aerospace Force had not fired at the terminal — that the damage was caused by a failed American Patriot interceptor that fell on the building. U.S. Central Command called the claim “Totally FALSE” and stated that Iran had “struck the civilian airport with drones.” Iran’s foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, offered a separate explanation: Iran was targeting sites the United States was permitted to use to launch attacks, and the strikes were “self-defense” in response to Secretary Rubio naming Kuwait and the UAE as “very cooperative” U.S. allies.

Self-defense. The airport was self-defense.

Bahrain was targeted the same morning — three Iranian missiles and several drones aimed at civilian sites, all intercepted, the IRGC separately claiming it had struck the headquarters of the U.S. Fifth Fleet. Central Command denied that too.

The cease-fire, both sides still insist, remains in effect.

III. The Testimony

On the same morning the airport burned, Secretary of State Marco Rubio appeared before a Senate appropriations subcommittee and told Congress the war “has concluded.”

Any American strikes on Iran now, Rubio explained, were “completely defensive in nature.” The original aim of the operation was to demolish the “shield” of conventional weapons Iran used to protect its nuclear program — missiles, drones, its navy. That mission, he said, was accomplished.

He also said the administration was seeking a long-term agreement under which Iran would commit to twenty years of zero uranium enrichment, which he offered as substantially better than the Obama-era JCPOA, with its fifteen-year sunset clause. He did not rule out a sunset clause in any new agreement. He said sanctions relief would be “conditions-based.” He said the administration would press Iran on the “disposition” of its highly enriched uranium — without explaining what “disposition” meant.

He told Representative Joaquin Castro of Texas, who asked whether the United States knew Israel had nuclear weapons, that “most of the world assesses that they do,” but that American foreign policy had long declined to discuss it officially. He told lawmakers the U.S. did not support “greater Israel” or West Bank annexation, and did not mention a Palestinian state — a concept that had been U.S. policy for decades.

Senator Cory Booker, Democrat of New Jersey, interjected during Rubio’s testimony: “The war is not over.” The secretary continued.

The photograph from the hearing room captures something the transcript does not. Rubio is seated at the witness table, hand resting on his notes, jaw set, looking forward. An older white-haired senator leans across his shoulder from behind, mouth near Rubio’s ear, mid-whisper. The packed gallery watches without expression. What is said into the microphone is the cover story. What is said into the ear is the negotiation. Rubio insisted “the war is over.” The airport was on fire.

IV. The Situation Room

The Atlantic published a reconstruction this week — by Vivian Salama, Jonathan Lemire, and Nancy Youssef — of what has gone wrong in the eight weeks since the April cease-fire was supposed to produce a deal. It is the most important single piece of reporting on the war’s diplomatic failure since Karim Sadjadpour’s structural diagnosis three weeks ago.

The centrepiece is a Situation Room conference call Trump convened in late May. He had gathered leaders from eight countries — Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Turkey, Egypt, Jordan, Bahrain, and Pakistan — to build regional support for a deal he believed was within reach. He went through them one by one seeking sign-off, his tone suggesting, according to officials briefed on the call, that each country owed him a debt for having taken on Iran.

Then Trump reached further. He proposed linking the Iran negotiations to a major expansion of the Abraham Accords. Get on board, he suggested. Several countries that hadn’t normalized relations with Israel should do so now.

One leader said it was “an interesting suggestion.” The rest: silence.

“Hello? Hello? Anyone there?”

The president of the United States, convener of the call, interlocutor to eight heads of state, asked three times if anyone was listening. The silence was their answer.

The Atlantic’s diagnosis for what the silence represents is “forever limbo.” Not a forever war — something more insidious. All sides have sufficient incentive to stay at the table but not enough to make binding commitments. The deal’s most significant clause may be the one permitting indefinite 60-day extensions of the negotiating period after the initial two months expire. The deal whose most durable feature is the mechanism for not making a deal.

The Obama obsession runs through the reconstruction. Trump repeatedly complained to aides that the emerging framework was being compared to the JCPOA — the agreement he spent years attacking and tore up in his first term. He wanted something he could claim was better than what Obama got. An Iranian pledge to never develop a nuclear weapon was one answer. Transferring Iran’s enriched uranium stockpile to a third country was another. Both stalled. The pallets of cash phobia blocked sanctions relief discussions.

The second round of diplomatic talks — with Iranian Foreign Minister Araghchi expected to participate, Vance’s team prepared to travel, reporters already assembled at Joint Base Andrews — was cancelled at the last minute. Trump told advisers he was in no hurry. “We’re not gonna be traveling 15, 16 hours to have a meeting with people that nobody ever heard of. Too much travel.”

Intermediaries in the region who believed negotiations were gaining momentum were stunned. Trump’s read: “When they want, they can call me. We have all the cards. We won everything.”

V. The Big Thing

President Trump has said the main reason he went to war on Feb. 28 was to prevent Iran from acquiring a nuclear weapon. Credit...Doug Mills/The New York Times

On Sunday, the New York Times published a fact-check of Trump’s claim to have secured Iran’s commitment never to develop a nuclear weapon — the breakthrough he has repeatedly described as “the big thing,” “very interesting,” a signature achievement of the war.

Nuclear experts were puzzled. Iran has made this exact pledge for more than fifty years.

Iran joined the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty in 1970. Its supreme leader issued a religious ruling — a fatwa — declaring that the production, stockpiling, and use of nuclear weapons are forbidden under Islam. The 2015 JCPOA, in its very first paragraph, contains Iran’s explicit reaffirmation that “under no circumstances will Iran ever seek, develop or acquire any nuclear weapons.” Trump withdrew from that agreement, called it weak, and spent years denouncing it.

The “big thing” was in the preamble of the deal he destroyed.

Gary Samore, a veteran arms control expert who served on Obama’s National Security Council: “It’s not much of a concession.” Robert Einhorn, a former State Department arms control official: “In reality, no conceivable negotiated outcome can guarantee that Iran will never acquire nuclear weapons.” What matters, experts agreed, is not the pledge but what specific, verifiable limits are placed on Iran’s enrichment program — exactly the technical detail the JCPOA spent eighteen months negotiating and Trump declared worthless.

Trump went to war to extract a commitment Iran had already made, that was already enshrined in the agreement he tore up, and is now presenting it as the war’s defining achievement. The circle, at last, is complete.

VI. The Iranians

A street vendor in Tehran in front of a wall with an anti-U.S. message.Credit...Arash Khamooshi/Polaris for The New York Times

Farnaz Fassihi of the New York Times, who has covered Iran for three decades, spent recent days on voice calls with more than twenty Iranians in Tehran, Isfahan, Ahvaz, and Mashhad. Their accounts arrived as the missiles were already in the air on Sunday evening. They deserve to be read in that context.

Kimia is twenty-five years old, a designer in Tehran. “I’m angry. I feel alone,” she said. “We don’t matter to the world at all; we’re only seen as tools for war and negotiation, while we are human beings.”

Amirali is sixty-two, an engineer in Tehran. He had hoped the war might end the theocratic government. Then he learned — from a New York Times disclosure — that the United States and Israel had initially planned to install Mahmoud Ahmadinejad as Iran’s replacement leader. A former hard-line president. A man many Iranian opposition figures despise as deeply as they despise the regime itself. “What was this all for?” Amirali asked. “They bombed and destroyed our country, our airports, roads and factories in the name of regime change so they could bring Ahmadinejad? This shows the goal was never to make Iran better or free.”

Hamed Mirzaei did not give an interview. He posted on Instagram. He is the sole survivor of an Israeli airstrike on Resalat Square in Tehran in March that killed twelve members of his family: his wife of one year, his parents, his grandparents, his uncle, his cousins. He shared his wedding photographs. “Until the last day I’m alive, I will not let your names be forgotten, I will speak of each and every one of you. I will not let you die in vain.”

Mehdi is fifty-two, a government employee. He has attended pro-government rallies with his wife and children. His salary runs out by the middle of the month. He cannot afford beef or chicken. He bought groceries on credit from a neighbourhood shop; when he returned to pay, the prices had doubled. “Everybody is angry over the economy and if the government doesn’t fix things, there will be trouble.” A pro-government conservative named Hamed added: “The rising prices don’t distinguish between supporters and opponents of the government; it’s affecting us all.”

Iran’s official statistics centre reported last week that cooking oil has increased in price by 430 percent compared with this period last year. Eggs: 345 percent. Rice: 287 percent. Milk: 139 percent. Pharmacies are rationing medicine. The health ministry has instructed doctors to prescribe only essential medications because of shortages. A plastic bottle factory near Mashhad has shut down and furloughed all employees — it cannot get raw materials after Israeli strikes on Iran’s petrochemical industry. Iran has no reserves of haemophilia medication. The internet was blocked from the start of the war until late May.

Ellie Geranmayeh of the European Council on Foreign Relations: “Accepting the resilience of the regime despite all your hopes is a very bitter moment for those opposing the regime to digest. It has arguably left the regime more powerful than it was.”

Sanam Vakil of Chatham House: “Nobody is thinking of them or taking their opinions into consideration. They are just a casualty in this conflict that is beyond their influence and grasp.”

On Sunday evening, Iran fired ballistic missiles at Israel. The Iranians Fassihi interviewed were already living inside the consequences of the last round. The next round began before her article had finished its first day of publication.

VII. The Spies

President Trump and Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu last December. The United States and Israel were largely aligned at the war’s outset, with Mr. Trump endorsing Mr. Netanyahu’s goal to oust the Iranian government.Credit...Tierney L. Cross/The New York Times

On Friday, the New York Times reported that the Pentagon’s Defense Intelligence Agency has raised its counterintelligence threat assessment for Israel to “critical” — the highest level — from “high.” Israel’s counterintelligence threat rating is now higher than any other U.S. ally. It is higher than some adversarial countries. Of all American allies, only South Korea approaches it in certain situations.

The recent reporting triggering the upgrade centres on Israeli spy agencies eavesdropping on American negotiators working to end the war. Specific targets named in intelligence reports include Steve Witkoff, Trump’s top negotiator; Elbridge Colby, the Pentagon’s chief policy official and a prominent advocate of strategic restraint; and one of Colby’s main deputies, Michael DiMino, who handles Pentagon Middle East policy.

The DIA report — to which the Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency also contributed — documents American defense personnel in Israel detecting spyware surreptitiously installed on their phones. It also chronicles earlier incidents: in 2021, Israeli military intelligence officers were caught planting listening devices at DIA headquarters. Last year, Shin Bet officers were discovered attempting to plant a listening device in a Secret Service vehicle.

One senior American official, speaking on condition of anonymity, described Israel’s intelligence collection on top U.S. officials during the second Trump administration as “unhinged.”

The White House denied the account. The Israeli embassy said Israel does not spy on American officials.

The strategic logic requires no interpretation. The United States and Israel were aligned at the war’s outset — Trump endorsed Netanyahu’s long-sought goal of regime change on Day One. But war aims diverged quickly: the U.S. moved toward a negotiated settlement that would constrain Iran’s capabilities; Israel moved toward degrading Iran and Hezbollah comprehensively, with regime change still the horizon. Israel is now spying on the American negotiators working to end the war Israel wants to continue. The ally is surveilling the ally’s attempt to stop fighting on the ally’s behalf.

They are working side by side at U.S. Central Command. There is a U.S.-only floor and an Israeli-only floor. The building has walls. The phones do not.

VIII. The Congress

On Wednesday evening, the House of Representatives voted 215 to 208 to direct President Trump to withdraw U.S. forces from the Iran conflict or obtain congressional authorization to continue.

Four Republicans crossed the aisle: Tom Barrett of Michigan, a first-term member facing a competitive re-election race; Brian Fitzpatrick of Pennsylvania; Warren Davidson of Ohio, who had previously crossed in March, reversed under intense party pressure, and crossed again; and Thomas Massie of Kentucky. Democrat Jared Golden of Maine, who had previously opposed similar measures, switched to support.

The vote was the second time in five weeks that a handful of Republicans had broken with the president on the war. Republican leaders had postponed this vote two weeks earlier, recognizing they lacked the votes to defeat it, hoping to win converts. They did not win a single one.

Speaker Mike Johnson called passage “a very dangerous prospect” that would “weaken” the president’s ability to seek a peaceful resolution. Trump, informed of the result, posted on social media that the vote came “right in the middle of my final negotiations to end the War with the Islamic Republic of Iran,” and called it “unpatriotic.”

The four Republicans who voted against him are unpatriotic. The definition of patriotism, in this framework, is deference to a president who finds the war boring and called it “not a big thing.”

Warren Davidson’s statement deserves to stand in the record: “Define the mission. Authorize the mission. Accomplish the mission.” Three sentences. Ninety-nine days in, none of the three has occurred.

The War Powers Resolution now heads to the Senate, which must take it up within approximately two and a half weeks. A similar Senate measure, which passed with four Republican defections last month, faces procedural hurdles. Even if both chambers passed their resolutions, the Supreme Court’s 1983 ruling makes legal enforcement nearly impossible without Trump’s signature or a two-thirds override. The practical effect is limited. The political signal is not. Mike Murphy, a Republican strategist, put the dynamics plainly: “He’s a gorilla in the Republican primaries, but he is a wounded sparrow among the general electorate.”

The midterms are five months away.

IX. The Lebanon That Wasn’t — And Then Was

On Wednesday evening, as the House vote was being tallied, the State Department released a joint statement: Israel and Lebanon had agreed to renew their ceasefire, after two days of U.S.-mediated talks in Washington. The agreement called for “a complete cessation of Hezbollah fire and the evacuation of all Hezbollah operatives” from southern Lebanon, the creation of “pilot zones” under exclusive Lebanese Armed Forces control, and the exclusion of all non-state actors. The two sides “reaffirmed no hostile intent toward one another” and agreed to reconvene June 22.

There was one notable absence from the negotiations. Hezbollah — the organization the agreement required to disarm, withdraw, and cease fire — was not a party to the talks. The Lebanese government, which signed the statement, has no power to compel Hezbollah’s compliance.

By Thursday morning, Hezbollah had fired two rocket salvos at Israeli troops in the border region. By Thursday evening, Hezbollah’s leader, Naim Qassem, had formally rejected the agreement, calling it “humiliating” — tantamount to “surrender.” His terms: any ceasefire must include Israel’s withdrawal from southern Lebanon. “As long as the occupation continues, the resistance will continue.” Israel’s defense minister, Israel Katz, confirmed that Israeli forces would continue operating in Lebanon “at this stage.”

Smoke rising from an Israeli airstrike in front of the Beaufort Castle, as seen from Nabatieh, Lebanon, last week.Credit...Daniel Berehulak/The New York Times

By Friday, the ceasefire was dead. Israeli evacuation orders extended to Anqoun — a hillside town sixteen miles from the Israeli border, within commuting distance of Beirut, sheltering twenty-five hundred people who had already fled their homes and were now required to flee again. Six UNIFIL peacekeepers have been killed this year. Parliament Speaker Nabih Berri, the key intermediary between Washington and Hezbollah, said the agreement had been “booby-trapped” with one-sided conditions.

Lebanese President Joseph Aoun, in remarks to CNN, turned his criticism toward Tehran: “This is not your country. It’s our country. They are using Lebanon as a bargaining chip in their negotiation with the United States. It’s unacceptable.”

And then Sunday arrived.

Earlier this week, Trump had expended real political capital to stop Israel from striking Beirut. The phone call was ugly enough that he admitted it publicly — confirming he had called Netanyahu “fucking crazy” and told him “I’m saving your ass.” Netanyahu backed down. Trump claimed credit. The intervention was presented as a diplomatic success: the Lebanon track had been preserved, the Iran negotiations could continue, the ceasefire would hold a little longer.

On Sunday, Hezbollah fired rockets into northern Israel. Netanyahu — who had publicly promised to strike Beirut if Hezbollah attacked Israeli territory again — ordered strikes on the Dahiya, Hezbollah’s stronghold on the southern outskirts of the Lebanese capital. At least two apartment buildings were hit. At least two people were killed. No civilian warning was issued before the strike.

The NYT’s David Halbfinger noted Monday that for Netanyahu, resuming the fighting with Iran offered clear short-term advantages: it showed his restive political base he was willing to stand up to Trump, who had publicly scolded him, and it complicated any peace agreement that would tie Israel’s hands against Hezbollah. “There are no good choices here,” said retired Israeli military intelligence officer Danny Citrinowicz — but the absence of good choices did not make the sequence accidental.

The sequence deserves to be read plainly. Trump humiliates Netanyahu into standing down earlier in the week. Netanyahu finds his pretext within days. Strikes Beirut anyway. Iran retaliates against Israel directly — ballistic missiles, the first since April. Iran’s chief negotiator, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, declares that U.S. and Israeli bases and assets in the region are now “legitimate targets.” The Israeli military says Iran committed “a grave error” and that it “will not allow” Iran to forge a new equation. Hard-liners in Netanyahu’s cabinet: “Tehran must burn tonight.”

The timing of all this is not coincidental. Netanyahu does not want the deal Trump is trying to make. A U.S.-Iran agreement that leaves Hezbollah functional and the Islamic Republic governing — perhaps weakened, perhaps not — is not what Israel went to war for. Netanyahu has spent decades working toward a joint U.S.-Israeli campaign to eliminate the Iranian threat comprehensively. Every escalation that collapses the ceasefire framework, from his perspective, is not a setback. It is the point.

Trump told Fox News his message to Iran was: “You’ve shot your missiles, that’s enough. Get back to the table and make a deal.” By Monday morning, Israeli jets were striking petrochemical plants across Iran. By Monday afternoon, Iran’s Revolutionary Guards signaled they were standing down — warning that any resumed Israeli strikes, “including in southern Lebanon,” would bring “much harsher and more forceful actions than before.” Netanyahu had still not commented publicly, eighteen hours into the crisis he started. The New York Times reported the de-escalation as it unfolded.

The cease-fire Trump spent his credibility preserving on Monday was undone by the ally he preserved it for on Sunday. The April Lebanon ceasefire and the June Lebanon ceasefire have now produced identical results. Sunday’s missiles and Monday’s airstrikes produced a third sequence on top of them. Iran’s Foreign Ministry warned of a “crushing and comprehensive” response to any further Israeli action. The building is still smoking. The ceasefire both sides insist is in effect has now survived a burning airport, two collapsed Lebanon agreements, a Beirut strike, an Iranian missile barrage, and an Israeli assault on Iran’s largest petrochemical complex.

Not a big thing.

X. The Economics

The New York Times introduced a term this week that the energy industry has been using since March: “demand destruction.” Goldman Sachs flagged it in their March analysis. The International Energy Agency projected in April that oil demand would shrink by 1.5 million barrels per day this quarter. What began as a price shock is becoming a structural shift.

In the short term: people cannot afford the prices and are finding alternatives. Zoom calls instead of drives. Vacations closer to home. South Korea has advised citizens to ride bicycles, take shorter showers, and has ordered government vehicles off the road one workday per week. Lower-income American consumers are already cutting back on gas, according to Federal Reserve Bank of New York research.

In the longer term: the changes people are making now — including accelerated turns toward electric vehicles and renewable energy — may permanently suppress demand for oil. The last comparable demand destruction event was the 1970s energy crisis, which permanently reshaped American fuel-economy policy.

Even if the Strait of Hormuz reopens tomorrow, the MIT energy economist Catherine Wolfram noted, prices may not simply normalize. Iran has bombed refineries. The physical damage to energy infrastructure will take far longer to repair than any diplomatic resolution. The price of oil is not simply a function of the Strait’s status.

Following Sunday’s Iranian missile barrage on Israel, Brent Crude rose nearly three percent. Oil closed the week at approximately ninety-six dollars a barrel. Regular gasoline at the pump: $6.92. The S&P 500 is at a record high. The president says everybody is making a lot of money.

XI. What He Said

The TACO framework — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — has operated throughout this war on diplomatic positions: what the administration says it wants versus what it will settle for, what it announces versus what it delivers. This week it operated on something more fundamental: the campaign promise that the war was never supposed to happen.

Trump ran on ending endless wars. The pledge was explicit, repeated, and central to his 2024 coalition — veterans, libertarian-leaning Republicans, the entire wing of the party that had grown exhausted by two decades of Middle Eastern conflict. On Sunday, he told NBC’s Kristen Welker it never happened. “I didn’t guarantee no war. Why would I have built the strongest military in the world?”

He did promise. On election night 2024, in his victory speech, he said: "They said, 'He will start a war.' I'm not going to start a war. I'm going to stop wars."

The Obfuscate move, applied retroactively to electoral history. The war that was not supposed to exist has now lasted ninety-nine days, cost approximately twenty-nine billion dollars through May, killed thirteen Americans, and the president’s response is to deny the premise of the original promise.

He raised Vietnam himself, unprompted. Endless wars are bad — unless they’re his, in which case Vietnam is the relevant benchmark, and three months is nothing by that standard.

The Oval Office appearance on Wednesday produced its own inventory of statements worth preserving.

On the enriched uranium removal Trump announced last week: “Very overrated. I’m the one that overrated it. To me it was important, to other people it’s not important.”

On whether Iran had agreed to his nuclear condition: “We’re going to have to stop them from having a nuclear weapon — that’s what we’ve done — and they’ve agreed to it, by the way.” Then, in the same breath: “if they sign the agreement.” Then: negotiations were now about whether Iran would buy a nuclear weapon rather than develop one. Then: “in the end we got that.” Then: “if they sign the paper.” Then: “In theory they’re pretty close to signing a paper.”

On what comes next: “We could go another two, three weeks and just wipe everybody out. I’d rather not do that. Very easy to do. They’re ready to do it. They want to do it. But if we can get something down in writing, which will accomplish the same thing without killing everybody, I’d like to do that.”

On whether the Strait of Hormuz would still be blocked by Labor Day: “I don’t know.”

On Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei — who has not been seen in public since inheriting the supreme leadership in March, and who is believed to have been injured on the first day of the war Trump started: “We probably will meet at some point.” And: “If you believe the stories, he’s missing a lot of different parts.”

On the NBC interview, recorded Friday, Trump said he was not demanding a Lebanon ceasefire as part of the final Iran deal. “I think they’d like to see it, but I’m not demanding.” Within hours of that recording, Israel struck Beirut. Within hours of the Beirut strike, Iran fired missiles at Israel.

The man at the other end of these negotiations is rumoured to be missing limbs. The ally conducting the negotiations is being spied on by the partner conducting the war alongside it. The ceasefire has produced a missile barrage. The war cost twenty-nine billion dollars through May and is accelerating. The paper may or may not be signed. The Strait may or may not open by Labor Day.

It’s not a big thing.

The Atlantic‘s reconstruction of the diplomatic failure closes with a question the editors left deliberately unanswered: whether Trump “may be content to simply wait rather than do a deal that invites unflattering comparisons to one that already existed — and which didn’t come at the cost of 13 U.S. service members and at least 1,700 Iranian civilians, tens of billions of dollars, the depletion of U.S. munitions stockpiles, and a global energy crisis.”

The question is rhetorical. The answer is in the Oval Office transcript. And in the NBC interview. And in the Truth Social posts. And in the Situation Room silence. And in the missiles over northern Israel on Sunday night.
