Every Canadian of a certain age remembers the Orange Wave. In the federal election of 2 May 2011, the New Democratic Party won 59 of Quebec’s 75 seats, many of them held by candidates who had barely campaigned and in at least one celebrated case had spent part of the writ period on holiday in Las Vegas. The party became Official Opposition for the first time in its history. Four years later, on 19 October 2015, it kept 16 of those Quebec seats. The wave had been real. The seats, it turned out, had been rented.

That distinction, between seats a party wins because the tide is running and seats it wins because it has built something that will still be standing when the tide goes out, is the question now facing the Democratic Party in the last days before the 3 November midterms. Shane Goldmacher, writing for The New York Times on 6 October 2026, framed it with a landlord’s bluntness: do Democrats seek to rent, or to own? The question is not academic. It decides, district by district and day by day, where a party with less money than its opponent spends what it has.

This series has spent thirteen instalments asking whether the Democrats are functioning as a loyal opposition in the Westminster sense: a government-in-waiting with a coalition, a message, and the discipline to hold both together. Part IX looked at the money and found it short. Part XI looked at Republicans who had crossed over to run as Democrats. This instalment looks at the reverse case, a Republican who has spent a decade wearing the Democrats’ endorsements, and at what it costs to try to take his seat away.

The map and the bank account

The arithmetic first. Donald Trump’s support has fallen far enough that the House battleground now runs well past the districts anyone had on a list in January. Strategists are eyeing two Ohio Republicans, Mike Turner and Mike Carey, plus Bill Huizenga in Michigan, and are spending more heavily against Bryan Steil in Wisconsin. All four sit in seats Trump won by single digits in 2024. In Miami, private polling shows a dead heat for Maria Elvira Salazar in a district Trump carried by 15 points, though the price of Miami television may put it out of reach. On 5 October, the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee added six candidates to its “red-to-blue” programme, two of them in districts Trump won by more than 20.

Then the bank account. Ian Russell, formerly the DCCC’s national political director, told Goldmacher that the map has grown larger than the bank account can sustain. That is the whole problem in one sentence. Every dollar sent to a reach seat in Ohio is a dollar not spent defending a Democrat already in office, and Republicans have noticed. Tom Suozzi of New York has faced $6.9 million in outside spending against him, answered by roughly $340,000 in support. Marie Gluesenkamp Perez in Washington is taking similar fire, also largely unanswered. Hakeem Jeffries, who will need every one of these seats if he is to be Speaker in January, called the Republican deluge against Suozzi a wild waste of money. It may be. It is also money the Democrats are not matching.

Phil Gardner of Blue Dog Action, the super PAC tied to the moderate caucus whose members are being targeted, put the defensive case in a memorable phrase: Democrats need to make sure their eyes are not bigger than their stomachs. His argument is that the odds favour shoring up incumbents with a proven ability to win, or reinforcing challengers who have spent a year building a campaign, over late bets on untested candidates in far-flung districts.

He is not wrong. Neither is the counter-argument, which is that the only way a minority party ever becomes a durable majority is by winning seats it has no business winning. Suzan DelBene, who chairs the DCCC, tried to hold both thoughts at once: the strongest majority possible in 2026, she told the Times, but with an eye on 2028. Goldmacher supplies the uncomfortable fact underneath that balance. For all the bullish talk, Democrats currently hold not one House seat in a district Trump won by double digits in 2024.

Exhibit A: the last of a kind

Which brings us to Pennsylvania’s First District: all of Bucks County and a slice of Montgomery County, Philadelphia’s swing suburbs. Its congressman, Brian Fitzpatrick, is the only Republican seeking re-election to the House who has won a district that Trump lost in all three of his campaigns, 2016, 2020 and 2024. In 2024, according to Erin Covey of the Cook Political Report, Fitzpatrick won re-election by 13 points while Kamala Harris narrowly carried the district. That is not a seat held against the tide. That is a seat held in defiance of it.

Democrats have mostly declined to fight him seriously. The Philadelphia media market is among the most expensive in the country, and Fitzpatrick was judged too hard to beat. His closest call came in the Democratic wave of 2018, when, as PoliticsPA has noted, he held on by fewer than 8,000 votes in a year Democrats flipped more than 40 seats nationally.

This autumn is different. Bob Harvie, the Bucks County commissioner who is challenging him, told Goldmacher that the DCCC called the week of 28 September to say it would put $900,000 into his race. The House Majority PAC has reserved more than $6 million in television time on top of that, according to the ad tracker AdImpact. By the standards of Pennsylvania’s First, this is the party finally walking into the expensive shop.

The reason is the ownership logic. If Fitzpatrick goes, strategists believe, the seat leans Democratic for years. Only three seats that Trump lost in 2024 were held by Republicans when this cycle began, and Democrats want all three. One of them, in Nebraska, looked safe enough by 5 October that a Democratic group began pulling its advertising. Pennsylvania’s First did not.

The independent who wears the other side’s colours

Fitzpatrick is a former FBI agent and, as the Times describes him, a throwback to a nearly extinct breed: the moderate Northeastern Republican. His yard signs at Bucks County intersections carry endorsements from labour unions, gun-control groups and environmental organisations that normally line up behind Democrats. His latest advertisement bills him as the most independent member of the House and scrolls through support from groups including the LGBT Unity Fund and an arm of the Humane Society.

In an interview at a Jewish deli in Langhorne on 5 October, he told Goldmacher that the political parties are the worst thing the country has right now, and that he had not attended a single House Republican conference meeting this entire Congress. His brother held the seat before him; the family brand runs deep, he said, and no poll had him behind.

There is evidence for the brand. In July 2025, as NOTUS reported, he voted with Democrats against final passage of Trump’s sweeping tax bill. There is also evidence against it, and Harvie has built his campaign on that evidence: the procedural votes that helped move the same bill forward, and a string of recent votes alongside the president. Harvie’s new ad argues that Congress changed Fitzpatrick. His one-line summary of his opponent, delivered to the Times, is the cleanest rebuttal to the independent brand this series has encountered: Fitzpatrick, he said, is independent whenever his party tells him he is allowed to be.

Readers of Part XI will recognise the shape of the argument. There, the question was whether ex-Republicans running as Democrats had genuinely changed or were simply wearing a new jersey. David Jolly passed that test because his break with Trump was a decade old and fully documented. Fitzpatrick presents the mirror image: a Republican who has worn many of the Democrats’ colours for years but never left the club. Harvie is asking voters to judge the label by the votes. Fitzpatrick is asking them to judge the votes by the label. For most of a decade, Bucks County took Fitzpatrick’s side of that bargain.

What the barbecue heard

What may be shifting is visible in two people Goldmacher met at a Democratic barbecue in Upper Hanover Township on 4 October.

The first was Sharon Bastone, 47, a party activist who told him the district is about as hard to flip as districts come, but that this is the year to do it, and that if it does not happen now it will not happen at all. That is the ownership argument in a citizen’s voice. A wave year is the only time a seat like this comes loose.

The second was Rich Fain, 74, who suspected he was the only Republican at the event. He has never voted for Trump, but he has voted for his local Republican congressman in the past. Not this year. What he cannot stomach, he said, is not only the administration but the way Republicans in Congress have gone along with it while knowing better.

Fain is the most important person in the article. He is not a convert to Democratic policy. He is a Republican who has stopped accepting the distinction between his congressman and his congressman’s party. It is also the finding Sarah Longwell drew from more than 400 focus groups, which Part XIII, “The Wrecking Ball”, took up in full: voters who have soured on Trump are not asking Democrats to become something new. They are asking whether anyone is willing to stand up. If enough Fains exist in Bucks County, Fitzpatrick’s decade-long distinction between himself and his party collapses, and the seat changes owners.

Harvie, notably, is not running away from his own party to get there. At the barbecue he told one voter he would support statehood for Puerto Rico and Washington, D.C., if their residents wanted it, and he name-checked both Barbra Streisand and Jamie Raskin. That is a striking choice in a cycle when many Democrats are building brands deliberately apart from the national party, as Rob Sand has done in Iowa (Part V). It may be a misjudgement. It may also be the point. A challenger who wants to own the seat rather than rent it has to win as a Democrat, not as a Fitzpatrick with a different letter beside his name. Otherwise the next Republican moderate simply rents it back.

The Philadelphia Inquirer, which endorsed Fitzpatrick in 2018 and 2020, endorsed Harvie the weekend of 3 and 4 October. And Fitzpatrick has already spent more than $3 million on advertising, far more than Harvie and his allies so far. That is not the spending of a man who believes his own line about the polls.

The coattails

A few days later, Harvie gave his strategy a name. Speaking to Democratic activists in Green Lane, a borough in suburban Montgomery County, he said that he and every other Democrat on the Pennsylvania ballot would be holding on to Josh Shapiro’s coattails with white knuckles, because those coattails were going somewhere. The numbers explain why. The latest Times/Inquirer/Siena poll put the governor’s approval at 60 per cent and the president’s at 34, with three in four Pennsylvania voters disapproving of Trump’s handling of the cost of living.

Readers will notice the irony of a candidate in an essay about owning seats announcing that he intends to ride in on someone else’s popularity. But there is a difference between renting from a national wave and borrowing from a home-state governor of your own party. A wave recedes on its own schedule. A popular governor is a local asset, and a seat won on his coattails can be kept if the new member spends the next two years earning it.

The more revealing detail is what Shapiro has not done. He has endorsed Harvie and appeared with him on two consecutive weekends, and on neither occasion did he say Fitzpatrick’s name. He spoke instead, narrowly, about what the race means for control of the House. Fitzpatrick, interviewed over coffee two days after the first event, said that was not a coincidence. He calls Shapiro a friend. He invited him to his wedding over the summer; Shapiro did not attend but sent a lovely note. Fitzpatrick has also pointedly declined to endorse the Republican running for governor.

Shapiro’s silence is the ownership problem seen from the other side. A governor running up a large margin in Bucks County needs the same split-ticket voters who have kept Fitzpatrick in office for a decade, and attacking their congressman by name is no way to keep them. National Democrats have no such constraint. They have begun using footage of Fitzpatrick in black tie, raising a glass at that wedding, in their attack ads. The party wants the seat. Its most popular figure in the state wants Fitzpatrick’s voters. Both are entirely rational, and it is not obvious that both can be had at once.

The gamble on both sides

There is a second gamble here, and it is Republican. National Republicans have neither spent heavily nor reserved closing airtime for Fitzpatrick. He told Goldmacher he assumes they think he is fine, and that this is a gamble on their part. It is an unusually candid thing for an incumbent to say about his own party a month before an election. It also tells you why the Democrats’ money might go further in Bucks County than anywhere else on the map: they are spending into a seat the other side has, at least for now, left to fend for itself. The Speaker, at least, has paid a visit. Mike Johnson blitzed through all four of Pennsylvania’s Republican-held battlegrounds in a single October day, campaigning on behalf of all four incumbents. A day of the Speaker’s time, however, is not $6 million of airtime.

And as he got up to leave the deli, an anti-Fitzpatrick advertisement came on the television in the corner. He asked the cashier whether the set was tuned to cable or streaming. It is a small detail, and Goldmacher was right to keep it. A man who has survived three Trump campaigns by knowing his district precisely was checking which channel the attack was coming through.

The other three Pennsylvanias

Bucks County is the hardest of Pennsylvania’s four battlegrounds, but it is not alone. Goldmacher returned to the state for The New York Times on 17 October to report on all four Republican-held seats Democrats are targeting, which together would be enough to give them the House. More than $105 million in broadcast, cable and streaming advertising is running across those four districts. Jesse Ferguson, a national Democratic strategist, put the stakes simply: if Democrats can win every kind of fight on offer in Pennsylvania, no Republican district in the country is safe. A clean sweep would be a wave; holding none would be an unexpected Republican revival.

The other three are each a different version of the rent-or-own question.

In the Seventh, centred on the Lehigh Valley, Ryan Mackenzie took the seat in 2024 on the strength of a Republican surge in Allentown, a majority-Hispanic city where some precincts swung 20 points or more. Victor Martinez, who hosts a popular Spanish-language radio programme there, now says the Latino voters who gave Trump a chance on the economy have found that gas, groceries and electricity all got more expensive. The Democrat, Bob Brooks, a firefighters’ union leader running a Hispanic outreach effort branded “Bombero Bob,” says those voters have the same complaints as his members: they feel lied to. Mackenzie insists the race turns on individual candidates, and cites 10,000 constituent cases resolved. One of his own supporters, Lucy Lennon, offered Goldmacher the more honest assessment: people are voting party this year. She is Rich Fain’s mirror image.

In the Eighth, around Scranton, Rob Bresnahan unseated a long-serving Democrat in 2024 after campaigning against congressional stock trading, then became one of the most prolific stock traders in his freshman class. Paige Cognetti, Scranton’s mayor, has tied the two issues together, arguing that there is now a clear line between corruption and costs, in an advertisement filmed in her husband’s grocery store. She is running on her old mayoral slogan, “Paige Against the Machine,” in a district Trump carried three times. This is the vocabulary war of Part II, fought on populist ground.

In the Tenth, in central Pennsylvania, Janelle Stelson, whose crossing from Republican to Democrat Part XI examined, is in a rematch with Scott Perry, the former chairman of the House Freedom Caucus, whom she lost to by about a point in 2024. She has embraced parts of Trump’s agenda, including ending taxes on tips and overtime. Republican advertising has tied her to Nancy Pelosi, Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, Zohran Mamdani and Bernie Sanders all the same. This is the stacking problem from Part VI, applied to a candidate who is not even on the party’s left. Days before Goldmacher’s visit, the Department of Homeland Security announced the arrest of a man on an indecent-exposure charge. Its news release asserted that he had worked for Stelson, quoting an I.C.E. deputy director. Her campaign says he never worked or volunteered for it, and a watchdog group has asked for an investigation into whether the department engaged in partisan politics.

Seen through this essay’s lens, the Seventh and Eighth are seats the Democrats held until 2024 and are trying to take back. The Tenth is a seat that only a wave would open at all. Which of them, if won, is still Democratic in 2028 is the question the morning after will not answer, and the next two years will.

What the choice reveals

Put the pieces together and the rent-or-own question turns out to be a proxy for the question this series opened with. In Part I, “Wake Up, Dems”, the warning was that a party which mistakes its primary electorate’s intensity for a governing coalition will win the occasional surprising race and then lose the seat back. Renting is the electoral version of that error. It wins seats on the strength of the moment and the opponent’s unpopularity, with no plan for the cycle when the moment has passed. The NDP learned this in Quebec. The Democrats themselves learned it when many of the seats they flipped in 2018 went back to Republicans in 2020.

Owning is harder and slower. It means picking fights where the voters are already, like Bucks County, Democratic at the presidential level and loyal to a Republican on everything else, and then persuading them that the distinction they have been drawing no longer holds. It is expensive, because the voters worth owning tend to live in expensive media markets. And it can fail spectacularly, as it has failed against Fitzpatrick in every cycle since 2016.

A loyal opposition needs both. It needs the wave seats, because majorities are built from them and because, as Part IX argued, contesting the edges forces the other side to spread its own money thin. It also needs the owned seats, because those are what keep a party in contention once the wave subsides. The Democrats’ problem is that they are trying to do both with roughly half the money, while Republicans spend freely against incumbents the Democratic committees cannot afford to defend.

Russell, the former DCCC strategist, offered one more caution: October can move quickly, and anyone who remembers how the 2016 campaign lurched in its final weeks knows that a comfortable environment can turn. If it does, the rented seats go first. The owned ones, if there are any, are the ones still standing.

Bucks County will report its results late on the night of 3 November. If Harvie wins, the Democrats will have bought the most expensive seat on their map and may keep it for a decade. If he loses, the party will have spent more than $6 million confirming what it has assumed since 2016. Either way, the decision to try was the first sign in this series of a party behaving as though it means to be in charge for longer than one cycle.
