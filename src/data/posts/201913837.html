On Friday, June 12, 2026, the U.S. government called Anthropic at 1:00 p.m. and told the company to disable its two most advanced AI systems. A formal letter arrived at 5:30 p.m. By Friday evening, Anthropic had posted a message to its social media accounts confirming that it was suspending access to Claude Fable 5 and Mythos for all foreign nationals, effective immediately. Foreign nationals who work for Anthropic were included. The order cited national security authorities but named no specific threat. No duration was given. Before the Friday afternoon call, Anthropic had received no communication from the government about any national security concern whatsoever.

“We apologize for this disruption to our customers,” the company said. “We believe this is a misunderstanding and are working to restore access as soon as possible.”

The timing carries its own irony. The directive landed just days after Dario Amodei published an essay arguing for “serious and binding regulation of AI,” including the government’s explicit authority to block unsafe models. “Frontier AI models, like airplanes, should be required to go through technical testing and auditing, and their release should be blocked or reversed as a threat to public safety if they do not meet high standards of safety,” Amodei wrote. Anthropic got the authority it asked for. It did not get the process it asked for. In its own blog post responding to the directive, the company made the distinction explicit: “As we have stated publicly, we believe the government should have the ability to block unsafe deployments, as part of a statutory process that is transparent, fair, clear, and grounded in technical facts. This action does not adhere to those principles.”

That sentence is the thesis of this essay, and Anthropic wrote it, not this series. The company is not arguing against governance. It is identifying the absence of one specific thing: process. A misunderstanding. One of the most consequential AI governance decisions in the history of the technology — restricting access to the most capable AI system ever publicly available, for an undefined period, across an undefined population — was disclosed in a social media post on a Friday evening, characterized as a probable error, with no further explanation from the government that issued it.

This is not a story about Fable 5 specifically. It is a story about what AI governance actually looks like, as opposed to what everyone from Bernie Sanders to Donald Trump is promising it will look like. The gap between those two things is the subject of this series. This week, it became impossible to ignore.

I. The Dual-Use Dilemma

To understand why the Commerce Department acted, it helps to understand what Mythos is, and what Fable 5 was built to be.

Anthropic unveiled Mythos in April 2026, but declined to release it publicly. The reason was specific and alarming: the system was powerful enough to serve as a tool for hackers seeking vulnerabilities in computer networks. This was not theoretical. Researchers who gained access to the system confirmed that it could identify software vulnerabilities with a speed and precision that no previous AI had matched.

But the same capability that makes a system dangerous to network security also makes it valuable to network defense. The same AI that a hacker can use to find a hole in a firewall is the AI that a cybersecurity firm can use to patch the hole before the hacker finds it. This is the dual-use dilemma that has haunted cybersecurity since the discipline began, applied now to AI at a scale the discipline has never encountered.

Anthropic’s response was to build Fable 5: a version of Mythos with guardrails designed to block responses in cybersecurity, biology, and other sensitive areas. Released on June 9, Fable 5 was described by Anthropic’s head of product management, Dianne Penn, as the core reason the company could release anything at all. “That is the core of why we have been able to release Fable,” she said.

Cybersecurity experts were immediately divided. Some applauded the restriction. Others pointed out the obvious problem: guardrails that prevent hackers from using Fable also prevent defenders. A straitjacket is not selective about who it restrains.

Outside researchers have demonstrated repeatedly that guardrails on AI systems are not reliable. Anthropic’s own researchers acknowledge this. “This is why we have invested so much in safety research,” Penn said. “It is a continuing progression.”

A continuing progression. Three days after that statement, the government gave Anthropic 90 minutes to take down Fable 5 before issuing the export control. Anthropic did not comply in 90 minutes. The export control was issued.

What triggered the order is now known, and the independent assessment of it matters. The government’s concern was a jailbreak — a scenario in which a user could bypass Fable 5’s cybersecurity guardrail by asking it to “fix this code” after it refused the prompt “review the code for security issues.” Anthropic contests whether this constitutes a jailbreak at all, describing the actions elicited as “either entirely benign responses or are minor findings.”

The most authoritative independent verdict came from Katie Moussouris, CEO of Luta Security and a cybersecurity expert with no financial relationship with Anthropic. Moussouris reviewed the actual White House report on the alleged jailbreak at Anthropic’s request. Her conclusion: the sequence involved IT experts asking Fable to help find and patch bugs, and when given deliberately insecure code, the model was doing exactly what a cyberdefense tool should do. “The model working as intended,” she said. Alex Stamos, chief security officer at AI-coding company Corridor, added that the jailbreak did not appear to have elicited the kinds of cyber abilities “that made Mythos famous” — and that “this kind of vulnerability discovery is already well within the capabilities of other models.”

A White House spokesperson told The Atlantic that the jailbreak “was very serious” but said specific details are classified. Whether the bypass was serious is, in Matteo Wong’s precise formulation, “not at all clear.”

Anthropic’s response to the government was direct: “We disagree that the finding of a narrow potential jailbreak should be cause for recalling a commercial model deployed to hundreds of millions of people. If this standard was applied across the industry, we believe it would essentially halt all new model deployments for all frontier model providers.”

That last sentence is not hyperbole. It is a description of what the government’s standard, if applied consistently, would produce. The question of whether it will be applied consistently is the subject of Section III.

II. The Pattern

Friday’s order was not the first time the Trump administration had moved against Anthropic specifically.

In March 2026, the Pentagon declared Anthropic an unacceptable supply chain risk, a designation that banned defense contractors from using the company’s technology. The declaration came after what were described as tense talks over how Anthropic’s AI could be used in military and intelligence settings. No public explanation was provided for that designation either. Anthropic sued the Trump administration to reverse it, and that litigation is ongoing. On Saturday — the day after the Friday night order — Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth posted on X that “every passing day” proves blacklisting Anthropic was “the right move.”

The pattern is worth noting. Anthropic is, by most measures, the AI company most publicly committed to safety and governance. It publishes its safety research. It releases its Constitutional AI methodology. It accepted restricted access agreements before releasing Mythos, sharing it initially with only 40 organizations through Project Glasswing, a cybersecurity initiative designed to let critical infrastructure operators patch vulnerabilities before hackers found them. It worked with government agencies to test Fable 5 and Mythos 5 ahead of release, and received approval to deploy them. It built Fable 5 precisely because it was trying to navigate the dual-use problem responsibly.

Its reward, twice in three months, has been targeted action by a federal government that has otherwise taken what former officials described as a very hands-off approach to AI. The same administration that allowed advanced AI chips to be sold to China issued a Friday-night order barring a Canadian employee of Anthropic from working on the company’s flagship model.

The ideological backstory has been on the record for a year. David Sacks, the former White House AI czar, said publicly that Anthropic has an “agenda to backdoor Woke AI” and is a “Resistance organization.” This is not a neutral regulatory posture. It is a political characterization of a private company by the administration’s own AI policy chief, made before the supply chain designation, before the export control, and before Hegseth’s Saturday post declaring that blacklisting Anthropic was “the right move.”

The NSA detail adds a further dimension. The National Security Agency reportedly made exceptions to the earlier supply chain risk designation specifically to use Mythos’s advanced cyber abilities. The government designated the technology dangerous, banned contractors from using it, and simultaneously carved out exceptions so its own intelligence agencies could deploy it. The irony is not accidental. It is structural: the same administration that calls Anthropic a security risk has been quietly relying on Anthropic’s technology for national security operations.

Dean Ball, a senior fellow at the Foundation for American Innovation who previously served as an AI adviser under President Trump, posted two words on social media: “I have no words.” He called the decision “baffling.”

When a former adviser to the administration that issued an order calls it baffling, something has gone wrong in the governance chain — and it is not Anthropic.

III. The OpenAI Contrast

The comparison to OpenAI is instructive, and not because OpenAI handled the dual-use problem correctly. It is instructive because OpenAI handled it differently, and received different treatment.

A week after Anthropic unveiled Mythos, OpenAI said it was limiting the release of its comparable system, GPT-5.4-Cyber. But where Anthropic shared Mythos with 40 organizations, OpenAI shared GPT-5.4-Cyber with hundreds, with plans to expand to thousands. Where Anthropic built guardrailed Fable 5 and released it cautiously, OpenAI moved faster and wider.

The Commerce Department order applied to Anthropic. Not to OpenAI.

The independent cybersecurity assessments make this asymmetry harder to explain on technical grounds. Moussouris stated that OpenAI’s GPT-5.5, a model with comparable cybersecurity capabilities, could be used in the same way as Fable 5 was in the alleged jailbreak sequence. Stamos confirmed that the vulnerability discovery at issue was “already well within the capabilities of other models.” GPT-5.5 is not subject to export controls. Neither are less advanced Anthropic models such as Opus 4.8, which can perform many of the same tasks. The export control targeted the one model from the one company, for a capability that multiple comparable systems share.

Dozens of cybersecurity experts from companies including Nvidia and Zoom have signed a letter to White House officials stating that the export control “has taken the best models away from defenders, created market uncertainty, and risked America’s AI leadership without any real risk to justify it.” The letter, organized by Stamos and addressed directly to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and National Cyber Director Sean Cairncross, has since drawn more than 150 signatures from executives and technical leaders. These are not Anthropic employees. These are practitioners in the field the export control was ostensibly designed to protect. Stamos’s framing of the underlying problem is the sharpest available: “the fear is that at any moment you can now, if you run afoul of the administration, be shut down for a completely arbitrary decision capriciously.” He added the obvious point that fair enforcement would require equivalent action against OpenAI and Google — which has not happened.

Sacks’s own position shifted over the weekend, from outright hostility to something closer to a dare. After Anthropic’s blog post used what he called “minimizing language” inconsistent with its brand as “the AI safety company,” Sacks wrote: “The Admin values Anthropic’s technical capabilities and feels that this issue, while serious, should be easily resolved. The ball is in Anthropic’s court.” A year earlier, after an Anthropic executive published an essay on AI risk, Sacks had accused the company of “running a sophisticated regulatory capture strategy based on fear-mongering.” The throughline across twelve months is consistent: whatever Anthropic does to manage the dual-use problem responsibly, the administration’s AI policy voices characterize it as bad faith.

There are possible explanations for the asymmetry that do not involve favoritism. Perhaps the government had classified intelligence about Fable 5 specifically that goes beyond what Moussouris reviewed. Perhaps the order was genuinely a misunderstanding — senior Anthropic staffers are meeting with Trump administration officials in Washington today, Monday June 15, to try to resolve it.

But the circumstances of how the order came about are not reassuring. The Wall Street Journal reported the precise mechanism, not just the involvement: Amazon CEO Andy Jassy told administration officials, including Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, that Amazon’s own researchers had used a series of prompts to get Fable 5 to produce information that could aid cyberattacks. This was not an outside party flagging a vulnerability to a neutral regulator. This was Anthropic’s largest investor and cloud partner conducting its own red-teaming exercise and taking the results directly to the administration, which then issued an export control within days. Amazon has invested $8 billion in Anthropic and committed to up to $25 billion more. Anthropic runs on Amazon’s cloud infrastructure and Trainium chips. Amazon depends on Anthropic’s models for many of its own services. The two companies are, in CNBC’s word, “deeply linked.”

The CEO of Anthropic’s largest investor — a company with $33 billion reasons to shape Anthropic’s competitive position — directed his own researchers to find a way to make Fable 5 look dangerous, then delivered the finding to the government that ordered the models disabled. Amazon’s spokesperson said the company doesn’t share details of such discussions. That is not a denial. It is a statement about what Amazon will and will not disclose.

Then there is the FDA paradox. The export control, by forcing Anthropic to withdraw Fable 5 from the market, functionally created exactly the government licensing requirement for AI models that David Sacks had himself argued against. Weeks of infighting had delayed Trump signing a recent executive order on AI and cybersecurity specifically because of concerns about what Sacks called an “FDA for AI.” The final executive order explicitly stated it did not establish “a mandatory governmental licensing, preclearance, or permitting requirement” for AI. The export control on Fable 5 is, in practical effect, precisely that. Sacks proceeded to defend it anyway, accusing Anthropic of prioritizing its “consumer model over safety.” The self-contradiction was complete within the same week.

The optics of the situation are what they are. The company that moved more cautiously, shared less widely, built more guardrails, and worked with government agencies before release received a Commerce Department order on a Friday afternoon with 90 minutes’ notice. The company that moved faster and shared more broadly did not. If the lesson the industry draws from this is that caution is penalized and speed is rewarded — and that your investors’ relationships with the administration matter more than your safety record — the governance implications are not difficult to trace.

IV. The Governance That Exists

This series has spent forty-nine parts documenting the gap between the AI governance that is promised and the AI governance that exists. The taxation debate covered in Part 48 is the promised version: deliberate, legislative, accountable, debated in public by elected representatives with constituencies to answer to.

The Friday night order is the version that exists.

No legislation authorized it. No congressional vote preceded it. No public comment period was offered. The rationale — national security — was asserted but not explained. The duration was not specified. The scope — all foreign nationals, including employees — was sweeping enough to surprise former government officials who work in this space. The disclosure mechanism was a social media post.

This is not an argument that the order was wrong on the merits. It is possible that the Commerce Department had genuine, specific intelligence suggesting that Fable 5 or Mythos posed an imminent risk that justified emergency action. It is possible that the Friday-night timing reflected operational necessity rather than bureaucratic convenience. These things are possible.

But “possible” is not a governance standard. Democratic governance does not ask the public to trust that the executive branch is acting correctly when it makes sweeping decisions without explanation. It asks the executive branch to show its work — through legislation, through oversight, through the mechanisms that distinguish accountable government from arbitrary power.

What happened on Friday night was arbitrary power. It may have been well-intentioned arbitrary power. That is not a meaningful distinction in a constitutional system, and it is not a meaningful distinction in a series about whether AI will be governed democratically or oligarchically.

The oligarchic version of AI governance, as this series has documented it, is the governance of AI by the companies that build it, for the benefit of the shareholders who fund it, without accountability to the public that will live with the consequences. The Friday-night order is a different failure: the governance of AI by an executive branch acting without legislative authorization, without public explanation, and without the oversight mechanisms that distinguish democratic governance from something else.

Both failures lead to the same place. The public is not in the room. The decisions are made by people who are not accountable to them. The technology that will reshape their lives is governed by processes they cannot observe, appeal, or change.

The Atlantic’s Matteo Wong, writing today, put it with precision that this series cannot improve on: “Decisions are being made in a hurried, contradictory fashion. There do not appear to have been any outlined standards and process, robust consultation, or even agreement on the facts.” The Trump administration wants to beat China in the AI race but has clamped down on one of the few U.S. companies that stands a real chance of doing so. It has declared Anthropic a national security threat multiple times while racing to incorporate Claude Mythos into government operations. It wants to demonstrate a light-touch approach to AI regulation but just established a de facto licensing requirement for frontier models. “America and its tech companies have many factors in their favor when it comes to leading the way in AI development,” Wong concluded. “As of now, the White House is not one of them.”

V. The View From Evian

On Wednesday, June 17 — five days after the export control, two days after Anthropic’s staffers flew to Washington to negotiate — Dario Amodei sat down at a G7 working lunch in Evian-les-Bains, France, beside Sam Altman, Demis Hassabis, and roughly a dozen other tech executives, while heads of state from the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, France, Germany, Italy, and Japan looked on.

The image is difficult to improve on as an illustration of this series’ central argument. Five days after his own government unilaterally disabled his company’s flagship products with 90 minutes’ notice, the CEO of that company was seated at the table where the leaders of the world’s industrial democracies were deciding how to respond to the fallout — not as a witness called to testify, but as a participant whose presence the summit organizers considered essential. Jessica Brandt of the Council on Foreign Relations told CNBC the dynamic plainly: “In order to make credible commitments on AI, heads of state now need the cooperation, if not endorsement, of a handful of private sector executives actually building the technology. We’re seeing a shift in who gets a seat at the table and a signal of where power sits.”

The export control did not stay a domestic matter. Reuters reported that G7 leaders spent part of the summit’s opening dinner discussing a “trusted partners” framework — a system, floated by U.S. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick alongside European diplomats, that would grant select allied countries or companies access to American AI models currently restricted under the directive. The European Commission’s technology chief, Henna Virkkunen, had already warned Washington against “discriminatory” action and argued Europe should be treated as a “trusted partner” rather than a security risk. Euronews described allied leaders arriving at the summit “unsettled,” writing that the export control had confirmed “one of Europe’s worst-kept fears: that the Americans could shut down access to their most powerful technology at a moment’s notice.”

That fear has a name in Brussels and other allied capitals: the kill switch. For years, European officials have worried privately about strategic dependence on American AI infrastructure controlled by a small number of U.S. companies subject to U.S. government direction. The Fable 5 and Mythos 5 shutdown did not just disable two products. It demonstrated, to every government and every company outside the United States that depends on American frontier models, that the dependency is real and that the switch can be thrown with 90 minutes’ notice and no public explanation. A White House official told Reuters the administration has “an open line of communication with our allies, and we remain committed to addressing national security concerns with Anthropic’s model” — language that addresses the diplomatic friction without addressing the governance question underneath it.

This is the Asimov Gap at its widest aperture. The decision to disable Fable 5 and Mythos 5 was made by one government, for reasons it has not explained, with a process no statute authorized — and that decision now shapes AI policy conversations among the leaders of every G7 nation, who are negotiating a workaround rather than a remedy. The “trusted partners” framework, if it materializes, will not restore the statutory process Anthropic asked for. It will simply extend the same unaccountable discretion outward, deciding by executive negotiation which allied governments get access to which models under which undisclosed criteria. The gap does not close. It becomes multilateral.

VI. The Misunderstanding

Asimov’s Three Laws of Robotics were, at their core, an attempt to answer a question that no one in 1942 was taking seriously: what happens when a technology becomes powerful enough that the question of who controls it is the same as the question of who controls everything? He embedded the answer in the machines themselves — hard-coded rules that could not be overridden, not by users, not by owners, not by governments.

We chose a different path. We built systems without hard-coded rules, governed by guidelines that can be changed, restricted by guardrails that researchers have repeatedly shown are unreliable, controlled by a combination of corporate self-governance and executive-branch orders issued on Friday afternoons with 90 minutes’ notice — orders that, within days, were already being renegotiated multilaterally among governments that had no role in issuing them.

The misunderstanding, if there is one, is not the Commerce Department’s. It is ours.
