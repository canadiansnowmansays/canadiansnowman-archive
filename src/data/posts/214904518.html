There is a particular kind of vertigo that comes from reading two dispatches about the same country, filed within three weeks of each other, and finding that they might as well describe two different nations. In mid-August 2026, the New York Times ran a photo-essay by Brook Larmer, with photography by Chien-Chi Chang, titled “The Defenders”, which followed civilians at Kuma Academy in Taipei as they drilled in trauma triage, disaster response, and what the piece called “cognitive warfare” — the low-grade, everyday business of resisting disinformation before the missiles ever need to fly. Three weeks later, on September 8, 2026, the Atlantic published a long report by Simon Shuster, with photographs by Cheng-Chia Huang, titled “Will Taiwan Just Give Up?”, which followed Cheng Li-wun, the chair of Taiwan’s Nationalist Party, through a spring of blue neckties, bubble tea delivered by drone, and a lunch with Xi Jinping built around a bowl of soup last served to Richard Nixon.

Both pieces are honest. Both are reported with care. And both, read together, describe a country arguing with itself about whether the argument is even worth having.

This postscript to the Formosa series exists because the two portraits cannot simply be filed side by side and left to cancel each other out. They are not describing different Taiwans. They are describing the same Taiwan at war with itself over which of its own instincts to trust — the instinct that built bomb shelters into apartment blocks and taught schoolchildren to apply tourniquets, or the instinct that looks at Hong Kong’s fall from grace, does the arithmetic on the size of China’s navy, and concludes that dignity is a luxury good.

The civilians who assume no one is coming

Kuma Academy did not spring up because Taiwan’s government asked for it. It grew out of a colder assessment, one that the island’s civil-defense movement has been making with increasing bluntness since Russia’s 2022 invasion of Ukraine: that resupply, if it comes at all, will come late, and that the interval between the first missile and the first American cargo plane is an interval Taiwanese citizens will have to survive on their own. The NYT piece described trainees — teachers, office workers, retirees — learning to stop arterial bleeding, to coordinate a street-level response when hospitals are overwhelmed, and to recognize the shape of a coordinated disinformation campaign before it metastasizes into panic. One of the named initiatives, Operation Blue Magpie, treats information itself as a front line, on the theory that a population that can be made to believe its government has already fled is a population that has already lost, whatever the state of its air defenses.

The phrase the article used — “epistemic sovereignty” — is worth sitting with, because it names something the Formosa series has circled since its introduction: that China’s preferred method of absorbing Taiwan has never been primarily military. It is persuasion, applied patiently, at the level of the individual citizen’s confidence in her own government’s durability. Xi Jinping does not need to win a war if he can win the argument about whether a war is winnable. Kuma Academy exists to make sure that argument has a second side.

What struck me rereading the piece against the Atlantic’s is how apolitical the civil-defense movement insists on being, at least in its own self-description. It is not, on its face, a DPP project or a green-camp mobilization. It sells itself as civic infrastructure, the way a fire department is civic infrastructure — necessary regardless of which party governs, useful regardless of whether reunification talks succeed or fail. That framing is doing real work, and it is worth noticing precisely because the KMT-DPP polarization the NYT piece flagged around civil defense itself has not gone away: even a project built to look nonpartisan operates inside a legislature where one camp keeps voting against the money that would make its lessons actionable rather than merely psychological. Training a citizen to apply a tourniquet is a humane and necessary thing. It is not a substitute for the citizen having a drone overhead that keeps the wound from happening to a thousand people at once.

A confidence that used to be real

To understand why Kuma Academy feels urgent now in a way it would not have felt urgent a decade ago, it helps to go back to the last time Taiwan and the United States actually tested the proposition that America would show up. In 1995, Washington granted a visa to Taiwan’s then-president to speak at his alma mater, Cornell, and Beijing responded with missile tests and disrupted shipping through the Strait. Bill Clinton, under congressional pressure, sent two aircraft carriers, and one of them, the USS Nimitz, sailed straight through the Strait as a deliberate signal. China backed down.

Lee Hsi-min, then a young officer commanding a Taiwanese submarine called the Sea Dragon, spent that crisis stationed beneath the Strait with orders to ambush any Chinese warship that tried to cross. Interviewed decades later for the Atlantic piece, he described the confidence he’d felt at the time without hesitation — his torpedoes could sink anything in China’s fleet, and the Nimitz stood behind him. Asked how he feels about the same scenario today, his answer was blunter: he does not have that confidence at all. In the years since, China’s defense spending rose by more than half within a few years of the crisis, aimed specifically at the capability gap the Nimitz had exposed — submarines and missile systems built to keep American carriers at a distance they cannot again close so casually. By the time Barack Obama took office, the naval logic that had won 1995–96 for Taiwan no longer applied. The porcupine strategy that followed — arm Taiwan with cheap, mobile, deniable weapons rather than a fleet it could never match ship-for-ship — was Washington’s answer to that shift, and it is the strategy the current legislature has now half-funded.

That history matters for reading Kuma Academy correctly. Its drills are not an expression of confidence that Taiwan can win a symmetrical fight. They are the opposite: an acknowledgment that the symmetrical fight was lost sometime around 2010, and that whatever comes next will be decided by how quickly ordinary people can adapt when the state’s conventional deterrent no longer holds by itself.

The soup, the ties, and the ten incentives

If Kuma Academy’s animating premise is that no one is coming, Cheng Li-wun’s animating premise is that no one needs to. Shuster’s Atlantic piece opens with an image almost too on-the-nose for satire and yet entirely factual: Xi Jinping, not a man given to public warmth, hosting an April 2026 lunch for a Taiwanese Nationalist delegation and steering the conversation toward the chicken-and-sea-clam soup served at the 1972 state dinner for Nixon — the summit that, within a decade, cost Taiwan its UN seat, its Washington embassy, and its status as the internationally recognized government of China. Xi and his aides wore blue ties that day, the color of Cheng’s party. Whatever else one wants to say about Chinese Communist Party messaging discipline, it is rarely accidental.

Cheng returned home with what Chinese state media branded “the ten incentives” — market access for Taiwanese farmers and fishermen, mainland broadcast slots for Taiwanese television, the general shape of a relationship in which good behaviour is rewarded and bad behaviour, implicitly, is not. Taiwan’s government has called the incentives a transparent attempt at political interference, which they plainly are; the more interesting question the Atlantic piece raises is why they are working on a meaningful slice of the electorate anyway. Part of the answer is structural: the KMT’s roughly 65,000 dues-paying members who elected Cheng chair are not remotely representative of Taiwan’s 23 million citizens, but they control a legislative majority’s worth of leverage over the defense budget regardless. Part of the answer is generational and economic: Taiwan’s economy, once more than half the size of China’s, is now roughly one-twentieth of it, and residents of Kinmen can watch skyscrapers rise in Xiamen from their own beaches.

And part of the answer, the uncomfortable part, is that Cheng’s central claim — that Taiwan could secure “a better deal than Hong Kong” — is not obviously falsifiable in the way her critics would like it to be, because no one has tested it and no one particularly wants to be the one who tests it. When Shuster pressed her on Hong Kong directly, she waved the comparison off with a flat “No,” delivered in English for emphasis, and a promise that Taiwan would “not need to sacrifice or make any compromise” regarding its democracy, economy, or rule of law. It is the kind of assurance that means everything if true and nothing at all if false, and Beijing has given her no verifiable way to distinguish the two before the deal is signed.

The strategic backdrop makes her case easier than it should be. Trump’s May 2026 summit with Xi produced a president who described the pending $14 billion arms package to Taiwan not as a deterrent commitment but as “a very good negotiating chip,” and who volunteered, unprompted, that “the last thing we need right now is a war that’s 9,500 miles away” — this from a president simultaneously prosecuting a war in Iran roughly 7,000 miles from Washington. Biden, whatever his other failures on Taiwan, said on four separate occasions that the United States would defend the island militarily. Trump has said it zero times, and the Atlantic piece is careful to note that no China hand of Matt Pottinger’s or Mark Montgomery’s stature currently has real influence inside this White House. Ivan Kanapathy, the one credentialed Asia expert who made it onto the Beijing delegation, reportedly spends more time fending off loyalty accusations from Laura Loomer than shaping policy — and the White House would not let Shuster speak to him at all.

Layer onto that a Taiwanese public-opinion survey, cited in the same piece, showing fewer than a quarter of respondents consider the U.S. a “trustworthy ally” and only 35 percent expect American help in the event of invasion — both figures down sharply since Trump’s second term began — and Cheng’s argument stops sounding like collaboration and starts sounding, to a meaningful number of voters, like realism with better manners.

The legislature is the real battlefield, and it already has a winner

Here the two dispatches converge on the same terrain from opposite directions, and the convergence is more damning than either piece manages alone. When Shuster visited the office of Wang Ting-yu, a senior DPP member of the defense committee, he found him mid-meeting with representatives from AeroVironment, the American manufacturer pitching Taiwan on its Switchblade loitering munition — a weapon that performed poorly in Ukraine and that the U.S. military itself has reportedly cut back on ordering. It is a small, almost absurd scene, but it captures the underlying problem precisely: Taiwan’s arms relationship with Washington runs through a backlog of expensive, sometimes underperforming American hardware, while the cheap, mass-producible, homegrown alternative — the drone industry the porcupine strategy actually depends on — sits starved of funding a few floors away in the same building.

That starving is not accidental. When Taiwan’s legislature finally passed a pared-down special defense budget this year, worth roughly $25 billion against President Lai’s requested $40 billion-plus, the Nationalists made sure that essentially all of it flowed toward American arms purchases and none of it toward Taiwan’s own nascent drone manufacturers — companies like Thunder Tiger, which makes dental drills and remote-control toys by day and is trying to retool a garage-sized production room into a war footing, and I-Mei, the cookie and snack conglomerate whose delivery-drone subsidiary qualified it, almost accidentally, for a seat on the national drone team. A competing KMT drone proposal submitted in late June added oversight requirements calculated to slow the program rather than fund it. The industry remains, in Shuster’s phrase, in limbo — which is precisely where Beijing wants it, since a Taiwan that has not built its own porcupine quills is a Taiwan whose civil-defense drills, however psychologically valuable, are triaging a wound the legislature declined to prevent.

There is a human cost to this limbo that the Atlantic piece renders in miniature through Puma Shen, a DPP legislator who has campaigned loudly for higher military spending and a stronger culture of service — and who nonetheless discharged his own mandatory duty giving directions to tourists at the transportation ministry rather than in uniform. A poster in his office, a gift from a soldier he met on a research trip to Ukraine, shows David sitting atop Goliath’s severed head. Shen’s own account of basic training makes the poster feel aspirational rather than descriptive: grass-cutting and bayonet drills persisted as the default curriculum until only last year, when drone piloting instruction finally began to displace them. Draft evasion, meanwhile, remains widespread enough that some men reportedly eat their way to medical exemptions. None of this is unique to Taiwan — most democracies discover that mandatory service and genuine martial readiness are not the same thing — but it does mean that “the will to fight,” invoked constantly on both sides of this argument, is not a fixed quantity so much as a policy outcome, one the legislature that stripped drone funding has made measurably harder to build.

What the wargames actually say

The Center for Strategic and International Studies has run dozens of Taiwan invasion scenarios across three rounds of tabletop wargaming since 2023, and the results deserve to be stated plainly rather than gestured at, because both sides of this argument tend to cite them selectively. The scenarios that end in Chinese defeat require two conditions to hold simultaneously: Taiwan has to fight with real determination, and the United States has to intervene quickly with the full weight of its military — not eventually, not partially, immediately and completely. Even in those victories, the projected cost is severe: dozens of American and allied ships, hundreds of aircraft, thousands of dead troops, and weeks of bombardment inflicted on Taiwan’s own infrastructure before the fighting stops.

Two of the report’s authors, Eric Heginbotham and Matthew Cancian, took those findings to Taiwan in person in 2024, and the reception was not uniformly grateful. In Kaohsiung, a group of retired Taiwanese naval officers invited them to an ice cream parlour and, between spoonfuls of sundae, told them bluntly that Taiwan possessed neither the will nor the means to fight and that America would never actually come. Heginbotham, whose own son serves aboard a U.S. submarine in the Pacific, later described the exchange in language sharper than academic papers usually allow: his son’s life, he told them, was on the line to defend people who had just told him the defense was pointless. The circularity of that argument — Washington will not intervene if Taiwan looks defeated, and Taiwan is more tempted to concede if it believes Washington will not intervene — is the exact mechanism by which confidence collapses without anyone technically lying at any single step. It is also, not coincidentally, the mechanism Operation Blue Magpie was designed to interrupt.

The chip that cuts both ways

One thread the Atlantic piece raises but does not resolve, and that its own comment section fought over at length, is whether Taiwan’s chip industry is protection or provocation. The argument for protection is familiar and was rehearsed extensively across earlier Formosa parts: TSMC produces the overwhelming majority of the world’s most advanced semiconductors, the ones underwriting the current AI buildout, and no government — including Beijing’s — benefits from an invasion that reduces those fabs to rubble or triggers the kind of workforce flight that would make the technology worthless to whoever ends up controlling the buildings. The argument for provocation, made by some of the article’s own commenters, runs the other way: that Chinese ownership of TSMC’s talent and intellectual property, acquired through absorption rather than destruction, would hand Beijing the exact monopoly leverage over advanced computing that decades of American export controls have tried to prevent, and that a Taiwan integrated peacefully into China’s economy might in fact be the more dangerous outcome for Washington, not the safer one. Both readings assume Beijing is capable of behaving rationally in its own long-term economic interest. Whether that assumption is safe to make about a government that has already shown itself willing to gut Hong Kong’s economic standing to make a political point about control is, at minimum, an open question — and it is the same open question the porcupine strategy and Kuma Academy are each, in their own register, built to hedge against.

Whose Taiwan is this, and who gets to say so

It would be a mistake, though, to read the Atlantic piece as simply correct about where Taiwanese opinion sits, and the response beneath the article itself makes that case better than I can. A commenter identifying as Taiwanese and posting under the handle “Icey” pushed back hard and at length in the piece’s own discussion thread, arguing that Cheng represents an extreme, unrepresentative faction even within her own party — one that has not delivered a KMT presidential win in twelve years — and pointing to spring 2026 polling putting the share of Taiwanese willing to resist a Chinese invasion even without American help above 60 percent, a figure hard to reconcile with the Atlantic’s headline framing. Other Taiwanese and Taiwanese-American commenters weighed in with the specificity of people who have actually lived the distinction the article collapses: that “wanting the status quo” is not the same claim as “wanting capitulation,” and that the KMT’s institutional persistence owes more to a patronage system built during four decades of one-party rule than to any broad electoral mandate for reunification.

None of this makes Shuster’s reporting dishonest. Cheng Li-wun really did say what she said, and she holds a real office with real leverage over a real defense budget. But it does mean the Atlantic’s chosen frame — Taiwan, as a collective subject, edging toward surrender — mistakes one loud, well-connected faction’s position for the country’s settled mood, in exactly the way the civil-defense movement’s own insistence on nonpartisan framing is designed to counteract. Kuma Academy’s organizers are betting that resilience can be built as infrastructure precisely because they know the political argument about resilience is contested and will remain contested through the 2028 presidential election, which Cheng has all but announced she intends to contest herself, presumably with Beijing’s ten incentives as a campaign asset rather than a liability.

What the postscript adds to Formosa

The eleven parts of Formosa: The Beautiful Problem argued, across its run, that Taiwan’s central strategic problem was never really military — it was the slow erosion of the international consensus that Taiwan’s ambiguity was worth defending. These two dispatches, filed weeks apart in the same season, are the clearest illustration yet of that erosion operating on two fronts simultaneously. Externally, an American president treats the island’s defense as a bargaining chip rather than a commitment, and Taiwanese public opinion has adjusted its trust accordingly, with the numbers to prove it. Internally, a legislative majority elected by a fraction of a fraction of the population is translating that external drift into concrete underfunding of the one asymmetric strategy — cheap, mass-produced drones — that Ukraine has demonstrated can make an invasion prohibitively expensive even against a vastly larger power.

Kinmen’s fate is the quiet coda to both stories. The island that repelled a Communist invasion by force in October 1949, whose beaches still bristle with barnacle-crusted anti-landing spikes, now draws its visitors mainly from mainland ferries, and the propaganda tower that once broadcast liberation calls loudly enough for people in Xiamen to hear has had its volume turned down, converted from strategic instrument into tourist curiosity. That is not what capitulation looks like when it happens all at once, in a signing ceremony with flags and a soup course. It is what capitulation looks like when it happens gradually enough that no single year’s headlines have to say the word.

Whether Taiwan’s answer to that erosion turns out to be more Kuma Academy or more Cheng Li-wun is not a question this postscript can settle, and neither dispatch settles it either — which may be the most honest thing that can be said about where the country actually stands as 2026 heads toward its final quarter.
