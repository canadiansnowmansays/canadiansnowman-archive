The red earth of Moa: nickel and cobalt laterite ore, rich in the critical minerals driving global supply chains

Stephen Gordon for CanadianSnowmanSays

In 1960, the revolutionary government of Cuba seized two mining complexes sitting above some of the largest nickel and cobalt deposits in the world. The United States had acquired one of them during World War II to secure nickel for armor plating and aircraft engines. The revolutionaries took it. The Americans never forgot.

Sixty-seven years later, they are coming back to collect.

Follow the Minerals

This series has spent eighteen installments documenting the pressure campaign against Cuba — the oil blockade, the diplomatic talks, the Ratcliffe visit, the Raúl Castro indictment, the USS Nimitz in the Caribbean, the twenty-two-hour blackouts, the two-pound baby in the broken incubator. We have examined the stated reasons: democracy, human rights, political prisoners, Russian and Chinese intelligence posts, the San Isidro Movement, the Brothers to the Rescue shootdown. We have examined the unstated reasons: regime change, Rubio’s lifelong fixation, the Venezuelan playbook, the political power of the Miami exile community.

We have not yet examined the simplest reason of all. The one that has explained American foreign policy interventions more reliably than any other variable for a hundred and fifty years.

Money. Specifically: what is in the ground.

On May 24, 2026, The Atlantic’s Vivian Salama published a piece titled “How to Break Cuba” that did what the best investigative journalism does — it followed the transaction. The transaction goes like this:

On May 1, 2026, Donald Trump signed an executive order expanding sanctions on Cuba, making the embargo comparable in scope to those imposed on Iran, Russia, and North Korea. The order specifically targeted companies doing business with the Cuban regime. Within a week, Sherritt International — the Canadian nickel and cobalt mining company that has been Cuba’s most important foreign mining partner since 1994 — announced it would dissolve its partnership with the state-owned General Nickel Company, ending the Moa Nickel joint venture and its interests in electricity generation and natural gas.

Then Sherritt reversed course. It would not dissolve the partnership. It would suspend it. And it was in talks to sell a controlling ownership stake in Sherritt to Gillon Capital — a Dallas-based firm belonging to the family of Ray Washburne, a real-estate executive who served in Trump’s first administration.

The deal, Salama reported, had the apparent blessing of the US State and Treasury Departments.

Read that sequence again. The United States imposed sanctions so punitive that a Canadian company was forced to abandon its Cuban operations. A firm connected to the Trump administration then moved to buy that company. With the approval of the departments responsible for enforcing the sanctions that created the buying opportunity.

This is not a conspiracy theory. It is a transaction, conducted in public, reported in The Atlantic, and denied by no one involved. Neither Gillon Capital nor Sherritt responded to requests for comment.

Moa

The scale of Cuba’s eastern nickel deposits. Nearly 25 years of reserves remain, according to recent estimates, beneath this striking red landscape.

The story of Cuba’s nickel and cobalt begins in the red earth around the eastern town of Moa, where some of the richest laterite deposits in the world sit close to the surface. The United States built the Nicaro nickel plant during World War II because the metal was essential for military production. When the revolution came, Cuba seized it, along with the Moa Bay complex. The revolutionaries did not know how to run the operations. Within two years, the New York Times was reporting that Cuba’s mining industry had been “virtually destroyed.”

The Soviet Union stepped in, as it did for everything in Cuba’s early decades. Soviet engineers retooled the plants, and nickel became a pillar of the Cuban economy — funding power stations, schools, hospitals. When the Soviet Union collapsed and the Special Period began, Cuba turned to Canada. Sherritt International signed the deal in 1994: Cuba provided the ore and the labor, Sherritt brought the capital, the refining technology, and the access to global markets.

The partnership survived for thirty-two years. It survived the Clinton administration’s Helms-Burton Act, which specifically barred profits from confiscated property. It survived the Bush years, the Obama thaw, the first Trump term’s reimposition of sanctions. Sherritt was sanctioned, its executives barred from entering the United States, and it kept operating. Nickel — raw or semifinished — was Cuba’s third-largest export in 2024. China was the top buyer.

The site still holds nearly a quarter-century of remaining reserves, according to Sherritt’s own estimates. What changed was not the geology. What changed was that an American president decided to make the sanctions so comprehensive that even Sherritt, which had weathered every previous iteration of the embargo, could not continue. And then an American firm connected to that president appeared to buy the carcass.

The Canadian Exception — Revisited

The Moa Nickel plant at night. Once a U.S.-built wartime facility, later Soviet-retooled, and for 32 years a Canadian-Cuban joint venture — now at the center of shifting ownership

This series examined Canada’s relationship with Cuba in Part 8 — “The Canadian Exception: Friends Across the Water.” That exception was never just about tourism and diplomacy. It was about business. Sherritt International was the corporate embodiment of the Canadian exception — a Toronto-based company that maintained a major industrial presence in Cuba in defiance of American sanctions for three decades.

The forced dissolution of Sherritt’s Cuban operations is the end of the Canadian exception in its most consequential form. Canada could send tourists and maintain an embassy. But the thing that actually mattered — the thing that gave Cuba a non-American, non-Russian, non-Chinese economic partner with real capital and real market access — was Sherritt. That is now being transferred to an American firm with ties to the administration that engineered its departure.

There is a word for this, and the word is not “market forces.” The word is expropriation — conducted not by revolutionaries in fatigues but by regulators in suits, using the secondary sanctions mechanism that the United States has refined over decades of embargo enforcement. The result is the same: a foreign company’s assets in Cuba are being transferred to a politically connected buyer under duress. The duress is not a revolution. It is a sanctions regime. The effect on the company losing its assets is identical.

Sherritt’s Canadian shareholders might reflect on the irony. The Helms-Burton Act was designed to punish anyone who profited from property confiscated from Americans by the Cuban revolution. What is happening now is the confiscation of Canadian property by the American sanctions regime, for the benefit of Americans. The revolution, it turns out, goes both ways.

Critical Minerals and the China Problem

The nickel-cobalt story has a second dimension that extends far beyond Cuba: the global race for critical-minerals dominance.

Both nickel and cobalt are essential to modern manufacturing — cellphones, car batteries, defense applications. China currently dominates global supply chains for both. The Trump administration’s broader strategy, managed by Interior Secretary Doug Burgum, involves sanctions, export controls, tariffs, and diplomatic pressure to redirect mineral supply chains away from China and toward the United States. Cuba’s deposits represent a potential piece of that puzzle — if Washington controls the island’s mining operations, it adds capacity outside the Chinese supply chain.

Burgum told The Atlantic that the United States would like to see a “critical-minerals partnership” with Cuba that could create economic opportunities for “both the American and Cuban people.” He then added — using language now indistinguishable from Rubio’s — that “the economic trajectory of Cuba will not change as long as the people who are in charge of it now remain in power.”

Democracy, human rights, political prisoners — these are the public vocabulary. “As long as the people who are in charge remain in power” is the operational vocabulary. The people in charge control GAESA. GAESA controls the mining partnerships. The mining partnerships control the nickel and cobalt. The nickel and cobalt are what the United States wants. Every other element of the pressure campaign — the oil blockade, the carrier, the indictment, the surveillance drones — is the apparatus that creates the conditions under which the mineral wealth changes hands.

This is not a novel pattern. It is the oldest pattern in the hemisphere’s history. United Fruit in Guatemala. Anaconda Copper in Chile. Standard Oil in Venezuela. The specific mineral changes. The structure does not. A Latin American country possesses something the United States wants. The United States identifies the country’s government as a problem. The government is pressured, sanctioned, or removed. The resource is transferred to American ownership. The word “democracy” appears somewhere in the press release.

Nickel and cobalt are essential for EV batteries, electronics, and defense applications. Cuba’s deposits represent a strategic piece in the global race to reduce dependence on Chinese supply chains

The Boomerang

But the pattern has a flaw, and The Atlantic identified it: if Washington pushes too hard, Cuba may respond by offering its mining operations to China or Russia. Diego von Vacano, a political-science professor at Texas A&M, noted that this is “a good chance for China and Russia to step back in.” The administration’s drive to pry Cuba out of the Chinese-Russian orbit could accomplish the opposite — pushing Havana to hand its most valuable strategic assets to precisely the adversaries Washington is trying to outcompete.

This is the Cuba paradox that this series has documented from the beginning: every escalation designed to weaken the Cuban government risks strengthening the relationships Washington claims it wants to sever. The oil blockade was supposed to force Cuba to the table. Instead, it was a Russian tanker — the Kolodkin — that delivered the only significant fuel shipment in four months. The sanctions were supposed to isolate Cuba economically. Instead, China remains the top buyer of Cuban nickel. The carrier in the Caribbean is supposed to intimidate Cuba into compliance. Instead, it validates every argument Havana makes to Beijing and Moscow about why they need to deepen their partnerships.

If the Gillon Capital acquisition goes through and Cuba’s nickel and cobalt operations pass to American ownership, Washington gets what it wants. If it doesn’t — if Cuba balks, or if China or Russia make a competing offer — Washington may find that it has destroyed a Canadian company’s business, antagonized a NATO ally, and handed a strategic mineral asset to its principal adversary. The administration is betting that the squeeze will work before the boomerang does.

Batista’s Ghost

The pre-revolutionary model that the current U.S. policy finds ‘enamoring’ — American-friendly economic control returns in spectral form.

Trump, Salama reported, “is enamored of the opportunities a pliant Cuba could offer domestic businesses, much as it did in the Fulgencio Batista era, leading up to the 1959 revolution.”

This sentence should be read carefully, because it is the most revealing thing any journalist has reported about the administration’s Cuba policy. The Batista era is the model. Not the Obama opening, which sought normalized relations between sovereign governments. Not even the Cold War containment model, which sought to isolate Cuba as a geopolitical adversary. The model is the pre-revolutionary period when American companies owned Cuba’s sugar mills, mines, utilities, and casinos, and the Cuban government existed to facilitate American profit.

Part 4 of this series — “Washington’s Cuba: The Batista Years” — documented what that era looked like. American corporations controlled 90 percent of Cuba’s mines, 80 percent of its utilities, and 50 percent of its railways and sugar industry. The Mafia ran Havana’s casinos with the government’s blessing. Batista’s regime tortured dissidents, rigged elections, and maintained power through a combination of American support and domestic terror. When the revolution came, it came because a majority of Cubans decided that this arrangement was intolerable.

That is the era the current administration finds “enamoring.”

The gap between the stated policy — democracy, human rights, freedom for the Cuban people — and the revealed preference — a return to Batista-era American economic control, facilitated by regime change and enabled by the transfer of mineral assets to Trump-connected firms — is the gap this series has been documenting since Part 1. The gap is not subtle. It is the width of the entire foreign policy. On one side: the vocabulary of liberation. On the other: the mechanics of extraction.

What the Red Earth Knows

Cuba’s nickel deposits have been there for millions of years. They were there when the Taíno lived on the island. They were there when Spain colonized it for four centuries. They were there when the United States replaced Spain. They were there when Batista ruled for the Americans. They were there when Castro seized them in the name of the revolution. They were there when Soviet engineers rebuilt the plants. They were there when Sherritt signed the deal in 1994. They are still there now — sitting in the red earth around Moa, indifferent to the flags that fly over them.

The nickel does not care who owns it. The people who mine it do.

Baby Alejandro is in his incubator, if the power is on. The red earth is patient. The empire is not.
