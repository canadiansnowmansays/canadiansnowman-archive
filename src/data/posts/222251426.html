I. Eight Days Out

When I closed the last instalment of this series, I promised to keep writing down the quiet rooms until November 3, and after. I did not expect the quiet to end quite so loudly, or quite so close to the wire.

On Monday, October 26, 2026, eight days before Election Day, Democrats on the House Judiciary Committee released a staff report of more than fifty pages and called for an independent special counsel to investigate President Trump’s ties to Jeffrey Epstein. As Michael Gold reported for The New York Times, the report accuses the president of being an active participant in Epstein’s abuse of young women, while relying largely on allegations that were already public. Representative Jamie Raskin of Maryland, the committee’s top Democrat, said the Justice Department had deliberately withheld evidence and appeared to be covering up crimes to protect the president.

The timing is not subtle, and nobody pretends otherwise. Democrats have spent around $36 million on Epstein-related advertising this cycle, according to an AdImpact analysis cited by Politico Playbook. A Republican pollster told the same newsletter the issue was breaking through in the homestretch.

But timing is not the same as substance, and there is substance here worth separating from the campaign noise. That is what this essay is for.

II. What the Report Says, and What It Doesn’t

The report’s title tells you its ambition. It lays out what Democrats call the case against Donald Trump emerging in the Epstein files, and what they describe as the Justice Department’s desperate efforts to cover it up.

According to The Daily Beast, the report gathers allegations from eleven women who say Trump abused or assaulted them, in some cases alongside Epstein. One account is new. Beatrice Keul, a Swiss former pageant contestant, told committee staff that Trump raped her during a 1993 pageant, an encounter she had previously described publicly as a rebuffed advance, as HuffPost reported. The White House has previously called her allegations fake, and Trump denies them.

The documentary claims are, to my mind, more important than any single accusation, because documents can be checked. As Travis Gettys summarised for Raw Story, the report points to an FBI search linking Trump and Epstein to a 2007 case number for exploitation of minors, along with a whistleblower who says records tied to that case are being withheld. It cites a redacted 2009 document in which Trump reportedly said Epstein was never asked to leave Mar-a-Lago, which sits awkwardly beside the president’s long-told story that he threw Epstein out. And it accuses Todd Blanche, now Attorney General and formerly Trump’s defence lawyer, of repeatedly steering Ghislaine Maxwell away from a survivor’s account implicating Trump when he interviewed her in 2025. Democrats suggest that could amount to witness tampering, and that the department’s conduct may rise to criminal obstruction of justice.

Blanche says the department carried out a Herculean review of millions of files. White House spokeswoman Lauren Bis said Trump has been totally exonerated on anything related to Epstein, and that he has done more for Epstein’s victims than anyone before him.

I want to be fair about the report’s limits, because this series has always tried to count rather than shout. It is a minority staff report, unsigned, written by one party in the final week of a campaign. Several of its accounts rest on women’s own statements to committee staff, without independent corroboration. None of that makes it false. It makes it what it is: a prosecution memo without a prosecutor. Which is precisely why Democrats are asking for one.

III. The Special Prosecutor He Already Got

Here is the part of the story the campaign ads tend to skip, and it is the part I find most revealing.

Democrats are not the first people to ask the Justice Department for a dedicated Epstein investigator. Donald Trump was.

On November 14, 2025, days after House Democrats released emails in which Epstein wrote that Trump “knew about the girls,” the president demanded that the Justice Department investigate Epstein’s ties to prominent Democrats, among them Bill Clinton, Larry Summers and Reid Hoffman, as the Boston Globe reported at the time. He called the whole affair another Russia scam with all arrows pointing to the Democrats. That same afternoon, as Fox News reported, Attorney General Pam Bondi announced that Jay Clayton, the U.S. Attorney in Manhattan, would lead it.

So the principle has already been conceded. The White House accepts that Epstein’s network warrants a dedicated federal prosecutor. It simply believes the prosecutor should be pointed in one direction only.

That is the definition of carte blanche: a blank cheque, signed in advance, with the payee filled in by the man holding the pen. The Democrats’ report asks for something different. It asks for an investigator the president does not appoint, cannot direct, and would not enjoy reading about. Their report concedes there is little reason to believe this administration will grant one. That, I suspect, is the point. The request is not addressed to Todd Blanche. It is addressed to voters.

IV. The Courtroom That Stayed Open

Readers of Part XXXIX will remember Judge Emmet Sullivan, the Reagan appointee who has spent the summer trying to make the Justice Department obey a law that passed the House 427 to 1.

His September 16 opinion deserves a closer look than I gave it last time. As Hannah Parry reported for Newsweek, the department had argued, among other things, that because Congress did not object to a letter it sent on January 30, 2026, that silence somehow amended the statute. Sullivan noted that the department cited no authority for the idea that congressional silence functions as an implied amendment. Nor, he wrote, does administrative inconvenience excuse a statutory obligation.

Then came the appeal. In asking the D.C. Circuit to pause Sullivan’s order on foreign-language records, Blanche’s lawyers argued that Katie Phang, the journalist who brought the case, would not even have a right to sue under the proposed Epstein Files Transparency Act II, as Raw Story separately reported.

Pause on that for a moment. The Justice Department is using, as a shield in court, a bill that the Speaker of the House sent members home to avoid voting on. It is citing the sequel to explain why the original should not be enforced, while the sequel sits in a drawer on Capitol Hill. Leacock wrote a story about a man who rode off in all directions. He never imagined a government department that could litigate in all of them at once.

Sullivan, for his part, has been unusually plain about where this could lead. At an earlier hearing he reminded government lawyers that he is probably the only judge in his circuit to have held Justice Department attorneys in civil contempt, adding that he had no desire to go down that road again, as All Rise News reported. A judge who tells you he has done something before, and would rather not do it again, is not making a threat. He is giving directions.

V. The Retirement Caucus

In Part XXXIX, I described how Thomas Massie and Ro Khanna gathered commitments from 218 members for a discharge petition to force a vote on the Transparency Act II, only for the House to leave town before the last signatures could be filed.

One detail I should have stressed. Massie lost his own Republican primary to a Trump-endorsed challenger on May 19, 2026, as ABC News 4 reported. The author of the Epstein Files Transparency Act is himself a lame duck. He has nothing left to lose, which in Washington is the closest thing to freedom.

Khanna has already named the constituency he is counting on. Speaking to Roll Call in September, he pointed to the lame-duck session and what he called the retirement caucus: members who are not coming back. Whatever happens on November 3, a few dozen defeated or departing members will return to Washington in November with no primary to fear and no president to please. The petition may well reach the floor then.

VI. Follow the Money

The other development of the past month got less attention than it deserved.

On October 15, 2026, Senate and House Democrats unveiled the Epstein Wall Street Records Act. As the Boston Globe reported, the bill would require four federal financial regulators, including the Federal Reserve, to release their records on Epstein and his associates, and would direct them to investigate banks and executives who did business with him. It is led by Elizabeth Warren, with backing from Ron Wyden and Robert Garcia, the top Democrats likely to chair the Senate Finance and House Oversight committees if their party prevails.

Wyden’s office has already identified some $1.5 billion in Epstein-linked transactions moving through four major banks. As American Banker noted, if Democrats win both chambers and pass the bill, the president would face a choice: sign it, or veto a bill whose only purpose is to show where Jeffrey Epstein’s money went.

I spent eighteen years handling escalated complaints at a large company. I learned that when someone says a matter is closed, the first thing to ask for is the billing record. It is rarely closed.

VII. Meanwhile, Abroad

It is worth looking beyond the American border, because other countries are handling the Epstein files very differently.

In Britain, police arrested Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor, the former prince, in February. On October 8, a High Court judge ruled that the warrants used to search his homes were unlawful, as the Associated Press reported, but added that quashing the warrants does not end the investigation. In Norway, police opened a corruption inquiry into former prime minister Thorbjørn Jagland, who chaired the Nobel Peace Prize committee, over his ties to Epstein, according to Al Jazeera. The Nobel committee has since adopted ethics rules for the first time in its 125-year history, as The New York Times reported on October 9. In New York, Leon Botstein resigned from every position he still held at Bard College after Raskin’s committee raised new allegations, as Inside Higher Ed reported. Botstein denies any knowledge of or involvement in Epstein’s crimes.

A former prince arrested. A former prime minister investigated. A college president gone. Note what each of these has in common: in every case, someone outside the subject’s control was allowed to look.

At home, by contrast, the House held Leon Black in contempt by unanimous consent on September 16 and sent the referral to the U.S. Attorney for the District of Columbia. The department said that office would review it carefully, as Reuters reported. Six weeks later, I can find no public sign that the review has gone anywhere. Black denies any knowledge of Epstein’s crimes.

VIII. Carte Blanche

Which brings us to the question so many readers have put to me: if Democrats win, can they actually do anything?

The honest answer has two halves, and I owe readers both.

The first half is what a Democratic House could do. As HuffPost noted, if Democrats retake the House in January, the Judiciary Committee would lead any potential impeachment, and Monday’s report reads very much like the opening brief. Subpoenas would follow. The FBI notes Judge Sullivan has been fighting to see, the 2007 case file, the Black referral and the bank records would all become fair game for a committee with a gavel. A House majority can impeach on a simple majority vote.

The second half is what it cannot do. Conviction in the Senate requires two-thirds of the senators present: 67 if all 100 vote. No plausible result on November 3 gives Democrats anything close to that. Impeachment would be a record, a trial and a vote. On present arithmetic, it would not be a removal.

And yet the president would not be able to do the thing he always does when he loses: go to court.

In Nixon v. United States (1993), a case about a federal judge named Walter Nixon, not the president, the Supreme Court unanimously held that the Senate’s handling of an impeachment trial is not something courts can review. The Constitution gives the Senate the “sole Power to try all Impeachments,” and the Court took the word “sole” at its word.

Nor would the 2024 immunity ruling in Trump v. United States help him. That decision shields presidents from criminal prosecution for official acts. Impeachment is not a criminal prosecution. The Constitution says so explicitly: a convicted official may be removed and disqualified, and then remains separately liable to indictment. A president who argued that his official acts are immune from impeachment would be asking the Supreme Court to read the impeachment clause out of the Constitution. Even this Court is unlikely to go there.

So here is the shape of it. The administration has handed out carte blanche: to a prosecutor aimed at its opponents, to an Attorney General defending in court a law he has conceded he is violating, to a contempt referral that has gone quiet. The president has sought carte blanche from the courts and largely received it. But the Founders left exactly one room where no judge can sign that cheque. It is the Senate chamber, sitting as a court.

IX. A Canadian Observation

From up here, the strangest thing about the American Epstein story has always been how many people seem to think it can be closed by declaration. A hoax, a distraction, old news, totally exonerated. Each phrase is an attempt to stamp a file “closed” without anyone having read it.

Other democracies, for all their faults, have tried a different method. They let somebody independent read the file.

That is all Monday’s report finally asks for. Not a verdict. A reader.

On November 3, Americans will decide whether the next Congress has the power to insist on one. The Senate will almost certainly not have the votes to remove anyone. But a House with subpoena power, a docket in Judge Sullivan’s courtroom, a lame-duck retirement caucus and a bank-records bill would make the next two years very different from the last fourteen weeks.

The women at the centre of this story have been waiting since the 1990s for somebody to read the file properly. Eight more days is not long. It only feels that way because they have been made to wait so long already.
