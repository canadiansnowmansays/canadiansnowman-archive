By Stephen Gordon

In July 2026, the President of the United States and his Homeland Security Secretary, Markwayne Mullin, announced that they had found more than 270,000 noncitizens on the voter rolls of just four states: Nevada, California, New Jersey and Pennsylvania. They did not say “may have found” or “are looking into.” They said it the way you would say the sun came up.

By Tuesday, September 22, 2026, the Nevada share of that figure had been reduced to zero.

Not reduced to a smaller but still troubling number. Zero. Every name the federal government had actually put in front of Nevada’s election officials belonged to a citizen of the United States.

Three days later, on Friday, September 25, 2026, the Supreme Court allowed the administration to put a vastly expanded citizenship database into the hands of every state election office in the country, in time for a midterm election that begins, for millions of voters, in a matter of days.

Those two events are the same story. This series has argued for months that the administration’s election strategy does not depend on winning. It depends on friction: claims that cost more to disprove than to make, investigations that never have to end, tools that sort voters into the suspect and the unsuspected. This week supplied the clearest example yet of how the arithmetic works, and then the Court handed over a machine for producing more of it.

The arithmetic of Nevada

Here is how the Nevada number travelled, as Nick Corasaniti reported for the New York Times on September 24, 2026.

In July, Secretary Mullin said Nevada had as many as 15,903 noncitizens on its rolls. The letters that followed to the four states did something the public announcement had not: they hedged. Each qualified the President’s flat assertion with the phrase “there may be as many as.” None offered proof of a single noncitizen voter.

In August, Nevada election officials met virtually with Homeland Security staff, and the meeting was recorded. C. Murphy Hebert, the chief deputy in the Nevada secretary of state’s office, asked the obvious question: had the 15,000 figure been vetted? Kimberley Vogt of Homeland Security declined the word “vetted,” explained that the department had “done something” with the data to arrive at 15,000, and conceded that it had not had a full manual review of each case.

Ms. Vogt then sent a clarifying email. The department had not reduced 15,903 to 185, she wrote. It had manually reviewed some of the 15,903 and confirmed that 185 were not citizens. The distinction matters, because it means the 15,903 was never withdrawn. It was left standing, unexamined, beside a smaller number that had been.

Then Nevada examined the 185. On September 22, Deputy Attorney General Greg D. Ott wrote back to Homeland Security that the state’s records showed every one of them to be a citizen, and that the department had “provided nothing of substance to suggest otherwise.”

The department’s reply was that Nevada’s characterisation was “false,” and that the 185 were merely a subset that had been through a rigorous manual review at the time of the last meeting. That is, the rigorously reviewed subset was the one that turned out to be entirely wrong.

Meanwhile, on September 9, the department had written again, demanding that Nevada carefully review each of the 15,903. Nevada’s answer is the most revealing detail in the whole affair: it says it was never given a list of 15,903 people. What it received was a batch of 6,218 entries, identified by something Homeland Security called a “voter case number,” with no names, no dates of birth, no addresses. The department says the smaller batch was sent at Nevada’s request. The Times found no record of Nevada making that request.

Mr. Ott’s letter explains why a bare number is useless. Nevada recently moved from county-run voter registration to a statewide system, so a single voter may carry one number from the old county system and another from the new one. And a number, he wrote, says nothing about whether a person was born in the United States, naturalised, or derived citizenship through a parent. A matching number establishes only that the number exists, or once did, somewhere in a registration database.

So the full sequence runs like this: 270,000 announced across four states; 15,903 claimed for Nevada; 6,218 sent as unlabelled numbers; 185 named; zero confirmed. And to generate that sequence, the Times reported, hundreds of Homeland Security agents were pulled from investigating international crime and terrorism for a roughly three-week search of voter rolls.

“There may be as many as”

There is a grammar to this, and anyone who has worked a complaints desk will recognise it.

At the top, a claim is stated as fact. By the time it reaches the people who will have to act on it, it has acquired a qualifier. By the time anyone asks for the underlying records, the records turn out to be partial, or unlabelled, or not yet reviewed. And by the time the claim is disproved, the people who made it are no longer talking about it. They are talking about the next one.

The qualifier is the whole game. “There may be as many as 15,903” is technically true of any number at all, including zero. It commits the department to nothing. But the public version, the one said on camera, carried no qualifier, and the public version is the one that shapes what voters believe about their own elections. Nevada’s secretary of state, Francisco Aguilar, put it plainly: repeating unsupported claims does not make them true. He is right about the truth. He is less right, unfortunately, about the effect. Repetition does not make a claim true, but it does make it familiar, and familiarity is what the claim was built to achieve.

This is the Obfuscate stage of the TACO pattern this series keeps returning to, but with a twist. Usually obfuscation follows a retreat: the administration threatens, loses, and then muddies what the loss meant. Here the obfuscation is built in from the start. The claim is made in a form that cannot lose, because it was never specific enough to be checked, and it is withdrawn in a form that cannot be noticed, because nobody announces that a number has quietly become zero.

The database gets its green light

On Friday, September 25, 2026, the Supreme Court allowed the administration to deploy its expanded version of SAVE, the Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements system, for use by state and local election officials. The order was unsigned and gave no vote count, but Justices Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson dissented, as Abbie VanSickle reported for the New York Times on September 25, 2026.

Readers of Part XLVI will remember this system as one of the doors that kept losing in court. In June 2026, Judge Sparkle L. Sooknanan blocked states from using it to screen their rolls, finding that federal agencies, in their scramble to comply with an executive order, had haphazardly combined and repurposed the private information of millions of Americans, including citizenship data they knew to be unreliable. On September 5, the D.C. Circuit declined to let the system back online. On September 14, the Supreme Court handed the administration a 7-2 loss on the Postal Service’s mail-ballot rule, and the President responded by accusing the Court of rulings so bad the country might not recover.

Eleven days later, the same Court let the database through.

The difference between the two rulings is not mysterious. The Postal Service rule had no statute behind it. SAVE, the majority concluded, probably does: the 1996 Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act appears, in the Court’s reading, to contemplate an information-sharing arrangement like the modified system. The dissenters disagreed, but it is a legal argument rather than a political one.

What the ruling puts into circulation is another matter. As Nicholas Nehamas and Zach Montague explained for the New York Times the same day, SAVE was built to check whether immigrants were eligible for benefits and licences. In 2025, with help from Elon Musk's Department of Government Efficiency, it was expanded to pull in Social Security's master files: names, Social Security numbers, dates and places of birth, citizenship indicators and death records for hundreds of millions of Americans. For the first time, it could query natural-born citizens who had never dealt with immigration authorities. It was modified to allow bulk searches. The administration is working to add passport numbers and state driver's licences.

The Privacy Act of 1974 was written to prohibit precisely the thing this resembles: a centralised federal information system built from sensitive personal data. The challengers argued that point. The Court’s majority did not accept it, at least not at this stage.

The shrinkage table

Two cautions before reading too much into it.

First, the Nevada figures did not come from SAVE. According to what federal officials have told state election supervisors, that effort relied on other Homeland Security immigration data. That cuts in an uncomfortable direction: the inflation shows up in two separate pipelines. It is a property of the campaign, not the software.

Second, the bottom lines are not findings of illegal voting. Texas’s 117 are referrals to the attorney general’s office for investigation. The national 10,000 are referrals to federal investigators. A referral is the beginning of an inquiry, not the end of one. Yet even taken at face value, Texas ran more than 18 million voters through the system, and more than one flag in five turned out to be a citizen before anyone looked closely.

The reason is not hard to find. Social Security did not begin maintaining citizenship information until 1981, and many people who first registered with the agency as noncitizens have since naturalised. The file does not update itself when a person takes the oath. And Homeland Security knew. Its own internal records last year acknowledged “shortfalls in data accuracy” that could cause the system to produce inaccurate results.

Which means the people most likely to be wrongly flagged are not random. They are naturalised citizens. They are the Americans who did everything the process asked of them.

Retail, not wholesale

The most important sentence in Friday’s ruling may be the one that sounds like a limit.

The majority appeared to accept that federal law still prevents states from removing voters from their rolls en masse in the weeks before an election. What states can still do, the Court noted, is use the tool to check individual voters.

That reads like a guardrail. In practice, it is a description of how friction works. A mass purge is visible, challengeable and, this close to an election, plainly illegal. An individual check is none of those things. It is one letter asking one voter to prove citizenship. It is one name forwarded to one prosecutor. It is one poll worker told that a particular registration has been “flagged.” None of it requires striking anyone from the rolls. All of it raises the cost of voting for the person on the receiving end, and the people on the receiving end are, disproportionately, the recently naturalised.

Justice Jackson’s 12-page dissent says this directly: the majority’s decision creates the conditions for states to cancel the registrations of at least some recently naturalised citizens, or to demand proof of citizenship before they vote. Her balancing is blunt. The harm of burdening or disenfranchising even a few lawful voters, she wrote, outweighs the nonexistent harm the government suffers when it is stopped from doing something it likely lacks the authority to do.

The timing sharpens the point. On the same Friday, in a separate order, the same Court blocked Missouri from switching congressional maps for the third time this month, as Ann E. Marimow and Mitch Smith reported for the New York Times on September 25, 2026. "The election is not merely upcoming; it has already started," the justices wrote, warning of "electoral chaos" if the rules changed now. Ballots are printed, absentee voting is under way, and the lines must hold still. The database ruling is the other side of that coin. A map is a rule the Court can see changing. A flag on one voter's record is not. The Court will not let the district lines move in the final weeks, but it has let a new sorting tool into election offices in those same weeks, because a tool that checks voters one at a time never looks like a change to anything.

Part LI argued that an investigation needs no court’s permission to start and no resolution to keep running. SAVE is the same mechanism with a user interface. It does not need to find a single noncitizen to do its work. It only needs to generate flags, and it generates them reliably, including against citizens.

Where democracy is fighting back

The fighting back, this week, was done almost entirely by people whose names you have not heard. A deputy attorney general in Carson City who took 185 names and checked every one. A chief deputy who asked, on a recorded call, whether a number had been vetted, and did not accept a non-answer. In Travis County, Texas, the tax assessor, Celia Israel, and the county clerk, Dyana Limon-Mercado, filed a brief with the Supreme Court warning that the system had introduced flawed data and would cause eligible voters to be wrongly purged. They lost. Their warning is now on the record, which matters when the errors arrive.

It is also worth noticing who is not rushing forward. The Times reports that it remains to be seen how eager state and local officials will be to use a system that has already mislabelled citizens. In March 2026, the President directed Homeland Security to use SAVE to compile lists of verified citizens for each state. As of this week, it has not done so. A tool is only as dangerous as the hands that pick it up, and many of the hands in American election administration belong to people who have spent this year learning exactly how unreliable the federal numbers are.

The Court opened a door on Friday. Whether anyone walks through it at scale will be decided in thousands of county and local election offices, by officials who have just watched Nevada’s 15,903 become zero.

The next number

There will be another figure. There always is. It will be announced without a qualifier, sent to the states with one, and reviewed by people who were not in the room when it was invented. It will shrink, as every one of these numbers has shrunk. And by the time it reaches zero, or 117, or 277, the announcement will have done its work, and a new tool will be ready to produce the one after that.

A civics teacher might say the answer is simply to check the numbers. Nevada checked. Iowa checked. Texas checked. The checking works. What the checking cannot do is travel as fast as the claim, and that asymmetry, not any single database, is the thing the administration has learned to exploit.

The Canadian in me keeps returning to one small fact from the Nevada exchange. Asked whether her department’s number had been vetted, a federal official said she would not use that sentence. It is the most honest thing anyone in the federal government said about noncitizen voting all month.
