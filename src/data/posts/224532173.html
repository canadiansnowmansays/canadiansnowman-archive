“But I say unto you, Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you.” — Matthew 5:44

Near the end of an interview that aired on 60 Minutes on Sunday, November 8, 2026, Ross Douthat changed the subject. “Let’s talk about death,” he said.

As Peter Wehner recounted in The Atlantic on November 10, 2026, Douthat asked the president what he expected to happen to him when he died. Trump said that as a religious person he would, in theory, go to heaven. Pressed on whether he really believed it, he said: “I hope so. I don’t want to go to hell.”

Then Douthat set a test. Suppose, he said, that all Trump had to do to enter paradise was to feel love and forgiveness for James Comey, Nancy Pelosi and the others he had said he hated. Could he do it?

Trump knew the right answer. He said so. He then declined to give it. The people in question were “very bad people, very bad for our country,” and the answer was no. Douthat raised the stakes as far as they go: before the judgment throne of God? Still no. “I dislike, I can use the word hate, I do hate some of my enemies.” And then, to close the door on any misunderstanding: “No, because I do hate some of my enemies and I always will.”

This series has spent more than a year auditing the distance between what Donald Trump professes about faith and what he does. Usually the work is slow. You set a scripture beside a speech, a commandment beside a policy, and you measure the gap. This time the subject did the measuring himself, on national television, with eternity as the unit.

The honest answer

Wehner, a former speechwriter for three Republican presidents and a Christian who writes often about faith, does something unexpected with the exchange. He believes Trump was telling the truth, and he gives him a measure of credit for it. The easy thing would have been to say yes. That is the answer every churchgoer is taught, and Trump has given easy answers about religion all his life.

Wehner is also careful not to pretend forgiveness is simple. He warns against what he calls cheap forgiveness, a cousin of the “cheap grace” that the German theologian Dietrich Bonhoeffer condemned before the Nazis hanged him. Forgiveness takes time, he argues, and it should. Forgiving someone instantly usually means the hurt has not been faced. He cites the Christian psychiatrist Curt Thompson, who argues that real forgiveness requires remembering rather than forgetting: going back over the injury, ideally with others, until the memory is reshaped rather than buried. Forgiveness that comes too fast, at no cost, lets the person who did the damage skip remorse and reinforces his sense that he can do it again.

That is a serious point, and it cuts in an awkward direction. Comey and Pelosi did not wrong Trump in the way a thief wrongs his victim. They held him to account, and Wehner notes that the president has a habit of confusing the two. The people who were genuinely wronged in the Trump years, the families separated at the border, the officials threatened for counting votes, the reporters and judges and prosecutors named as enemies, would be entitled to take their time. The man asking for no forgiveness is not the one with the grievance. He is the one who caused most of them.

No God in the picture

What made the 60 Minutes answer so revealing was not that forgiveness is hard. It was that Trump does not seem to think it is necessary.

He said years ago that he was not sure he had ever asked God for forgiveness. “I don’t bring God into that picture,” he explained. The logic is consistent. A man who has never needed to be forgiven has no reason to understand why anyone else should be.

Readers of this series will remember where it began. Part 1 called Trump’s public religion “prayer as performance art,” and the National Prayer Breakfast has always been its main stage. For seventy years the breakfast has been one of the few places in Washington where politicians of both parties sat together and prayed for each other. Wehner points out that Trump has turned it into a platform for denouncing his opponents as dishonest and corrupt.

The pattern is not new. On February 6, 2020, the day after the Senate acquitted him in his first impeachment trial, Trump spoke to more than 3,000 people at the 68th breakfast. As Catholic News Service reported that day, three members of Congress and an economist used their time to call for healing and love of neighbour. Trump used his to attack the Democrats who had impeached him. Six years later he has taken the next step. In 2020 he would not pray for his enemies. In 2026 he says he will hate them for ever.

The audit

In September 2025 this series ran Trump through the Ten Commandments, one by one. The Sermon on the Mount is a harder examination, because it does not ask only what a man does. It asks what is in his heart.

Matthew 5 has three requirements that bear on the 60 Minutes answer.

  • Love your enemies (Matthew 5:44). Trump: “I do hate some of my enemies and I always will.” Failed, by his own statement.

  • Pray for those who persecute you (Matthew 5:44). Trump at the 2020 Prayer Breakfast mocked people who say they are praying for him. He has never been heard to pray for anyone who opposed him. Failed.

  • Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy (Matthew 5:7). Trump on Robert Mueller’s death in March 2026: “Good, I’m glad he’s dead.” Failed.

There is no partial credit available. The passage is not ambiguous, and it is not obscure. It is among the most famous words in the most widely read book in the country Trump leads, and he was given the chance, on camera, to say he believed them. He said he did not.

The drug

Wehner’s central claim is that hate, more than narcissism or anything else, is what drives Trump. It fuels the posts on Truth Social. It explains his appetite for power. It feeds his obsessions. And it behaves like a drug: the more he indulges it, the more of it he needs. Not even the deaths of the men he considered enemies have satisfied it. Wehner lists three: John McCain, Rob Reiner and Robert Mueller.

He adds two observations that are easy to miss. The first is that hate has crowded out joy. It is hard to remember Trump looking truly happy. The second is that it leaves no room for intimacy, because anyone close to him might become an enemy tomorrow. Hate does its worst damage to the person who carries it.

A Canadian writing this should be honest about the limits of his vantage point. Nobody outside Trump’s head knows what is in it, and this series has avoided diagnosis elsewhere for good reason. But the president did not leave us to guess. He was asked directly and he answered directly. When a man tells you, under that much pressure, that he will hate his enemies for ever, the decent thing is to believe him.

Castle Rock

Wehner’s sharper charge is against the party and the movement that formed around Trump. He argues that the Republican Party, and much of the Christian right with it, has come to look like the man: the brutal personal attacks, the open cruelty, the pleasure in watching the strong hurt the weak. The party of family values, law and order and the formation of character in the young, he writes, has become Castle Rock, the stronghold of Jack’s tribe in William Golding’s Lord of the Flies. Power comes from force, and hatred is the badge of belonging.

This is where the hypocrisy in this series’ title finally lands, and it is worth being precise about it. On November 8, Trump was not a hypocrite. He was, for once, consistent: a man who hates his enemies said that he hates his enemies. The hypocrisy belongs to the movement that spent a decade presenting him as the defender of Christian values.

In a companion essay for The Atlantic on November 5, 2026, Wehner traces how that happened. Roughly eight in ten white evangelicals voted for Trump in each of his three campaigns. The alliance began in 1979, when Jerry Falwell launched the Moral Majority and declared, “We are fighting a holy war, and this time we are going to win.” Over the following decades, Wehner argues, a religious tradition was turned into a political instrument. Its rhetoric became apocalyptic, its temper fearful and aggrieved, and many of its leaders went looking for a fighter who would not just beat the other side but bloody it. They found him. In Wehner’s words, the cruellest and most corrupt president in American history became the champion of many of those who most loudly professed to follow Jesus.

Now that champion has told the country that, given the choice, he would rather keep his hatreds than enter heaven. The movement that chose him owes its own members an answer to a simple question: which of those two things did you think you were voting for?

Locked on the inside

Wehner reaches for two writers to explain what Trump chose. The first is C. S. Lewis, who wrote in The Problem of Pain that “the doors of hell are locked on the inside.” Lewis did not mean that God locks people out. He meant that people lock themselves in. They prefer their own will and pride; they would rather hold on to their grievances, their self-pity and their enmity than let them go, even to God.

The second is James Baldwin, who wrote that one reason people cling to their hates so stubbornly is that they sense, once the hate is gone, they will be forced to deal with pain. Wehner allows that the pain beneath Trump’s hatred may be enormous, and that in other circumstances he might be a tragic figure. He is not, Wehner concludes, because he has inflicted so much pain on so many people, especially the weak, and has so plainly enjoyed it.

That is the image this essay is named for. Douthat did not ask Trump to change a policy or apologise to anyone. He asked whether, on the far side of death, with everything at stake, Trump could let go. The answer was that the door would stay locked, and that Trump would keep the key on his side of it.

The other gospel

The same week offered a contrast so sharp that it almost reads as a reply.

On November 3, 2026, James Talarico won the Senate race in Texas, the first time a Democrat has won a Senate seat there since 1988, and Adam Hamilton won in Kansas, where Democrats had not won a Senate race since 1932. Both, Wehner noted, are committed and prominent Christians. Talarico, whose grandfather was a Baptist preacher in South Texas, holds a master’s degree in theological studies and set aside training for ordination to run. Hamilton has been a United Methodist pastor for more than 35 years. In 1990 he founded the Church of the Resurrection in Leawood, Kansas, whose first services were held in a funeral home. It is now the largest United Methodist congregation in America, and its members are split almost evenly between Republicans, Democrats and independents. He intends to keep preaching there part-time while serving in the Senate.

The two men speak a different language from the one Trump spoke on 60 Minutes. On election night Talarico said that “the politics of division is no match for the politics of love.” Both draw on the prophet Micah, who said the Lord requires us “to act justly and to love mercy and to walk humbly with your God.” Both draw on the Good Samaritan, the Sermon on the Mount, the two great commandments, and Matthew 25, where Jesus says that whatever we did for the least of these, we did for him. That last passage was the rubric for this series’ Order of Service for Stephen Miller. It reads differently in the mouth of a pastor who means it.

Wehner quotes the pastor and author Brian Zahnd, who once said he felt the Jesus he knew deserved a better Christianity than the one he had been shown. Many Americans, Wehner argues, and not a few evangelicals, have come to feel the same about the version of Christianity that has been preached from political stages for a decade. The results in Texas and Kansas suggest it.

The same yardstick

Fairness requires one more step, and Wehner takes it himself. Talarico and Hamilton will face the temptations that have undone others: to politicise their faith, to use scripture to bless their own side’s positions, and to lose their prophetic voice under pressure to be loyal to the party. Proclaiming a politics of love, Wehner writes, is easier than practising it. Many evangelicals are wary of their theology and of their stances on particular issues, and those disagreements are real.

This series will hold them to the same yardstick it has held Trump to. If a Senator Talarico uses Micah 6:8 to justify cruelty, or a Senator Hamilton turns the pulpit in Leawood into a campaign stage, they will get the same audit. That is the point of measuring against scripture rather than against party. The text does not change depending on who is quoting it.

From Canada, all of this can look slightly foreign. Canadian politicians rarely quote scripture from the platform, and when they do, voters tend to wince. But the question on the table is not a peculiarly American one. It is whether a person who claims a faith will accept that faith’s hardest demands when they cost him something. On November 8, the president of the United States was asked that question in its simplest possible form. He answered it honestly. He said no.

The doors of hell, Lewis wrote, are locked on the inside. On 60 Minutes the president showed the country his side of the door, and told it, in so many words, that he intends to keep it locked.
