The first four parts of this series went abroad: to the robotaxi fleets of China, to Wayve’s careful debut in London, to the cabbies who spend years learning the Knowledge, and to the money gap between European and American start-ups. This one stays in North America, and it starts at the front door.

The electric vehicle you see most often may not belong to anyone on your street. It is the van that drops off your parcels. On November 16, 2026, Andrew Moseman wrote for The Atlantic that Amazon now runs at least 50,000 electric vans, largely thanks to its partnership with Rivian. The Postal Service is rolling out electric mail trucks. FedEx is doing the same. IKEA delivers most of its flat-pack furniture in electric vans and trucks.

Moseman’s argument is simple and slightly awkward for everyone who has spent the past two years declaring the EV dead. American families are still reluctant to buy battery cars, even with high petrol prices this year. But the vehicles around them are going electric regardless. Every robotaxi operating in more than a dozen American cities is electric. About one in ten Uber miles in the United States and Canada is now driven by a zero-emission vehicle. AT&T and PepsiCo use EVs as company cars. So do governments and police departments. Moseman’s name for them is shadow EVs: vehicles Americans depend on but do not own.

Since Part I, this series has asked who is winning the race to take the driver out of the car. Part V asks a narrower question that turns out to be the same one: who is paying for the car in the first place?

The heart and the spreadsheet

Jeremy Michalek, who directs the Vehicle Electrification Group at Carnegie Mellon University, gave Moseman the explanation. Private buyers shop with their hearts. You buy the brand your father drove, or you avoid an electric car because you are used to filling up in two minutes. Fleet managers do not shop with their hearts. They do the arithmetic.

The arithmetic favours the battery. An EV usually costs more up front but less to run. It saves on fuel and needs less maintenance, because an electric motor has far fewer moving parts than an engine and gearbox. A ride-hail car covers roughly three times the mileage of an ordinary family car, so it earns back the higher price quickly. Michalek adds that some newer EVs can sell power from their batteries back to the grid when electricity is expensive. A parked fleet becomes a small power station that buys low and sells high.

None of this is new. What is new is how many organisations have done the sum and reached the same answer.

The patrol car

The best test of the fleet argument is the police car, because police buyers are about as far from the stereotype of the EV owner as you can get.

In August 2024, Matteo Wong reported for The Atlantic that the South Pasadena Police Department in California had become the first in the United States with a fully electric fleet, 20 cars in all. Four officers who tested the cars for the department went out and bought one for themselves. Departments in at least 38 states had bought, tested or deployed electric cars. The sergeant running the conversion, Tony Abdalla, told Wong it was “primarily an operational decision.”

The performance numbers explain why. In the Michigan State Police’s annual vehicle tests, which departments across North America use to judge police cars, the Chevrolet Blazer EV and the Ford Mustang Mach-E went from zero to 100 mph in about 11 seconds. That is roughly half the time of many popular petrol police cars. South Pasadena expected to save about $4,000 a year per car on fuel alone. New York City’s fleet chief, Keith Kerman, reported maintenance savings of 60 to 70 per cent on the city’s electric vehicles. In Bargersville, Indiana, the chief said fuel savings from 13 EVs would pay for two more officers.

Wong was candid about the limits. Some departments found the cars too small. Long shifts and highway patrols push against range, and chargers are slower and scarcer than petrol pumps. That is why the NYPD ran more than 2,000 hybrids alongside its first 200 or so EVs.

Two years on, those 200 cars look like a beginning. On April 2, 2026, the New York City Department of Citywide Administrative Services announced that the city’s enforcement agencies had passed 1,000 electric vehicles, which it called the largest electric law-enforcement fleet in North America. Law enforcement is the biggest part of the city’s fleet, with roughly 11,000 vehicles across 15 agencies. The city also began using the Blazer EV, its first pursuit-rated electric police car.

The pattern is not only American. In August 2026, electrive reported that South Korea plans to make all new police vehicles electric or hydrogen-powered. At the end of June 2026, only 2,185 of the country’s roughly 17,600 police vehicles, about 12 per cent, ran on either. The national police buy or lease about 1,900 new vehicles a year, so the fleet will turn over within a decade.

Wong also noticed the politics. In April 2024, Donald Trump stood in Michigan with a row of sheriffs behind him, called Joe Biden’s support for EVs one of the dumbest decisions he had ever heard of, and then praised the officers as the best people in the world. Police officers lean Republican. EV buyers lean Democratic. Yet the officers who actually drove the cars kept coming back converted. Mike Murphy of the EV Politics Project told Wong that Republicans who trust the police might trust the car more after seeing it in a patrol livery. Wong closed by suggesting that taking the petrol out of police cars could prove as divisive as taking money out of police budgets. Republican legislators in Kentucky tried to stop public agencies from buying EVs in 2024.

The robotaxi as customer

Police cars and delivery vans prove the economics. Robotaxis are where the volume is going, and this is where Part V rejoins the series’ main subject.

A self-driving car is almost bound to be electric. Moseman makes the point that autonomous driving systems, like the AI behind them, are hungry for power, and a large battery can run the computers and drive the wheels at the same time. Every robotaxi operator in this series has chosen an EV. (Tesla’s version belongs to another series.)

The orders have become very large:

  • Uber and Rivian. In March 2026, Engadget reported that Uber would invest up to $1.25 billion in Rivian to deploy up to 50,000 R2 robotaxis. The first 10,000 are due in San Francisco and Miami by 2028, and the plan reaches 25 cities across the United States, Canada and Europe by 2031. Only $300 million is committed so far. The rest depends on Rivian hitting autonomy milestones. Rivian’s chief executive, R. J. Scaringe, told Moseman the partnership is “one of the most powerful levers we have for putting electric miles on the road.”

  • Uber, Lucid and Nuro. In July 2025, TechCrunch reported that Uber would invest $300 million in Lucid and buy at least 20,000 of its Gravity SUVs over six years, fitted with Nuro’s self-driving system. Lucid, short of money for years, now counts robotaxi deals in the United States and Europe among its lifelines.

  • Waymo and Hyundai. Waymo runs about 4,000 vehicles in 14 American cities, most of them converted Jaguar I-Paces. In September 2026, Tim Levin reported for InsideEVs that Hyundai will build tens of thousands of Ioniq 5 robotaxis for Waymo at its Georgia plant. Hyundai’s chief executive, José Muñoz, said the robotaxi manufacturing business is already profitable. He also said Hyundai had shifted the same Georgia plant towards more hybrids and fewer EVs for private buyers. While rivals wrote down their assets, he said, Hyundai “decided to make the most out of our assets.” Moseman adds that Hyundai’s chief has said the Waymo deal will help it avoid retreating from EVs.

  • Zoox. Amazon’s own robotaxi company builds a purpose-made electric pod with no steering wheel. According to Built In, Zoox began paid rides in Las Vegas in August 2026 and ran about 100 vehicles as of September. Its federal exemption allows up to 2,500 vehicles a year, and its new plant in the San Francisco area can build 10,000 a year at full scale.

Read that list as a set and the shape is clear. Rivian is a consumer brand whose survival rests on two corporate buyers, Amazon and Uber. Lucid is a luxury brand being rescued by the same ride-hail company. Hyundai is building fewer EVs for families and more for Waymo. The robotaxi has become the EV industry’s anchor customer before it has become anyone’s daily ride.

The rest of the world

This is not a uniquely American story, and the earlier parts of this series show why.

Part I described how China’s robotaxi companies, Baidu’s Apollo Go, Pony.ai and WeRide, grew by putting fleets on city streets under a state that wanted them there. Pony.ai alone ran about 1,700 vehicles in the Greater Bay Area. China’s electric car market was already so large that the robotaxi was a passenger rather than a lifeline.

Europe is different again, and Part IV’s money gap has a regulatory twin. As The Next Web pointed out in its report on the Hyundai plan, Europe’s approval rules for fully automated vehicles cap production at 1,500 units a year for each vehicle type. A single Georgia factory planning tens of thousands of Ioniq 5s for one customer is operating at a scale Europe’s rules do not yet allow. Wayve can raise billions in London, but under that cap its robotaxis will be counted in the low thousands at most.

So the fleet-first model is spreading at different speeds. China got there through state planning and a domestic EV boom. America is getting there through corporate purchase orders, even as Washington pulls back. Europe is still drafting the paperwork.

The catch

Fleets are not a cure-all, and Moseman lists the gaps. Long-haul trucking is still a hard sell: a lorry that can cross the continent on batteries needs a battery so heavy it eats into the cargo. School districts used Biden-era infrastructure money to buy electric buses, and many then struggled to maintain an unfamiliar kind of vehicle. The police experience points the same way. EVs suit short urban shifts and struggle on long rural patrols.

There is a deeper risk in an industry kept alive by a few large customers. Building the supply chains and plants for EVs takes years and billions of dollars. Apart from Tesla, very few electric carmakers are profitable. President Trump made things harder by cutting EV incentives, and several automakers have written off billions. A fleet buyer gives a carmaker the steady revenue it needs to keep a production line running. But Rivian’s deal with Uber is mostly conditional, and Hyundai’s robotaxi business depends on Waymo continuing to grow. When a single buyer decides whether a factory keeps building EVs, the factory’s fate rests with that buyer.

Moseman ends by saying the future of the electric car now rests with fleet managers crunching numbers until enough Americans are ready to take the plunge. A Canadian watching from across the border might borrow Leacock’s Mariposa for a riff of his own: in a town like that, nobody trusts a new contraption until the bank manager has one, and after that nobody can remember life without it. In this case the bank manager is a fleet manager, and the invention is already outside the door: in the parcel van, the police car, and the taxi with nobody in the front seat.

The family car may be the last thing to go electric. The vehicles that serve the family are getting there first.
