Back to the Beginning

Last November, over Thanksgiving dinner — that most American of rituals, observed by a Canadian looking south with something between bewilderment and grief — I sat down to write what I thought would be a single essay. A reflection on accountability, or more precisely, on its absence. On how one man had managed, across five decades, to evade every mechanism that democratic society has built to hold powerful people responsible for their actions.

That single essay became this series.

It is now May. I have been publishing twice a week for six months. Forty-seven essays and over 400,000 words. And I am sitting here, at the end of it, with a feeling I did not anticipate when I started.

I expected to feel angry. I do.

I expected to feel vindicated — that the record, once assembled, would speak for itself. It does.

What I did not expect was how profoundly disheartening it would be to see it all in one place.

The Weight of the Whole

Towering stacks of documents and files — representing the overwhelming cumulative record assembled across six months and more than 400,000 words.

When you write about Trump in the way most people encounter him — one scandal at a time, one news cycle at a time, one outrage chasing the last — the sheer scale of it gets lost. Each individual story is shocking on its own terms, but the human mind has a way of compartmentalizing. The fraud exists in one box. The sexual misconduct in another. The criminal cases in a third. The racism in a fourth. The climate destruction in a fifth. The corporate immunity in a sixth. The environmental poisoning in a seventh. The undeclared wars in an eighth. The market manipulation in a ninth. We process each one separately, react to it, and move on to the next.

This series does not let you do that.

Go back and read the table of contents. Just the titles. Just the section headings. Do it slowly.

A foundation of impunity built across decades of fraud, bankruptcy, and tax evasion. A university that swindled students out of their savings. A charitable foundation that was a personal slush fund. A billion dollars in losses converted into decades of paying no income tax.

Ten documented instances of obstruction of justice in the Mueller Report. A pandemic response that lied to the public while hundreds of thousands died. Two impeachments, two acquittals.

Classified documents scattered across a beach club. An insurrection encouraged from the White House. A criminal conspiracy to overturn an election in Georgia. All three cases dismissed — not on the merits, but because the defendant won the presidency.

Twenty-six women who accused him of sexual assault. A civil verdict of $88.3 million. Thirty-four felony convictions — the first ever for an American president. Zero punishment.

A $454 million fraud judgment, functionally eliminated. Emoluments violations, Hatch Act violations, security clearance overrides. Fifty-three years of documented racism. A 16-year conspiracy to destroy the nation’s ability to fight climate change. Presidential power weaponized to grant corporate immunity — $4.8 million in donations buying 13,000 nursing home deaths per year, 5,300 workers dying annually, $19 billion stolen from consumers. The woman who defeated Pacific Gas & Electric’s contamination in 1996 watching Trump roll back drinking water protections for 73 million Americans, gut PFAS reporting by 97%, and slash the EPA’s budget by 54% while eliminating 2,000 staff.

And through it all, a grift that never stopped — from gold sneakers to meme coins to the transformation of the presidency into the most profitable trading desk in history. A Truth Social account used to manipulate markets, generating $400 million in a single hour while supporters lost fortunes buying at peaks the president himself created. Five documented instances of suspicious trading activity preceding presidential announcements. Twelve to fifteen more tracked by former White House officials but never investigated. The SEC — which should police insider trading — led by a Trump appointee who opened no investigation.

Seven military operations launched without a single congressional vote. A bombing campaign against Iran whose objectives shifted from nuclear disarmament to unconditional surrender in the space of a single week. War rhetoric that echoed Vladimir Putin’s invasion of Ukraine almost word for word — the same euphemisms, the same calls for surrender, the same demands that civilians overthrow their own government. A war timed to bury politically damaging Epstein media coverage. And the moment when the country that spent seventy years defining itself against authoritarian aggression became indistinguishable from the thing it claimed to oppose.

That is one man’s record. One man.

And he is currently the President of the United States.

I did not fully grasp the cumulative weight of it until I had written it all down. I suspect most readers will have the same experience — that seeing the full catalogue, assembled chronologically and documented meticulously, produces a reaction that no single essay could. Not anger, exactly, though there is that. Something more like vertigo. The dizzying recognition that the system built to prevent exactly this — built across 250 years of constitutional design, legal precedent, institutional norm, and democratic practice — failed at every single point.

Not at one point. At every point.

A dramatic spiral staircase viewed from above — evoking the vertigo of seeing every failed mechanism of accountability laid out in one place.

Six Months, Twice a Week

Let me put the scale of this in personal terms, because I think it matters.

I am a retired businessman in Toronto. I am not a professional journalist, though I have come to practice something that looks a lot like investigative journalism over the past year. I do not have a newsroom behind me, or a legal department, or a team of fact-checkers. I have a Substack account, a collection of AI research tools, a stubbornness that my partner would confirm is my defining characteristic, and a Canadian’s inability to look away from the slow-motion disaster unfolding next door.

It took me six months, publishing twice a week without a break, to document what Donald Trump has done. Not what he might do. Not what his critics fear. What he has actually, documentably done — in court filings, government reports, sworn testimony, investigative journalism, and his own public statements. There were weeks when the material was so relentless — the sexual assault testimony, the contaminated water, the nursing home death counts — that I had to step away from the desk and remind myself that documenting it was not the same as living it, and that the people who lived it did not have that option.

And I could have written more.

I did not include a section on immigration policy — the family separations, the Muslim ban, the border wall. I did not write about the destruction of the State Department, or the hollowing out of the civil service, or the use of the Justice Department as a personal enforcement arm. I did not cover the attacks on the free press, the firings of inspectors general, the pardons of political allies, or the ongoing dismantlement of democratic institutions through DOGE and executive overreach.

Each of those topics could have been its own multi-part section. Each would have added to the weight. The series as it stands is not comprehensive — it is merely representative. A cross-section of the failure of accountability across every domain in which accountability should have functioned: legal, electoral, institutional, constitutional, financial, social, and moral.

That it took 47 essays and six months just to document a representative sample tells you everything you need to know about the scale of the problem.

The Publishing Question

People have suggested — and I have considered — approaching a literary agent about publishing this series as several books as I have written 400,000+ words. The argument is straightforward: this is, as far as I am aware, the most comprehensive single-author documentation of the Trump accountability failure in existence. It is chronological, sourced, analytical, and structured as a cumulative argument rather than a collection of hot takes. There is, presumably, a market for it.

And that is precisely what gives me pause.

The Trump era has produced an entire industry of books, documentaries, podcasts, and commentary. Some of it is excellent — Bob Woodward’s reporting, the Mueller Report itself, the January 6th Committee’s findings. But there is also a cottage industry of Trump content that exists primarily to monetize outrage. Books written quickly, promoted loudly, consumed by people who already agree with the premise, and remaindered within six months.

I do not want to be one more author extracting profit from the wreckage of American democracy. I do not want to be, in other words, a grifter feeding off the grand grifter himself. A 47-part series about a man who monetizes everything he touches, published by an author who monetized his critique of that monetization. Trump would appreciate the symmetry. He has always respected the hustle, even from his critics.

So I have not called the agent. Not yet. And I am not sure I will.

The value of this work is in the record itself. One person, working from Toronto with no institutional backing, assembling a documented, AI assisted, chronological account of the most comprehensive accountability failure in modern democratic history — publicly, permanently, on the internet, accessible to anyone with a browser and the stomach to read it.

If someone finds it useful in 10 years, or 20, or 50 — if a historian or a law student or a political scientist stumbles across it while trying to understand how a country of 330 million people allowed all of this to happen — then it will have served its purpose. It does not need a dust jacket for that.

What Thanksgiving Taught Me

In the Introduction to this series, written over that Thanksgiving weekend, I posed a question that I thought was rhetorical: Why has every mechanism of accountability failed when it comes to Donald Trump?

Forty-seven essays later, I have an answer. It is not the one I expected.

I expected to find a single, systemic failure — a flaw in the Constitution, perhaps, or a weakness in the prosecutorial system, or a captured judiciary. Something identifiable, something that could theoretically be repaired.

What I found instead is that every mechanism failed for a different reason.

The legal system failed because prosecutors were cautious, judges were sympathetic, and the defendant became president before the cases could be tried. The electoral system failed because voters either did not know the full record, did not believe it, or did not care. The congressional system failed because partisanship overwhelmed institutional duty — twice during impeachment, and again when Congress abdicated its constitutional war powers authority rather than challenge a president of its own party. The institutional system failed because norms are not laws, and a president willing to violate every norm faces no consequence for doing so. The social system failed because outrage fatigue is real, and the volume of misconduct was so vast that no single act could sustain public attention long enough to produce accountability. The media system failed because the pursuit of balance — the felt need to present “both sides” — is structurally incapable of grappling with a subject who has no sides, only angles of attack.

The climate system failed because the time-lag between action and consequence is so long that the perpetrators will be dead before the damage is fully realized. The environmental system failed because communities could be poisoned and simultaneously silenced. The financial system failed because when the insider is the president, no securities regulator investigates. The war powers system failed because Congress refused to defend its own constitutional authority.

In May 2026, as this series concluded, Trump dropped his $10 billion lawsuit against the IRS — the shakedown documented in Part 40. He withdrew it "with prejudice," meaning he cannot refile. The timing, weeks before the 2026 midterm elections, suggests political calculation rather than accountability. No court forced him to drop it. No prosecutor threatened charges. No investigation compelled the withdrawal. He simply decided the optics were too damaging as voters prepared to render judgment on his party. This is not accountability. This is a grifter reading the room and choosing to fold one hand while continuing to play a dozen others. The Truth Social market manipulation continues. The emoluments violations continue. The undeclared wars continue. The pattern remains intact: Trump only stops when continuing costs more than it gains. And even then, he stops only the grift that's become unprofitable — never the instinct that produced it.

Each failure is different. Each is understandable in isolation. Together, they create something unprecedented — a system-wide collapse of accountability that no single reform can address, because no single point of failure caused it.

That is what 47 essays taught me. Not that accountability failed, which I knew when I started. But that it failed everywhere, for different reasons, simultaneously. And that the man at the center of all of it understood this instinctively — understood, perhaps better than anyone in American political history, that if you create enough chaos across enough domains, no single mechanism can keep up.

What the World Learned, Too

As I write this conclusion, the consequences of that accountability failure are unfolding in real time — not just domestically, but on the world stage. The pattern this series documents is no longer an American problem. It is a global one.

Last week, The Atlantic‘s Vivian Salama published an article — Trump Has Gone From Unpredictable to Unreliable — that describes what happens when the accountability void meets international diplomacy. Her central distinction is precise, and it maps directly onto the arc of this series: what allies and adversaries once treated as strategic unpredictability — unorthodox, disruptive, but manageable — has curdled into something more corrosive. Unreliability. The sense, shared by officials across multiple continents, that commitments made by this president, his Cabinet, or his negotiators may not survive the next social-media post.

Joshua Schwartz, a Carnegie Mellon professor who has studied Trump and the madman theory extensively, told Salama that the president’s modus operandi has become “relatively predictable” — escalation and belligerent rhetoric followed by a foreseeable climbdown, usually prompted by economic or political pressure. The theatrics that once wrong-footed opponents have become legible. Allies have learned the playbook. And they are drawing conclusions.

Robert Malley, the lead negotiator on the 2015 Iran nuclear deal, put it to Salama in terms that could serve as an epitaph for this entire project: when unpredictability veers toward unreliability, fear gives way to something worse — lack of confidence. The other side loses motivation for a deal because it cannot trust that any agreement will stick. The Canadian scholar Stephen Nagy told Salama that the institutional guardrails which once constrained the damage are largely gone, replaced by a new kind of uncertainty: allies can anticipate the theatrical moves but cannot identify a coherent vision driving them.

One senior European official was blunter: “He’s been unpredictable for so long that we are now forced to think of a future that doesn’t rely so heavily on U.S. partnership. It’s forcing us to take care of ourselves.”

That is where the accountability failure leads. Not just to domestic impunity — the fraud unpunished, the wars unauthorized, the grift uninterrupted — but to a world that has quietly begun planning for a future without American reliability at its center. The man who was never held accountable at home has taught the world that American promises, like American institutions, can be broken without consequence.

Forty-seven essays to document what went wrong inside the system. One article to show what it looks like from the outside.

A Canadian’s Farewell (For Now)

Canadian and American flags standing side by side — the view from north of the border that framed this entire project: a concerned neighbor keeping the record.

I began this project as an outsider and I end it the same way. I am not American. I cannot vote in American elections. I have no standing — legal, political, or moral — to tell Americans what to do about the situation I have documented.

But I live next door. And when your neighbor’s house is on fire — sometimes literally, as the residents of Los Angeles and the forests of northern Canada can attest — you do not need standing to express concern. You do not need citizenship to document what is happening. And you do not need permission to keep the record.

Today is May 21st — four days before Memorial Day. The series began over Thanksgiving, a holiday about gratitude. It ends as America prepares for a holiday about the cost of war. The thirteen Americans killed in Iran, the nearly three hundred wounded, the 225 with traumatic brain injuries — they will be honoured on Monday alongside the dead of every authorized war this country has ever fought. Congress declared war against Germany. Congress declared war against Japan. Congress authorized force in Korea, in Vietnam, in Iraq. Congress did not vote on Iran. The names from Dover will be read alongside names from Normandy and Inchon and Fallujah, but they arrived there by a different path — one that bypassed every constitutional checkpoint the founders built. Thanksgiving asks what you are grateful for. Memorial Day asks what it cost. This series is the space between those two questions.

Over the past year, I have published 1,000+ essays on Substack across more than 75 series — on fascism, on AI governance, on the autonomous vehicle revolution, on Canadian-American relations, and on the accountability crisis that this series represents. I am a retired businessman with a laptop, an AI assistant, and an inability to look away. That is all.

But I will say this to my American readers, with the directness that Canadians are not always known for but that this moment demands:

The record exists and I offer no apology for that. It is documented. It is sourced. It is public. It is not going away.

What you do with it is your business. It always was.

I wrote in the Introduction that Thanksgiving is a time for gratitude and reflection. I am grateful — deeply grateful — for the readers who have followed this series from that first essay to this last one. For the corrections, the suggestions, the arguments, and the encouragement. For the act of reading itself, which in an age of infinite distraction is a more generous gift than most people realize.

And I am grateful for the clarity that this project has given me. Not about Trump — I understood him well enough before I started. But about the systems that were supposed to contain him, and about the nature of the failure when those systems, every last one of them, did not.

The Final Accounting

Fraud. Bankruptcy. Tax evasion. A fake university. A sham charity. Obstruction of justice. A pandemic of lies. Two impeachments. Classified documents. An insurrection. A criminal conspiracy. Twenty-six accusations of sexual assault. Eighty-eight million dollars in civil verdicts. Thirty-four felony convictions. Zero punishment. A $454 million fraud judgment, erased. Ethics violations across every category. Fifty-three years of racism. A 16-year conspiracy against the climate. Corporate immunity costing thousands of lives and billions of dollars. Seventy-three million Americans drinking contaminated water. An environmental protection agency destroyed from within. A Truth Social account transformed into a trading desk, generating $400 million in a single hour while supporters lost fortunes. Seven undeclared wars waged without a single congressional vote. A bombing campaign justified with an autocrat’s rhetoric. A conflict timed to bury damaging media coverage. And a grift — from the first dollar to the last — that never, ever stopped.

Every mechanism of accountability — legal, electoral, congressional, institutional, constitutional, financial, social, environmental, and temporal — failed.

And the man at the center of all of it is the 47th President of the United States.

Forty-seven parts. Forty-seven. This was not planned. The series grew organically, one accountability document at a time, following the evidence wherever it led. It arrived at forty-seven on its own — the same number as his presidency, the same number he prints on his golf caps and sells on his merchandise and has legally trademarked as a brand. He made the number his identity. The record made it its own. The coincidence is either meaningless or perfect, and I have decided it is perfect.

The solemn expanse of the Vietnam Veterans Memorial wall at dusk — a symbol of a permanent, unflinching public record. This series aimed to create its own version of that wall for the Trump accountability failure.

That is the record. I have done my best to make it complete, accurate, and honest. It is now part of the permanent public archive — roughly 1,000 essays and counting, from a Canadian who believed that someone should write it all down.

Someone did.
