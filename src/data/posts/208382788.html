Every organization eventually retains counsel who understands a simple truth: it is cheaper to plead to the crime you cannot hide than to be convicted of the one you can. This week, the family took that advice.

IN THE MATTER OF: THE UNITED STATES OF AMERICA, ON BEHALF OF THE EXECUTIVE BRANCH

RE: Stipulated Admission in Lieu of Discovery

FILED: July 2026, U.S. District Court, Northern District of California

COUNSEL OF RECORD FOR THE GOVERNMENT: Office of Management and Budget, Department of Energy, and associated agencies

I. STATEMENT OF THE INSTANT OFFENSE

Come now the defendants, by and through counsel, and — rather than proceed to a discovery process that might reveal considerably more — stipulate to the following facts.

As the New York Times has reported, when the Department of Energy canceled more than $7.5 billion in Biden-era clean energy grants last October, it told the public the cancellations were an urgent correction to protect taxpayer funds from waste. This was not true. In court documents filed this month, the government’s own lawyer conceded that of the 284 grants terminated, all but one shared a single defining characteristic: “the 284 terminated grants had a recipient location and/or at least one place of performance in a state that awarded its electoral votes to Kamala Harris in the 2024 election and has two Democratic-caucusing senators.” None, the lawyer further conceded, were selected for any programmatic, statutory, cost-reduction, or performance-based reason.

The defendants would like the record to reflect that this concession was offered voluntarily — in the specific sense that a man surrenders his wallet voluntarily to avoid a more thorough search of his pockets. As the Times noted, the admission was extracted as part of an arrangement to spare the government “an exhaustive, evidence-uncovering process known as discovery,” which might have required handing over considerably more damaging material. The defendants confessed to the burglary to keep the jury from touring the house.

II. STATEMENT REGARDING CONTRADICTORY PRIOR TESTIMONY

The defendants acknowledge certain tension between the instant admission and prior sworn and public statements, as follows:

Energy Secretary Chris Wright told a House hearing this June that “No decisions were made on politics.” The defendants now stipulate that decisions were made on nothing else.

OMB Director Russell Vought, in a public statement announcing the very cancellations now at issue, wrote: “Nearly $8 billion in Green New Scam funding to fuel the Left’s climate agenda is being canceled.” The defendants note, for the sake of completeness, that Mr. Vought’s statement and Mr. Wright’s testimony cannot both be accurate, and decline to specify which of their own officials was lying, on the grounds that the answer is contained within the filing itself.

III. PRIOR RECORD — PATTERN OF CONDUCT

The Court is respectfully directed to the defendants’ relevant history, which the government’s counsel would prefer not be characterized as a pattern, but which the following prior adjudications may fairly be described as exactly that.

United States District Court, District of Columbia — Judge Amit Mehta. In a separate 2026 action concerning $7.6 billion in clean energy grants across sixteen states, Judge Mehta found the defendants’ conduct unconstitutional on its face. As the Associated Press reported, Mehta wrote that “defendants freely admit that they made grant-termination decisions primarily — if not exclusively — based on whether the awardee resided in a state whose citizens voted for President Trump in 2024,” and further found that the government offered no explanation for how the targeting of recipients based on their state’s electoral support “rationally advances their stated government interest.” The defendants would note that this finding predates, and does not appear to have deterred, the conduct described in Section I above.

United States District Court, District of Massachusetts — Judge Indira Talwani. Judge Talwani ruled on July 17 that the defendants could not rely on an Office of Management and Budget termination clause to cancel grants after the fact simply because they no longer aligned with the administration’s priorities, siding with a coalition of twenty-one attorneys general and three governors. The government had argued that the clause imposed no time limit on when an agency’s priorities must have been adopted — a reading the Court declined to adopt, on the theory that Congress, and not a subsequent change of mind, appropriates the money.

United States District Court, Northern District of Illinois — Judge Manish Shah. In a third and earlier matter, as the Associated Press reported, the defendants were temporarily restrained from rescinding $600 million in public health funding from four states — California, Colorado, Illinois, and Minnesota — supporting disease-outbreak tracking and public health research. Illinois Attorney General Kwame Raoul characterized the cancellations at the time as "a transparent attempt to bully" the states "into compliance," a characterization the defendants did not then, and do not now, meaningfully dispute.

The defendants respectfully submit that three federal judges, sitting in three different jurisdictions, independently reaching the identical factual conclusion about the identical defendant, in the same calendar year, is not a pattern of conduct within the meaning normally assigned to that phrase, and reserve the right to argue this point at a later date, before a fourth judge.

IV. THE COURT’S OBSERVATIONS ON THE RECORD

The Court, for its part, has been notably unpersuaded that changing the subject constitutes a defense. Judge Mehta observed, of the equal-protection question, that a state’s electoral preference is not among the criteria Congress authorized any agency to consult when spending money it has already appropriated. Judge Talwani observed that the “Termination Clause” cited by the government functions, on the government’s own reading, less as a regulation than as “limitless authority to cut grant funds that support essential services throughout the country” — a phrase which the government’s own filings did not meaningfully dispute inventing.

The Court notes, further, that the defendants’ position — that a change of administration is sufficient justification to retroactively defund only the half of the country that voted the other way — was offered as a matter of ordinary representative democracy. As government attorneys wrote in one of the underlying cases, “a change in administration brought about by the people casting their votes is a perfectly reasonable basis” for reappraising which programs to fund. The Court finds this reasoning persuasive only up to the word “reappraising,” and considerably less persuasive at the word “retroactively.”

V. TERMS

In consideration of the foregoing, the defendants agree:

  1. To characterize the admitted conduct, going forward, as a “programmatic realignment” rather than as what three federal courts have now separately called it.

  2. To continue drafting a new regulation — expected by October — that would grant the Executive Branch discretionary review of more than one trillion dollars in annual federal grants, on the theory that the previous discretionary authority, having been found unconstitutional three times, was insufficiently discretionary.

  3. To make no further public statements, of the kind made by Mr. Vought, that might inadvertently corroborate the sworn testimony of Mr. Wright, or vice versa.
