By Stephen Gordon

When this series closed in September 2026, it ended on a verdict and an open question. The verdict was the word the series had withheld until the record earned it: across 2020 and 2026, Donald Trump had applied no consistent standard to election administration, except one. He accepted only the verification that favoured him. The open question was what would happen at the polls in November 2026, with the Election Assistance Commission emptied, federal election security gutted, and the president openly musing about emergency powers.

Six weeks later, the question is less open than it was.

On 15 October 2026, Zolan Kanno-Youngs reported in the New York Times, under the headline “Trump’s Attacks on Voting Raise Fears He Will Try to Challenge Midterm Results,” that election officials and Democrats fear the president’s long campaign against voting systems is “crystallizing” into an attempt to lay the groundwork for contesting the results. The reporting does not prove intent, and it says so. But read against the four essays that came before this one, it describes something this series did not see in 2020: the grievance being assembled in advance.

This coda applies the same method as the rest of the series. It is a consistency audit, not a character judgment. The question is not whether Trump is a bad loser. The question is whether the machinery being built now matches the machinery he used then, and whether it is being built before or after the votes it is meant to explain.

From reaction to preparation

In 2020 the sequence ran in a familiar order. The votes were counted, Trump lost, and only then did the claims arrive: the Dominion machines, the dead voters, the suitcases of ballots, the Venezuelan software. Part I of this series catalogued those claims. Every one of them was a story told after the fact to explain a result he did not like.

The 2026 sequence runs the other way. According to the Times, the president has warned that election systems are vulnerable to foreign meddling. His homeland security secretary, Markwayne Mullin, has asked the Justice Department to examine whether a widely used voting machine can be trusted. Federal agents are scouring state rolls and federal databases for noncitizen voters. Trump and Mullin claimed this summer that 278,000 noncitizens were registered across several states, a figure the government has not been able to substantiate.

None of those claims has been tested by a single November ballot. They are not explanations of a result. They are explanations in search of one.

That is the shift this coda is about. The Grievance Machine of 2020 was reactive. The Grievance Machine of 2026 is pre-loaded.

The pairing, updated

The concluding essay of this series paired the 2020 record with the 2026 record claim by claim. The October reporting allows several of those pairs to be updated.

Machines, then and now. In 2020 the target was Dominion, and Fox News eventually paid $787.5 million to settle the defamation suit those claims produced. In 2026 the target is again the machines, now with a foreign-interference framing. At a rally in Arizona on 14 October, the Times reports, Trump repeated his false claim that machines “were broken in Republican areas” in 2020. Earlier this year he said he regretted not seizing voting machines after that election. And Tina Peters, the former Colorado county clerk convicted of tampering with voting machines after 2020, met the president at the White House in June and again in early October, according to two people familiar with the meetings. She has told a pro-Trump outlet that she urged him to get rid of the machines before the midterms.

The pairing holds, and it has tightened. In 2020 the machine story was a lawsuit. In 2026 it is a conversation in the Oval Office with someone convicted of acting on it.

The referees, then and now. In 2020 Trump fired Chris Krebs for certifying that the election was secure. Part III of this series recorded the July 2026 removal of every remaining member of the Election Assistance Commission, the bipartisan body that certifies voting machines. The Times now reports that Peters says she urged Trump to fire those commissioners. He did so in July. Whether her urging made the difference is unknowable, but the sequence is on the record: an election denier asked for the referees to be removed, and they were.

The monitors, revisited. In Part III, this series drew a careful line between the open threats about ICE and National Guard deployments at polling places and the Justice Department’s 1,000-monitor programme, which it described as a separate and lawful deployment under the Voting Rights Act. Fairness requires revisiting that line. The programme is still lawful on its face, and Attorney General Todd Blanche has defended it as enforcing federal law. But during Wyoming’s August primaries, two Justice Department monitors tried to interview voters and examine machines at polling places, and Wyoming’s Republican governor, Mark Gordon, called their conduct “aggressive, unexpected, disruptive.” The N.A.A.C.P. has sued for details of the deployment. The line between monitoring and interference has not been crossed in any documented way. It is, however, being tested, and this series should say so.

The force question. The concluding essay paired the Jan. 6 mob with the open threats of ICE, the Guard and emergency declarations. On that pairing, the October reporting adds two voices. When the conservative host Wayne Allyn Root told Trump in August that he had the right to declare a national security emergency over elections, Trump replied that “stranger things have happened.” And Peter Ticktin, a lawyer who has represented both Trump and Peters, told the Times that heavy Republican losses, combined with evidence that “something was wrong,” could lead to “martial law or something.” Against that stands Gen. Dan Caine, chairman of the Joint Chiefs, who said in late August that there were no plans to deploy troops in connection with the elections. Both statements are on the record. Only one of them comes from the chain of command.

What has not worked

This series committed from the start to recording failures as well as mechanisms, and the October record contains several.

In September the Supreme Court blocked Trump’s effort to have the Postal Service screen mail ballots. The Times reports that the underlying executive order, signed in March, began as a draft circulated by Stephen Miller, and that some of Trump’s own aides initially considered it too draconian. Trump signed it only after it became clear the Senate would not pass mail-voting restrictions. The Times notes that the administration’s attempts to assert federal control over elections have been repeatedly stopped by the courts.

Lawrence Norden of the Brennan Center offered the most deflating assessment in the piece: there has been “a lot of posturing,” he said, and most election officials are tuning it out because they have elections to run.

That is worth taking seriously. The American election system is decentralised to a degree that frustrates anyone trying to seize it, from either direction. Thousands of county clerks, most of them unglamorous and many of them Republican, administer the vote. Federal power over that machinery is limited by the Constitution, and the courts have so far enforced the limit.

But the purpose of a pre-loaded grievance is not necessarily to seize the machinery. It is to discredit the result. A claim does not have to win in court to do its work. Sixty-plus lawsuits failed in 2020, and the stolen-election story still produced a riot at the Capitol and a durable belief among tens of millions of voters. Losing in court was never the point. Being able to say it was stolen was the point.

The people preparing for it

The most striking part of the Times report is not about Trump at all. It is about the officials getting ready for him.

Deidre Henderson, Utah’s Republican lieutenant governor, told a George Washington University panel this month that for the first time, her state had planned for possible interference by federal officials, including the prospect of a federal agent arriving at a polling station with a warrant. She did not say she expected it. She said it would be foolish not to think it through.

Senator Mark Warner of Virginia, the top Democrat on the Senate Intelligence Committee, said he has been contacting business leaders, former military leaders and veteran federal law enforcement officials to ask them to stand up if Trump contests the results. “Good people of both parties and independents,” he said, will have to refuse.

To a Canadian reader, this is the strangest detail in the whole record. In most democracies, state and local election officials prepare for storms, power failures, cyberattacks and foreign interference. The idea that a state’s chief election officer, a Republican, must prepare for interference from her own federal government is not a partisan talking point. It is a description of how far the baseline has moved since the first essay in this series was written.

The consistency test, one more time

The series’ method asked a simple, falsifiable question: does Trump hold a consistent principle about how elections should be run and verified?

If the principle were federalism, he would leave the states alone in 2026 as he demanded Washington leave them alone in 2020. He has not. If the principle were federal oversight, he would have welcomed Krebs’s and Barr’s findings in 2020. He fired one and broke with the other. If the principle were election security, he would not have emptied the Election Assistance Commission weeks before a national vote.

The only principle that survives every test is the one the concluding essay identified: verification counts when it favours him. The October record adds a corollary. When he expects verification not to favour him, he begins discrediting it before it happens.

Asked whether he would accept the results, Trump told reporters earlier this month: “Of course I will. But we don’t want cheating.”

The second sentence is the one to watch. After four essays and a coda, this series can say with some confidence what “cheating” will mean on the night of 3 November 2026. It will mean losing.

This coda was written on 15 October 2026, before the 3 November midterm elections.
