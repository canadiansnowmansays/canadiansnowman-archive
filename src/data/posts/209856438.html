Postmark: A subway platform, New York City. A five-foot robot with four cameras sits on its charging pad. A sign nearby says it is here to help. It has not moved in months.

This series has visited the battlefield, the operating table, the rubble of a collapsed building. This postcard visits the space between all three — the street, the subway platform, the standoff — where a robot’s job is neither to heal nor to fight in any clean sense, but to stand in for an officer in the moment before anyone knows which of those two things the situation actually calls for. It is, of all the territories in this series, the one where the machine’s usefulness and its danger are hardest to pull apart, because both grow from exactly the same capability: a robot can go first, into a place too dangerous to send a person, and see what is there before a human has to decide anything at all.

The uncontested case

Start with what almost nobody in this debate disputes, because it is worth stating plainly before the argument begins. As Built In documents, a robot helped identify the Boston Marathon bombers in 2013, and robotic tools have been part of American law enforcement’s toolkit in one form or another for well over a decade. When a gunman killed five Dallas police officers in 2016 and barricaded himself in a parking garage, threatening more violence, police made the decision to end the standoff by sending in an explosives-carrying robot rather than risking another officer’s life in a direct assault — a decision that remains controversial in its particulars but that few dispute was, in the moment, aimed at saving lives rather than ending one prematurely.

As Robotics and Automation News reported, more than sixty bomb squads and SWAT teams across the United States and Canada now use Boston Dynamics’ Spot platform for reconnaissance — sending the robot through a door first, into a room that might hold an armed suspect or a live explosive device, so that the information an officer needs to make a life-or-death decision arrives before the officer’s own body has to be exposed to find it out.

This is the least controversial territory in the entire debate, and for good reason: a robot that can scout a hazardous-materials leak, defuse a suspicious package, or peer into a room before an officer enters is doing something structurally identical to the search-and-rescue robot of the previous postcard — extending human senses into a place too dangerous for a human body, with the human’s judgment still very much in charge of what happens next. As one industry account emphasized, even in these clearly beneficial deployments, robots cannot replace officers entirely — they can scout and gather information, but a human still has to step in for the arrest, the rescue, the decision that carries legal and moral weight. The robot goes first. The officer still decides.

Where the agreement ends

The trouble begins exactly where this series has learned to expect it: the moment the robot moves from the clearly hazardous, narrowly defined task into the broader, murkier work of simply watching people in public.

As one 2026 analysis observed, several American cities have faced real public backlash when police departments proposed using robot dogs or wheeled patrol units not for bomb disposal or armed standoffs but for routine patrol, public-space surveillance, or crowd monitoring — precisely the applications where the benefit is least clear and the civil-liberties stakes are highest.

New York City’s own experience is the clearest case study in the country. As Police1 reported, the NYPD deployed a four-hundred-pound Knightscope K5 robot to patrol the Times Square subway station in 2023, and the rollout drew immediate, sustained criticism from legal watchdogs who argued the deployment subjected New Yorkers to surveillance with no clearly demonstrated benefit over the cameras already installed in the same station. The Legal Aid Society specifically questioned whether the deployment had complied with the city’s own POST Act, which requires a public comment period before new surveillance technology goes into service. By the following year, the robot had quietly gone into storage despite the city still holding an active contract for it — an ending a Legal Aid attorney described bluntly as confirmation that the whole program had been a gimmick from the start, one that the department was never able to show did anything the existing cameras in Times Square were not already doing.

The critics’ strongest argument is not speculative; it rests on documented incidents. As Governing magazine reported, in 2024 police in Lubbock, Texas used a robot to pump tear gas into a room where a suspect was barricaded, and when the suspect fled the room, the operators drove the robot on top of him and pinned him down — a use of physical force against a human being, executed by machine, with no clear statute anywhere governing whether that was an appropriate application of the technology. A senior policy analyst at the Electronic Frontier Foundation put the underlying problem starkly: in most police departments across the country there are, at present, no standards at all governing acceptable uses of these machines — not weak standards, not contested standards, simply none. That absence is what critics point to as the real danger, distinct from any particular robot or any particular incident: a genuinely new capability is being deployed, city by city, faster than any democratic process has been able to write rules for it.

The case the machines’ defenders make

Set against that, defenders of continued and even expanded deployment make an argument this series should represent fairly, because it is not merely corporate boosterism.

A robotics law scholar at the University of Washington, quoted in coverage of the debate, has argued that robot dogs and similar platforms can play a genuinely valuable role in policing when used transparently and within boundaries defined clearly and in advance — the objection, on this view, is not to the robot itself but to departments deploying capable new technology without first doing the harder work of writing down, publicly, what it will and will not be used for. On this reading, the failure in cases like the NYPD’s K5 rollout was not that a robot patrolled a subway station, but that the department could never articulate what the robot achieved that a well-placed camera did not, and never subjected the deployment to the kind of public process the city’s own law required. A more disciplined rollout — one with clear rules, genuine public input, and a demonstrable gap the robot actually fills — might, defenders argue, look very different in practice and draw far less resistance.

There is also a genuine safety argument for the officers themselves that deserves a fair hearing. As Robotics and Automation News noted in a piece specifically about the human side of robot-assisted policing, officers who follow a robot into a dangerous building still face the greatest risk in these operations, because the robot can scout and report, but a human being still has to make the arrest, execute the rescue, or resolve the situation once the robot’s information runs out — and that means officers require better protective equipment than ever before, not less, in an era of robot-assisted response. The robot, on this account, does not remove the human from danger. It delays the moment of human exposure and narrows it, which is a meaningful safety gain even though it is not the complete removal of risk the more utopian framing sometimes implies.

The vendor landscape behind these deployments is worth a brief note, because it shows how quickly a niche has become an industry. Companies like Asylon Robotics build security variants directly on Boston Dynamics’ Spot chassis, adding electro-optical cameras and real-time analytics packages aimed specifically at the security market rather than the industrial-inspection use cases Spot was originally built for; Micropolis Robotics has built its entire business around urban policing deployments, partnering directly with Dubai Police to field systems with facial recognition, license-plate reading, and anomaly detection built in from the start. What began as a handful of bomb-disposal robots borrowed from military surplus has become, within a few years, a dedicated commercial sector with its own specialized manufacturers competing for police contracts — a clear sign that whatever the regulatory debate concludes, the market has already decided this territory is permanent.

The line that some want drawn now

One proposal has emerged from this debate that deserves particular attention, because it attempts to draw a bright line rather than litigate every deployment case by case. As the Policing Project at NYU Law has recommended, a clear moratorium should apply specifically to any robotic use of force against a person — prohibiting agencies from equipping a robot with a weapon meant to be used against a human being, from deploying a robot in any manner reasonably likely to cause death or injury, and from using a robot or its accessories to physically threaten a person. This proposal notably does not oppose robots in policing generally; it draws its line at the specific and narrower question of force, leaving reconnaissance, bomb disposal, and even routine patrol as separate questions to be argued on their own terms. It is worth noting as one of the few concrete regulatory frameworks to emerge from a debate that has, so far, produced far more controversy than settled rules.

A different answer, elsewhere

The American debate, fractious and unresolved as it is, is not the only model on offer, and this series should note the alternative fairly rather than treating the American civil-liberties framework as the only lens available.

As Standard Bots documented in a global survey of the field, agencies from Dubai to Singapore have moved well past the pilot stage into routine deployment, with far less of the public controversy that has attended similar efforts in the United States. Dubai Police field autonomous wheeled patrol units in malls and public spaces, engaging directly with the public and issuing verbal guidance; Singapore’s police force uses similar autonomous patrol robots to monitor public housing estates. China has gone further still, deploying robotic dogs for crowd control alongside AI-driven bots equipped for facial recognition and behavioural analysis at a scale few Western jurisdictions have attempted. These are not identical societies making an identical calculation — they operate under very different traditions of privacy, free assembly, and the presumed relationship between citizen and state, and what reads as an acceptable trade-off in one context may be precisely the outcome American critics are trying to forestall in theirs. I raise this not to suggest one model is correct, but because the American debate can look, from outside it, like an argument about whether the technology should exist at all, when much of the rest of the world has already answered that question and moved on to the different, and in some ways more honest, argument about degree and oversight rather than existence.

The pledge some manufacturers won’t break

One detail sits underneath every police deployment described in this postcard and deserves to be named directly, because it shapes what these machines are even capable of being ordered to do: the manufacturers themselves are not unanimous on how far this technology should go, and their choices matter as much as any legislature’s.

Boston Dynamics, whose Spot platform underlies the large majority of police and security deployments this postcard has described, has taken a public and repeated position that it will not weaponize its robots — a policy the company has held to even as competing quadruped manufacturers have not. Ghost Robotics, a rival firm building a similar four-legged platform for military and security clients, has drawn sustained controversy for the opposite choice: mounting sniper rifles and remote weapon systems onto its Q-UGV robots for military special-operations customers, drawing condemnation from AI-safety researchers who argue the sight of a rifle strapped to a dog-shaped machine crosses a moral line that a wheeled or humanoid weapon somehow does not. One prominent computer scientist working on autonomous-weapons policy called the development a step toward “a dark and dangerous world” precisely because the canine shape recruits an emotional trust in the machine that a more obviously mechanical weapon never earns — the same uncanny-resemblance argument Part VIII made about humanoids, applied here to something even more visceral, because dogs have been humanity’s companions for millennia in a way no robot, however human-shaped, can yet claim.

This distinction matters directly for the domestic policing debate this postcard has otherwise focused on, because it explains why American police departments overwhelmingly use Spot rather than a Ghost Robotics platform: Boston Dynamics’ terms of service for law-enforcement customers explicitly prohibit weaponization, giving departments a technical and contractual backstop against the very “robotic use of force” the Policing Project’s proposed moratorium is trying to prevent through regulation instead. Where the law has not yet drawn a hard line, at least one major manufacturer has drawn one voluntarily — a reminder that in a territory this unsettled, the terms of service written by a private company in Waltham, Massachusetts, may currently be doing more to prevent an armed robot dog from patrolling an American street than any statute currently on the books.

What the balcony sees

This series has generally found, territory by territory, that the moral shape of a robot depends less on the machine itself than on the human intent behind it — the same lever, as Part XV put it, lifting a scalpel in one hand and a gun in the other. Policing robots may be the purest test of that principle in the entire series, because the very same platform — a quadruped robot dog, built by the very same company, running the very same software — does uncontested, life-saving work when it scouts a bomb threat and becomes a genuine and unresolved civil-liberties controversy the moment it is asked instead to simply watch a crowd. The technology has not changed between those two uses. The task has, and with it, everything about how a democratic society ought to feel about the machine performing it.

I do not think this series should pretend the argument is settled, because it plainly is not, and reasonable people of good faith land in very different places depending on how much they weigh the documented safety benefit against the equally documented absence of any governing rules. What does seem true, reading across every account of this debate, is that the jurisdictions handling this well are the ones doing the unglamorous, unrewarding work first — writing the rules before the robot arrives, rather than after the backlash does. The K5 sitting unused on its charging pad in a New York subway station, an active contract quietly running out the clock on a robot nobody wanted, is as good a monument as this series has found to what happens when a capable machine arrives ahead of the conversation about what it should be allowed to do.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one is still sitting on its charging pad, waiting to be told what its job actually is.
