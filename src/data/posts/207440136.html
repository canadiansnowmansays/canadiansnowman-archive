If you only do one thing

The CRTC — the federal regulator that oversees your phone, internet, and TV providers — is right now deciding the rules that will govern how those companies treat you. It’s called the Consumer Protection Code harmonization proceeding, and the public is allowed to weigh in. Ordinary Canadians. You.

The deadline to add your voice is August 11, 2026. It takes about fifteen minutes. You don’t need to be a lawyer or write anything long. Full step-by-step instructions are at the bottom of this piece, and here is the link to file: CRTC proceeding 2026-134.

If you read no further, please do that one thing. The regulator counts what it hears. A question raised by three hundred Canadians is far harder to ignore than one raised by a single retired man in Toronto with a husky and too much time on his hands. (That would be me. The husky’s name is Dior. She has opinions.)

Now, the argument.

The short version

Every essential utility in Canada bills you after you use it. Your electricity company measures what you consumed last month, then sends the bill. Same with water. Same with natural gas. You use it, then you pay for it. This is called billing in arrears, and it’s the normal, fair way essential services work.

Telecommunications is the exception. Your phone, internet, and TV providers bill you roughly thirty days in advance — you pay now for service you haven’t received yet.

Most of the time you don’t notice. But the moment something changes — you cancel, you switch providers, you move — the problem surfaces. You’ve already paid for service you’ll never receive. The company owes you a refund. And their systems, mysteriously, are very slow to show you that. You can end up chasing money that’s rightfully yours, or worse, being charged a late fee on an amount you never actually owed.

Here’s the part that took me a year to fully appreciate: the CRTC has never actually examined whether this practice should exist. It has spent a decade managing the consequences of advance billing — requiring companies to eventually issue prorated refunds, for instance — without ever asking the prior question: should telecom companies be allowed to bill in advance at all, when no other essential utility does?

That’s the question I’ve put on the public record. And this harmonization proceeding is the first real chance to make the regulator confront it.

How I got here

This started, as these things do, with a small amount of money.

Late last year I helped a family member switch wireless carriers. We cancelled four days into a billing cycle. The old provider’s system still showed the full month’s charge — about $42 — instead of the roughly $4 we actually owed for four days. When I tried to pay the correct amount, I discovered the company had quietly removed every way of reaching a human being: no inbound calls, a chatbot that wouldn’t connect me to anyone, and callback requests that were refused because the account was now cancelled.

Then they charged a $1.22 late fee. A penalty for declining to pay for service that was never going to be provided.

It was never about the $1.22. It was about the realization that this wasn’t a glitch — it was the system working as designed. And when I looked into it, I found that the regulator had never questioned the design. It had simply inherited advance billing from the earliest days of the telephone and assumed it was necessary.

Why the old justification no longer holds

Advance billing isn’t new. In fact it’s older than the phone companies themselves. The very first telephone arrangement in Canada — an 1877 line connecting the Prime Minister’s office to the Governor General at Rideau Hall — was billed at $42.50 a year, “payable in advance.”

In 1877, that made sense. A fragile new company was stringing copper wire across an enormous country, with no way to measure usage and no access to capital. Collecting money up front was how it survived.

It is no longer 1877. The companies billing you thirty days early today are among the largest corporations in Canada. They have sophisticated financial operations and real-time billing systems that can calculate your charges to the day — I know they can, because they do it the instant you owe them money. Every original reason for advance billing has dissolved. The practice simply outlived its justification.

And here’s what makes this more than a consumer complaint: fixing it would help the companies too. Billing in arrears would eliminate the entire machinery of refunds, and it would cut the volume of billing complaints — which the companies themselves pay fees to have handled. It’s a rare case where the fair thing and the efficient thing are the same thing. A win for consumers and providers alike.

“But telecom is really more like streaming”

You may see it argued that telecom shouldn’t be compared to utilities at all, but to streaming services like Netflix, which also bill a month ahead. It’s a clever reframing, and it falls apart on two points.

First, the history runs backwards. Telephone advance billing dates to the 1870s; streaming is barely two decades old. If anyone is following anyone, streaming followed telecom’s century-old convention, not the other way around. You cannot defend an 1870s practice by comparing it to a business model that didn’t exist until the 2000s.

Second, and more importantly, streaming and telecom are not the same kind of thing. Netflix is optional entertainment you can cancel in two clicks — no phantom balance, no late fee, no unreachable call centre, no consequence for leaving mid-month. Telecom is an essential service — it’s how you reach 911, your bank, your employer — and cancelling it triggers exactly the harms streaming doesn’t. You don’t get to be regulated as essential infrastructure, subsidized as essential infrastructure, and mandated as essential infrastructure, while billing like a movie subscription. The honest comparison is the one the providers avoid: electricity, water, and gas. Every one of them bills in arrears.

How to file your own intervention — step by step

I’ve now done this myself, so these are the real steps, in order.

1. Go to the proceeding. Use this link: CRTC proceeding 2026-134. On that page, clicking Interventions shows you everyone who has already filed, so you can see what others have written. Clicking Submit begins your own filing.

2. Choose your stance. The form asks whether your intervention is a comment, in support, or in opposition. If you support the CRTC modernizing the rules but want it to go further and examine advance billing, choose in support. You’re not against the proceeding — you want it to do more.

3. Write your comment. There’s a comment box. Put your point here, in your own words, even if you also attach a document. Whatever you type appears directly on the public record. A few honest sentences about your own experience is plenty.

4. Attach a document if you have one. Optional. Most people simply type into the comment box, and that’s completely fine.

5. Fill in the contact fields — carefully. The form asks for name, email, address, postal code, phone, and fax. Some fields are optional, and whatever you enter becomes public. You can put just your city and province in the address field (”Toronto, ON” is enough) and leave postal code, phone, and fax blank. Your full street address and phone number do not need to be on a public record.

6. Decide about the hearing. The form asks if you want to appear at the public hearing in Gatineau this November. You can answer No and your written intervention still counts fully. Appearing is optional.

7. Submit. That’s it. You’ll get a confirmation screen and an email with your own case number — mine came through in seconds. Your intervention is now part of the official record.

The deadline is August 11, 2026, at 8 p.m. Eastern. After that, the door closes.

One last thing

For most of my dealings with the telecom industry, the frustrating part was that no one on the other end was required to listen. This is the rare exception. When you file an intervention, someone at the regulator is actually obligated to read what you wrote.

So write something. Tell them what happened to you. Tell them what you think should change. It takes fifteen minutes, and unlike almost everything else about your phone bill, this time your voice is on the record.

Go be heard.

If you file, I’d genuinely love to know — leave a note in the comments. And if advance billing has ever cost you money you didn’t owe, that story is evidence. Put it on the record while the record is open.
