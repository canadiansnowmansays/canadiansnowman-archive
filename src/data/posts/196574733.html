There is a long and honourable tradition in American governance of calling things what they are not.

A highway bill that funds a senator’s cousin’s asphalt company is called infrastructure investment. A weapons purchase for a country that has not been invaded is called foreign aid. A tax cut that enriches the top one percent of earners is called relief for working families. Washington has elevated euphemism to a structural feature of democracy, the way Venice elevated water.

So perhaps we should not be surprised that a billion-dollar subsidy for a presidential vanity project has been quietly inserted into an immigration enforcement bill. Not labelled as what it is — a public subsidy for a ballroom that was explicitly and repeatedly promised to be privately funded — but as something called “security adjustments and upgrades.” Below-ground features. Perimeter enhancements. The sort of language that sounds like it belongs in a NORAD briefing rather than a decorating invoice.

The White House East Wing — demolished last year on the president’s personal initiative, not Congress’s, not the people’s — is being replaced, we are told, with an enormous ballroom. The president promised the ballroom would be paid for with private donations. He said this multiple times, with the confidence of a man who has always found other people’s money conveniently available when the bill arrives.

And now here we are: a billion dollars, tucked into a $70 billion immigration package, earmarked for the ballroom’s security infrastructure. The ballroom itself, to be clear, would still technically be paid for privately. Just the fencing, the underground features, the fortifications necessary to protect a space that — in a more innocent age — would never have been built in the first place. The china, perhaps, will come from donors. The moat is on us.

Senator Schumer, the Democratic minority leader, observed that Republicans had “looked at families drowning in bills and decided what they really needed was more raids and a Trump ballroom.” This is correct. It is also, it must be said, the most compressed summary of the second Trump administration’s domestic policy agenda yet attempted.

Senator Brian Schatz of Hawaii noted, with admirable brevity, that everyone would now get “an up or down vote on the ballroom.” This too is correct, and it carries the additional virtue of being funny. In six months, with midterms approaching, Republican senators in swing states will be asked to explain why they voted to fund perimeter security for a room where the president plans to entertain people wearing evening clothes. The political consultants who sold them this package deserve a special recognition, perhaps in a ballroom.

There is a pattern here, and it deserves to be named. The Qatar jet — the $400 million aircraft gifted by a foreign government, to be used as Air Force One and then transferred to a presidential library where it will, in the fullness of time, become Donald Trump’s personal property — follows the same architecture. Public resources, in service of private enrichment, dressed in the language of national security and diplomatic normalcy. The plane is a gift to America. The ballroom is a security upgrade. The gift ends up in Mar-a-Lago. The upgrade ends up in a federal immigration bill. The pattern, once seen, cannot be unseen.

Rand Paul, the Kentucky Republican who has made a career of objecting to government spending on grounds of principle, is supporting the $70 billion package. He has explained that Democrats forced his hand by refusing to fund border enforcement. This is the Washington equivalent of explaining that you only ate the entire cake because someone suggested you might not finish it. The logic is structurally sound and completely untethered from reality.

From Canada, where we watch American political theatre with the helpless attention of people sitting in the splash zone at SeaWorld, the ballroom story has a particular resonance. We have a prime minister who was just re-elected, in part, by Canadians who felt that the chaos emanating from the south required a steady hand. Our steadiness, to be honest, consists largely of watching and being agog.

But even for us, the speed with which “the ballroom will be privately funded” became “the ballroom’s security features require a billion dollars in public money” is impressive. It is not quite the land speed record for a broken promise — that remains contested territory — but it is comfortably in the top ten.

The West Wing has a press briefing room. The East Wing, apparently, will have a ballroom.

One wonders what the South Wing will have. Given the trajectory, probably a golf simulator. Paid for, no doubt, through a border security appropriation.

The chandeliers, though. Those will be private.
