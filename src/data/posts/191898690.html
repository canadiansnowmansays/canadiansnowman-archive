45-51 Park Place, Lower Manhattan. A former Burlington Coat Factory two blocks from Ground Zero. The proposed site of a community center with a mosque, a swimming pool, and a culinary school. None of them were built.

In the summer of 2010, a group of Muslim Americans in Lower Manhattan announced plans to build an Islamic community center two blocks from the World Trade Center site. The Cordoba House — later renamed Park51 — was planned as a thirteen-story facility that would include a mosque, a swimming pool, a culinary school, an auditorium, and a September 11 memorial. Its founders, Imam Feisal Abdul Rauf and Daisy Khan, were American Muslims who had spent years working in interfaith dialogue and had been valued partners of the FBI in countering extremism. Rauf had been sent by the Bush State Department on outreach missions to Muslim-majority countries. He was, by any reasonable assessment, precisely the kind of American Muslim leader that the government’s community partnership programs were designed to cultivate.

The announcement generated a political crisis that consumed the American summer of 2010. Sarah Palin called it a “stab in the heart of the families of the 9/11 victims.” Newt Gingrich compared it to a Nazi sign near a Holocaust museum. Conservative commentators described it as a “victory mosque” — a monument to the September 11 attacks constructed on the ground where they had occurred. The city of New York, which had jurisdiction over the building permits, approved the project. A proposed state landmark designation that would have blocked construction failed. The political pressure continued. The project was scaled back, delayed, defunded, and eventually converted into a small prayer space. The swimming pool and culinary school were never built.

The Ground Zero Mosque episode is the bridge — the moment between the post-9/11 surveillance apparatus documented in Part VII and the explicit political Islamophobia documented in Part IX. It is the moment when the exception clause moved from the administrative state into mass political culture — when the argument that Muslim Americans did not belong in certain spaces, could not be trusted with certain civic acts, were required to prove their loyalty before exercising rights that other Americans exercised without question, became a mainstream political position rather than a fringe one.

The Argument That Was Made

The opposition to Park51 was not, officially, an argument against religious freedom. Its proponents were careful to acknowledge, when pressed, that Muslims had a legal right to build a mosque anywhere in New York City that zoning permitted. The argument was different: that building a mosque near Ground Zero was, regardless of its legal permissibility, an act of insensitivity — a failure to recognize how the location would feel to the families of September 11 victims, a refusal to acknowledge the association between Islam and the attacks that killed three thousand Americans.

The argument is the most sophisticated version of the exception clause logic this series has encountered. It does not deny the right. It conditions its exercise on the sensitivities of others. It requires the targeted community to ask permission — not from a government authority but from the collective emotional state of the dominant culture — before exercising a constitutional right in a specific location.

Applied consistently, this logic would mean that Muslim Americans cannot build mosques within a certain radius of the World Trade Center site, because the association between Islam and the attacks is too strong for the dominant culture to tolerate. It would also mean — though this implication was rarely drawn — that Christian Americans cannot build churches near the sites of atrocities committed by Christian perpetrators, that no religious community is required to account, architecturally, for the acts of the worst members of any group that shares their faith.

The principle was not applied consistently, because consistency was not the point. The point was the specific application: this religion, in this location, at this time, is required to obtain a permission that other religions are not required to obtain.

That is the exception clause in its purest contemporary form.

The Political Mechanism

What the Ground Zero Mosque episode demonstrated, with unusual clarity, was the mechanism by which local anti-Muslim sentiment becomes national political permission.

The campaign against Park51 was initially organized by Pamela Geller, a blogger and activist who had been writing explicitly anti-Muslim content for years, and Robert Spencer, the founder of Jihad Watch, which the Southern Poverty Law Center had designated as a hate group. Both had been largely confined to the online networks of the explicitly Islamophobic right — known quantities in civil rights circles, marginal figures in national politics.

2010 protests near the proposed Park51 site. The campaign transformed a local zoning issue into a national political flashpoint

The Ground Zero Mosque gave them a story — a concrete, visual, emotionally legible story that could be told on cable television and in congressional floor speeches without requiring any of the ideological framework that made their broader arguments seem extreme. You didn’t need to believe that Islam was inherently violent to believe that building a mosque near Ground Zero was insensitive. You didn’t need to be an Islamophobe to find the image of an Islamic center two blocks from where three thousand people had been killed troubling. The story was designed to be accessible to people who would have recoiled from Geller’s more explicit content.

Sarah Palin’s Facebook post — “Ground Zero Mosque supporters: doesn’t it stab you in the heart, as it does ours throughout the heartland?” — was not written by Geller. But it moved Geller’s argument into the mainstream of Republican politics. Newt Gingrich’s Nazi comparison was not written by Spencer. But it moved Spencer’s rhetorical framework into the public statements of a former Speaker of the House.

This is the transmission mechanism: the fringe provides the framework; the mainstream provides the amplification; the targeted community bears the consequence. The fringe can be disavowed when convenient — Palin and Gingrich were not endorsing everything Geller had ever written. The argument traveled from the fringe to the mainstream regardless.

The Loyalty Demand

The deepest implication of the Ground Zero Mosque episode was the demand it encoded — not always explicitly, but structurally — that Muslim Americans prove their loyalty to American civic life before exercising the rights that civic life guarantees.

The demand appeared in op-eds, in cable news commentary, in the statements of politicians who were careful to acknowledge the legal right while questioning its exercise. The argument: that responsible Muslim American leaders would voluntarily relocate the project, out of sensitivity to victims’ families, as a gesture of solidarity with the broader American community — and that the failure to do so demonstrated something troubling about the community’s relationship to American values.

Even the president could not hold the line. On August 13, 2010, Barack Obama used the White House Iftar dinner to defend the right to build Park51, invoking the principle that “Muslims have the same right to practice their religion as everyone else in this country.” By the following afternoon, he was walking it back: he had been commenting on the right, he clarified, not on the “wisdom” of the decision. The retreat took twelve hours. If the President of the United States accepted the framing that the right existed but its exercise required separate justification, the demand was already structurally irresistible.

This is the Houston speech demand in a new register. Kennedy was required to prove, in 1960, that his Catholicism did not override his civic commitments. Wilford Woodruff was required to prove, in 1890, that the Latter-day Saints were willing to conform their institutional practices to federal demands. The Ground Zero Mosque demand was structurally identical: prove your loyalty by voluntarily limiting the exercise of your rights, and the limitation will be accepted as evidence of belonging.

Muslim American leaders responded in various ways. Some, including Imam Rauf, attempted to engage the argument on its own terms — emphasizing the community center’s interfaith character, its September 11 memorial, its role in countering extremism. Others rejected the premise: why should they be required to prove, by limiting their constitutional rights, a loyalty that no other religious community was asked to demonstrate?

Both responses were correct. Both were also, in different ways, traps. Engagement accepted the legitimacy of the demand. Refusal was characterized as evidence of the disloyalty the demand was designed to test for.

This is what the exception clause does to communities caught within it. It creates a situation in which every available response confirms the framing it has imposed.

What 2010 Built for 2016

The Ground Zero Mosque episode ended without the community center being built. Geller and Spencer moved on to other campaigns. Palin and Gingrich moved on to other controversies. The cable news cycle moved on. The episode appeared to fade.

It did not fade. It built.

What 2010 built was a demonstrated political market for anti-Muslim content in mainstream American political discourse. Palin’s Facebook post had generated enormous engagement. Gingrich’s Nazi comparison had generated enormous media coverage. The argument that Islam required special scrutiny, that Muslim civic participation required special justification, that the exception clause applied to this religious community in ways it did not apply to others — that argument had been shown to work politically. It moved votes. It generated donations. It built platforms.

The infrastructure of the Islamophobia industry — the think tanks, the websites, the speakers’ circuits — had produced content for years without achieving mainstream political legitimacy. The Ground Zero Mosque gave them the mainstream vehicle they had been waiting for. By 2015, when Donald Trump announced his candidacy for the presidency and proposed a ban on Muslim immigration to the United States, he was not introducing a new idea. He was harvesting a crop that 2010 had planted.

The Ground Zero Mosque episode is the pivot point in the contemporary history of the exception clause — the moment when the argument moved from administrative practice to mass political culture, and from mass political culture to explicit political platform. The distance from Pamela Geller’s blog in 2010 to Andy Ogles’s X post in 2026 is not as large as it appears. It is a straight line, traveled in stages, each stage normalized by the one that preceded it.

The Families of September 11

The Ground Zero Mosque debate invoked, repeatedly and with genuine emotional force, the families of September 11 victims — their grief, their pain, their right to determine what was appropriate near the site of their loss. The invocation was not cynical in every case. The grief was real. The association was real. Some families of September 11 victims genuinely opposed the project.

NYPD Cadet Salman Hamdani as a boy, who died at the World Trade Center on September 11, 2001. He was Muslim

Others supported it. Talat Hamdani, whose son Salman — a New York City Police cadet — had died in the World Trade Center on September 11, spoke publicly in favor of Park51. Her son was Muslim. He ran toward the towers. He died in them. The argument that a mosque near Ground Zero dishonored the victims of September 11 dishonored him specifically.

The families of September 11 victims were not a monolith. The debate treated them as one — treated the subset who opposed Park51 as the authentic voice of collective grief, and treated the Muslim Americans among the victims and their families as either invisible or as required to defer to the dominant framing.

This is the exception clause’s most precise operation: making some members of a community’s grief more legible than others, some victims more real, some losses more worthy of architectural accommodation. The three thousand people who died on September 11 included Muslims. The city they died in, the country they died for, and the constitutional principles they lived under did not require their families to prove a loyalty that the families of non-Muslim victims were not asked to prove.

That principle was not what the summer of 2010 demonstrated.
