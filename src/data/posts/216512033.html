Stephen Gordon

Part L of this series told the story of Missouri’s redistricting fight from the perspective of the courts and the clerks: the flurry of contradictory orders, the ninety-six minutes between a Missouri Supreme Court ruling and a federal district court reversal, the clerks left three weeks behind schedule by the time the dust settled. That story was about institutions. This one is about what those institutions did to the people who don’t get to argue in front of them.

A Church in Three Pieces

Start with Independence Boulevard Christian Church, in northeast Kansas City. Under the 2025 map — the one Missouri Republicans drew, the one that won the August primary, the one a federal district court briefly ordered restored before the U.S. Supreme Court put it back down again — the church building sits in one congressional district. Its parking lot sits in a second. A market across the street sits in a third.

This is not an accident of geography. It is the specific, deliberate technique known in redistricting as “cracking”: splitting a community, a neighbourhood, or in this case a single city block, across multiple districts so that no one piece retains enough concentrated voting power to matter on its own. Missouri’s 2025 map used the technique on a larger scale against Kansas City as a whole, carving the city long represented by Democratic Representative Emanuel Cleaver into three districts that stretch out into rural central Missouri, each one individually favourable to Republicans in a way the compact, unified city electorate was not.

Mindy Fugarino, the church’s pastor, described what this does to people who were already inclined to disengage from a system they didn’t trust. “Before, there already were people who were saying [voting] doesn’t matter; they had already felt disenfranchised,” she said. “I think there are some folks just using this as another reason to disengage.” A single line drawn through a parking lot does not, by itself, decide an election. But it tells the people standing on either side of that line something about how seriously the mapmakers considered them as a community, and that message does not require the line to survive judicial review to land.

Two Maps, One Campaign

Bethany Mann has been campaigning for Missouri’s Third Congressional District seat while, in her own description, not entirely certain which Third Congressional District she was running to represent. Both major versions of the district — the 2022 lines and the 2025 lines — had to be stumped simultaneously, at real cost in manpower, organising time, and money that a challenger campaign does not have to spare.

“We knew that they were going to be enacting this gerrymandered scheme,” Mann said. “So our strategy, not just in the Third Congressional but as a Democratic Party as a whole, was to beat these congresspeople on any map that they offer.” That is a coherent strategy. It is also an extraordinary thing to have to say about a congressional campaign in the United States: that a candidate’s electoral strategy had to be map-agnostic, built to survive a boundary dispute that wouldn’t be settled until weeks before the election itself.

Her opponent, Republican Representative Bob Onder, took the opposite bet and sued over the consequences of having lost it. Onder campaigned on the assumption that the 2025 map — his party’s map, the one that would have redrawn him a friendlier district — was the one that would govern the general election. When the Missouri Supreme Court restored the old lines, Onder placed the blame entirely outside his own campaign’s choices: “To the extent this has been thrown into confusion and chaos, the fault is entirely [on] the part of the Missouri Supreme Court.” Whatever the legal merits of that position, it describes a candidate for federal office who built a general election campaign on a map that had already been challenged in court, and then asked a different court to make that bet retroactively correct.

A year earlier, on a since-recirculated clip, Onder had described asking Trump directly for the map that produced this fight: “I said, ‘Mr. President, do you want us to draw you an extra congressional seat in Missouri?’” The state had voted for Trump by eighteen points and held Republican supermajorities in its legislature, and was nonetheless, under the old map, sending what Onder called “two progressive Democrats” to Congress. That is the origin of the map that went through three trips to the U.S. Supreme Court this month — and the reason a pastor’s parking lot sits in three congressional districts at once.

A Postcard and a Question Without an Answer

Pat Harcourt lives in Wright City, Missouri, and writes postcards to voters urging them to participate: your vote counts. During the August primary, she thought her own Democratic ballot had been printed incorrectly. It hadn’t. Her address had shifted into a different congressional district under the map in effect for the primary. She was, in other words, briefly unable to tell whether the postcard she was writing to strangers described her own situation accurately.

“And here I am writing postcards to people, ‘Your vote counts,’” Harcourt said. “Well, I don’t know. Maybe it counts, but it may not be in your congressional district.” That sentence is worth sitting with, because it captures something the court filings and the clerk interviews in Part L could only gesture toward: the redistricting fight did not just create administrative backlog. It created a genuine, first-person uncertainty, in a volunteer actively working to build voter confidence, about whether her own vote was being counted in the race she thought she was voting in.

Not everyone affected blames the same people. Bob Eno, chair of the St. Charles County Republican Central Committee, argued that the Missouri Supreme Court’s ruling — not the legislature’s original map, not Hoskins’ handling of the referendum deadline — was what took away voters’ chance to choose their representation, by reverting to the old map after primary votes had already been cast under the new one. Mitchell Clem, a Republican voter in O’Fallon whose district also moved under the 2025 map, offered a more resigned verdict that cut across party lines: he doesn’t like gerrymandering, understands Missouri is only one front in a national trend, and thinks, simply, “we need to fix this... it’s just gotten out of hand.”

Hoskins himself did not emerge from this fight merely bruised. The Missouri Supreme Court found him in contempt after he directed local election officials to use the 2025 map in direct violation of the court’s own order — and later ruled that he had “purged” that contempt only once he reversed course and told officials to revert to the 2022 map. A secretary of state held in contempt of his own state’s highest court, in the middle of an election, for defying an order he was legally bound to follow: that is not background noise to this story. It is the story, condensed into a single judicial finding.

What the Legal Experts Say, and What the Record Now Shows

Nate Persily, the Stanford law professor whose warnings about mail-ballot friction featured earlier in this series, offered a parallel diagnosis for the redistricting fight: “We’re talking about last-minute changes to election infrastructure, which could lead to either confusion or affect the outcome of the election. And it is a serious problem, particularly in our current context when voter confidence in the election infrastructure has collapsed.” Richard Pildes, the NYU constitutional law scholar, described the broader pattern across this year’s redistricting wars as a “downward spiral” with a specific psychological cost: “It leads to many voters feeling the system is being manipulated, that the system’s rigged.”

A three-judge panel of the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals heard oral arguments on Thursday, September 17th — one day before Missouri’s deadline to send ballots to military and overseas voters — in a last-ditch effort by Representative Onder and state Senator Rick Brattin to revive the 2025 map for November. The judges, according to reporting from the hearing, appeared sympathetic in places to the Republican plaintiffs’ arguments, even as one judge pressed on timing directly: “When do we reach the point of no return?” Another raised a separate, potentially case-ending concern about whether the parties defending the referendum even had standing to intervene in the first place.

Legal experts following the case were divided on what a ruling, whenever it arrived, would actually change. Michael Wolff, a former judge on the Missouri Supreme Court, called the underlying problem an “insuperable obstacle” regardless of what the Eighth Circuit decided: the Missouri Supreme Court had already ruled, as the state’s final authority on its own law, that the 2025 map “is not the law and never was,” because it was never approved by voters. A ruling favourable to Onder and Brattin, in his view, would not by itself revive a map the state’s highest court said never took legal effect. One columnist covering the fight for the St. Louis Post-Dispatch summarized the state of play more bluntly: “The war’s over, but there’s still soldiers fighting.”

That framing turned out to describe only part of what the panel actually did. On Monday, September 21st, the Eighth Circuit — three judges nominated by Republican presidents — sided with Onder and Brattin, finding that “Missouri’s current plan to use the 2022 map in the November 2026 general election violates the Constitution.” Wolff’s argument was answering a question about whether the 2025 map was valid under Missouri law. The panel never reached that question. It ruled instead on a separate federal constitutional claim: that using one map for the primary and a different one for the general election, after candidates had already campaigned, advertised, and been nominated under the first, violates the constitutional rights of both the candidates and the voters who nominated them — regardless of what state law says about the map’s validity. That is a different legal theory than the one Wolff described as foreclosed, and it succeeded where the state-law theory had not.

The ruling did not survive the week. The panel stayed its own order to give the U.S. Supreme Court time to weigh in, and ballots using the 2022 map had already gone out to overseas and military voters. Richard von Glahn, the leader of the referendum group, filed an emergency appeal on the night of Tuesday, September 22nd. Brianna Lennon, whose refreshed browser tabs opened Part L of this series, had put it as plainly as anyone quoted here: “I expect that the Supreme Court will have a say in this before it is over.” It did, on Friday, September 25th, blocking the 2025 map for the third time this month in an unsigned order with no noted dissents, as Ann E. Marimow and Mitch Smith reported for the New York Times. “The election is not merely upcoming; it has already started,” the justices wrote. Missouri will vote in November under the 2022 lines. One figure is worth holding onto regardless: roughly 970,000 voters across six of Missouri’s eight congressional districts — nearly a quarter of the state’s registered voters — are assigned to a different district for the general election than they were for the primary.

That is, in miniature, the entire argument of this series. The institutions did their job. The Missouri Supreme Court ruled, twice. The U.S. Supreme Court backed that ruling three times. A secretary of state was held in contempt for defying it and reversed course. A federal appeals court ruled the opposite way on a different question entirely, stayed its own answer, and was set aside within the week — while a pastor’s parking lot sat in a different congressional district from her church, while a candidate stumped two maps at once, and while a volunteer wrote postcards she could not be certain applied to her own address. The map is settled for November. The people are not: close to a million Missourians will cast ballots in a district other than the one in which they nominated their candidates.
