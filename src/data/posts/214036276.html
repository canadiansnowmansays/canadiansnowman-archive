There is an old fable, retold so often it has become shorthand for a particular kind of dishonesty, about a boy who cries wolf until nobody comes when the wolf is real. The State Department’s recent adventures with the “censorship–industrial complex” invert the fable in an interesting way. Nobody cried wolf here because a wolf was ever sighted. The wolf was invented first, and the crying came after, sustained across sixteen months and several government agencies, in the apparent hope that repetition might eventually manufacture the evidence that investigation kept failing to produce.

Regular readers of this series will recognize the shape immediately, even though the department is new. This is not the Pentagon controlling what reporters may photograph, or reclassifying a casualty count until the arithmetic no longer adds up in public. This is a different ministry, doing the same job with a different instrument. The Pentagon parts of this series have concerned themselves with a body count — what gets hidden, split across pages, or quietly revised once nobody is counting anymore. This part concerns a different kind of record: not who died, but who gets to speak, and who gets to say so without being sanctioned, sued, or driven out of their job for having said it first.

It is worth pausing on why this particular conspiracy has proven so durable despite its total absence of supporting evidence. A functioning conspiracy theory needs no proof to sustain itself; it only needs an absence of disproof convincing enough to satisfy people who were already inclined to believe it. Every failed lawsuit, every dismissed case, every discovery process that turns up nothing becomes, in this logic, further evidence of how thoroughly the cover-up has been executed — proof of absence recast as absence of proof. That is precisely the epistemic trap into which Beattie, Rogers, and their allies walked, and it is why sixteen months of searching produced not a shred of confirming evidence and yet somehow strengthened, rather than weakened, the underlying institutional conviction that something was still being hidden.

The hunt that found nothing

The search began early and searched widely. Darren Beattie — founder of a far-right conspiracist website before his appointment as undersecretary of state for public diplomacy — issued a sweeping request for files on anyone who had corresponded with or written about the Global Engagement Center, the State Department agency that had spent years tracking propaganda campaigns run by Russia, China, and Iran. As Anne Applebaum reported for The Atlantic on September 2, 2026, the request named an unusually broad cast: Bill Gates, commentator Bill Kristol, former cybersecurity official Chris Krebs, a German government minister, researcher Renée DiResta, and Applebaum herself.

The premise was that a “censorship–industrial complex” — a coordinated, Biden-era scheme run through government agencies, social-media platforms, and university researchers to suppress conservative speech online — had operated in the shadows for years and left a paper trail somewhere in the GEC’s files. Since 2020, this premise has powered a small industry of lawsuits, congressional hearings, and cable-news segments. What it has never powered is a finding. The Missouri attorney general’s suit reached the Supreme Court before justices asked, in essence, where the censorship actually was, and received no answer that survived scrutiny. A parallel suit against the Stanford-based Election Integrity Partnership, brought by Stephen Miller’s America First Legal, collapsed in July 2026 for lack of standing, lack of jurisdiction, and — after the plaintiffs got the discovery process they had asked for — lack of evidence. Other cases were simply settled by the Trump Justice Department without any finding of wrongdoing at all.

None of this dampened the enthusiasm inside the department. It simply moved the hunt to a new filing cabinet.

From investigation to leak

Beattie’s successor, Sarah Rogers, inherited the GEC inquiry and, after months of searching turned up nothing worth publishing under her own name, took a different approach: she leaked the raw material to an X personality called DataRepublican — in private life Jennica Pounds, a woman with an established habit of finding conspiracies in datasets that do not contain them. The plan misfired almost immediately. Pounds examined the screenshots, decided Rogers herself must be a Deep State plant, and refused to publish. It later emerged that Pounds had her own undisclosed employment arrangement with the Pentagon, which is the kind of detail this series would ordinarily spend a full paragraph enjoying, except that it is, strictly speaking, someone else’s story.

Rogers published the documents herself, in a long thread that Applebaum’s reporting picks apart in some detail. A State Department official’s dozen or so emails with Stanford researchers, almost entirely about Russian-linked accounts, became evidence of collusion. Congressional testimony from a former GEC deputy coordinator explaining, accurately, that the center’s mandate covered foreign disinformation rather than American speech, was flatly declared a lie, with nothing offered to support the claim. A number pulled from a Stanford research table — a count of tweets the researchers had noted, not censored — was recast as proof the project had targeted hundreds of thousands of Americans directly. A jointly published, multilingual, entirely public report on gendered disinformation — the coordinated harassment campaigns and deepfake pornography regularly deployed against female politicians such as Annalena Baerbock and Giorgia Meloni, and against Iranian dissidents — was folded into the conspiracy on the theory that studying the tactic is itself the crime.

A former State Department official summarized the whole affair for Applebaum more efficiently than this essay can manage: whatever this was, it was not Watergate. Rogers, for her part, is not Bob Woodward. She is a public servant who published raw, unredacted material about American citizens on a personal social-media account, apparently without pausing to ask who might be harmed by the release or what interest, beyond her own vindication, it might serve. That distinction — between an investigative reporter working through verified sources toward a documented conclusion, and an official dumping unverified fragments onto the internet in search of a conclusion that had already been decided in advance — is the entire difference between journalism and the thing this series keeps finding standing in journalism’s place.

The cost of chasing a phantom

If the story stopped at a badly sourced X thread, it would belong in the category of institutional embarrassment rather than institutional damage. It does not stop there, because the fictional threat was used to dismantle a real capability. In April 2025, Secretary of State Marco Rubio closed the Global Engagement Center outright, on the grounds that it had spent millions of dollars silencing American voices. This was the closure of the one office in the U.S. government whose actual job was identifying foreign information operations — including websites in Africa and Latin America dressed up as local outlets while being run out of Moscow or Beijing. The people who did that work were, in effect, accused of the crime they had been hired to detect.

The damage did not stay confined to one agency. Stanford shut down its internet-monitoring research after years of litigation and harassment. DiResta left the university and has written, in these same pages of The Atlantic, about the sustained online abuse that followed her out the door. Other research programs around the country scaled back or closed as university administrators, watching the pattern, decided the safer course was to stop studying the problem rather than risk becoming the next target of an investigation that requires no evidence to begin and no evidence to justify its conclusions.

And the technique did not stay confined to the United States. Undersecretary Rogers has since told a Hudson Institute audience that a “censorship class” had “scuttled to Europe” to continue its work from across the ocean — a claim offered with the same evidentiary rigor as everything preceding it. Senator Eric Schmitt of Missouri, whose own censorship lawsuit as attorney general had already failed, used the same platform to describe a “deeply troubling assault on free speech” underway in Europe. What followed was not merely rhetoric. The State Department committed four million dollars to support sympathetic media outlets in Europe. Thierry Breton, the former EU commissioner who helped write the Digital Services Act — a law aimed at platform transparency and the protection of minors online — was placed under a U.S. visa restriction as a private citizen. Four European researchers connected to the same law or to hate-speech advocacy were sanctioned outright, among them the leaders of the German organization HateAid, who described the visa ban, not unreasonably, as an act of repression aimed at silencing its critics.

Here is the inversion this series exists to name: an agency built to fight a censorship conspiracy that never existed has become, in the process, an actual instrument of censorship — visa bans and sanctions deployed against researchers and a former official for the specific offense of having studied or legislated against disinformation. The ministry did not find the thing it was hunting. It became it.

Notice, too, how neatly the targets sort themselves by what they were actually doing. Breton helped write a law requiring platforms to disclose how their algorithms work. HateAid’s leaders campaigned against coordinated hate speech and the kind of deepfake harassment the gendered-disinformation report was written to document. None of them censored an American. All of them made the work of understanding online manipulation slightly harder to dismiss as unnecessary — which turns out, in this administration’s accounting, to be close enough to the same offense to warrant the same punishment. The complaint was never really about censorship. It was about the inconvenience of being observed.

Hiring the people it just finished firing

The story’s final turn belongs, again, to Orwell, who understood better than most that bureaucracies rarely abandon a function; they simply rename it and reassign it once the political cost of admitting the original mistake becomes too awkward to pay. In May 2026, the State Department quietly issued a tender for software developers capable of analyzing foreign adversarial propaganda. In August, a State Department contractor posted an opening for research analysts to evaluate how foreign governments and other malign actors use social media to shape public opinion — a description that could have been lifted, with only minor editing, from the Global Engagement Center’s own charter.

As DiResta observed this week, the department is now hiring people to perform the very job it spent sixteen months insisting was a conspiracy. Applebaum’s own accounting of the cost is worth sitting with: sixteen months, several careers, an entire research field made radioactive, and a genuine foreign-policy vulnerability reopened — all spent chasing a phantom that dissolved, every single time, the moment anyone actually looked for it. Now the department will spend more time, more money, and presumably more institutional credibility rebuilding, under a different letterhead, precisely what it destroyed.

Winston Smith’s job at the actual Ministry of Truth was to make the past agree with the present by feeding inconvenient documents into the memory hole and writing new ones to replace them. The State Department has managed something almost more efficient: it fed a real institution into the hole, invented a document to justify doing so, and is now drafting a replacement institution from scratch. Orwell, one suspects, would not have needed to reach very far for the metaphor. This time, the ministry didn’t have to lie about the censorship. It only had to become it.
