I need to tell you something, and I’m not proud of it.

I have something in common with Donald Trump.

We both love numbers.

He loves big numbers. The biggest numbers. Numbers so big that other numbers look at them and feel inadequate. “We had the biggest crowd in the history of crowds.” “The ratings were through the roof — 97 million, maybe more.” “$10 billion — that’s what I’m worth, probably more.” He once claimed his inauguration crowd was the largest ever assembled, which it was not, but the number he felt it should have been was more important than the number it was, and if you don’t understand that distinction, you don’t understand Donald Trump.

I, meanwhile, spent the last month of my life obsessed with reaching 1,000 essays on Substack.

One thousand. A nice, round, psychologically satisfying number. I set out to reach it by my one-year anniversary on May 15, 2026 — exactly one year after I published my first essay — and I did, because I am a retired Canadian businessman with a husky, a husband from Bhutan, and apparently no hobbies that do not involve typing.

I hit 1,000 on Friday, May 15. I felt a surge of accomplishment. I told people about it. I may have mentioned it more than once. I definitely mentioned it more than once. I am mentioning it right now. The number had become important to me — a marker of output, of commitment, of something that resembled productivity even if it was actually just a man at a keyboard who had forgotten how to stop.

Then I woke up the next morning and it was 1,001. And the day after that, 1,002. And by the time I sat down to write this essay — ten days into my second year — the count was 1,066.

Yes this is essay 1,066.

And here is where the numbers start doing things I did not ask them to do.

I am, ancestrally speaking, British. My roots are in the United Kingdom — the country that, for better or worse, exported its language, its legal system, its parliamentary model, and its cooking to approximately a quarter of the planet, and was forgiven for all of it except the cooking.

In 1066, William the Conqueror crossed the English Channel from Normandy, landed at Pevensey, marched to Hastings, and defeated King Harold II — who, in addition to losing the battle, took an arrow to the eye, which is the medieval equivalent of losing the news cycle.

William’s victory changed everything. The language. The law. The class structure. The architecture. The relationship between crown and country. Everything that makes England England — and by extension, everything that makes Canada Canada, and everything that makes America America, including the constitutional system that Donald Trump is currently using to sue himself for $10 billion — traces back, in one way or another, to what happened at Hastings in 1066.

And I have now written 1,066 essays.

This is, I recognize, a coincidence. It is not a sign. It is not destiny. It is not the universe telling me that I am the literary equivalent of William the Conqueror. William had an army. I have a Substack. William conquered England. I have conquered the attention span of approximately 50 subscribers, some of whom, I suspect, have not actually read all 1,066 essays but are too polite to unsubscribe.

But numbers do this. They acquire significance whether you want them to or not. 1,000 felt like an achievement. 1,066 feels like a historical allusion I didn’t intend but cannot ignore. By this logic, 1,215 will feel like the Magna Carta. 1,492 will feel like Columbus. And 1,776 will feel like — well, $1.776 billion, apparently, because that is what the number means now. Donald Trump took 1776 — the founding year of the American republic — and turned it into the price tag on a slush fund for insurrectionists.

He colonized the number. He does that.

Donald Trump has always understood something about numbers that the rest of us pretend not to know: they are feelings disguised as facts.

When he says his building is 68 stories, but it’s actually 58, he is not lying. He is feeling. The building feels like 68 stories. It feels tall. It feels like the tallest. And if the number on the elevator says 68, then it is 68, because the number is not a measurement. It is a brand.

When he says he is worth $10 billion, and Forbes says it’s $2.5 billion, he is not mistaken. He is narrating. The $10 billion is the story he tells himself about himself, and the story is more real to him than the audit. The audit is just a number. His number is an identity.

When he says the crowd was the biggest, the ratings were the highest, the deal was the greatest, the wall will be the tallest — he is not describing reality. He is constructing it. One number at a time. Bigger is better. The biggest is the best. And if the actual number is smaller than the claimed number, the solution is not to correct the claim. The solution is to repeat it louder, more often, until the repetition becomes the record.

$10 billion in a lawsuit against himself. $1.776 billion in a slush fund. $400 million for a ballroom. 90,000 square feet. 100-foot granite helipad. 34 felony convictions. Zero punishment.

The man speaks in numbers the way other people speak in sentences. And every number is a superlative. Every number is the biggest, the most, the first, the best. There is no such thing as a small Trump number, although Stormy may disagree. Even his losses are the biggest losses — $916 million in a single year, which he then converted into decades of tax avoidance, because even his failures have to be record-setting.

I understand this impulse better than I would like to admit. I spent a month overlapping series, accelerating output, pushing deadlines — all to reach 1,000 by my anniversary. I was, in my own modest Canadian way, doing the Trump thing. Making the number matter. Making the milestone real by forcing reality to conform to the calendar.

The difference — and I will cling to this difference like a life raft — is that my 1,000 essays actually exist. You can read them. They are there. They contain words, arranged in sentences, supported by sources, and published in sequence. Trump’s $10 billion valuation exists only in the space between his ego and his accountant’s migraine.

But the impulse? The impulse is the same. Numbers are mirrors. We see in them what we need to see.

Here is the thing about 1,066 essays that I did not expect.

The number forced me to look backward. And looking backward, I discovered something about myself that I had not fully understood: I love history.

I did not know this about myself until relatively recently. I suspect the seed was planted decades ago, during my first marriage, by a woman who was a history major and who could not visit a city without visiting its past. We walked the streets Charles Dickens walked in London. We stopped at his house — the one where he wrote. We popped into Ye Olde Curiosity Shoppe — yes, the original one. She saw history everywhere, in everything, and her seeing it taught me to see it too.

It rubbed off. These things do. And so history became another number — another thing to count, another series to add to the total, another reason the husky stopped making eye contact.

And so, somewhere in the middle of writing 1,066 essays about the present, I started writing about the past. China — five thousand years of it. Taiwan — the beautiful problem. Cuba — five hundred and thirty years of empire and resistance. Afghanistan — the graveyard of every army that thought it was different. Israel and Palestine — the original sin and its compound interest. Canada — because someone has to. The history of American healthcare, American debt and the history of political money. Series after series, reaching backward to explain why the present looks the way it does.

The Afghanistan series is the one I’d point you to if you wanted proof that history matters. Every empire that entered Afghanistan believed it would be different. The British believed it. The Soviets believed it. The Americans believed it. They all had bigger numbers — more troops, more money, more technology, more confidence. And they all left the same way: slowly, then suddenly, with the numbers trailing behind them like a receipt for a meal nobody enjoyed.

Numbers told them they were winning. History told them they were not. They believed the numbers.

Does learning from the past help us make decisions in the present to shape our future? I used to think this was a rhetorical question. Now I think it’s the only question. And the answer, depressingly, is: it should, but it usually doesn’t, because the people making the decisions are too busy counting.

Now, about William.

Canada has a holiday called Victoria Day. It celebrates Queen Victoria, who ruled the British Empire for sixty-three years, presided over the largest territorial expansion in human history, and gave her name to an era, a city in British Columbia, a lake in Africa, a train station, a sponge cake, and a secret that has nothing to do with her.

Victoria Day falls on the Monday before May 25. It is, in practice, the unofficial start of summer. Canadians use it to open cottages, plant gardens, and set off fireworks — activities that have nothing whatsoever to do with Queen Victoria but that she would, one suspects, have disapproved of, being a woman who was not famously amused.

I would like to propose a replacement: William Day.

Not William Shakespeare. Not William Lyon Mackenzie King. William the Conqueror. The man who, in 1066, crossed a body of water, landed on a foreign shore, defeated the existing power structure, and reshaped a civilization.

The parallels to the current moment are left as an exercise for the reader. Another man has crossed a line, seized power, and is reshaping the structure of governance according to his personal preferences. Whether this constitutes conquest or merely renovation depends on whether you are in the ballroom or outside it.

William the Conqueror, at least, built castles that are still standing. The current occupant of the Oval Office is building a ballroom out of impenetrable glass, which is not the same thing, though he insists it is better.

I have written 1,066 essays. William conquered England. Donald Trump conquered the number 1776. Victoria is still on the holiday calendar. And numbers, in the end, mean whatever we decide they mean — which is to say, everything and nothing, depending on who is counting and what they are trying to prove.

I was trying to prove I could write 1,000 essays in a year. I did. Then I wrote 66 more in ten days, because apparently I have a problem.

Donald Trump is trying to prove he is worth $10 billion, that his crowd was the biggest, that his building is the tallest, and that $1.776 billion is the appropriate price for rewriting the history of an insurrection.

The difference is that my numbers are real and his are aspirational.

The similarity is that neither of us can stop counting.

A BRIEF NUMERICAL ADDENDUM

The good news, for my readers, my husband, and my husky — all of whom have shown remarkable patience — is that the pace will slow. Several series have concluded. The Trump Accountability Project ended at 47 documents plus an addendum, which is either comprehensive or obsessive, depending on your perspective. The Charlie Kirk Debates wrapped after 35 virtual debates and 70 essays, in which I took on the task of doing what Charlie loved doing best — debating — and proving him wrong, which was not as difficult as he made it look on television. Other series — The Asimov Gap, The Great Energy Challenge, the Iran war diary — will continue as events warrant.

The output will thin. I am forecasting lower numbers.

Donald Trump would never say that. Donald Trump would say the numbers are going to be even bigger. The biggest numbers anyone has ever seen. Tremendous numbers.

I am not Donald Trump. My numbers will be smaller. They will be accurate. And they will, God willing, continue to be real.

1,066 today. William the Conqueror would be proud. Or confused. Probably confused.

But it’s a good number. It feels important.

And I have something in common with Donald Trump: neither of us is going to stop counting.

A POSTSCRIPT, 14 OCTOBER 2026

Today is the 960th anniversary of Hastings. 960 is not a round number, which has never stopped me before.

When I wrote this essay in May, the Bayeux Tapestry was still in Normandy. It isn’t now. France has lent it to the British Museum until 11 July 2027. It is the first time it has been in England since it was stitched, almost certainly by English hands, to commemorate an English defeat. Britain borrowing back the souvenir of its own conquest is the most British thing to happen since the invention of the queue, and the British are, naturally, queueing for it. It is worth remembering what they’re queueing for: some 70 metres of the winner’s approved version of events. William had a tapestry. Others have Truth Social.

The fever has reached the battlefield. As Alex Marshall reported in the New York Times on 14 October 2026, roughly 1,000 re-enactors gathered at Battle, East Sussex, on the slope where Harold made his stand. Some 13,000 spectators came to watch over two days. (Numbers, again.)

Harold was played by a British Army engineer. Before the battle he gave his troops a rousing speech, asking them to believe in him and believe in “our England.” He promised to drive the foreigners back into the sea, and the crowd chanted his name like a stadium. Then one of his aides asked whether anyone wanted a selfie with the king. A rally, a chant, a promise about foreigners, and a photo line afterwards. I did not make this up. I would not have needed to.

William was played by a German soldier stationed in France: a German, playing a Norman, invading England. Europe has had worse arrangements, most of them in the twentieth century.

The Normans won, as they do every year. The crowd booed, as it does every year. I note that nobody in Battle chanted William’s name, so my William Day proposal may need work in East Sussex.

But here is the part I would like Mr. Trump to notice. Nobody demanded a recount. Nobody claimed the arrow was rigged, or that the shield wall had been infiltrated by fake Normans, or that Harold actually won in a landslide Battle Abbey refused to certify. Nine hundred and sixty years on, the English still don’t like the result. They accept it anyway. That is what a result is.

One recent medieval-studies graduate, holding a nearly empty bottle of mead, pointed out the re-enactment’s one advantage over the British Museum: you’re allowed to drink at it. William would have approved. Harold, given the day he’d had, would have needed it.
