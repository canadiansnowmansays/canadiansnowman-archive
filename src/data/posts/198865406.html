THE FOUNDING FRAUD / $1.776 Billion — The Nose That Pays

Stephen Gordon aka CanadianSnowman, May 31, 2026

Previously on The Pinocchio Presidency: Our hero’s nose had grown from investment fantasies (Part 1, 47 feet), metastasized through 18 distinct falsehoods on national television (Part 2, 94 feet), and learned to profit from the stock market by firing the scorekeeper (Part 3, 94 feet, 6 inches). Today, it discovers its true purpose.

Prologue: The Nose Finds Its Calling

For sixteen months, the nose had been growing. Investment trillions, televised falsehoods, manipulated job numbers — each lie added its inch, its foot, its absurd extension into the airspace of American credulity. The nose was a monument. A medical textbook. A tourist attraction.

But it was also, let’s be honest, an inconvenience.

It knocked over senators. It required structural reinforcement. It had its own ZIP code and, as of February, its own weather system. The maintenance alone cost a fortune — Nose Wranglers don’t come cheap, and the Nose Navigator had unionized.

And then, on Monday, May 19, 2026, someone in the workshop at Mar-a-Lago had a revelation so elegant, so brazen, so perfectly suited to the age of shamelessness that the cosmic accountant herself — the Blue Fairy’s CPA — had to sit down:

What if the nose wasn’t a bug? What if it was a feature?

What if, instead of growing from lies, the nose could dispense from them? What if all that hollow, inflated, hot-air-filled infrastructure could be repurposed — not as a monument to mendacity, but as a pipeline?

A pipeline for $1.776 billion of taxpayer money. Flowing directly to the people who helped the nose grow in the first place.

The cosmic accountant wept.

The nose quivered with purpose.

The founding fathers — every last powdered wig — spun in their graves.

Because someone, in a moment of audacity so towering it made the investment trillions look like pocket change, had set the amount at exactly $1,776,000,000. One billion, seven hundred and seventy-six million dollars. Not $1.8 billion. Not $1.75 billion. One-point-seven-seven-six.

Seventeen seventy-six.

The year of the Declaration of Independence. The year Americans told a king that his power had limits. The year the idea of accountability was literally signed into existence with a quill pen and a death wish.

And now, 250 years later, a president had stamped that sacred number on a fund designed to pay the people who stormed the Capitol to keep him in power.

If irony were a currency, the fund wouldn’t need the Judgment Fund at all.

Chapter One: The Man Who Sued Himself (And Won)

Let’s start with the mechanics, because the mechanics are where the satire writes itself.

In January 2026, Donald J. Trump — President of the United States — filed a $10 billion lawsuit against the Internal Revenue Service and the Department of the Treasury. These are agencies of the executive branch. The executive branch is run by the President of the United States. The President of the United States is Donald J. Trump.

Donald J. Trump sued Donald J. Trump for $10 billion.

Now, Donald Trump has been told to go fuck himself many times over a long and colorful career. So the concept of doing something to himself was not entirely foreign. But somewhere between the filing and the settlement, he appears to have realized that fucking the taxpayers served a better purpose.

The nose, had it been a law student, would have flagged this immediately. You cannot sue yourself. This is not a matter of legal nuance — it is the kind of thing they teach in the first week of law school, right after “don’t eat the evidence” and “the judge is not your therapist.”

And yet.

The case was settled on Monday. Not by a court — no judge reviewed the terms, no jury weighed the evidence, no independent arbiter determined whether the claims had merit. It was settled by an agreement between the president’s personal lawyers and the government’s lawyers, who are also, in a plot twist that would embarrass a soap opera, working for the president.

Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche — who was, until recently, Donald Trump’s personal criminal defense attorney in the classified documents case — sat across the table from the president’s current personal lawyers and hammered out a deal. His former client on one side, his current boss on the other, and both of them being the same person.

The nose grew six inches on the word “settlement” alone.

The terms were exquisite. Trump, his two elder sons, and the Trump Organization would receive no money. (Pause for applause.) They would receive a “formal apology” from the government. (Resume applause, but slower.) They would also be permanently shielded from any prosecution or tax claims related to their returns. A supplemental agreement disclosed on Tuesday revealed that the IRS would be barred from auditing Trump’s past tax returns — an arrangement that, according to the New York Times’ analysis of his earlier filings, could save the president approximately $100 million from an adverse audit ruling.

No money. Just an apology and a hundred-million-dollar tax shield. And $1.776 billion for his friends.

The nose, calculating the net benefit, grew another foot. Then it bent toward the Treasury Department, as if sniffing for the Judgment Fund.

Chapter Two: The Judgment Fund (Or: Congress’s Wallet, Picked Clean)

The Judgment Fund is one of those government mechanisms that exists precisely because nobody pays attention to it. Created by Congress in 1956, it is a permanently appropriated Treasury account used to pay court judgments and legal settlements against the federal government. It is, in essence, the government’s checkbook for losing lawsuits — a sensible mechanism for a nation that believes in the rule of law.

The key word in that description is court. The Judgment Fund pays judgments — decisions rendered by judges after adversarial proceedings in which both sides present evidence and an independent arbiter determines fault. It also pays settlements — but historically, these have been settlements of actual lawsuits, filed by actual plaintiffs, with actual legal claims, reviewed by actual courts.

The Anti-Weaponization Fund has none of this. No class action preceded it. No laws were alleged to have been violated. No court reviewed the terms. The eligibility criteria are open to, in Blanche’s own words, “almost anyone alleging ‘weaponization’ or ‘lawfare.’” The maximum payout is unspecified. The time frame is undefined. The fund can spend part of its own $1.776 billion on itself — staff, travel, facilities — with no cap. And it ends in December 2028, just in time for the next election cycle, which is surely a coincidence in the way that rain at a picnic is surely a coincidence when someone has been seeding clouds all morning.

Adam Zimmerman, a law professor at USC who has written extensively about mass tort litigation, put it with the kind of precision that makes noses nervous: “The president is suing himself and compensating other people for legal claims that have not been identified from people that we don’t know. We just haven’t seen anything like that.”

Paul Figley, formerly of the Justice Department’s civil division, agreed: “The power of the purse is one of the key checks and balances that we have. The way it is now, the executive branch has a little area where it has its own source of money, and it shouldn’t.”

The nose, now functioning as an aqueduct, began carrying its first load. Not water — money. Taxpayer money, flowing from the Treasury through 96 feet of hollow, lie-inflated infrastructure, directly into the pockets of people whose primary qualification was loyalty to the man who built the nose in the first place.

The founding fathers, still spinning, upgraded to centrifugal force.

Chapter Three: The Defense (Or: When the Nose Discovers It Can Grow and Dispense Simultaneously)

Now, you might think that a fund this brazen would be defended with silence — the bureaucratic equivalent of a mob boss saying “nice fund, shame if someone asked questions about it.” But this is the Pinocchio Presidency. Silence is not the house style. Lies are.

And so, within days of the fund’s announcement, the president, the vice president, and the acting attorney general mounted a defense built on claims so inaccurate that the nose — which had been busy converting itself from monument to pipeline — was forced to multitask. It could funnel money and grow at the same time. Nature finds a way.

Lie the First: “There’s precedent for this.”

Todd Blanche, appearing before the Senate on Tuesday, conceded that the fund was “unusual.” This was like the captain of the Hindenburg conceding that the landing was “atypical.” But Blanche had a precedent: the Keepseagle settlement, a $760 million fund from 2010.

The nose perked up. Precedent! Legal cover! Perhaps the aqueduct was legitimate after all.

It wasn’t. The comparison collapsed on contact with reality, like a sandcastle meeting the tide.

Keepseagle was a class-action lawsuit filed by Native American farmers in 1999 alleging systemic discrimination by the Agriculture Department. It took six years. It was approved by a court. It had specific eligibility criteria: Native American farmers who had applied for USDA loans between 1981 and 1999 and filed discrimination complaints during the same period. Maximum award: $250,000. Administrator: Epiq Systems, an independent legal software company. Every element was defined, bounded, and judicially supervised.

The Anti-Weaponization Fund has no class action, no court approval, no specific eligibility criteria, no defined time frame, no maximum payout, and no independent administrator. It has Todd Blanche picking the commissioners and Donald Trump holding the power to fire them.

The nose sagged. So much for precedent.

Asked whether there were other examples of a president suing his own government and resolving the case before court review, Blanche acknowledged there were not. “The underlying case is not the same,” he admitted — even as he insisted the structures were similar, like a man conceding that a bicycle and a Boeing 747 have different engines while maintaining they’re both basically the same because they have wheels.

The nose grew two feet. The Keepseagle comparison, had it been a patient in the Part 2 medical ward, would have been pronounced dead on arrival.

Lie the Second: “The president will not benefit financially.”

Vice President JD Vance offered this assurance with the serene confidence of a man who has never read a supplemental agreement. The deal, he noted, bars Trump, his two sons, and the Trump Organization from seeking compensation.

This is true. It is also approximately one-third of the relevant truth.

What the deal also does — disclosed in a supplement released on Tuesday, after the initial announcement, in the bureaucratic equivalent of hiding vegetables under the mashed potatoes — is bar the government from prosecuting or pursuing tax claims about Trump, his family, and his businesses. An adverse ruling from a pending audit could have cost the president roughly $100 million, according to the New York Times’ analysis of his earlier tax returns.

No money from the fund. Just a hundred-million-dollar tax shield, delivered as a side dish to a settlement he negotiated with himself.

Moreover, the deal does not preclude current or former members of the Trump administration from seeking compensation. Peter Navarro, who works in the White House. Waltine Nauta, who works in the Oval Office. Michael Caputo, who filed his $2.7 million claim before the press conference ended. The president will not benefit — but his orbit will, and in the Pinocchio Presidency, the orbit is the benefit. Every dollar paid to an ally is a dollar of loyalty purchased, a debt of gratitude created, a relationship cemented with taxpayer concrete.

The nose grew three feet. The left nostril — the one that had developed infrared heat signatures during the 60 Minutes Syndrome — flared with the particular warmth of a financial misrepresentation. Somewhere, the health care worker in Akron from Part 3 was still making $16 an hour and still wondering why the system seemed to work for everyone except the people it was supposed to serve.

Lie the Third: “I don’t know about any promises to victims.”

This one arrived during Tuesday’s hearing, when Senator Chris Van Hollen raised the case of Andrew Paul Johnson — the pardoned January 6th rioter who molested children and promised one of his victims $10 million from anticipated government restitution.

Blanche’s response was a denial. He didn’t know about it. Hadn’t heard of it.

The police report filed in July 2025 by the Hernando County Sheriff’s Office documented the promise in detail. Johnson had told one of his victims he would receive $10 million in restitution money from what he cast as persecution stemming from January 6th, and promised the victims some of that money. This was not buried in a classified file or hidden in an obscure database. It had been reported by NPR, NBC News, and The Intercept months before Tuesday’s hearing.

It is true, as Blanche noted, that Johnson’s promise was made before the fund was announced. But discussion of compensation for January 6th rioters — among the rioters themselves and the Justice Department — long predated the announcement. Johnson himself had been posting on social media as early as May 2025 about rumors of restitution, citing contact with people who “sat in meeting with Ed” — Ed Martin, who ran the Justice Department’s weaponization working group. In another post, Johnson singled out Martin directly, asking for an update on the money: “There’s a lot of us that are really in bad positions because of the weaponization of the justice system used against us.”

The man who wrote that — the man who used the promise of that money to silence a child he was abusing — is now serving a life sentence. The fund he dreamed of now exists. The acting attorney general told the Senate he didn’t know about the connection.

The nose grew one foot. Reluctantly. Even the nose found this one difficult.

The Running Total

Six feet of growth in a single Senate hearing. The nose, which had entered this chapter at 96 feet, now stood at approximately 102 feet — having crossed the century mark for the first time, propelled past the milestone not by the grand investment fantasies of Part 1 or the machine-gun falsehoods of Part 2, but by the quiet, bureaucratic lies told in defense of a fund that shouldn’t exist.

One hundred and two feet. Longer than a blue whale. Taller than a ten-story building. The distance from home plate to first base, plus change.

The nose paused, as if recognizing the milestone. Then it resumed dispensing.

Chapter Four: The Guest List (Or: A Rogues’ Gallery in Formal Attire)

Who, exactly, will collect from the Anti-Weaponization Fund?

The administration says there are no “partisan requirements.” Anyone who suffered “weaponization and lawfare” can apply. Democrats are welcome. Independents too. The DOJ’s one-page memo to Republican senators explicitly states: “There is no partisan restriction.”

This is technically true in the way that a country club with a $10 million membership fee has no racial restriction. The door is open. The threshold is designed to ensure that only the right people walk through it.

Let’s examine the guest list that was already forming before the ink was dry.

Michael R. Caputo, a former HHS spokesman during the first Trump administration, posted a letter on social media requesting $2.7 million within hours of the fund’s announcement. Hours. The paint wasn’t dry and the line was forming.

Peter Navarro, now a senior White House trade adviser, was convicted of contempt of Congress in 2023 for refusing to comply with the January 6th Committee’s subpoena. He served four months in federal prison. Trump has personally characterized him as a victim of “weaponization.” Navarro currently works in the White House. He could, conceivably, walk from his office to the commission’s office to collect his check, assuming the nose doesn’t block the hallway.

Waltine Nauta, Trump’s former personal aide who was charged in the classified documents investigation and later listed as the director of Oval Office operations, is another potential claimant. Trump has said the Justice Department tried to “destroy” Nauta’s life — the same Justice Department now administering the fund that would compensate him.

And then there are the 1,500 pardoned January 6th defendants.

The nose twitched. Not with growth — with anticipation.

Chapter Five: The Pedophile’s Promise

I need to stop being funny for a moment, because what follows is not funny.

You met Andrew Paul Johnson in Chapter Three, where his name appeared in the context of Todd Blanche’s denial at a Senate hearing — a lie about a lie about a promise made to a child. But the hearing exchange was a thirty-second clip. The story behind it deserves more than thirty seconds. The child who lived it certainly got more.

Johnson was a Florida handyman. He entered the Capitol on January 6th through a broken window and cursed at police officers. Nonviolent charges. He pleaded guilty in 2024, received a year in prison and a $2,336 fine. On January 20, 2025, Donald Trump pardoned him along with roughly 1,500 other defendants. A clean slate. A fresh start.

What Johnson did with that fresh start was molest two children.

The Hernando County Sheriff’s Office began investigating in July 2025 after a mother found inappropriate messages between her son and Johnson on Discord. The child — eleven years old — told deputies that Johnson had molested him three times beginning in April 2024. A second victim, between 12 and 16, was also identified. The abuse took place “over a many-month span.”

When the eleven-year-old confronted Johnson, Johnson apologized and told the child not to tell anyone “since he would get in trouble.” When that didn’t work, he tried money. He told the child he was entitled to $10 million from the Trump administration as restitution for his January 6th arrest, and that he would share the money with the victim in his will.

A pardoned Capitol rioter sexually abused a child and attempted to buy the child’s silence with money he expected to receive from the government for storming the Capitol.

In February 2026, a Florida jury convicted Johnson of five charges. In March, he was sentenced to life in prison.

The fund he dreamed of — the one he dangled before a child to keep him quiet — now exists. It has $1.776 billion. And the acting attorney general told the United States Senate, under oath, that he was unaware of the connection.

The nose did not grow. For the first time in sixteen months, the nose had nothing to add. The truth was worse than anything it could manufacture.

Chapter Six: The Officers Who Fought Back (While the Nose Watched)

On Wednesday, May 21, two police officers filed a federal lawsuit to block the Anti-Weaponization Fund.

Harry Dunn, a former Capitol Police officer. Daniel Hodges, a current officer with the D.C. Metropolitan Police Department. Both were at the Capitol on January 6, 2021. Both fought to defend it. Hodges was the officer famously crushed in a doorway by the mob, screaming in pain as rioters pressed forward. Dunn was called a racial slur by insurrectionists as he tried to hold the line.

Their lawsuit called the fund “the most brazen act of presidential corruption this century.” It invoked the Fourteenth Amendment — the one ratified after the Civil War, the one that says neither the United States nor any state “shall assume or pay any debt or obligation incurred in aid of insurrection or rebellion against the United States.”

The Fourteenth Amendment. Written by men who had just watched 600,000 Americans die because some people believed the rules didn’t apply to them. Invoked now because some people still do.

The lawsuit argued that money from the fund would go to the Proud Boys and other paramilitary organizations, allowing them to “arm and equip themselves.” It noted that the officers continue to receive death threats from the very people who stand to benefit.

“The Anti-Weaponization Fund will both compensate and empower the very people making those threats,” the complaint reads. “Militias like the Proud Boys will use money from the Fund to arm and equip themselves.”

More than 140 police officers were injured defending the Capitol on January 6th. One hundred and forty officers. Beaten with flagpoles, sprayed with bear mace, crushed in doorways, tased, punched, kicked. Four officers who responded that day later died by suicide.

And the fund set at $1,776,000,000 — the number chosen to invoke the founding of American democracy — may pay the people who did the beating.

The nose, for once, hung its head.

Chapter Seven: The Republican Revolt (Or: The Bridge Too Far They Keep Crossing)

Here is where the story takes its most improbable turn, and I say that as a man who has spent three essays describing a sentient, weather-system-generating, K-shaped proboscis.

The Republicans revolted.

Not Democrats. Not the press. Not the usual coalition of fact-checkers and constitutional scholars and concerned citizens who have been sounding alarms since 2016 with the success rate of a car alarm in a Walmart parking lot.

Republicans.

On Thursday, May 22 — today, as I write — Senate Republicans abandoned plans to vote on a $72 billion immigration enforcement package. The bill was supposed to be the centerpiece of the GOP’s legislative agenda. It would fund ICE and Customs and Border Protection through the end of Trump’s presidency. Senate Majority Leader John Thune had been whipping votes for weeks. The Memorial Day recess loomed. The president had set a June 1 deadline.

And then the Anti-Weaponization Fund landed on the bill like a piano falling from a cartoon cliff.

The $1.776 billion fund and a separate $1 billion request for Secret Service security at the White House — including infrastructure for Trump’s $200-million-turned-$300-million-turned-$400-million privately funded ballroom — had been attached to the reconciliation package. Republican senators, who had been willing to swallow an extraordinary amount of policy and principle over the past sixteen months, looked at this particular meal and gagged.

Senator Susan Collins, the top Senate appropriator and a woman who has elevated the art of furrowed-brow concern to Olympic levels, told CNN: “I do not support the weaponization fund as it has been described. I do not believe individuals that were convicted of violence against police officers on Jan. 6 should be entitled to reimbursement of their legal fees.”

Senator Thom Tillis threatened to vote against the entire reconciliation bill if it included the fund, calling the additions “gimmicks that are coming in at the 11th hour.”

Dick Durbin, the Illinois Democrat, allowed himself a moment: “Is it possible, on May 21, 2026, the Republicans finally found an ethical bridge too far?”

One GOP senator — unnamed, but quotable — described the fund as a “payout pot for punks.”

The nose, sensing danger, vibrated at a frequency previously associated with seismic activity. Todd Blanche was dispatched from a planned press conference to Capitol Hill to salvage the situation. He arrived with a one-page memo. He left with the full force of Republican angst attached to his briefcase like a barnacle — the nautical kind, not the kind that sprouted on the nose during Part 2.

Hardly any senators spoke up to defend the fund during Blanche’s private meeting. The vote was postponed. Senators left for Memorial Day recess. The immigration bill — the actual legislative priority — lay in the wreckage.

The nose had accomplished something unprecedented: it had united both parties. Against itself.

Chapter Eight: The Ballroom in the Wreckage

A brief word about the other item that helped sink the bill, because it deserves its own paragraph in the annals of chutzpah.

One billion dollars for Secret Service security upgrades related to the White House East Wing Modernization Project. The East Wing Modernization Project is the polite name for the demolition of the historic East Wing — first built in 1902, enlarged under FDR — to make way for a 90,000-square-foot ballroom. The ballroom was initially announced at $200 million, privately funded. Then it became $300 million. Then $400 million. Now the “security” component alone required a billion dollars of taxpayer money, tucked into an immigration bill like a flask in a churchgoer’s coat.

The ballroom will seat 650 guests, a significant upgrade from the East Room’s 200-person capacity. Trump, who owns a grand ballroom at Mar-a-Lago with glittering chandeliers and gold flourishes, felt the People’s House needed something similar.

The nose, which has been measuring presidential ambitions in feet since Part 1, noted that the ballroom’s 90,000 square feet would comfortably accommodate approximately 882 noses laid end to end. It chose not to comment further, sensing that even satire has its limits.

The billion-dollar ballroom request was stripped from the bill. But the damage was done. The immigration enforcement package — the one thing Republicans needed to pass before recess — was dead on the floor, killed by a slush fund and a dance hall.

Somewhere, a founding father set down his quill and stared.

Chapter Nine: The $1,776 Problem

Let’s return to that number. $1,776,000,000.

I’ve been staring at it for three days now, and I still can’t decide whether it’s the most cynical thing I’ve ever seen or the most revealing. Perhaps both. Perhaps that’s the point.

Though there is a third possibility, and it’s the one the nose finds most plausible: he got confused.

There are 1,760 yards in a mile. Sixteen yards short of 1,776. And if you’ve spent your career in real estate — a profession where distances are measured in feet, lots in acres, and truth in whatever unit moves the deal forward — it’s not hard to imagine a moment in the workshop where someone said “make it patriotic” and someone else, reaching for the founding of the republic, grabbed the nearest round number from the only measurement system they’d ever truly loved.

The Declaration of Independence or a unit of linear measurement? In the Pinocchio Presidency, the distinction has never mattered less. The man who turned $20 trillion into $8.8 trillion, who claimed grocery prices were “underground,” who told 18 lies in 80 minutes on national television, may have accidentally honored the mile instead of the Enlightenment. And nobody in the workshop caught it — or if they did, they understood that in this administration, close enough is government work.

The nose, which is itself measured in feet, appreciated the unit-of-measurement solidarity. It also noted, with the quiet satisfaction of a creature that has been tracking numerical fraud since Part 1, that the man who can’t tell a trillion from a billion probably can’t tell a yard from a year either.

But let’s give him the benefit of the doubt. Let’s assume it was deliberate. Let’s assume someone in that room actually meant to invoke 1776.

Seventeen seventy-six. The year fifty-six men signed a document that began with “We hold these truths to be self-evident” and ended with them pledging their lives, their fortunes, and their sacred honor. Twenty-four of them were lawyers. Twelve were merchants. The youngest was 26. The oldest was 70. Several would die in poverty. One had his home burned by the British. Another was captured and tortured.

They signed because they believed — with a conviction so total it bordered on foolishness — that no person should wield unchecked power over another. That the governed had the right to check the governor. That public money belonged to the public. That accountability was not optional but foundational.

Two hundred and fifty years later, that number adorns a fund in which a president sued his own government, settled with his own appointee, shielded himself from tax audits, and directed $1.776 billion to a commission whose members he can fire at will, using criteria so vague that — in the words of one law professor — “we don’t even know what the legal claims are from people that we don’t know.”

The founding fathers didn’t just create a government. They created a set of architectural drawings — a blueprint for how power should flow and, more importantly, how it shouldn’t. The power of the purse was given to Congress, not the president. The Judgment Fund was created to pay for lawsuits the government lost, not for grievances the president invented. The executive branch was designed to be checked, not to be its own plaintiff, defendant, judge, and beneficiary simultaneously.

The nose, which has been many things over the course of this series — a growth chart, a medical ward, a stock portfolio, a weather system — today became something new: a eulogy.

Not for the republic. Not yet. Perhaps not ever, if enough people read the number on the fund and remember what it’s supposed to mean.

But a eulogy for the idea that shame has limits. That audacity has a ceiling. That you can’t stamp the most sacred date in American history on a slush fund for insurrectionists and expect the country to nod along.

The Republicans, to their credit — a phrase I type with visible strain — refused to nod. For one week, on this one issue, the architectural drawing held.

The nose noted the precedent. It also noted that Memorial Day recess is only a week long.

Chapter Ten: A Note from the 49th Parallel

I write these words from Toronto, where it is a long weekend and the lilacs are blooming along the lakeshore. My husky, Dior, is asleep on the couch, unbothered by constitutional crises south of the border. She has the right idea.

But I can’t look away, and not just because the nose is visible from here. (It is. On a clear day, you can see it from the CN Tower. The observation deck sells commemorative binoculars.)

I can’t look away because what happened this week isn’t just an American story. It’s a test case. A proof of concept. A demonstration project for every democracy watching to see what happens when the guardrails are not just tested but instrumentalized — turned into tools for the very consolidation of power they were designed to prevent.

The Judgment Fund was a guardrail. The independence of the Attorney General was a guardrail. The separation between a president’s personal legal interests and the government’s was a guardrail. The principle that taxpayer money requires congressional appropriation was a guardrail. All of them, in a single settlement agreement, converted from barriers into highways.

Up here in Canada, we have our own problems — our own populist temptations, our own institutional erosion, our own moments where someone in power eyes a guardrail and wonders if it’s really load-bearing. But we haven’t yet reached the point where the prime minister sues his own government for $10 billion, settles for a formal apology and a tax shield, and directs $1.776 billion to a commission he controls to pay people who attacked the seat of democracy on his behalf.

Yet.

The “yet” is the part that keeps me writing.

Epilogue: The Nose at Rest (The Republic, Less So)

The nose, as of this writing, measures approximately 102 feet. It grew nearly eight feet this week — modest by Part 1 standards, when investment fantasies added yardage by the hour, but significant for a different reason. The growth came not from the grand fabrications that built the infrastructure, but from the smaller, more bureaucratic lies told in its defense: a false precedent, a misleading denial of financial benefit, a claimed ignorance of a police report that had been national news for months. The fund itself was the invoice for lies already told. The defense of the fund required new ones.

But something else changed in the nose’s architecture this week. For sixteen months, it had grown outward — longer, wider, more elaborate, more medically complex. This week, for the first time, it grew inward. It hollowed. It became a conduit. The infrastructure of falsehood, built lie by lie since January 2025, finally revealed its engineering purpose: not to impress, but to transport.

Money in. Loyalty out. Taxpayer dollars flowing through 102 feet of hollow narrative, from the Treasury to the commission to the claimants to the people who stormed the Capitol, beat the police, and went home expecting a check.

The founding fathers, I think, would recognize the structure. They’d seen something like it before — not in Philadelphia, but in London. They called it the Crown. They wrote a document about it. They pledged their lives, their fortunes, and their sacred honor to dismantle it.

They charged $0 for the privilege.

The current administration charges $1,776,000,000. Or possibly $1,760,000,000, plus sixteen million for rounding. In this workshop, the mile and the Enlightenment are equally negotiable.

The nose grows on.
