INT. HIGH-STAKES ROOM — CONTINUOUS. A single table under a low light. The PIT BOSS, in a red tie and an ill-fitting suit, leans on the rail. Across from him, a DEALER shuffles a deck that never seems to run out of new rules. At the table, one chair sits empty — pushed back, as though someone recently stood up from it.

PIT BOSS: Where’d he go?

DEALER: Walked, boss. Friday night. Said the fine print changed on him mid-hand.

PIT BOSS: The fine print always changes mid-hand. That’s the game. You don’t walk from the game, you play the game.

DEALER: He said something about trucks. Medium-duty. Heavy-duty. Said the deal was on the table, inches from done, and then it wasn’t the deal anymore.

PIT BOSS: That’s not what killed it and you know it.

DEALER: No, boss. There was the language thing too. He wanted to run the table in English and French both. Said it’s how he talks at every table, always has, and he wasn’t about to change it for us.

PIT BOSS: We run one language at this table. That’s not negotiable. You start dealing two languages, next thing you know everybody wants their own rules.

DEALER: He brought up the showroom too. Said the house books the same Quebec singer every year — the big voice — and still won’t let her do a single number in French. Full house, half the room singing along under their breath in a language they’re not allowed to hear on stage. Said that one stuck with him more than the trucks did.

PIT BOSS: The showroom runs in English. Always has. That’s what sells.

DEALER: He said the room’s half French. Said it’s not really about what sells. Said it felt like the house forgot who half the audience was.

DEALER: And then there was the one-table rule. He didn’t love that either. Said if he wants to sit down at somebody else’s table — Europe’s, Asia’s, whoever’s dealing — that’s his business, not ours.

PIT BOSS: It becomes our business the second he’s sitting at my table. You don’t get to play both sides of the room. One table, one house, one set of chips. He knew the rule going in.

DEALER: He said he didn’t sign up for it going in. Said it got added.

PIT BOSS: It’s still the deal. It’s just a better deal. For me.

DEALER: For you.

PIT BOSS: That’s how the house explains “better.” (calling out toward the empty chair) Hey! HEY. You walk away from twenty-five, I go to fifty. That’s not a threat, that’s arithmetic. January first. Cars, trucks, big ones, small ones, parts, steel — all of it, straight to fifty percent. You had a seat. You had a hand. You folded on the turn.

DEALER: He can’t hear you, boss, he’s in Ottawa.

PIT BOSS: He’ll hear about it. Everybody hears about it. I put it out on the feed myself, this morning. (reciting from memory, proudly) “Canada will be treated like a state no longer.” Good line. Punchy. I workshopped that one.

DEALER: You also said we don’t need them.

PIT BOSS: We don’t! They do ninety-five percent of their business with us. We do — what, the opposite. That’s not me being tough, that’s just the math on the table. I’m the house. The house doesn’t need any one player. The house needs the room full.

DEALER: The room’s getting a little thin, boss.

PIT BOSS: The room is never empty. There’s always a new table. (He straightens the empty chair, almost tenderly, then flips it back on two legs, casual, dismissive.) You know what gets me? Sixty billion. That’s what I’m owed at this table. Sixty billion dollars of deficit, sitting right there on the felt, and he wants to talk to me about trucks.

DEALER: Is it sixty billion, boss?

PIT BOSS: It’s sixty billion if I say it’s sixty billion. That’s also the house rules.

(A FLOOR MANAGER enters, checking a clipboard.)

FLOOR MANAGER: Boss, word from upstairs. The player’s saying he’s coming back to the room. Not to your table. A different table. Says he’s bringing dollar-for-dollar chips. September eighth.

PIT BOSS: Dollar-for-dollar. Cute. What’s he betting with, maple syrup?

FLOOR MANAGER: Steel, lumber, some consumer goods. Matched, dollar for dollar, against whatever we put down.

PIT BOSS: (unbothered, waving a hand) Let him match. I doubled my number before he even sat down to count his chips. That’s not a counter-bet, that’s just — following. I’m not playing his game. He’s playing mine, badly, one table over.

DEALER: For what it’s worth, boss, the pit’s noticed. Word is some of the regulars — the ones who buy the cars, the parts guys — they’re not thrilled watching the number double on a Monday with no warning.

PIT BOSS: They’ll adjust. Everybody adjusts. That’s the beautiful thing about a table with no fixed rules — nobody can ever say I broke one.

FLOOR MANAGER: There’s also a note here that the January date gives everybody about four months. Some folks are reading that as — leaving the door open. For another hand.

PIT BOSS: (a beat, then, almost gently) The door’s always open. I built this room with doors. Big doors. Beautiful doors. Doesn’t mean anybody’s walking through one without paying the toll on the way out.

DEALER: So it’s not really “double or nothing.”

PIT BOSS: No. See, that’s the mistake everybody makes. Double or nothing means both sides are betting something. (He gestures at the empty chair, at the felt, at the whole quiet room.) I already won the hand he walked away from. This isn’t a bet. This is just me telling you what the number’s going to be while I wait for the next player to sit down and pretend it’s still a game.

DEALER: And if nobody sits down?

PIT BOSS: (smiling, for the first time, at nothing in particular) Somebody always sits down. That’s the house’s whole business model.

FLOOR MANAGER: Boss, for what it’s worth — some of the old regulars are saying the house doesn’t usually raise the number on an empty table. Doesn’t look good to the folks still standing at the rail.

PIT BOSS: Let them look. I built this house. I own the felt, I own the cards, I own the number on the board, and I can change any one of the three whenever I feel like it. That’s not a rigged game, that’s just what a house is. You ever seen a casino lose, long-term? Once? In the history of casinos?

DEALER: (a beat too long, eyes somewhere else) No, boss.

PIT BOSS: That’s not luck. That’s design. The player thinks he’s betting against me. He’s not. He’s betting against the room, the lighting, the free drinks, the fact that the exit’s always a little further than it looks. I don’t need to win the hand. I just need the game to keep running long enough that eventually it wins itself.

DEALER: So what happens January first?

PIT BOSS: January first, the number goes to fifty, whether he’s sitting there or not. Win, lose, walk away, come back — doesn’t matter. The house doesn’t play hands. The house sets the odds and lets everybody else find out, eventually, that they were never really playing me. They were playing the table.

(He straightens his tie, satisfied, and turns to go. The DEALER waits until he’s nearly out of earshot.)

DEALER: (quietly, to the FLOOR MANAGER) He know this is the same house that’s gone under, what, three, four times now?

FLOOR MANAGER: Atlantic City. Nineties. Then again after that.

DEALER: And the one after that.

FLOOR MANAGER: Different name on the door each time. Same guy holding the cards.

DEALER: So the house doesn’t always win.

FLOOR MANAGER: (shrugging, already shuffling the next deck) No. But he’s never once been the one sitting at the table when it goes under. That’s the actual trick.

(Lights hold on the empty chair a beat too long before the scene cuts.)
