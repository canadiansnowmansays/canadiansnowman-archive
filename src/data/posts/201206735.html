What the Arduous March actually did to North Korean society — the informal markets it created, the loyalty it shattered, the information economy it accidentally built, and why Kim Jong-un’s brutal crackdown decades later was aimed precisely at the survival mechanisms his own people invented to outlive his father’s failures.

There is a photograph of the Korean Peninsula taken from the International Space Station, and it is one of the most eloquent political documents of the modern era.

South Korea blazes. Seoul is a supernova of white light, its glow spreading across the lower peninsula in the dense, overlapping circles of a fully electrified industrial civilization. City after city after city, all the way to the coast, all the way to the DMZ. North of that thin bright line — the security lights of the Demilitarized Zone, marking the most heavily fortified border on earth — there is almost nothing. A smudge of light where Pyongyang sits. A few faint specks in the surrounding countryside. And then darkness, stretching from the 38th parallel all the way to the Chinese border, broken only by the reflected glow of the country it has spent its entire existence refusing to become.

The average South Korean consumes roughly fourteen times more electricity per year than the average North Korean. In some rural provinces, the grid delivers power for only a few hours a day. The Juche Tower in the centre of Pyongyang — the monument to self-reliance, illuminated around the clock as a symbol of ideological conviction — burns through the night while the country it symbolizes sits in the dark.

The satellite image does not lie. It shows you, with the cold precision of orbital physics, the distance between the promise and the reality. Between the state that North Korea says it is and the state that it actually is. Between the mythology the Kims have spent three generations constructing and the electricity bills that tell a different story.

The darkness began, in earnest, in the 1990s. And what happened in that darkness — what people invented, what they discovered, what they stopped believing — is the story this essay tells.

The Collapse of the Social Contract

The North Korean state had, since its founding, operated on an implicit compact with its people. It was not a democracy. It was not a state that invited participation or tolerated dissent. But it offered something: the guarantee that the state would provide. Housing, employment, education, healthcare, and above all food — distributed through the Public Distribution System, the vast logistical apparatus that rationed grain to every household in the country according to their occupation and political status. The system was deeply unequal — the songbun hierarchy ensured that party officials and Pyongyang residents ate better than provincial workers and hostile-class families — but it was universal in its reach. Everyone received something. Everyone depended on the state.

When the Soviet Union collapsed and the subsidies and fuel shipments stopped, and when the floods and droughts of 1995 and 1996 destroyed the harvests, the Public Distribution System simply ceased to function in large parts of the country. Local authorities had no food to distribute. Provincial officials had no instructions from Pyongyang that could conjure grain from empty warehouses. The compact broke.

For the North Korean people, this was not merely a practical catastrophe. It was an ideological one. The state had told them their entire lives that self-reliance — juche — made North Korea invulnerable to the crises that plagued the capitalist world. The famine proved otherwise, with a finality that no amount of propaganda could fully paper over. Kim Jong-il eventually acknowledged that North Korea was experiencing an “Arduous March” — framing starvation as heroic revolutionary struggle — but the people living through it did not experience it as heroism. They experienced it as abandonment.

Defectors who survived the famine years describe a society in which the familiar markers of state authority — the portraits, the loyalty rituals, the mandatory political meetings — continued even as the food ran out, creating a grotesque dissonance between ideological performance and material reality. You praised the Great Leader in the morning meeting and then spent the afternoon searching for something to eat. The rituals continued. The rations did not.

The Birth of the Jangmadang

Into the vacuum of the collapsed distribution system, something entirely unplanned emerged: the jangmadang.

The word translates roughly as “market grounds” — a phrase that in North Korea’s ideological framework carried the faint stigma of capitalism, of unregulated exchange, of the very market forces that the state had spent fifty years insulating its people from. In the early famine years, jangmadang were illegal. You were not supposed to buy and sell. The state was supposed to provide. But the state had stopped providing, and so people began to buy and sell anyway — first food, then clothing, then medicine, then anything that could be traded for anything that could keep a family alive.

The markets spread with the speed of necessity. What began as informal exchanges between neighbours became organized trading spaces in town centres and railway stations. Women — who under North Korean law had more freedom of movement than men, who were largely tied to work units and military assignments — became the primary traders. They sourced goods from wherever they could find them: farms in the countryside, factories that had stopped paying wages but whose workers still had access to equipment and materials, border traders who crossed into China and returned with goods unavailable in the North Korean economy. Between 1998 and 2008, by one estimate, informal market activities generated seventy-eight percent of average household income in North Korea. The state had become an irrelevance to the economic survival of its own people.

The jangmadang generation — a phrase coined by researchers to describe the North Koreans born in the 1980s and 1990s who came of age during and after the famine — grew up in a fundamentally different North Korea from the one their parents had known. Their parents had received rations. They purchased rice. Their parents had depended on the state. They depended on the market. And they drew from this experience a set of values — individualistic, entrepreneurial, materialistic, pragmatic — that were in direct and irreconcilable conflict with the collectivist ideology the state continued to teach in schools and insist upon in political meetings.

As one expert at a Seoul research institute put it: “The older generation grew up on rations from the regime. The younger generation grew up on rice purchased from the market. They think they didn’t get any benefit from the regime’s system.”

That sentence is as subversive as anything ever written about North Korea. It describes a generation that does not feel it owes the Kim dynasty anything — because it doesn’t. The dynasty let it starve. The market fed it.

The USB and the Drama

The jangmadang were not only markets for food and goods. They became markets for information.

As traders moved between cities, as border crossers brought goods from China, as the networks of exchange that the famine had forced into existence extended across the country, something else began to flow through those networks: foreign media. At first it was VHS tapes and CDs, physically bulky and therefore risky to transport. Then came DVDs. And then — the technology that changed everything — the USB flash drive.

Small, cheap, concealable in a waistband or a shoe, capable of holding dozens of episodes of a television drama or hundreds of songs in a format that could be played on the Chinese-made DVD players that had begun appearing in North Korean homes — the USB drive became the delivery mechanism for a cultural invasion that no border security apparatus could fully stop. South Korean dramas and K-pop videos flooded across the Tumen and Yalu rivers, smuggled by traders who had discovered that entertainment commanded as high a price as food in a society starved of both.

The effect was quietly catastrophic for the regime’s ideological monopoly. North Koreans had been taught their entire lives that South Korea was a poverty-stricken puppet state, its people ground down by American imperialism and exploited by capitalist landlords. The dramas showed them something else: clean apartments, fashionable clothes, abundant food, ordinary people living lives of ease and colour that bore no resemblance to the propaganda. A defector named Jihyun Kang, who escaped in 2010, described watching South Korean dramas and thinking: “Everything portrayed was so clean and everyone seemed so wealthy. I used to think ‘Wow, there is such a world out there.’ We were taught that South Korea was a poor country, but I wondered — why can’t we live like that?”

That question — why can’t we live like that? — is the most dangerous question a North Korean citizen can ask. It is dangerous not because it leads immediately to action, but because it cannot be unanswered once it has been posed. The propaganda that had sustained the state for fifty years rested on a single premise: that North Korea’s sacrifices were necessary because its enemies were implacable and its way of life was superior. The dramas demolished the second half of that premise in the span of a single episode.

By the mid-2010s, a defector-turned-diplomat named Thae Yong-ho — who had served as North Korea’s deputy ambassador to Britain before defecting to Seoul in 2016 — estimated that between seventy and eighty percent of the jangmadang generation had watched South Korean films or dramas. The information war, in other words, was being lost. Not in a dramatic single moment, but quietly, under blankets, with earbuds in, in the small hours of the night, one episode at a time.

The Underground Railroad

The famine also produced something else: a flow of people out of North Korea that has never fully stopped.

The exodus began in the mid-1990s, when people crossed the Tumen and Yalu rivers into China simply looking for food. They intended to return. Many did not. In China they encountered a reality so different from North Korea’s that the prospect of return became unthinkable. They found work, discovered relative freedom of movement, and learned — often for the first time — that their government had been lying to them about almost everything.

China officially classifies North Korean defectors as illegal economic migrants rather than refugees, which means they are subject to arrest and forced repatriation. Repatriation means torture, prison camps, and sometimes execution. The fear of this outcome, paradoxically, pushed many defectors to go further — to take the long, dangerous route that came to be known as Asia’s Underground Railroad.

The route is harrowing. From the Chinese border provinces, defectors travel by train or bus, often using forged documents or borrowed identification cards, through thousands of miles of Chinese territory to the southwestern provinces bordering Laos or Myanmar. From there, on foot through jungle and mountain terrain, they cross into Laos or Thailand. The Mekong River crossing — in darkness, by boat, watched for by border guards — became one of the most famous stages of a journey that could take weeks, months, or years. Those who reached Thailand could apply for asylum and, in most cases, eventually reach South Korea, which grants citizenship to all North Koreans who arrive.

In 1992, nine North Koreans made it to South Korea this way. By the peak years of the mid-2000s, the number had risen to over three thousand per year. The network of activists, missionaries, brokers, and Korean-Chinese intermediaries who made the route possible — operating in deliberate secrecy, borrowing their name from the antebellum American system that brought enslaved people to freedom — was and remains one of the most significant acts of sustained civil courage in modern Asia. Many were caught. Some were killed. Pastor Chun Ki-won, a South Korean missionary who pioneered the Mongolian escape route in the early 2000s, was arrested by Chinese police in 2002, spent eight months in a Chinese prison, and was banned from returning to China — the nine North Koreans he had been guiding when he was caught were repatriated and never heard from again.

The defectors who reached South Korea brought with them something more valuable than their own testimony: they became nodes in a network of information. They placed calls to relatives still inside North Korea. They funded brokers to extract family members. They established defector-run radio stations that broadcast Korean-language news back into the North. The wall was porous in ways the regime could manage but could not seal.

The Fracture

By the time Kim Jong-il died in 2011, North Korea had become, in a fundamental sense, two countries inhabiting the same physical territory.

There was the official country: the Pyongyang of illuminated portraits and military parades and Workers’ Party congresses and mandatory loyalty rituals — the theatrical North Korea that the state presented to itself and to the world. And there was the actual country: the jangmadang economy that kept most people alive, the USB networks that circulated forbidden knowledge, the border trade that connected the peninsula to a world the official narrative pretended didn’t exist, and the quiet, corrosive erosion of the belief system that the Kim dynasty had spent sixty years constructing.

The regime was aware of all of this. It had not been passive. It periodically cracked down on markets, confiscated foreign media, arrested traders, and attempted currency reforms designed to wipe out the savings of the newly wealthy merchant class and reassert state control over the economy. The 2009 currency redenomination — which effectively zeroed out the value of most North Koreans’ savings overnight — was one of the most savage expressions of the state’s anxiety about the market forces it had accidentally licensed. The popular anger it provoked was sufficiently intense that the regime took the extraordinary step of publicly executing the official who had implemented it, blaming him personally for the disaster.

But the markets returned. The USB drives kept coming. The dramas kept circulating. The defectors kept leaving, and their voices kept reaching back in.

The fracture was real, structural, and growing. Kim Jong-un, who inherited this fractured country in 2011, understood what he had inherited with a clarity that his father had never quite achieved — or had achieved too late to act on. The informal economy was not simply an inconvenience or an embarrassing gap in ideological discipline. It was an existential threat to the regime’s foundational claim: that the state alone provided, that the people alone could not survive without it, and that gratitude and loyalty were therefore rational responses to the Kim family’s benevolence.

The jangmadang had disproved that claim empirically. A generation had grown up knowing that the state had abandoned them and the market had saved them. They owed nothing. They knew it. And some of them, watching South Korean dramas under their blankets in the dark, were beginning to want something that their grandparents had never been permitted to want.

That is the inheritance Kim Jong-un received. And everything he has done since — the crackdown on markets, the executions for K-pop, the shoot-to-kill orders at the Chinese border, the pandemic lockdown that was also a purge of information — has been aimed at one objective: closing the fracture before it becomes a rupture.

Whether he can do it is the question this series will spend its remaining parts trying to answer.
