Part of the series: Israel/Palestine: The Original Sin and Its Compound Interest

By Stephen Gordon | CanadianSnowmanSays

David Ben-Gurion reads the Israeli Declaration of Independence, Tel Aviv Museum, May 14, 1948. For Jews, a miracle after two millennia. For Palestinians, the beginning of the Nakba

On May 14, 1948, at four o’clock in the afternoon, David Ben-Gurion stood beneath a portrait of Theodor Herzl in the Tel Aviv Museum and read aloud the Israeli Declaration of Independence. The British Mandate expired at midnight. Eleven minutes after Ben-Gurion finished reading, the United States recognized the new state. Three days later, the Soviet Union followed.

It was, depending entirely on where you stood, either one of the most inspiring moments of the 20th century or the beginning of one of its most sustained catastrophes. Both things were true simultaneously. They remain true simultaneously today. The failure to hold both truths at once — the genuine miracle of Jewish statehood after two millennia of exile and the genuine catastrophe of Palestinian dispossession that made it possible — is the central intellectual failure of every dishonest account of this conflict, and there are many of them, on all sides.

This instalment will not add to their number.

What Had Already Happened Before Independence

The declaration of May 14 is the date history records as Israel’s birth. But the war that determined the character of that birth had already been underway for months, and much of what would be called the Nakba — the Arabic word for catastrophe, used by Palestinians to describe the displacement of 1948 — had already occurred before a single Arab army crossed the border.

The UN Partition Plan of November 1947 had triggered an immediate civil war between the Jewish and Arab communities of Mandatory Palestine. The Arab Higher Committee called a general strike. Jewish paramilitary forces — the Haganah, the Irgun, and the Lehi — and Arab irregular forces began attacking each other’s communities, convoys, and infrastructure. By the time the British Mandate ended in May 1948, the civil war phase had already produced the displacement of tens of thousands of Palestinian Arabs.

The most consequential single event of that civil war phase was the massacre at Deir Yassin.

Deir Yassin, April 9, 1948. Irgun and Lehi forces killed between 107 and 120 Palestinian civilians — women and children among them. The Red Cross documented what they found. The Jewish Agency condemned it. Menachem Begin did not. The news spread faster than any army could march.

On April 9, 1948 — five weeks before independence — Irgun and Lehi forces attacked the village of Deir Yassin, on the western outskirts of Jerusalem. Between 107 and 120 Palestinian civilians were killed, including women and children. The killing was documented by Red Cross representatives who arrived afterward. The Jewish Agency — the proto-government of the future state — condemned the massacre publicly. Menachem Begin, who commanded the Irgun, did not.

The psychological impact of Deir Yassin on the Palestinian Arab population was enormous and, from a military perspective, deliberate. News of the massacre spread rapidly through Palestinian communities. The Irgun and Lehi distributed loudspeaker announcements warning other villages of what had happened and what might happen to them. Mass flight followed — from Jerusalem’s surrounding villages, from the coastal plain, from the Galilee. The line between flight driven by fear and expulsion driven by military pressure was, in many communities, impossible to draw clearly.

This ambiguity is at the centre of the historiographical argument about 1948 that has consumed Israeli and Palestinian scholars for decades. It is an argument worth understanding before you take anyone’s word for what happened.

Palestinian displacement, 1948. By the time Arab armies crossed the border in May, hundreds of thousands had already fled or been expelled. The Nakba was not a consequence of the Arab-Israeli war — it was already underway

The New Historians and What the Archives Revealed

For the first thirty years of Israel’s existence, the official narrative of 1948 was essentially unchallenged in the Western world: Israel had declared independence, been attacked by five Arab armies seeking to destroy it, survived against overwhelming odds, and the Palestinian refugee crisis was the result of Arab leaders telling their people to flee temporarily so the armies could finish the job — after which they would return. The refugees were the Arab world’s responsibility. Israel bore no accountability for their displacement.

This narrative began to unravel in the 1980s, when Israeli archives from 1948 were declassified and a group of Israeli historians — subsequently called the New Historians — gained access to documents that told a substantially different story.

Benny Morris, whose 1988 book “The Birth of the Palestinian Refugee Problem” and its 2004 revised edition remain the most comprehensive scholarly account of the refugee crisis, documented using Israeli military and government archives that the displacement of Palestinians in 1948 was not primarily the result of Arab leaders’ instructions to flee. It was the result of a combination of factors: genuine flight from war, deliberate expulsion by Israeli military forces in specific operations, psychological warfare designed to induce panic, and massacres — of which Deir Yassin was the most notorious but not the only example — that accelerated flight from surrounding areas.

Morris documented over two dozen massacres of Palestinian civilians by Israeli forces in 1948. He documented explicit expulsion orders in military operational records. He documented the destruction of Palestinian villages after their populations had fled — a pattern designed to ensure they could not return.

What makes Morris’s work uniquely important — and uniquely uncomfortable for both sides — is that he is not a Palestinian advocate. He is an Israeli historian who has expressed views about Arab culture that are themselves deeply controversial and who has argued, in interviews that shocked many of his admirers, that Ben-Gurion perhaps did not go far enough in 1948 — that a more complete expulsion might have prevented subsequent conflicts. Morris documented the Nakba as historical reality and simultaneously declined to condemn it as moral catastrophe. He separated the historian’s obligation to establish facts from the advocate’s obligation to render judgment, and his facts have never been seriously disputed even by those who dispute his conclusions.

Benny Morris, whose archival research transformed understanding of the 1948 refugee crisis

Ilan Pappé, another New Historian, reached entirely different moral conclusions from similar evidence. His 2006 book “The Ethnic Cleansing of Palestine” argued explicitly that what occurred in 1948 was a planned, systematic ethnic cleansing — not the improvised, chaotic, war-driven displacement that Morris described, but a deliberate political and military project with a clear goal: the demographic transformation of Palestine from an Arab-majority to a Jewish-majority territory. Pappé’s methodology has been challenged by other historians, including Morris, but his moral framing — that the displacement of 700,000 people was not an unfortunate byproduct of war but its intended result — is supported by documents that Morris himself uncovered.

The honest position, supported by the archival evidence, is that 1948 involved all of these things simultaneously: genuine Jewish self-defence against armies that explicitly intended to destroy the new state; deliberate expulsions of Palestinian civilians by Israeli military forces in specific operations; psychological warfare; the chaos and fear of a multi-front war; and the exploitation of that chaos by commanders who understood that a Jewish state with a large Arab minority would face permanent internal instability. The displacement was not entirely planned and not entirely accidental. It was, in the precise meaning of the term, a catastrophe — and catastrophes have causes, and the causes have names.

The Arab Armies and the War

On May 15, 1948 — the day after independence, the day the British Mandate expired — the armies of Egypt, Jordan, Syria, Iraq, and Lebanon crossed into the former Mandatory territory. Saudi Arabia sent troops that fought under Egyptian command. The Arab League’s stated objective was the prevention of Jewish statehood and the establishment of a unified Arab state in all of Palestine.

This invasion is the foundation of Israel’s legitimate security narrative. The new state, hours old, faced armies from five countries simultaneously. Its population was 650,000 people. The combined Arab states had populations in the tens of millions. The outcome, from the perspective of the Jewish leadership and the Holocaust survivors arriving daily in displaced persons ships, was genuinely uncertain in those first weeks.

What actually happened militarily is more complicated than either the Israeli David-versus-Goliath narrative or the Arab narrative of a powerful, Western-armed aggressor overwhelming disorganized resistance.

The Arab armies were in fact poorly coordinated, inadequately equipped, and commanded by officers whose political reliability was more important to their governments than their military competence. The Arab Legion of Transjordan — the best-trained and best-led Arab force — was operating under instructions from King Abdullah that limited its objectives to the West Bank areas allocated to the Arab state under the Partition Plan. Abdullah had his own territorial ambitions and his own separate diplomatic channel with the Jewish leadership that the other Arab states did not know about. The Arab coalition was not a coalition in any meaningful military sense. It was five separate armies with five separate political objectives fighting in the same territory without coordination.

The Haganah, by contrast — which became the Israel Defence Forces upon independence — was a disciplined, increasingly well-armed force with experienced commanders, many of whom had served in the British Army during World War Two. Arms shipments from Czechoslovakia, arranged through connections that ran through the international left and the Soviet desire to embarrass Britain in its former Mandate, gave the IDF a decisive materiel advantage as the war progressed.

By the time the armistice agreements were signed in 1949 — separately, with Egypt in February, Jordan in April, Syria in July — Israel controlled approximately 78 percent of the territory of Mandatory Palestine, significantly more than the 56 percent allocated to the Jewish state under the UN Partition Plan. The West Bank was held by Jordan. Gaza was held by Egypt. Jerusalem was divided: the western part controlled by Israel, the Old City and the eastern part by Jordan.

The Palestinian state that the Partition Plan had proposed never came into existence. It was not only Israel that prevented it. Jordan annexed the West Bank. Egypt administered Gaza without granting its population any form of self-governance. The Arab states that had gone to war in the name of Palestinian rights proceeded to treat the Palestinian people as stateless pawns — useful as a political grievance, unwelcome as citizens.

The Scale of the Nakba

By the end of the 1948 war, approximately 700,000 Palestinian Arabs had been displaced from their homes — roughly half the Arab population of Mandatory Palestine. They fled to or were expelled into Gaza, the West Bank, Jordan, Lebanon, and Syria. The United Nations established UNRWA — the United Nations Relief and Works Agency — in 1949 specifically to manage the refugee crisis, an agency that was intended to be temporary and is still operating today, seventy-five years later, because the crisis it was created to manage has never been resolved.

The villages from which Palestinians fled or were expelled numbered over 400. Many were subsequently demolished by Israeli forces — the houses pulled down, the rubble cleared, forests planted over the ruins by the Jewish National Fund, the village names replaced on maps with Hebrew equivalents. Today, some of those forests are popular Israeli hiking destinations. The Palestinian villages beneath them are documented in the records of Zochrot, an Israeli NGO dedicated to preserving Nakba memory, and in the scholarship of historians like Walid Khalidi, whose 1992 catalogue “All That Remains” documented 418 destroyed Palestinian villages with photographs, maps, and survivor testimonies.

The right of return — the Palestinian claim that the refugees of 1948 and their descendants have the right to return to their original homes and villages — is enshrined in UN General Assembly Resolution 194, passed in December 1948, which states that refugees wishing to return to their homes and live at peace with their neighbours should be permitted to do so at the earliest practicable date. Israel has never implemented this resolution. No Israeli government has been willing to implement it, because the demographic consequence of five million Palestinian refugees and their descendants returning to Israel would end the Jewish majority on which the Jewish state depends.

This is the irresolvable core of the conflict. The Palestinian right of return is recognized in international law and is existentially incompatible with the Jewish state as currently constituted. The Jewish state’s existence is recognized in international law and is existentially incompatible with the Palestinian right of return as Palestinians understand it. There is no solution that satisfies both claims simultaneously within the current political framework. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

What Ben-Gurion Knew

David Ben-Gurion was one of the most formidable political leaders of the 20th century — a man of genuine historical vision, extraordinary organizational capacity, and a ruthless clarity about what Jewish statehood required and what it would cost.

The archival record — including his own diaries, now available to researchers — makes clear that Ben-Gurion understood by the late 1930s that Jewish statehood in Palestine would require the demographic transformation of the territory. He wrote in 1937, responding to the Peel Commission’s partition proposal: “The compulsory transfer of Arabs from the valleys of the proposed Jewish state could give us something we never had, even when we stood on our own during the days of the First and Second Temple.” The “compulsory transfer” he was describing was the forced removal of the Arab population.

He did not use the word ethnic cleansing. That term did not exist in its current usage. But the concept he was articulating — that a viable Jewish state required a Jewish demographic majority that could not be achieved without removing the existing Arab population — was the political logic that drove the military operations of 1948.

This does not make Ben-Gurion a uniquely evil figure. It makes him a nationalist leader of his era, operating within the brutal logic of ethnic nationalism that had reshaped Europe through two world wars and the Holocaust, concluding that the survival of his people required the application of that same brutal logic in the territory where they were building their state. The horror is not that Ben-Gurion was a monster. The horror is that he was not — that the catastrophe of 1948 was produced by recognizable human logic operating under extreme historical pressure, which makes it both more comprehensible and more disturbing than the monster theory allows.

The Dispossession That Has No Precedent in the Dispossessors’ History

There is a sentence that requires saying plainly, because it is regularly avoided in Western discourse on this conflict.

The Palestinian Arabs of 1948 had nothing to do with the Holocaust. They did not participate in European antisemitism. They did not operate the concentration camps. They did not pass the Nuremberg Laws. They did not turn away the St. Louis. The catastrophe visited upon the Jewish people by European civilization was paid for, in significant part, by a people who bore no responsibility for it — whose land was designated as the solution to a problem that was entirely Europe’s creation.

This is not an argument that Israel should not exist. It is a statement of the moral architecture of the conflict that no honest account can avoid. The Palestinians of 1948 were asked to absorb the consequences of European antisemitism because the Western powers that had created those consequences were unwilling to absorb them themselves. Canada, the United States, Britain, Australia — none of them opened their doors to the Jewish survivors of the Holocaust in the numbers that would have been required to make Palestinian dispossession unnecessary. They found it considerably more convenient to support a Jewish state in someone else’s territory than to open their own borders to Jewish refugees.

The compound interest on that original sin is still being paid. Not by the governments that made the decision. By the Palestinians living in Gaza’s rubble, Lebanon’s targeted south, the refugee camps of Jordan and Lebanon that were built as temporary and are now three generations deep.

And by Israelis living under the permanent security threat that is the direct and predictable consequence of a dispossession that was never resolved and never acknowledged by the governments that enabled it.

The Armistice Lines That Became Permanent

The 1949 armistice agreements were explicitly not peace treaties. They were military ceasefire arrangements that all parties understood as temporary — pending a final political settlement that would determine borders, resolve the refugee question, and establish the permanent status of Jerusalem. That final settlement was never negotiated. The temporary lines of 1949 — the Green Line — became, by default, the de facto borders of Israel for the next eighteen years.

They were not internationally recognized borders. They were not accepted by the Arab states as permanent. They were not the borders that Israel’s government regarded as its ultimate territorial aspirations, which is a point that will become critical in Part VII when we examine what Israel did in six days in 1967 and what it has been doing in the occupied territories ever since.

What 1948 established was this: a Jewish state existed, it had survived a war of attempted annihilation, it controlled more territory than it had been allocated, it had displaced 700,000 people who would never be allowed to return, and the political, legal, and moral questions raised by all of those facts had been deferred rather than resolved.

Deferred problems accumulate interest. The interest on 1948 has been compounding ever since, in 1956, in 1967, in 1973, in 1982, in the First Intifada, in the Second Intifada, in the Gaza wars of 2008, 2012, 2014, 2021, and in the catastrophe that began on October 7, 2023 and continues as this series is written.

The statement of account from 1948 has never been settled. It never will be settled by military force, which is the only means any party to this conflict has seriously attempted. Military force can defer the reckoning. It cannot resolve it. Every government that has supplied the weapons, written the cheques, and cast the vetoes that have allowed force to substitute for justice has been complicit in the deferral.

The bill, as always, is paid by the people who had the least power when the original decision was made.

They never do anything else with it.
