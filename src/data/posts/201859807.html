There is a traffic circle at the western end of the Arlington Memorial Bridge, where the bridge crosses the Potomac River from Washington into Virginia, at the ceremonial entrance to the nation’s capital. It is called Memorial Circle. On one side of the river, the Lincoln Memorial looks out over the water with the composed expression of a man who has seen a great deal and has decided, on balance, to remain seated. On the other side, Arlington National Cemetery receives its dead with the permanent, non-negotiable dignity of a place that has been doing this since the Civil War and intends to continue.

It is, by any measure, one of the most weighted pieces of geography in the United States. The symbolism is not accidental. It was designed. The bridge, the circle, the sight lines between the Lincoln Memorial and the cemetery — all of it was conceived as a deliberate composition of national memory, grief, and continuity. You cross that bridge and you are in the presence of something that was here before you and will be here after you, and the appropriate response, which most visitors manage instinctively, is a certain quietness.

Donald Trump looked at Memorial Circle and saw a gap.

“Every time somebody rides over that beautiful bridge to the Lincoln Memorial,” he told a group of potential donors at a White House dinner in October 2025, holding up three scale models of increasing size, “they literally say something is supposed to be here.”

What is supposed to be there, in Trump’s conception, is an arch. Not a modest arch. Not a temporary arch. Not an arch scaled to the surrounding monuments and their accumulated weight of national meaning. The largest triumphal arch in any capital city in the world — 250 feet tall, taller than its inspiration the Arc de Triomphe in Paris by nearly 100 feet, visible from the Lincoln Memorial, straddling the ceremonial entrance to Washington like a gate that was not there yesterday and whose presence rearranges everything around it.

Memorial Circle at the western end of the Arlington Memorial Bridge, looking toward the Lincoln Memorial. This weighted ceremonial entrance to Washington is where Donald Trump envisioned placing the 250-foot Triumphal Arch

He held up the three models. Small, medium, large.

“I happen to think the large by far looks the best,” he said.

Of course he did.

When a reporter later asked him whom the arch was for, Trump’s answer was one word.

“Me.”

The arch had a name before it had a design, which is the Trump way. It was announced as the Triumphal Arch of Washington DC — officially — though the name that has attached to it in the public imagination, with the inevitability of a phrase that arrives fully formed and refuses to leave, is the Arc duh Trumphe. The pun requires no explanation. It requires no defence. It arrived because it was true.

The design process that followed the announcement was instructive in the specific way that Trump-era design processes tend to be instructive. The arch began at under sixty feet. Then it grew to seventy-six feet. Then it shot up to one hundred and sixty-four feet. Then it reached its current proposed height of two hundred and fifty feet — a number chosen, the White House explained, to represent the country’s two hundred and fiftieth anniversary, a coincidence of symbolism so convenient that one hesitates to call it a coincidence at all.

The architect whose firm was engaged to design the structure — Harrison Design, an Atlanta-based practice known for classical and neoclassical work — found the process less than collaborative. The architect behind the project publicly blasted its location and scale, saying it should be built in another part of town. This is, in the annals of architect-client relationships, a fairly unambiguous statement of professional dissatisfaction. The client proceeded regardless.

The Arc de Triomphe in Paris, the architectural inspiration for Trump’s proposed arch — which at 250 feet would stand nearly 100 feet taller.

The Commission of Fine Arts, the federal body whose statutory role is to review the design and aesthetics of all construction in Washington — and which is, in the current administration, staffed by Trump appointees — gave the arch its approval after a public comment period that produced a result the commission presumably found awkward. The National Capital Planning Commission subsequently received nearly 1,700 public comments about the proposed arch. Almost all of them opposed it. Commission staff raised concerns about whether the structure could disrupt flight patterns at nearby Ronald Reagan National Airport — one of the country’s busiest air hubs — and at a Pentagon heliport approximately one mile away. Staff also raised questions about whether the 250-foot structure would violate the federal Height of Buildings Act, which generally bars buildings taller than 160 feet in Washington.

The commission’s chairman, Will Scharf, acknowledged the staff’s concerns and voted to advance the project anyway. Scharf also serves simultaneously as Trump’s White House staff secretary — a conflict of interest so brazen it goes unacknowledged in the official record. He said he personally believed the Height of Buildings Act did not apply to federal construction. “I think that it is fitting and proper,” he added, “that we commemorate the 250th anniversary of our great republic with a suitable piece of monumental architecture.”

The commission voted 9 to 1 to advance the project. The single dissenting vote is not recorded in Scharf’s remarks.

The will of nearly 1,700 members of the public, expressed through the only mechanism available to them, was noted and set aside.

The legal obstacles accumulate regardless. A group of Vietnam War veterans has sued to stop construction, arguing that the arch would obstruct the sight line between the Lincoln Memorial and Arlington National Cemetery — the very sight line the original designers of Memorial Circle conceived as a deliberate composition of national memory. The Federal Aviation Administration is conducting its own review of the aerial hazard question. The Height of Buildings Act compliance issue remains unresolved. Congressional approval for the project has not been sought or obtained.

The construction timeline — targeted for summer 2026, completion before Trump’s term ends in 2029 — proceeds as though these obstacles are, in the president’s familiar formulation, minor details.

Donald Trump displays scale models of the proposed arch during a White House dinner in October 2025. When asked whom the arch was for, he answered: “Me

The Washington makeover is not limited to the arch. It is a comprehensive physical transformation of the capital, proceeding simultaneously on multiple fronts with the coherence of a man who has decided that if he cannot own Washington, he will rebuild it.

The Oval Office has been covered in gold decor. The Rose Garden’s grass has been paved. The National Mall is acquiring a Garden of American Heroes featuring 250 statues — a number chosen, again, for the anniversary, and a project whose selection criteria for heroism have not been made fully public.

At the Lincoln Memorial, Trump has announced his latest addition: a promenade running from the back of the memorial down to the Potomac River. “We’re going to call it the promenade,” he told reporters in the Oval Office. “They want to call it the Trump Promenade, but I don’t know if I want to do that. But it’s going to be beautiful.”

This sentence deserves a moment’s attention. For the first time in the catalogue of the naming project — across the Kennedy Center, the Institute of Peace, the currency, the arch, the baby savings accounts, the prescription drug website, the warship — Trump publicly performed reluctance about attaching his name to something. Whether this is genuine restraint or the setup for a later announcement that popular demand has prevailed upon him to accept the honour is a question the record of the preceding five years answers with some confidence. But the performance of reluctance is itself new, and the fact that it appeared in the context of a project at the Lincoln Memorial — the most symbolically loaded address in American civic geography — is not without significance.

The National Park Service is already conducting a $69 million renovation of the Lincoln Memorial, scheduled for completion in July, adding an immersive museum and visitor upgrades. Trump’s promenade would be a separate addition to a building whose stewardship is not his to direct. The pattern is consistent: the announcement precedes the authorisation, the construction precedes the approval, and the name — or the careful public non-naming, which achieves the same associative result — goes on regardless.

The White House ballroom deserves its own sustained examination, because it is the most literally physical expression of what this series has been calling the monument project — the difference between a name on a contract and a name carved into the republic itself.

In July 2025, Trump announced that he intended to build a grand ballroom on the White House grounds. The East Wing — the wing that has traditionally housed the offices of the First Lady, a structure with its own history and its own place in the accumulated meaning of the presidential complex — would be demolished. In its place would rise a new wing of ninety thousand square feet, containing a ballroom with a seated capacity of up to a thousand guests, forty-foot ceilings, bulletproof glass sides, and a drone-proof roof beneath which the military was constructing, the president told reporters, a massive fortified complex.

The inspiration, the White House confirmed, was Mar-a-Lago.

Barack Obama presented this slide at the White House Correspondents' Dinner in 2016. The East Wing was demolished in October 2025. Construction of the ballroom is ongoing.

The president was bringing Mar-a-Lago to Washington. The aesthetic of his Florida estate — the gilded ballroom, the white columns, the particular brand of luxury that announces itself loudly and without apology — was being transplanted to the grounds of a building that has housed American presidents since John Adams, a building whose architecture had been understood, by everyone who worked on it and everyone who lived in it, as belonging not to its current occupant but to the republic.

The demolition of the East Wing began in October 2025. It was, briefly, a tourist attraction. People came to watch the backhoe tear down a piece of the White House. This says something about the moment we are living in, though it is not entirely clear what.

The cost estimates for the ballroom evolved with the same upward momentum as the arch’s height. The initial figure was $200 million, described as privately funded through Trump’s own contributions and those of unnamed “patriot donors.” By January 2026, the estimate had grown to $400 million — double the original figure, attributed to a design that had expanded to twice the originally proposed size. By May 2026, Senate Republicans were including a $1 billion request for the project in a funding bill — a figure that, even accounting for the creative accounting that attends all large construction projects, represents a rather dramatic distance from the $200 million private funding commitment with which the project began.

Americans oppose the ballroom by a margin of two to one in polling. Senate Republicans, typically reliable allies of the president, expressed what Senator Jim Justice described as concern about a cost that was “enormous.” Senator John Kennedy of Louisiana said there were “a lot of questions.” Chuck Schumer, with the gift for a line that occasionally emerges from the Senate minority, observed that “Trump may be trying to build a ballroom but clearly he is living in the theater of the absurd.”

The ballroom is under construction. The arc of the cost estimates continues upward. The drone-proof roof is being built over the former site of the East Wing of the White House, and somewhere in the plans, presumably, there is gold.

The Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts, renamed as part of the broader Trump naming project.

And then there is the Kennedy Center.

We opened this series with Judge Cooper’s ruling, and we return to it here because the Kennedy Center story, in the context of the full Washington makeover, reveals something that is not visible when the case is examined in isolation.

The Kennedy Center was not a stand-alone act of renaming. It was one component of a coordinated project to remake Washington — and the republic’s broader symbolic landscape — as a terrain of controlled meaning. The naming project operates in one direction: Trump’s name goes onto institutions, buildings, currency, monuments, baby savings accounts, prescription drug websites, warships. But there is an equal and opposite dimension to the project that the naming catalogue alone does not fully capture.

In January 2026, the administration issued an executive order directing the National Park Service to remove or cover materials at national parks that “inappropriately disparage Americans” or cast the United States “in a negative light.” The Park Service complied. Plaques about slavery came down at Independence National Historical Park in Philadelphia. A sign about climate change was removed at Fort Sumter in South Carolina. A sign about Indigenous peoples was taken at Acadia National Park in Maine.

One direction writes a specific name onto the republic’s surfaces. The other direction erases inconvenient names and histories from them. Both are the same impulse: the conviction that the surfaces of the republic should say what the current administration wants them to say, and not say what it does not. In June 2026, a federal judge did more than block the directive going forward. Judge Angel Kelley ordered the administration to restore every sign it had removed, calling the removals censorship and sanitization and giving the Park Service until July 3 to put them back — the eve of the republic's two hundred and fiftieth birthday. The administration appealed to the First Circuit and asked for a stay. And so the calendar produced its own commentary: in the same week, one branch of government ordered the plaques about slavery and climate change and Indigenous peoples returned to the walls by July 3, while the executive raced to raise a two hundred and fifty foot monument over Memorial Circle by July 4. The republic was being told, on consecutive days, to remember and to be overshadowed.

The Washington makeover, understood in its full scope, is not merely an act of self-inscription. It is an act of editorial control over the national narrative — adding where the narrative is insufficient, removing where it is inconvenient, and ensuring that what remains reflects the version of America that the current occupant of the White House has decided the republic should present to itself and to the world.

The arch at the bridge, the ballroom at the White House, the promenade at the Lincoln Memorial, the name on the performing arts centre, the name on the peace institute, the name on the currency, the name on the parks passes, the name on the warship, the name on the baby savings accounts — and the absence, from the national parks, of the plaques about slavery and climate change and Indigenous peoples. Each individual act, examined alone, might be explained as the enthusiasm of a new administration, the vanity of a particular personality, the excesses of a moment that will correct itself.

Examined together, they constitute something that the historian Jeffrey Engel, with his careful academic restraint, called gauche, and that the Wikipedia editors who created the article “Branding of United States government programs and facilities after Donald Trump” described, in the dry vocabulary of encyclopaedic neutrality, as engendering a cult of personality.

The phrase “cult of personality” has a specific meaning in political history. It does not mean that a politician is popular, or vain, or even unusually self-promoting. It means that the apparatus of the state — the institutions, the currency, the monuments, the public buildings — has been enlisted in the service of projecting a single individual’s image and name onto the national landscape, such that the individual and the state become difficult to distinguish. It is the condition in which the leader’s face on the currency is not a historical honour but a present-tense political statement. It is the condition in which the peace institute bears the name of the man who destroyed it. It is the condition in which the performing arts centre dedicated to a slain president must be renamed in honour of the living one.

It is, in the language of comparative political science, a condition associated with a specific category of governance that the United States was, for two and a half centuries, understood to have chosen not to practice.

The Romans had rules about the triumphal arch.

A triumph — the formal procession through the city, the arch, the public inscription of a general’s glory — was awarded only after a significant military victory, and only by vote of the Senate. The honour was earned, constrained, and temporary. The arch was permanent, but the general who earned it could not simply decide he deserved one and commission it from a design firm in Atlanta. He required the Senate’s endorsement. He required genuine achievement. He required, in the Roman conception, the judgment of his peers and the verdict of history.

The Arc de Triomphe in Paris, which the Washington arch is modelled on, was commissioned by Napoleon Bonaparte to commemorate his victories at Austerlitz and Jena. Napoleon had actually won those battles. The arch celebrated specific, documented, historically verifiable events that had changed the course of European history. One may have complicated views about Napoleon as a figure — and most serious historians do — without disputing that the arc documented something real.

The Washington arch celebrates the two hundred and fiftieth anniversary of American independence, which is a genuine occasion for commemoration. It does not celebrate a specific military victory, a specific diplomatic achievement, a specific moment of national transformation. It celebrates the coincidence that the anniversary falls during a particular president’s term — a quirk of the calendar elevated, through the monument project, into an architectural statement about one man’s significance.

George Washington, who actually won the Revolution whose anniversary the arch commemorates, declined to put his face on the currency because he thought it looked like monarchy. He was the most natural candidate for self-commemoration in American history — the founder, the general, the president who established the precedent of voluntary relinquishment of power — and he chose, deliberately, to let the republic’s institutions speak for themselves rather than inscribing himself on them.

The question of whether the nation’s bigness and power are appropriately expressed through the personal scale of its current president’s preferred architectural monument is one that nearly 1,700 members of the public answered with overwhelming clarity, and that the planning commission staffed by his appointees answered in the opposite direction.

When a reporter asked Trump whom the arch was for, he did not cite the anniversary. He did not cite national pride. He did not cite the veterans or the tourists or the republic’s two hundred and fiftieth birthday.

He said: “Me.”

One word. The entire series, compressed to its essential form.

Donald Trump has spent a lifetime writing his name on things. Towers. Golf courses. Hotels. A university. A Bible. A perfume called Success. A peace institute he gutted before the paint on the letters dried. A performing arts centre dedicated to a murdered president he has spent his entire career trying to impress.

The name is always the same: gold where possible, large where mandatory, permanent where permitted, and imposed where neither permission nor permanence was ever his to grant.

This series is about what that compulsion reveals — psychologically, politically, historically, and occasionally comically. It is not a flattering portrait.

Then again, neither is a loading dock on Bay Street.
