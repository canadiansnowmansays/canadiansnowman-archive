In the very first essay of this series, writing about the houses of southern Europe, I made a promise I have been waiting fourteen essays to keep. I noted that the old Mediterranean architecture — the courtyards, the heavy shutters, the thick white stone — had once known how to keep a building cool in a brutal summer, and that this knowledge had largely been thrown away in the postwar rush to build fast and cheap on the assumption that air conditioning would handle what design used to handle. I said then that we would return, near the end, to the proposition that some of the future is a return rather than an invention. We have arrived at that essay, and I want to make good on it, because after fourteen essays largely concerned with failure, refusal, and loss, I owe you — and myself — a turn toward what actually works.

Here is the central fact that our air-conditioned century has allowed us to forget: for thousands of years, in some of the hottest inhabited places on Earth, human beings kept themselves cool without electricity, without refrigerant, without burning anything at all. They did it with intelligence, with design, with a patient empirical understanding of how heat and air and water behave — and the buildings they made are, in many cases, more comfortable in summer than the glass-and-concrete boxes we have replaced them with, at a fraction of the energy cost, which is to say none.

Consider the Persian windcatcher, the badgir. Across the desert cities of Iran — Yazd, Kerman, Kashan — you will see tall chimney-like towers rising above the rooftops, and they are not decorative. They are among the most elegant pieces of climate engineering humanity has ever devised. The tower catches the prevailing breeze high above the street, where the air is cooler and cleaner, and funnels it down into the living spaces below; and when there is no breeze, the tower works in reverse, the warm air inside rising and escaping up the shaft by simple buoyancy, drawing cooler air up from below to replace it. The system runs on nothing but the physics of warm air rising. And it works astonishingly well: studies of traditional windcatchers find them capable of lowering indoor temperatures by as much as ten degrees Celsius. Ten degrees, from a tower made of mud brick, burning nothing, in the middle of a desert.

Now connect the windcatcher to its companion technology, the qanat — the underground channel that carried water for miles across the Iranian plateau, from the mountains to the towns, beneath the cooling earth. The genius was in combining them: a building’s windcatcher could draw its air not directly from the hot exterior but up from a basement connected to the qanat, so that the incoming breeze passed first over cool underground water, picking up evaporative chill on its way into the rooms. The air arrived cooled twice over — once by the depth of the earth, once by the water. And the same Persians who built these systems used them to perform what still sounds like a magic trick: they stored ice through the desert summer, in great insulated mud-brick domes called yakhchals, keeping winter’s frozen water frozen through months of killing heat, with no power source but clever design. People who could keep ice in a desert summer eight centuries ago were not primitives waiting for the air conditioner to rescue them. They understood things about heat that we, in our conditioned comfort, have simply allowed ourselves to forget.

The principles were not unique to Persia, which is why I have titled this essay for the Romans, though I might equally have named it for the Andalusians or the Mughals or the builders of any hot civilization. The Romans built their villas around shaded courtyards and used the thermal mass of thick stone walls to even out the swing between scorching day and cool night, the walls absorbing heat all day and releasing it slowly after dark. Across the Islamic world, the central courtyard with its fountain and its shade created a pool of cool air that the surrounding rooms could draw upon, while high ceilings let the heat rise away from the people below. The whitewashed villages of Greece and southern Spain threw the sun’s radiation back at the sky. Everywhere that people lived with heat before the twentieth century, they evolved sophisticated, place-specific, low-energy ways of living with it — what architects now call vernacular design, the accumulated cooling wisdom of people who could not afford to get it wrong.

The variety of the solutions is itself instructive, because it shows that this was not a single trick but a whole science, adapted ingeniously to local conditions. In Mughal India and across the Arab world, builders used the jali — a pierced stone or lattice screen — to do three things at once: break the harsh sunlight into dappled shade, and accelerate the breeze passing through the small openings by the simple physics of compression, so that air squeezed through the lattice emerged faster and cooler, while still affording privacy to those within. In the old cities of Rajasthan and the Gulf, whole streets were laid out narrow and deep so the buildings shaded one another, the lanes kept in shadow through the worst of the day. The Andalusians of southern Spain perfected the patio — the descendant of the Roman and Islamic courtyard — with its central fountain and its canopy of vines, an outdoor room that stayed liveable in the Sevillian summer. In the American South before air conditioning, houses were built with high ceilings, deep porches, tall shuttered windows aligned to catch the cross-breeze, and a central hall — the “dogtrot” — open at both ends to funnel air through the house. None of these people had refrigeration or a physics degree. They had observation, accumulated over generations, and the powerful motivation of having no alternative. The result was a global library of low-energy cooling, written in brick and stone and timber and the careful placement of water and shade, every regional chapter different and every one of them working.

And then, in the span of a couple of generations, we threw nearly all of it away. The arrival of cheap electricity and the mechanical air conditioner made the old knowledge seem obsolete. Why orient your building carefully to the wind and sun, why build the thick walls and the courtyard and the windcatcher, when you could throw up a cheap box of any shape in any orientation and simply bolt on a machine to fight the heat the architecture no longer managed? The air conditioner did not just cool our buildings; it freed us, or seemed to, from having to think about heat at all. Design could become pure fashion, pure economy, indifferent to climate, because the machine would clean up the consequences. We forgot what the Romans knew because remembering it no longer seemed necessary. The furnace — or rather its inverse, the cooler — would provide.

This is, of course, the house built for winter in yet another guise, and perhaps the most poignant one. We did not merely build houses ill-suited to the coming heat; we unlearned the very knowledge that would have let us build well, trading hard-won wisdom for dependence on a machine that, as I argued in the essay on air conditioning, makes the larger problem worse even as it solves the immediate one. We are now in the absurd position of having to rediscover, through university research programs and pilot projects, things that the builders of Yazd knew in their bones a thousand years ago.

But here, finally, is the hopeful part, the reason this essay sits where it does, just before the close. We are, in fact, rediscovering them. This is one corner of the climate story where the news is genuinely good, and I have been saving it. In Seville — one of the hottest cities in Europe, baking in the same heat that opened this series — engineers have revived the ancient qanat principle, channeling water through underground tubes to cool the air for public spaces above, bringing down temperatures in the searing Andalusian summer with a technology a Persian engineer would recognize instantly. In Melbourne, a landmark sustainable building uses “shower towers” — tall shafts where water trickles down to cool the descending air by evaporation — which is to say a windcatcher with the cooling made explicit. Researchers are building hybrid twenty-first-century qanats that pair the old earth-and-water cooling with modern heat exchangers and achieving temperature drops of eight degrees and more, providing real cooling with a fraction of the energy an air conditioner would demand. The old wisdom is being married to new engineering, and the marriage is producing exactly the kind of low-energy cooling a warming, energy-constrained world is going to need.

And it joins the one other piece of genuinely good hardware I flagged earlier in the series — the heat pump, that elegant device which both heats and cools at a fraction of conventional energy and grows cleaner as the grid does. Put them together, the ancient and the modern, the passive and the electric: orient and build and shade the house so it needs as little cooling as possible, in the manner the Romans and the Persians understood, and then meet the cooling that remains with efficient electric machines running on clean power. That is not a fantasy. Every piece of it exists and works today. It is simply a matter of remembering what we knew, marrying it to what we have learned, and choosing to build that way instead of the cheap and thoughtless way.

Some places are beginning to insist on exactly that, which is the part that turns the nostalgia into policy. A growing number of jurisdictions are rewriting their building codes to require what the old builders did by instinct — minimum standards for shading and insulation, “cool roofs” that reflect the sun the way the whitewashed villages always did, rules that a new building must be able to hold a survivable indoor temperature through a heat wave without relying wholly on a machine that might fail when the grid goes down. Architects have a slightly clinical name for the revived discipline — “passive survivability,” the capacity of a building to keep its occupants alive through an extended power outage in extreme heat or cold — but the idea is as old as the courtyard and the thick wall. The point is not to abolish air conditioning; it is to build so that when the air conditioning fails, as it did for the hundred and twenty thousand French households who lost power in the June heat, the house does not immediately become a death trap. The Romans, who had no electricity to lose, built for passive survivability as a matter of course, because for them there was no other kind. We are relearning, at some expense, that a building which cannot keep you alive without a functioning machine is a building with a fatal dependency.

I should enter one honest limit here, because I promised throughout this series to refuse the easy comforts, and passive cooling has a ceiling. The old techniques work by moving heat around cleverly — shading it, storing it, venting it, cooling the air by evaporation — but they cannot make a place colder than the physics of its climate allows, and in the most extreme conditions the world is now producing, that is not enough. When the air itself is at 45 degrees and the night refuses to fall below 30, when even the sea has stopped cooling, a windcatcher can only bring you so far. There are places and days on which nothing short of active, powered cooling will keep a vulnerable person alive, and it would be a cruel romanticism to pretend otherwise. The lesson of the Persians is not that we can dispense with the machine. It is that we should lean on it far less than we do — build so that we need it only in the genuine extremes, rather than treating it as the sole and constant defense against a heat our architecture has simply given up on managing. The old wisdom does most of the work. The machine finishes it. Between them, done right, they could keep the world’s buildings habitable on a fraction of the energy — which is precisely the combination a warming, crowded, energy-constrained century is going to require.

The lesson of this essay, and very nearly the lesson of the whole series, is that adaptation does not always mean inventing something new under pressure. Sometimes it means recovering something old that we were foolish enough to discard. The future of staying cool on a hot planet looks, in part, remarkably like the distant past — like a tower catching the wind over a desert city, like a fountain in a shaded courtyard, like a thick wall holding the night’s coolness into the heat of the day. The Romans knew. The Persians knew. We knew, once, everywhere people lived with heat. The task now is to remember it in time, and to build, at last, the house for the summer that is actually coming.

Which brings us, in the final essay, to that house — and to what it will take to build it.

Degrees

10 — the number of degrees Celsius by which a traditional Persian windcatcher, a tower of mud brick burning nothing at all, can lower the temperature of the rooms below it. We spent the twentieth century forgetting that such a thing was possible. We will spend a good part of this one remembering.
