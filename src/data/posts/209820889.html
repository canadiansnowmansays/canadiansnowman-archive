Last September I wrote about the gap in Toronto’s trail rules: every cyclist in this province is legally required to carry a bell, and no one is legally required to ring it when passing. I proposed the embarrassingly modest fix — one line added to the parks bylaw, mandatory audible signaling on passing — and asked readers for their own near-miss stories. Calgary already does this. It costs nothing. It has not happened.

I did not expect New York City to hand me the epilogue this quickly.

On August 4, 2026, as Stefanos Chen reported for The New York Times, City Council Speaker Julie Menin stood outside City Hall and unveiled seventeen separate bills aimed at electric bikes and scooters — a package assembled, by her own account, because the city had waited too long for tragedy to force the issue. The proposals range from banning the sale of certain high-speed e-bikes still legal today, to requiring delivery platforms to register commercially and share trip data, to harsher penalties for hit-and-run riders, to a trade-in program that would let people swap illegal high-speed devices for compliant ones. A council hearing is scheduled for September 30, where transportation, police, and consumer-protection officials will be questioned on all seventeen.

This is what regulatory catch-up looks like once a city runs out of runway. Toronto has not run out of runway yet. That is the entire argument of this addendum: we still have the runway New York just used up, and the data suggests we should use it deliberately, rather than discover — the way New York did — that we needed it after the fact.

The body count that forced the issue

New York's numbers are not abstract. As Emma G. Fitzsimmons reported for The New York Times, a 17-year-old, Gabriel Nacato, died in Lower Manhattan last week riding an illegal Deepower e-bike capable of roughly 30 miles per hour, well above the city's 15 mph limit for legal devices, when he collided with an SUV. He is one of at least twenty riders killed in e-bike and e-scooter collisions so far this year, up from thirteen over the same stretch of 2025 — eleven e-bike deaths against eight last year, nine e-scooter deaths against five. Chen's reporting also found 560 e-bike collisions and 795 e-scooter collisions through the third week of July alone, both figures up 28 percent year over year. New York's overall traffic fatality rate is on track to be one of the lowest on record this year — which only sharpens the point. Micromobility is the one category moving in the wrong direction while everything else improves.

The bills reflect a city trying to regulate a runaway category rather than a bell law’s worth of etiquette. Even so, Menin pointedly declined to advance a local version of Priscilla’s Law — the state bill, named for an educator killed by an electric Citi Bike rider in 2023, that would require e-bikes and e-scooters to carry license plates. If the hardest sell in a seventeen-bill crisis response is registration and plates, that tells you something about how modest Toronto’s still-unpassed bell-signaling ask really is by comparison. We are not even in the argument New York is having.

What Toronto’s own numbers say

I went looking for a Toronto equivalent to New York’s collision counts, and the comparison is worth sitting with, because it cuts in an unexpected direction.

A Freedom of Information request obtained by TorontoToday and reported in February found that the Toronto Police Service laid just 24 careless-driving-related charges against micromobility riders — e-bikes, e-scooters, bicycles, and other wheeled devices combined — in all of 2025. In 2024, that figure was 28; in 2023, it was 11. For context, TPS laid more than 3,800 comparable careless-driving charges against car drivers in 2025 alone — over 158 times as many. Six micromobility-linked deaths were recorded in 2024, five involving e-bikes and one an e-scooter. And notably, city and hospital data cited alongside that FOI reporting turned up no pedestrian deaths attributable to collisions with cyclists, e-bike riders, or e-scooter riders in the same window — a category where New York’s ledger, by contrast, includes pedestrians among the dead.

Part of that gap is structural rather than cultural: stand-up e-scooters remain flatly illegal on Toronto streets, sidewalks, bike lanes, and multi-use trails. City Council has twice declined to opt into Ontario’s e-scooter pilot program, in 2021 and again in 2024, while New York has let companies like Lime rent them legally in parts of the Bronx and Queens. Toronto’s answer to one whole category of the problem has simply been: not here. That is a blunt instrument, and it will not survive the next e-bike delivery boom without complaint, but it has kept a real slice of New York’s casualty count off our books entirely.

Toronto is not without its own enforcement theatre, mind you. Police ran a three-week micromobility “education and enforcement blitz” from August 25 to September 13 last year, timed to back-to-school season, and it produced 179 tickets citywide — a rounding error next to the 3,355 tickets issued in the same period for driving offences within 150 metres of a school. The blitz got a press release. It did not get a bylaw.

Scale matters here too, and I don’t want to lean on raw numbers the way a city hall press release would. Toronto’s population is roughly a third of New York’s. Even adjusting for that, the charge and fatality counts on our side are not simply smaller in proportion — they are smaller by a wider margin than population alone would predict, on a base of far less regulation. That is either evidence that courtesy and a modest bylaw framework have quietly been working, or evidence that we haven’t yet had the volume of high-speed illegal devices New York has, and are one delivery-app growth spurt away from finding out the hard way. I suspect it’s some of both, and I don’t think we should wait to find out which dominates.

The part of New York’s approach worth resisting

Not everything in the New York package deserves a Toronto echo, and the seventeen bills are already generating the friction you’d expect from anyone who has watched a city try to regulate its way out of a crisis in public. Gabriel Montero of the Worker’s Justice Project has warned that registration requirements aimed at delivery e-bikes would expose immigrant delivery workers to more police and immigration contact, arguing that this kind of enforcement disproportionately lands on Black, brown, and immigrant communities regardless of intent. Menin’s response — that the obligation falls on the apps, not the riders — is a reasonable design goal, but goals and outcomes are different things, and Toronto’s own large delivery workforce would be the group living with whatever we borrow from New York’s toolkit.

If Toronto ever gets serious about a bell-signaling bylaw, or the modernized-licensing idea I floated last year — working bells, brakes, and lights, tied to trail-maintenance revenue rather than punitive fines — the design has to be built so it doesn’t become a new avenue for the same enforcement asymmetry the Worker’s Justice Project is describing in New York. A rule aimed at making trails safer for an aging population and pedestrians should not turn into a rule that mainly gets enforced against the couriers who can least afford a bad interaction with police. That’s a design constraint, not a reason to abandon the idea.

Infrastructure is the quieter half of the New York package

Buried among the enforcement and registration bills is a subtler admission, noted in Fitzsimmons's Times reporting: some experts have called for wider bike lanes designed to hold both slower pedal cyclists and faster electric devices side by side, on the theory that a meaningful share of these collisions come from mismatched speeds sharing a lane built for one pace. Mayor Zohran Mamdani made a version of the same point, arguing that the presence or absence of proper infrastructure is often the deciding factor in whether a given stretch of road turns dangerous — a line that could have been lifted straight out of a Vision Zero briefing.

Toronto has its own version of this conversation already on paper, in the trail design guidelines and the Municipal Code chapters governing multi-use paths that I dug through for the original piece — width standards, sightline requirements, the works. What we don’t have is the political attention New York is now being forced to pay to whether those standards are actually being met on the ground, on paths like the Beltline or the Don Valley trail system, where dog walkers, joggers, casual cyclists, and increasingly fast e-bikes are all sharing a corridor that was, in most cases, designed decades before anyone imagined a 32 km/h assist motor on it. New York is retrofitting its lanes under duress. Toronto could retrofit its trails on a calmer timeline, which is the entire advantage of not yet being in crisis.

The dog in this story is older now

One personal note, since I built the original piece around 6,500 kilometres logged with my husky. Dior is showing early signs of arthritis, and on the vet’s advice we’ve cut back the riding considerably this year — so if this reads as a slightly more armchair addendum than last September’s dispatch, that’s why. The trails haven’t gotten safer in the meantime. I’ve just been on them less to notice.

A brief acknowledgment, and nothing since

For what it’s worth: I sent last September’s piece to my city councillor directly, on the theory that a modest bylaw ask is exactly the kind of thing a councillor’s office can act on without needing a mayoral press conference. I received a brief acknowledgment and nothing further. No follow-up, no indication it went anywhere past the inbox. I’ll be forwarding this addendum too, though I’m not holding my breath that a second letter succeeds where the first one quietly didn’t. If nothing else, it’s a small, personal data point for the argument above: the runway I keep describing as available to Toronto is available in the abstract, and considerably harder to find in the actual correspondence of an actual councillor’s office.

Where this leaves the original ask

New York didn’t get seventeen bills because city council woke up one morning newly interested in micromobility. It got them because the informal, courtesy-based approach ran out of room, and it ran out of room in a city with far more high-speed illegal devices, far more delivery volume, and — not incidentally — far more political pressure once the death toll became impossible to ignore. Toronto’s numbers say we are not there. They also say we are the only city in this comparison that still gets to choose whether we arrive there deliberately, with a bylaw we wrote on our own terms, or belatedly, with a seventeen-bill package written in the aftermath of somebody’s Gabriel Nacato.

Twenty-four charges in a city this size is not a sign that nothing needs fixing. It’s a sign that nothing has broken badly enough yet to make fixing it politically unavoidable — which is precisely the condition under which good bylaws get written, and precisely the condition Toronto is about to spend or waste.
