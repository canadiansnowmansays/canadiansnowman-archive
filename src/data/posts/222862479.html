Don Davis was born in Snow Hill, a town of about 1,500 people on the coastal plain of North Carolina. He spent his Sundays at the local Presbyterian church and his summers cropping tobacco in the surrounding fields. At 29 he became Snow Hill’s youngest mayor. He went on to represent the town in the state legislature, and then in Congress.

He is now running for a third term, and if he wins, Snow Hill will not be in his district. Nor will his wife, his children or Greene County, where he still ministers at the church he grew up in. The Republican legislature cut them out. As Madeleine Carlisle reported in The Atlantic on October 30, 2026, Davis has promised that if he survives, he will move house to live inside the lines that were drawn to defeat him.

Part L of this series watched Missouri’s mid-decade map bounce between courts in a single afternoon. Part LX ranked the gerrymanders of 2026. This instalment looks at what may become the most instructive of them all: a map drawn with surgical precision to end one man’s career, which may end up demonstrating instead that a gerrymander is only a bet that voters will stay where you put them.

Three races, three maps

Davis first won in 2022 in a district that slightly favoured Democrats. The legislature redrew it to favour Republicans, and he held on in 2024 by less than two percentage points.

Then, in the summer of 2025, President Trump called on Republican legislatures to gerrymander every seat they could to protect the party’s House majority. Redistricting normally happens once a decade, after the census. North Carolina was one of seven states, red and blue, that hastily redrew in response. Four more followed after the Supreme Court’s decision in Louisiana v. Callais in April 2026 gutted the Voting Rights Act. Davis said at the time that 2026 would be his third race for Congress on his third different map.

The new First District is a careful piece of work. It drops four counties with large Black populations and adds six Republican-leaning ones along the coast. The Black share of the district falls from about 40 percent to about 32. Trump carried the old district by three points in 2024; on the new lines he would have won by almost twelve. North Carolina is about as evenly divided as any state in the country, yet its delegation already held just four Democrats among fourteen members. The legislature wanted three. The Cook Political Report labelled Davis the most vulnerable House Democrat in the country.

Democrats have represented the First District since 1899. On paper, that run was over.

The maths, and the arithmetic of anger

Davis’s opponent is the same one he narrowly beat in 2024: Laurie Buckhout, a retired Army colonel who commanded an 800-soldier battalion in Iraq and has since worked in a senior cyber-policy post at the Pentagon. She lost by about 6,000 votes last time. She understands perfectly what the map was designed to do. At a rally with the president in October, she told the crowd that these were Republican-majority districts, that it was simple maths, and that all they had to do was get out the vote. Trump turned to her afterwards and said he had heard Davis was a total disaster.

Simple maths, it turns out, is not so simple.

The race should have been over by now. Instead, recent polls show it close, and the two parties have spent more than $52.9 million on it, according to the ad-tracking firm AdImpact, which makes it the most expensive House general election ever recorded. Nobody spends fifty million dollars defending a seat that is already won.

Part of the reason is national. Trump’s approval in North Carolina has fallen to 34 percent in a recent Elon University poll, in a state that gave him 51 percent of the vote two years ago. Republicans are now forecast to gain fewer than half the seats they expected when they redrew the maps, largely because voters are angry about inflation, corruption and the Iran war. Ben Lindemann, 49, voted for Trump in 2024 and told Carlisle he will probably vote for Davis because he does not like the way Trump is ruining the country.

But the larger reason is local, and it is the map itself.

The voters who were moved

On Wednesday, October 28, 2026, early votes cast by Black North Carolinians were running 57 percent ahead of the same point in 2022, according to the Election Lab at the University of Florida. Voting-rights advocates say a backlash against redistricting is driving a surge in Black turnout across the South.

At the Nash County Democratic Party’s “Unapologetically Blue” banquet in October, more than 400 people turned up, the largest crowd in the event’s history. Carlisle heard the same complaint from table to table: the lines were unfair. Ernestine Stephenson, 69, said simply that they had all fought hard for their rights. Cynthia Pitchford grew up cropping tobacco in the 1970s, when there were few other jobs for Black people in eastern North Carolina. She liked Davis first because he came from the same country background. This year she began volunteering for him because she was so angry about the gerrymander; she saw it as a ploy to strip votes away from him.

At the homecoming game of Elizabeth City State University, a historically Black university, Davis worked the stands in a driving rain, shaking hands. People bring up redistricting all the time, he said. Nobody asked for it, and people feel left out.

That is the backfire in a sentence. A gerrymander assumes turnout is a constant. It treats the voters it moves as furniture, shifted from one room to another. But voters who are told, in effect, that their votes have been arranged so as not to matter are not furniture. Some of them stay home in despair, which is what the mapmakers count on. Some of them do the opposite.

A brand unto himself

Davis has made himself an awkward target in another way. He is not running as a Democrat so much as running as Don Davis.

His billboards line the highways with messages about lowering costs and thanking him for supporting veterans, and none mentions his party. He is an Air Force veteran who holds up his palms for reporters and calls them tobacco-cropping hands. In 2024 the tracking site GovTrack ranked him the most conservative Democrat in the House. He voted to end the 2025 government shutdown, to fund ICE, and to condemn socialism in all its forms. He was one of only fourteen Democrats who voted for this year’s Republican Farm Bill, arguing that the district’s farms needed it.

That record wins him voters no ordinary Democrat could reach. Thomas Joyner, a sweet-potato farmer, told Carlisle that Davis is the only Democrat he has ever voted for. Richard Anderson, 63, a tobacco grower and self-described independent who voted for Trump in 2024, said the China trade war wrecked tobacco’s profitability by leaving a glut of unsold crop, and that he will vote for Davis. He thinks the district proves people will cross the aisle.

The same record will cost Davis something with parts of his own party, and his votes and shifting positions on transgender issues, which Buckhout has made central to her campaign, deserve a fuller accounting than a single essay can give. That accounting belongs to the Loyal Opposition series, after the vote. For this series, the relevant point is simpler. The map was drawn on the assumption that the First District’s voters would sort by party label. Davis has spent his career refusing to wear one.

Buckhout, for her part, faces the problem every Republican faces this autumn. If your message is that the country is on the right track, how do you motivate voters who think it is not? Her answer has been fear: of socialism, of transgender athletes, and of unrest abroad. At the Trump rally she pointed to the turmoil in France and warned that the same chaos could come to America if voters stopped choosing Republicans, adding that she had fought “those people” before. Asked whom she meant, her campaign said violent extremists. Early-voting figures suggest Republican turnout in North Carolina is lagging.

The other dummymander

North Carolina is not alone. In South Texas, as Nick Miroff reported for The Atlantic on October 26, 2026, Republicans redrew Representative Henry Cuellar’s district using Trump’s 2024 margin rather than the closer results of local races, effectively drawing it for an electorate that has since moved. As Hispanic voters drifted back toward the Democrats, the district grew bluer instead of redder. Cuellar now leads by double digits, and the largest Republican House super PAC has pulled its money out.

Political scientists have a word for a gerrymander that overreaches and ends up endangering the party that drew it: a dummymander. The usual cause is greed, spreading a party’s safe voters too thinly across too many districts in pursuit of one more seat. The 2026 version adds a new ingredient. The maps were drawn in a hurry, mid-decade, on the president’s instruction, using the high-water mark of his own popularity as the baseline. They assumed 2024 was the new normal. They may prove to have been drawn for a moment that has already passed.

The second line of defence

Here is where this instalment connects to the last one, and where the good news needs a caveat.

If the First District is close on Tuesday night, the result will not be settled by voters alone. It will pass through county election boards. As Toluse Olorunnipa reported for The Atlantic in the October issue, North Carolina’s Republican legislators moved in December 2024, weeks before Democratic Governor Josh Stein took office, to transfer the power to appoint the chair of every one of the state’s 100 county election boards, and a majority of the state board, from the governor to the incoming Republican state auditor. Every one of those boards is now Republican-dominated, and several election deniers sit on them. The state has since moved to remove nonpartisan election supervisors, to challenge the eligibility of thousands of voters, and to make it easier to throw out mail-in ballots.

So the North Carolina story has two lines of defence for the party that drew the map. The first is the map. If the map fails because voters turned out in numbers the mapmakers did not foresee, the second is the machinery that counts and certifies those votes. Part LXI described how that machinery can be used: not to change a count, which is very hard, but to delay, question and cloud it until the decision drifts somewhere else.

That is why the size of the turnout matters as much as its direction. A narrow Davis win is an invitation to challenge. A clear one is not. The 57 percent surge in Black early voting is not only a protest against a map; it is, whether the voters intend it so or not, insurance against what might come after the map.

Where he will live

Davis says that if he wins, he will leave Snow Hill and buy a house inside the district’s new lines. It is a small, almost poignant detail. Federal law requires a member of Congress to live in the state, not the district; he could stay put. He has chosen instead to go where the voters are.

There is something in that choice that the mapmakers never accounted for. They treated the First District as a set of numbers to be rearranged, and Davis as a problem to be engineered out of existence. They forgot that the people being rearranged could see what was being done to them, and could answer it. In a year when the Supreme Court has hollowed out the Voting Rights Act and legislatures have redrawn maps at a president’s command, that answer, voters showing up in greater numbers precisely because someone tried to make their votes count for less, may be the most durable protection democracy has left.

Whether it is enough in the First District, we will know after Tuesday. Whether it is enough in the county boards that will count the result, we may not know for weeks.
