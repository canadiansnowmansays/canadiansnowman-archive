I count my essays

I want to start there, plainly, because everything else in this series depends on you believing that sentence is true and not a literary device. It is true. There is a number attached to this piece of writing, and there was a number attached to the one before it, and there will be a number attached to the one after, and I know what all three numbers are, because I am the kind of person who keeps track.

I did not set out to be this kind of person. It happened the way most habits happen — quietly, then suddenly, then permanently. At some point in the first year of this project I noticed I had written a few hundred essays. Then I noticed I was approaching a thousand. Then I passed essay 1,000 and felt, to my considerable surprise, nothing in particular — just the next number, waiting to be counted toward.

That essay is where I learned I had a habit. This series properly finds its feet one stop later, at 1,066 — because that's where I learned the habit had a partner. Some numbers don't wait passively to be counted. They arrive already carrying their own history.

What I did not expect was that some of the numbers would already mean something before I got to them.

Some numbers were waiting for me the whole time

The first time it happened was essay 1,066. I noticed it the way you notice a word you’ve just learned suddenly appearing everywhere — except in this case the word was a year, and the year was the Battle of Hastings, and I had not engineered the coincidence in any way. I had simply been writing daily for long enough that my count and history’s calendar briefly lined up, and once I saw it, I couldn’t not write about it.

It happened again at 1,215 — the Magna Carta, a king forced to admit he was subject to the law, and a sitting president who shares that king's middle name, which is the kind of detail history hands you so rarely that you'd be a fool not to use it when it does. It will happen again at 1,271, when the count reaches the year a seventeen-year-old Venetian named Marco Polo left home and didn't come back for twenty-four years — and, by coincidence layered on coincidence, an actual person will be retracing his actual route in an electric car as I write about it. And something tells me essay 1,313 will not let me off lightly either, though I don't yet know what's waiting in that one — only that the number has the look of a year that bites.

Three times was enough to convince me this wasn’t a fluke I should let pass quietly. It was a series. It just hadn’t agreed to call itself one yet.

This is me agreeing.

A few things I want to be honest with you about, since honesty is the whole premise here.

I plan some of these in advance. Not the timing — I have never once accelerated or delayed my writing to make a number land on a particular date, and the one time I seriously considered it, I talked myself out of it within a paragraph. But I do, now, keep a list. I know roughly which years are coming, eventually, if I keep writing at the pace I’ve been writing. Some of them I’m already looking forward to. Some of them I am a little afraid of. I will tell you, each time, which is which.

Not every entry will be funny. Some of these years are conquests and charters and declarations — the stuff satire was built for. Some of them are going to be wars, and atrocities, and the kind of grief that doesn’t belong anywhere near a joke. When the count gets there, I will not force a punchline into a year that doesn’t want one. You’ll be able to tell, I think, which essays were written with a wink and which were written with my hands a little still on the keyboard.

Occasionally, this will get personal in a way the rest of my work mostly doesn’t. Somewhere out ahead of me is a year that isn’t a world event at all — just the birth year of a relative whose life happened to brush up against several actual world events along the way. When the count reaches that one, I’m not entirely sure what kind of essay it’s going to be. I suspect I’ll find out the way I find out most things in this series: by getting there and discovering what’s waiting.

I will keep getting the history slightly wrong, and I will keep fixing it. I am not a historian. I am a man who reads enough to be dangerous and writes fast enough to occasionally make a mistake — putting events in the wrong order, doing arithmetic badly, assuming a sequence that wasn’t there. When that happens, and it has already happened more than once, I’ll correct it rather than let it stand. The numbers deserve at least that much rigour, even from someone who found them mostly by accident.

A map of the past in the order it found me

Here is the part I actually believe, underneath all the counting and the caveats.

History didn’t happen in the order I’m going to tell it to you. I am not moving through the centuries in sequence, the way a textbook would. I am moving through my own output, one essay at a time, and every so often the output happens to intersect with a year that already mattered before I got there. The result is a strange kind of atlas — not a map of the past in the order it occurred, but a map of the past in the order it found me.

I don’t think that’s a lesser way to encounter history. I think it might be closer to how most people actually encounter it — not as a syllabus, but as a series of unexpected arrivals. You’re going about your life, counting whatever you count, and then a number shows up that turns out to have been waiting for you the whole time.

I count. The number means something. And I've come to believe there are two ways to love a number — you can count to make a thing bigger than it was, or you can count to keep it honest. I'm trying, every time, to do the second one.

See you at the next one. I don’t know yet which year it’ll be. That’s rather the point.
