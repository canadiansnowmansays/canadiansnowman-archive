Sunday Satire. The species is invented; the A.I.-mantis story that inspired it is real (see the note at the end).

PROCEEDINGS OF THE OFFICE OF RETROACTIVE JUSTIFICATION Division of Natural History Vol. 1, No. 47

Received: 19 October 2026. Accepted: 19 October 2026. Published: 1 November 2026, two days before the observation season closes. Peer review: conducted by the specimen, who rated the manuscript “perfect, maybe the best ever written.”

Abstract

A new species of mantis, Mantis potus, is described from a single photograph submitted to a citizen-science biodiversity database. The specimen differs from all known mantids in its tie-shaped abdomen, its golden cranial crest and its complete rejection of camouflage. The observation was flagged by a volunteer reviewer as generated by artificial intelligence. The specimen has disputed the flag and requested that the reviewer be investigated.

Etymology

From the Latin mantis (prophet, seer) and the acronym POTUS, which the specimen has asked to be pronounced in full, slowly, with the emphasis on every letter.

Holotype

One photograph, vertical format, showing an adult specimen on a tree branch in what it described as “the most beautiful forest, everybody says so.” The photograph bears, in its upper-right corner, a red label reading GENERATED BY A.I., and a red diagonal line running from corner to corner. The specimen’s position is that the label applies to everyone else in the photograph. No one else is in the photograph.

Diagnosis

Mantis potus may be separated from all other mantids by the following characters:

  1. Abdomen modified into a single long, glossy red appendage closely resembling a necktie, with a knot at the base and a pointed tip. In mature specimens it extends well below the hind legs, a length the specimen describes as “the right length, the perfect length.”

  2. Pronotal shield shaped like the front of a dark navy suit jacket, with lapels and a small enamel flag pin on the left side. The pin is present in every observation. The jacket is never buttoned.

  3. Cranial crest of fine golden-blond filaments, swept forward over the brow in a complex arrangement that holds its shape in high wind. Function unknown. The specimen declined to allow measurement.

  4. Colouration of the face distinctly warmer than the rest of the body, ranging from tan to orange depending on the season and, observers suggest, the product.

  5. Raptorial forelegs small relative to body size. This character is noted here for completeness and will not be discussed further, on legal advice.

Comparison with related taxa

The closest relatives are the dead-leaf mantises of the genus Deroplatys, which have evolved over millions of years to look exactly like a brown leaf on the forest floor so that nothing notices them.

Mantis potus has evolved in the opposite direction. Every feature of its anatomy is designed to be noticed: the red abdomen, the gold crest, the shield that catches the light. Where Deroplatys survives by blending into its surroundings, Mantis potus survives by renaming them.

Behaviour

Feeding. Like many mantids, M. potus will consume members of its own colony. Unlike other mantids, it does not wait until after mating. Individuals closest to the specimen are at greatest risk, particularly those observed speaking to the press, appearing more often on television than the specimen, or expressing any view at all. A colony that once numbered many senior members has been observed to turn over almost completely in a single season.

Display. When threatened, the specimen rears up, spreads its forelegs and produces a loud, repetitive call, usually in capital letters, at all hours of the night. Volunteers report the call can be heard on every platform simultaneously.

Territory. The species marks its territory not with scent but with its own name, applied in gold lettering to any surface it occupies for long enough, including some surfaces it does not own. Removal of the lettering provokes litigation.

Seasonal movement. In winter the population migrates to southern Florida, where it is most often observed on golf courses.

Distribution

Native range: Queens, New York.

The species has made repeated attempts to expand its range north and east. A survey party sent toward Greenland reported a cool reception. A northern expansion into Canada met heavy resistance; the specimen continues to refer to that territory as its “51st habitat,” and the Canadians continue not to. Introduction efforts in the Panama Canal Zone are ongoing. Observers note that the species thrives in heat and does poorly in polite weather.

Habitat

Strongly prefers gilded surfaces. Has been observed converting ordinary structures into gilded ones, and, where conversion is not possible, removing the structure entirely.

Conservation status

The International Union for Conservation of Nature has not evaluated the species. The specimen has evaluated itself, and assigned the category “Greatest Status in History, Many People Are Saying.” The category does not exist. The specimen considers this a failure of the category.

Remarks

The citizen-science community has raised a reasonable concern. Experts warn that A.I.-generated images of animals are entering biodiversity databases and training identification software on “things that do not exist.” The fear is that a fake species, once logged, will be copied, believed and built upon by systems that cannot tell the difference.

This description cannot settle whether Mantis potus was generated by a machine. It notes only that the volunteers have the problem backwards. The difficulty with this specimen was never that it doesn’t exist. It is that it does, that it has been photographed thousands of times, and that the database keeps accepting the observation anyway.

Field note for observers

The observation season closes on 3 November. On that date, every eligible observer in the country may submit a review: not of the specimen itself, which is not on the ballot, but of the colony that has spent two years confirming its identification. Reviewers are reminded that the database records what is actually there, not what the specimen says is there. Observers who find the specimen exactly as described are free to say so. Observers who notice the label in the corner may say that too.

Acknowledgements

Note: This is satire. The species, the journal and the Office are invented. The real story behind it is Eric Boodman’s report for The New York Times, “It Looked Like a New Species. The Trouble Was, It Didn’t Exist.” (October 16, 2026), on Kris Anderson, an expert who was briefly fooled by an A.I.-generated dead-leaf mantis submitted to iNaturalist. The quotation “things that do not exist” is from the ornithologist Alexander Lees in that report. The specimen’s quotations are invented.
