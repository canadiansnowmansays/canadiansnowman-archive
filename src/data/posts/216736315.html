I. “A Very Good Negotiating Chip”

There is a sentence Donald Trump said in May, after the Beijing summit, that deserves more scrutiny than it got at the time. Asked about a proposed $14 billion sale of missiles, anti-drone weaponry, and air-defence systems to Taiwan, Trump didn’t deny the package existed or promise to move it forward. He described it, instead, as leverage. “It’s a very good negotiating chip for us, frankly,” he said.

Read that sentence again as a statement of policy rather than a passing remark, and it tells you almost everything about how this administration has decided to treat an island whose defence has been, since 1979’s Taiwan Relations Act, a bipartisan American commitment functionally immune to the ordinary swings of presidential preference. In Trump’s first term, the arms sales moved: roughly $18 billion worth, approved steadily, with hawkish officials treating Beijing as the defining long-term threat to American interests in the Pacific. In his second, the same category of sale has become something closer to a poker chip — valuable precisely because it hasn’t been cashed in yet, and less valuable the moment it is.

II. The Muted Pentagon

The freeze isn’t just happening in the Oval Office. Officials across the administration, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth prominent among them, have conspicuously avoided saying the word “Taiwan” in speeches where an earlier administration would have used it as a matter of routine — a pattern reporters covering the Pentagon have noted explicitly enough that it now counts as its own small story. This is not silence born of indifference. It is silence as instrument, the diplomatic equivalent of not naming a price in a negotiation you don’t want to accidentally conclude. Xin Qiang, who directs the Center for Taiwan Studies at Fudan University in Shanghai, put the underlying stakes about as starkly as an academic is likely to: “There’s only one issue that could cause relations between both sides to break down completely or erupt in conflict, and that is Taiwan.” Demanding that Trump keep delaying the arms sale, he said, may be the single most important thing Xi wants out of the Washington visit.

He has reason to think he can get it. When the two men last met, in Beijing, Xi warned Trump directly that mishandling Taiwan tensions could be “extremely dangerous” for both countries — a reminder, delivered leader to leader, of the risk sitting underneath a relationship both governments otherwise prefer to describe in the bloodless language of “constructive strategic stability.” Trump appears to have taken the warning, or at least the leverage embedded in it, seriously enough to act on it for four months running.

III. What Xi Can Offer in Return

Diplomacy of this kind rarely runs in only one direction, and Beijing has its own instruments available if Trump decides the chip is worth less than the sale itself. Analysts expect Xi to hint, during the Washington visit, at costs attached to approval — further restriction of the rare-earth exports that badly strained American manufacturers earlier in this trade war, or a more symbolic but still pointed option: skipping the G20 summit Trump is set to host in Miami this December, an absence that would read, globally, as a verdict on how the Washington visit actually went.

David Sacks, who studies the US-Taiwan-China relationship at the Council on Foreign Relations, frames the whole sequence as a single continuous campaign rather than two separate summits. “I think in Beijing we saw the opening move in what will be a sophisticated play to get the Trump administration to reduce support for Taiwan,” he said. “Xi will use his meetings with Trump in Washington to continue to implement this strategy.” Ryan Hass, a former National Security Council China director now at Brookings, described the underlying mechanics in similarly unsentimental terms: Beijing benefits simply by prolonging the process, since every month of delay is a month in which China can close the military gap faster than Taiwan can acquire the capability to widen it back out. “The balance continues to tilt in Beijing’s favor,” as he put it — not through confrontation, but through patience, applied against an American negotiating posture that has made patience cheap.

IV. Taipei, Watching From Outside the Room

No government has less control over this exchange, and more riding on it, than the one actually being negotiated over. Taiwan’s president, Lai Ching-te, has spent his time in office pushing the island’s legislature to approve increased defence spending and American weapons purchases — in part because Washington itself has demanded exactly that kind of investment from its security partners, in Taiwan and elsewhere. Every month Trump sits on the $14 billion package makes that domestic argument harder for Lai to sustain, and longer delays carry a second, more structural cost: pushing Taiwan further back in a global queue for scarce military hardware that every likely buyer wants faster than manufacturers can produce it.

Taiwan has tried the tools available to a government with no formal diplomatic relations with Washington — cultivating Trump’s inner circle, hiring lobbyists, arranging visits from prominent supporters of the president. Some of that effort has backfired in small but telling ways: several Taiwanese officials travelling to Washington have reportedly been told by administration officials to curtail their visits to research institutions in the city, a signal, Hass suggested, of “a clear preference by President Trump to ensure Taiwan does not become an obstacle to his ambitions as it relates to China.” Laura Rosenberger, who oversaw China and Taiwan policy under the previous administration, offered a blunter diagnosis of the structural problem: a “top-down” White House organized around one man’s personal, freewheeling instincts is simply harder for any foreign government to read, model, or influence than the more institutionally predictable administrations Taipei has dealt with in the past.

V. The Ballast Nobody Can Ignore

And yet — this is the complication that keeps the Taiwan story from being a simple tale of an ally being sold out — the economic relationship underneath all of this has never been stronger. Taiwan surpassed China this year as the third-largest source of US imports, trailing only Mexico and Canada, driven almost entirely by semiconductors and the high-end components feeding America’s own AI boom. TSMC, the island’s flagship chipmaker, has committed $265 billion to expanding its Arizona operations, a figure large enough that Trump himself brings it up — usually alongside a debunked but frequently repeated claim that Taiwan’s chip-making expertise was originally “stolen” from the United States, a grievance he aired again in a radio interview last month. Chen Ming-chi, a Taiwanese deputy foreign minister, made the island’s case for itself in plainly commercial terms: “We are probably the most cooperative partner in the whole world when it comes to tariffs and investment in the United States.” Jeremy Chih-Cheng Chang, of Taipei’s Research Institute for Democracy, Society, and Emerging Technology, put the strategic logic behind that cooperation more bluntly still: “Presidents come and go, but Silicon Valley and Wall Street remain constant” — a bet that structural economic interdependence will outlast any single administration’s appetite for treating Taiwan’s security as a bargaining chip.

Underneath the diplomatic freeze, in other words, the military and trade relationships have kept deepening on their own momentum. American advisers were unusually visible at Taiwan’s annual Han Kuang military exercises last month; Taiwan’s defence minister spoke, coyly, of growing numbers of “American English teachers” — the open-secret euphemism for US military advisers whose presence irritates Beijing regardless of what Washington’s officials say about Taiwan in public. “Our friends in the U.S. government understand how important continued arms sales are for Taiwan’s defense,” said Chen Ming-chi, choosing to describe the relationship by what happens quietly rather than by what gets said in speeches.

VI. The TACO Scorecard, Updated

Part I of this series opened a scorecard, borrowed from The Beijing Opera‘s TACO framework — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — and predicted Taiwan would land closest to Threaten, delivered quietly, through omission rather than declaration. Three essays and a great deal of reporting later, that prediction holds up better than most of my early guesses tend to, but it needs a footnote the original scorecard didn’t anticipate: the omission isn’t unilateral. Beijing is threatening by not-saying; Washington is accommodating by not-doing; and the two postures, from the outside, are becoming difficult to tell apart. A government that withholds a weapons sale as leverage and a government that withholds a redline as courtesy are, this particular September, running the same play from opposite ends of the board. Neither wants to be the one who says “Taiwan” out loud first, because whoever does forfeits the ambiguity that’s currently working in their favour.

VII. What the Chip Is Actually Worth

Here is the uncomfortable arithmetic underneath this whole essay. A negotiating chip only retains its value for as long as it stays unplayed — which means Trump’s stated strategy requires him to keep not doing the thing Taiwan most needs him to do, indefinitely, for the leverage to keep paying off. Some experts quoted in recent reporting expect the freeze to hold at least through the end of this year; others think approval could come sooner, particularly once congressional pressure — from both parties, Sacks predicts — builds in the weeks after the summit concludes. Both outcomes leave Taiwan in the same position in the meantime: a US ally whose actual security depends on a decision neither government currently has an incentive to make.

Dong Jun’s silence at Xiangshan and Trump’s silence on Taiwan in his own administration’s speeches are not, in the end, morally equivalent gestures — one is a government declining to state a threat it still holds; the other is a government declining to honour a commitment it has already made. But structurally, this September, they are performing the identical function: both delay the one conversation genuinely capable of upending the fragile, performed warmth this whole series has spent three essays documenting. Whether that conversation happens at the summit, or gets deferred again into some future Washington Opera nobody has written the outline for yet, may end up being the single most consequential thing that either does, or doesn’t, get said on Pennsylvania Avenue this week.
