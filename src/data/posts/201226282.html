Somewhere in America this morning, a high-school history teacher is handing out a worksheet.

It is probably a fill-in-the-blank worksheet, or possibly a multiple-choice review sheet for an upcoming test, or perhaps a timeline exercise in which students are asked to place events in chronological order without being asked what the events meant or why they happened or what they cost the people they happened to. The teacher is probably not a bad person. He may be, in fact, a genuinely good person — patient, encouraging, committed to his students’ success. He may also, on autumn Friday afternoons, be a fairly good football coach.

This is not a caricature. This is, as Appelbaum documents, an accurate statistical description of the American high-school history classroom. More than a third of social-studies educators in American high schools are simultaneously coaching sports or teaching physical education. They have, on average, fewer years of experience in the subject they teach. They rely more heavily on textbooks and worksheets than their non-coaching colleagues, who tend to use primary sources more frequently, assign more writing, and engage students in the kind of interpretive discussion that is the difference between learning history and memorizing it.

Students have noticed. The joke — told in high schools across the country with enough frequency that it has become a kind of folk wisdom — is that all history teachers have the same first name: Coach.

How did it come to this? The answer involves a federal education law that was supposed to raise standards, a political system that was too cowardly to apply those standards to the subject that mattered most, and fifty years of accumulated consequences that have left fourteen percent of American students rated proficient in history — while, at the same moment, the country is celebrating the 250th anniversary of a republic they cannot describe.

I. The Law That Broke the Classroom

The No Child Left Behind Act, signed by President George W. Bush in January 2002, was the most ambitious federal intervention in American education in a generation. It established a nationwide accountability framework: schools would be required to test students regularly in core subjects, report the results publicly, and face consequences if their students did not meet proficiency standards. The goal was to expose and address the chronic underperformance of American schools, particularly in poor and minority communities where low expectations had long been the de facto policy.

The law was bipartisan — Ted Kennedy was among its Senate champions — and its ambitions were genuine. It was also, in its design, the product of a political compromise that would prove disastrous for American history education.

The compromise was simple: the law required testing in English language arts and mathematics. It did not require testing in history, science, social studies, or civics. This was not an oversight. It was a deliberate decision made by legislators who understood, from the history wars of the previous decade, that there was no politically acceptable version of a national history test. The 1994 standards battle — which ended with the Senate voting 99-1 to reject the history standards the government had commissioned — had made clear that any attempt to define what American students should know about American history would immediately become a battleground in the culture war. The safer course was to test everything except the thing most likely to produce a fight.

The consequences were immediate and lasting. Schools that were struggling to meet proficiency standards in English and mathematics — which was most schools, particularly in underfunded districts — made rational decisions about resource allocation. Tested subjects got more time, more qualified teachers, more investment. Untested subjects got what was left over. At the elementary level, as Appelbaum documents, the time devoted to social studies dropped by more than a third within a few years — 76 minutes per week lost from the curriculum. At the high-school level, the history classroom became the path of least administrative resistance for coaches who needed a full-time teaching assignment to qualify for their salary.

States have, since then, haltingly developed their own history standards and assessments. But thirty years of reform have produced, by the evidence of the National Assessment of Educational Progress, a slight net decline in student history proficiency. Fourteen percent of American students are rated proficient. The number has not improved. In some states and some demographic groups, it has gotten worse.

II. What Proficiency Actually Means

It is worth pausing on that fourteen percent figure, because it is easy to let it pass as an abstraction.

The National Assessment of Educational Progress — the NAEP, sometimes called the Nation’s Report Card — tests American students in history at grades four, eight, and twelve. “Proficient” does not mean expert. It does not mean the student can write a research paper on the causes of the Civil War or analyze the constitutional implications of the New Deal. It means the student has demonstrated a solid understanding of the subject matter — that they can read a primary source, place events in context, and explain cause and effect at a level appropriate to their grade.

Fourteen percent of American twelfth-graders meet this standard.

To put this in concrete terms: if you assembled a random group of twenty high-school seniors in any American city and asked them to explain what the Declaration of Independence actually claimed, or to describe the basic structure of Reconstruction and why it matters, or to identify the central argument of the civil rights movement and how it was made — roughly three of them would be able to do so with any reliability. The other seventeen would have some fragmentary information, some cultural associations, some images from movies or television, and very little in the way of a coherent framework for understanding the country they live in.

These are the citizens who will vote in the elections that determine the future of the republic. These are the jurors who will sit in judgment on its laws. These are the parents who will explain to their children what America is and what it means to be part of it. And most of them have, through no fault of their own, been systematically denied the tools they would need to do any of this well.

This is not an accident. It is a policy. The policy is called No Child Left Behind, and its successor Every Student Succeeds Act, and the long chain of federal and state decisions that have treated history as a subject too contested to be seriously taught in a nation that defines itself by its history.

III. The Canadian Comparison

I want to be careful here, because Canada is not an advertisement for how to do history education right.

The Canadian curriculum has its own distortions — its own long history of suppressing Indigenous perspectives, its own convenient national myths, its own resistance to honest reckoning with the residential school system and what it cost. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission’s 2015 report, which documented in devastating detail the abuse and cultural destruction inflicted on Indigenous children in state-run schools, has produced genuine curriculum reform in some provinces and tokenistic acknowledgment in others. Canada’s history education is not a model.

But Canada does test history. The provinces include social studies and Canadian history in their assessment frameworks. The political consensus required to develop history standards is contentious — Quebec’s relationship to the national story is a permanent complication, as is the ongoing debate about how to integrate Indigenous history into a curriculum historically designed to erase it. But the consensus-building is attempted. The subject is taken seriously enough to fight about.

In America, the political system decided, in 2002, that the subject was too serious to fight about — and resolved the problem by not fighting. By not testing. By not requiring any agreement about what American students should know about America. By treating the question of the national story as so explosive that the safest course was to have no policy on it at all.

The result is fourteen percent proficiency and the football coach in the history classroom.

Canada’s result is not dramatically better in raw numbers — history education is imperfect everywhere. But the Canadian political system did not officially conclude that the subject was unteachable. It did not institutionalize the abandonment of the national story. It did not decide, in effect, that the argument about the country’s history was so dangerous that it was safer to not have the argument at all.

That difference — between a political system that struggles with the history question and one that has officially given up on it — matters. Not because the struggling system always produces better outcomes, but because the act of struggling is itself a commitment to the proposition that the story is worth telling. Giving up on it is a commitment to something else entirely.

IV. What Is Lost in the Worksheet

The difference between a history classroom that uses primary sources and one that uses worksheets is not merely pedagogical. It is the difference between two fundamentally different understandings of what history education is for.

The worksheet model treats history as a body of information to be transmitted — a set of names, dates, and events that students should know, in roughly the right order, for purposes of cultural literacy or standardized assessment. This is not nothing. A citizen who knows that the Civil War happened before the civil rights movement, that the Constitution preceded the Declaration of Independence, that the Founding Fathers were not unanimous about anything, is better equipped than one who knows none of this.

But the primary source model treats history as an argument to be made — a set of questions about evidence and interpretation that students can engage with directly. What did Jefferson mean by “all men are created equal”? What does it tell us that the Constitution’s framers disagreed so sharply about representation? What can we learn from reading Frederick Douglass’s 1852 address — “What, to the American slave, is your Fourth of July?” — that we cannot learn from a textbook’s summary of it?

These questions do not have simple answers. That is precisely the point. The history classroom that engages with them is teaching students something more important than historical facts: it is teaching them that the American story is an argument, that the argument has stakes, and that they are participants in it. It is transmitting, in the most direct way possible, the creedal nation’s fundamental requirement — not agreement about what the proposition means, but commitment to arguing about it seriously.

The worksheet classroom teaches none of this. It teaches that history is a list of things that happened, that the correct response is to memorize the list, and that the exam will reward those who have memorized it most accurately. It does not teach that the story is ongoing. It does not teach that the student is part of it. It does not teach that the argument matters.

A creedal nation that teaches history as a list rather than an argument is not transmitting its creed. It is transmitting the appearance of its creed — the names and dates and events without the argument that gives them meaning.

V. The Reckoning

The 250th anniversary of the American republic is being celebrated, in significant measure, by a generation that does not know the republic’s history.

This is not an exaggeration. Fourteen percent proficiency means that eight-six percent of American high-school seniors — the citizens who will vote in the next election, serve on the next jury, raise the next generation — cannot demonstrate a solid understanding of the country’s history at a grade-appropriate level. The number has not improved in thirty years of education reform. The football coach is still in the classroom.

The people responsible for this outcome are not primarily the coaches, who are doing their best with a job they were handed. They are not primarily the students, who cannot be expected to demand an education they were never told they were missing. They are the politicians who decided, in 2002, that history was too contested to test — and who have not, in the twenty-four years since, found the political will to revisit that decision.

Appelbaum identifies this as a political cowardice whose consequences compound over time. Every year that passes without a serious national commitment to history education is another cohort of students who reach adulthood without the tools to be participants in the argument about the creed. Every cohort that reaches adulthood without those tools is a larger constituency for the people who want to replace serious historical argument with mythology — whether the mythology of uncritical celebration or the mythology of total condemnation.

A creedal nation reproduces itself through argument. The argument requires participants who know enough about the story to argue about it seriously. The participants have to be taught. The teaching requires a curriculum. The curriculum requires political will.

The political will is not there.

But the hunger is. The fourteen percent who are proficient did not get that way by accident. They had teachers — some of them coaches, some of them not — who cared enough to make the argument come alive. They had parents who talked about history at the dinner table. They had access to books, documentaries, podcasts, newsletters that treated the American story as worth engaging with seriously.

The hunger for the story is not in question. The supply is.

At 250, that is the reckoning. Not that Americans don’t want to know their history. Not that the story is too complicated to be told. But that the institutions responsible for telling it have, through a combination of political cowardice and misaligned incentives, largely stopped trying.

The birthday party is this July. The homework is still not done.
