A house does not catch fire because someone strikes a match. It catches fire because, long before the match arrives, something has already dried the wood. This week, the ground I wrote about in June finally gave up what the heat had been asking of it since spring.

I ended Part I of this series with a number I told you I would rather have been wrong about: the count of the dead, mostly young, who drowned in France seeking relief from a heat wave that had turned the water colder and crueler than it looked. I wrote that essay in the immediate aftermath of a June that Copernicus, the European Union’s climate monitor, confirmed as the hottest ever recorded in Western Europe. I thought, at the time, that the heat wave was the story. I was wrong about that — not factually, but in scale. The heat wave was the opening chapter. What is now the largest wildfire season in the recorded history of both France and Spain is the chapter that follows, and it was never a separate story. It was the same story, continuing to do what it does.

By this week, the scale had become difficult to summarize in a single sentence, so I will not try. More than 300,000 people across the two countries have evacuated or sheltered in place. Flames have come within a few miles of both Bordeaux and Madrid. At least two people are confirmed dead in Spain. France’s interior minister has described the Gironde fire as behaving “in a completely erratic manner,” self-sustaining, spreading in all directions at once. None of that is this essay’s subject, exactly. This essay is about why.

This essay is about the mechanism — why a landscape that survives one heat wave becomes a landscape that cannot survive the next one. A second essay, arriving Thursday, will take up what this crisis is costing the people, industries, and politics built on top of that ground. Today is about the ground itself.

The mechanism, not the headline

Here is the physics I skipped past in June, because that essay had other work to do.

Warm air holds more moisture than cool air — roughly seven percent more for every additional degree Celsius. That fact, on its own, sounds almost pleasant, the kind of thing you’d read on a science placemat. It is not pleasant. It means a warmer atmosphere has a larger appetite for water, and it satisfies that appetite by pulling moisture out of whatever is nearest and least defended: topsoil, root systems, standing timber. Climate scientists call this the atmosphere’s thirst — vapor pressure deficit — and the term matters because it is not a metaphor. It is a measurable, worsening gap between how much moisture the air wants and how much the ground has left to give it, and Western Europe has been running that deficit since the same June that killed people through heat and drowning.

A landscape does not register a heat wave as a single event and then recover. It registers it as a withdrawal from an account that a wetter spring was supposed to have topped up, and this year’s spring did not top it up nearly enough. By the time July’s fires started, the fuel — pine forest in the Gironde and the Landes, scrubland and holm oak in the mountains west of Madrid — had already been desiccated by weeks of a debt the atmosphere had been running against it. Météo-France, the French weather service, confirmed as much on July 22, reporting that soil moisture levels across the country had reached unprecedented lows. The match, when it eventually came, was almost incidental. The house was already dry.

What burns, and why it burns differently now

Firefighters draw a distinction that is more useful here than anything I could invent: they separate “live fuel” — the standing, green vegetation of a healthy forest — from “dead fuel,” the fallen branches and desiccated undergrowth that carries a fire fastest. A forest running the kind of vapor pressure deficit Western Europe has run since June is a forest converting its live fuel into dead fuel in real time — the pines of the Landes are not merely surrounded by dry kindling, they are becoming it, needle by needle.

Two people who are not climate scientists arrived at this same argument independently this week, and I trust them more than I trust myself to make it. Roger Peuron, 89, a retired engineer near Saint-Médard-en-Jalles, in the Gironde, pointed out that the region’s pine forests were planted generations ago to drain swampland, not to withstand fire — their sap is flammable, their roots shallow. “The pines were planted to drain the region’s swamps, but they’re no longer suited to the environment,” he said. Víctor Resco, a professor of forest engineering at the University of Lleida, made the same point about Spain from the institutional side: decades of declining rural grazing and logging have left the countryside around Madrid loaded with untouched brush that no one is thinning anymore. “Fires now find fuel everywhere,” he said. An 89-year-old landowner and a forestry professor, working from opposite ends of the credibility spectrum, arrived at the same sentence: the house was built for a climate, and a land-management regime, that no longer exists.

The cloud with no rain

The detail from this week that deserves more attention than it has gotten is a phenomenon officials in France started calling by its proper name only after the Gironde fire had already grown large enough to require one: a pyrocumulonimbus, or “fire cloud.”

A fire of sufficient size creates a rising column of superheated air that acts, in effect, like a chimney — it draws in surrounding wind at ground level, feeding oxygen back into the blaze, while the rising heat cools and condenses at altitude into an actual cloud, capable of producing lightning and embers but, according to Britain’s Royal Meteorological Society, almost never rain. NASA has studied the phenomenon closely enough to warn that these fire-generated clouds are “linked to extreme fire behavior that impedes firefighting efforts and can devastate communities,” and that the risk is worth understanding precisely because fire activity and severity are both still climbing. One pyrocumulonimbus over the Gironde generated new blazes on its own over the weekend, according to Marc Vermeulen, the region’s director of fire and emergency services.

The line that stopped me came from Lt. Col. Éric Brocardi, a spokesman for France’s National Federation of Firefighters, who noted that this kind of fire is common in Canada and Australia — and unprecedented in France. I want to sit with that sentence for a moment, because a French firefighter just did, unprompted, the exact work this series has been trying to do since Part IV: he named the Canadian wildfire smoke I wrote about a month ago as the same species of event now arriving in his own country, on his own soil, for the first time. Canada and Western Europe are not experiencing two different climate stories that happen to share a summer. They are the same story, in two forests, on two continents, and the fire cloud is the clearest possible proof — it is not a French phenomenon or a Canadian phenomenon. It is what any sufficiently desiccated forest does once a fire inside it gets large enough.

No time to recover

The single fact from this week’s reporting that belongs closest to this essay’s thesis is not a fire statistic. It is a forecast. A new heat wave — the fourth to hit the continent since May — arrived Monday, with parts of Western Europe reaching 40 degrees Celsius by midweek. Spain’s national weather service has warned of highs near 42 degrees Celsius in the south before the week is out. Firefighters in both countries were given what one of them, Lt. Col. Brocardi, called a brief window “to race against the clock” — a night or two of rain-assisted relief before the heat returns and, with it, the wind.

This is what I mean when I say the deficit does not reset. A forest that just spent days losing whatever moisture it had left to firefighting and the fire itself does not get a rain-fed recovery before the next heat wave arrives — it gets another withdrawal, on a balance already at zero. The IPCC said in 2021 that “fire weather” would likely keep increasing across the Mediterranean over the course of this century. Reading that in a report is one thing. Watching the fourth heat wave since May arrive before the season’s fires are even declared contained is another. One French fire official, Lt. Col. David Annotel, put the timeline in terms that should unsettle anyone expecting a tidy ending: the Gironde fire, he said, will not be declared extinguished “for several weeks or even months.”

The record that keeps being broken

I want to resist the framing, common to this kind of coverage, that treats each summer’s fire as a shocking outlier rather than a data point in a trend anyone can plot. France’s Prime Minister, Sébastien Lecornu, put a number to it this week that I think should be the one people remember: more than 116,000 hectares — nearly 287,000 acres — burned in France since January, against a previous full-year record of 72,000 hectares set in 2022, a season considered so devastating at the time that it prompted the country to overhaul its firefighting resources entirely. This year has already burned through that record by more than half again its size, with two months of the fire season still ahead. Spain’s numbers tell the same story from the other side of the border: more than 425,000 acres burned so far this year, Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez said Monday — more than six times the pace of the same point last year. President Emmanuel Macron of France, visiting Bordeaux’s fire department this week, put it more starkly than I would have allowed myself to: “The situation we’re facing today is the hardest we’ve ever recorded, the hardest since World War II.”

A single catastrophic fire is a tragedy. A fire season that breaks the previous record by more than half, on two continents, in the same summer that produced a fire cloud nobody in the country had seen before, is a system finding a new equilibrium — and the equilibrium is not in our favor.

There is no tidy consolation to offer at the end of this one.

The heat wave I wrote about in Part I did not end in June. It changed forms, the way a debt changes forms when it goes unpaid long enough — from a temperature on a thermometer, to a moisture deficit in a forest floor, to a cloud that makes its own lightning and refuses to rain. The house was built for winter. It is currently finding out, one degree and one dry season at a time, what happens when the season it was built for stops arriving on schedule. Thursday's essay picks up where this one leaves off: not why the ground gave out, but what it costs the people standing on it when it does.
