The Starlink constellation in real time: not a telecommunications network but a planetary infrastructure. Three billion people lack reliable internet access. The physics has always permitted this solution.

The first principles question behind Starlink is as elegant as anything in the Musk stack: why does internet connectivity depend on ground infrastructure that can’t reach most of the planet?

The conventional answer is that laying fibre optic cable and building cell towers is how telecommunications has always worked. The infrastructure follows population density. Dense cities get fast internet. Rural areas get slow internet or none. Oceans, deserts, forests, mountains, and the southern hemisphere’s vast uninhabited spaces get nothing. Approximately three billion people on Earth lack reliable internet access — not because the physics prevents it, but because the economics of ground infrastructure do not justify the investment.

First principles says: put the infrastructure in orbit. Satellites cover the entire planet. A constellation of satellites in low Earth orbit — close enough for low-latency connections, numerous enough for continuous coverage — could deliver broadband internet to any point on Earth’s surface regardless of local infrastructure, population density, or national telecommunications policy.

This is brilliant. It is also, as this essay will argue, the point in the Musk stack where first principles thinking about physics transitions into something more complicated: the accumulation of strategic power at a scale that raises questions no amount of engineering brilliance can answer.

Starlink launched its first operational satellites in May 2019. By April 2026, the constellation comprises most of the roughly 14,000 active satellites orbiting Earth. More than ten million customers across at least 150 countries pay for the service. Airlines including United and Qatar Airways use Starlink for in-flight Wi-Fi. Maritime vessels, military units, humanitarian organisations, and remote research stations depend on it. Starlink has become, in less than seven years, the dominant satellite internet provider on Earth.

The engineering achievement is genuine. SpaceX’s reusable Falcon 9 rockets — the product of the first principles revolution documented in Part IV — reduced the cost of launching Starlink satellites to a fraction of what any competitor would pay. SpaceX launches its own satellites on its own rockets at its own marginal cost. Every competitor must purchase launch services at market rates from SpaceX or from more expensive alternatives. This vertical integration between launch provider and satellite operator is Starlink’s structural moat, and it is a moat no competitor can replicate because no competitor owns a reusable orbital-class rocket fleet.

A Starlink train crosses the night sky — sixty satellites launched in a single Falcon 9 mission, deploying in formation before dispersing to their orbital slots. Astronomers have been complaining since 2019. SpaceX has been launching since 2019.

The satellites themselves are manufactured at SpaceX’s facility in Redmond, Washington, at a pace and cost that traditional satellite manufacturers cannot match. Each Starlink satellite is a flat-panel design massing approximately 260 kilograms, equipped with phased-array antennas, krypton-ion thrusters for orbital manoeuvring, and inter-satellite laser links that allow data to pass between satellites without routing through ground stations. SpaceX launches approximately 60 satellites per Falcon 9 mission, and with launch cadence now exceeding one mission per week, the constellation grows at a rate that compounds the already insurmountable lead over competitors.

The first principles analysis is clean. The execution is impressive. The result is a global telecommunications service built by a rocket company that did not exist twenty-four years ago. If the story stopped here, it would be one of the great engineering narratives of the century.

The story does not stop here.

SpaceX has applied to the Federal Communications Commission for permission to launch up to one million satellites. One million. The current constellation of 14,000 already constitutes the majority of active objects in low Earth orbit. One million satellites would fundamentally alter the orbital environment — filling the usable bands of low Earth orbit to a density that raises serious questions about collision risk, space debris cascading, astronomical observation interference, and the ability of any other entity to operate in the same orbital space.

The application serves multiple stated purposes. Starlink’s next-generation satellites — larger, more powerful, and capable of direct-to-smartphone connectivity — require denser constellations to achieve the bandwidth necessary for mobile service. The orbital AI data centres that TeraFab’s chips are designed to power will require their own satellite infrastructure. SpaceX’s space-based AI compute vision — Musk’s claim that 80% of TeraFab output will be deployed in orbit — demands a satellite constellation of unprecedented scale.

SpaceX launches its own satellites on its own rockets at its own marginal cost. Every competitor purchases launch services at market rates — from SpaceX, or from more expensive alternatives. This is the structural moat. No competitor can replicate it because no competitor owns a reusable orbital-class rocket fleet.

But the application also serves an unstated purpose that industry observers have noted with increasing alarm: filling low Earth orbit with Starlink satellites makes it physically difficult for competitors to occupy the same orbital space. There are only so many satellites that can circle Earth’s low orbit before the risk of collision becomes unacceptably high. By filing for one million satellites, SpaceX is effectively staking a claim on orbital real estate. If approved, and if SpaceX executes even a fraction of the planned deployment, the company will have established a de facto monopoly on the most valuable orbital bands — not through regulatory capture or anti-competitive behaviour in the traditional sense, but through the sheer physics of filling the available space.

This is where first principles thinking about connectivity — put the infrastructure where it can reach everyone — becomes something the first principles framework was not designed to evaluate. The physics of satellite internet is value-neutral. The strategic implications of one company — one man — controlling the majority of objects in Earth orbit are not.

Starlink has already demonstrated what monopoly control over satellite infrastructure means in practice.

During the ongoing conflict between Russia and Ukraine, Musk made decisions about Starlink access that directly affected military operations. In the early months of the invasion, Starlink terminals were provided to Ukrainian forces, enabling battlefield communications when ground infrastructure was destroyed. Later, Musk restricted Starlink service over Crimea, reportedly to prevent Ukrainian forces from using it to guide drone attacks on the Russian naval fleet. The restriction may have altered the course of a specific military operation.

Musk made these decisions personally. Not a government. Not a military commander operating under civilian authority. Not an international body with a mandate to regulate conflict. A private citizen who happens to control the satellite network made strategic military decisions based on his personal assessment of escalation risk.

Walter Isaacson’s biography recounts Musk reasoning that enabling a Ukrainian attack on the Russian fleet could provoke a nuclear response. Whether this assessment was correct is debatable. That a satellite internet CEO was making the assessment at all is not debatable. It happened. And it happened because one person controls a communications infrastructure that militaries depend on in active conflict zones.

Ukrainian forces used Starlink terminals to maintain battlefield communications when ground infrastructure was destroyed. Later, Musk restricted service over Crimea. He made that decision personally. Not a government. Not a military commander. A satellite internet CEO.

In Venezuela, after the U.S. capture of Nicolás Maduro in January 2025, Musk made Starlink service free. The decision was positioned as humanitarian. It also meant that a private American citizen unilaterally decided to provide free communications infrastructure to a country undergoing a political transition — a decision that would normally involve State Department deliberation, interagency coordination, and congressional awareness.

These are not hypothetical power projections. These are documented decisions that affected wars and political transitions, made by a man accountable to no electorate, no regulatory body, and no institutional check beyond his own judgement.

The next phase of Starlink transforms it from a satellite internet provider into a global mobile carrier — and the power implications scale accordingly.

Musk has publicly stated his intention: “You should be able to have a Starlink — like you have an AT&T or a T-Mobile or a Verizon or whatever.” Unlike traditional mobile carriers, Starlink’s satellite-based service would work on any cellphone anywhere in the world. No cell towers required. No national licensing per country (in theory). No dead zones. Universal coverage.

The transition is already underway. Starlink’s partnerships with more than a dozen mobile carriers provide satellite connectivity in areas beyond cell tower range. T-Mobile’s T-Satellite service allows messaging, location sharing, and low-speed data through Starlink. The next-generation Starlink satellites — the ones the million-satellite application is designed to support — promise bandwidth increases of more than 3,000%, enabling full smartphone connectivity without ground infrastructure.

Amazon recognised the threat. In April 2026, Amazon acquired GlobalStar for more than $11 billion and struck a deal with Apple to operate satellite internet on iPhones and Apple Watches. This positions Amazon as Starlink’s primary competitor — and illustrates the scale of investment required to challenge a company that has a seven-year head start, its own rocket fleet, and the majority of active satellites already in orbit.

The competitive dynamics are daunting for any challenger. SpaceX launches Starlink satellites at marginal cost on its own rockets. Amazon must purchase launch services. SpaceX has 14,000 satellites operational. Amazon’s Project Kuiper had launched approximately two dozen test satellites as of early 2026. SpaceX has ten million customers generating revenue. Amazon is still building. The window for meaningful competition is closing, and Musk is racing to close it by filling the sky before anyone else arrives.

If Starlink becomes a stand-alone mobile carrier — not a supplement to existing carriers but a direct competitor — the power implications extend beyond telecommunications into the architecture of information itself.

A relatively obscure practice called “zero-rating” allows telecom providers to exempt certain apps or websites from data caps. Users can access zero-rated services without consuming their data allowance, creating a powerful incentive to use those services over alternatives. Facebook’s Free Basics programme used this approach in developing countries, partnering with local carriers to provide free access to Meta’s family of apps — and only Meta’s apps. The result was that in many low-income markets, for many users, the internet effectively was Facebook.

Starlink has already demonstrated the zero-rating approach. T-Satellite’s select collection of usable apps includes X and Grok. It does not include Instagram or ChatGPT. This is not a technical limitation. It is a business decision about which services Starlink’s carrier partnerships will subsidise.

Now extend the logic. If Starlink operates as its own mobile carrier — not through T-Mobile or other partners but directly — Musk controls the zero-rating decisions entirely. He could make X and Grok free for all Starlink subscribers. In developed markets, this would be a competitive perk. In developing markets — where Starlink may be the only affordable internet option — free access to X and Grok while competitors’ platforms count against data caps would be an overwhelming inducement. The internet, for hundreds of millions of people, would be shaped by which applications Elon Musk chooses to subsidise.

Cross-promotion across the stack becomes trivial. Starlink service as a free perk for Tesla drivers. X Premium included with Starlink subscriptions. Grok as the default AI assistant on Starlink-connected devices. Each layer of the stack driving traffic to every other layer. Each user drawn deeper into an ecosystem where the social network, the AI, the encyclopedia, the telecommunications pipe, and the vehicle are all owned by the same person.

This is not speculation. Each element exists. The zero-rating precedent is set. The carrier ambition is stated. The cross-promotion logic is obvious. The only question is pace of deployment.

Douglas Adams imagined the Sub-Etha Net — a galaxy-wide communications network through which the Hitchhiker’s Guide was distributed to every traveller. The Sub-Etha Net was infrastructure so pervasive that no one thought about who owned it or what editorial decisions shaped the content flowing through it. It simply was. The Guide arrived. The information appeared. The traveller consulted it and moved on.

Starlink is the Sub-Etha Net. It will deliver Grokipedia — the Guide — to every connected device on Earth. The content will be shaped by xAI’s models, which are shaped by Musk’s priorities. The discourse about that content will occur on X, which Musk owns. And the pipe through which all of it flows — the satellites, the ground stations, the smartphone connections — will be Starlink, which Musk controls.

Adams wrote the Sub-Etha Net as background infrastructure. He never asked who owned it because the comedy didn’t require it. The real-world version requires the question. And the answer — one man — is not one Adams would have written as comedy. He would have written it as the setup for a chapter in which Arthur Dent discovers that the Guide has been acquired by a galactic conglomerate whose CEO also owns the transport system, the communications network, and the planet-construction contract, and the entire galaxy is slowly realising that they are all, in every meaningful sense, his tenants.

That chapter would have been very funny.

This essay is the non-fiction version. It is not funny at all.

To be clear: Starlink provides genuine value. It connects remote communities. It enables disaster response. It provides internet access to populations that ground infrastructure cannot reach. The first principles insight — put the infrastructure in orbit — was correct, and the engineering execution has been extraordinary. People who have Starlink in rural Canada, in the Amazon basin, in sub-Saharan Africa, on research vessels in the Southern Ocean, have connectivity they would not otherwise have. This is real. It matters.

But value and power are not the same thing. A service can be genuinely valuable to its users and simultaneously represent an unprecedented concentration of strategic power in the hands of one person. These two facts coexist. The telecommunications history of the twentieth century is a history of recognising this coexistence — of understanding that the telephone was transformative and that AT&T’s monopoly over the telephone required antitrust intervention. The internet was transformative. The platforms that captured its value required regulatory response.

Starlink is more comprehensive than either analogy. AT&T controlled the domestic telephone network. Starlink controls a global telecommunications layer that operates in 150 countries, serves military forces in active conflicts, and is transitioning to direct-to-smartphone service that could make it the world’s largest mobile carrier. The regulatory frameworks designed for national telephone monopolies and domestic internet platforms were not built for a global satellite network owned by a man who also controls the rockets that put it there, the social media platform it delivers, the AI it serves, and the encyclopedia it distributes.

First principles thinking built Starlink. First principles thinking cannot tell us what to do about it. That requires a different kind of reasoning — political, legal, democratic — that Musk has never been interested in and that the speed of his deployment may outrun.

The sky is filling. The question of who governs what it carries has not yet been asked with the seriousness it requires.
