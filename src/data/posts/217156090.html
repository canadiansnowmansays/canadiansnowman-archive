Part I of this series traced Carney’s rhetoric from a standing ovation in Davos to a press scrum at the UN. Part II checked that rhetoric against the economic data — growth, rates, jobs, debt — and found a genuinely mixed picture rather than a clean vindication either way. This piece exists because of a single sentence Carney gave to the New York Times on Wednesday, in a wide-ranging interview at the paper’s Midtown headquarters, that neither of the first two essays anticipated and that changes what “sovereign country” actually has to mean in practice.

“That’s just risk management”

Asked whether he took Trump’s repeated talk of making Canada the 51st state seriously, Carney did not dismiss the question. He confirmed he had spent the past year examining the “extreme tail risk” that the American president might order military action against Canada, declining to say in any detail what that examination had actually involved. His own framing of why a sitting prime minister would even do this kind of planning was clinical rather than alarmed: “I think you have a responsibility in these roles to look at extreme tail risk. That’s just risk management. That’s not a base case, but it would be irresponsible not to” prepare.

It is worth being precise about what was and wasn’t said, because the sentence is capable of being overread in either direction. Carney did not say an American attack was likely, expected, or even plausible in the near term — “not a base case” is doing real work in that quote. What he confirmed is that the possibility crossed a threshold worth formal government attention, from a former central banker whose entire professional method, at both the Bank of England and the Bank of Canada, was built around modelling worst-case scenarios before they happened rather than reacting to them afterward. That is itself informative about how seriously Ottawa has taken a threat most external observers, this series included, have generally treated as rhetorical excess rather than operational planning.

Diversification, now with specifics attached

What separates this interview from the “rupture” and “sovereign country” material in Part I is that Carney moved, for the first time on the record at this length, from diagnosis to itemised plan. Three specific dependencies came up by name.

Starlink. Carney was unambiguous: “Starlink, we will diversify away from, no question.” He tied the decision partly to Elon Musk’s own past reluctance to give Ukraine the data needed to target parts of Crimea and Russia — a demonstration, in Carney’s reading, that a single private American owner’s own political judgement can sit on top of a partner country’s military capability whether that partner likes it or not. He acknowledged that current alternatives are less capable and less resilient, but pointed to scale achieved by pooling with European and potentially Korean partners as the route out of the dependency rather than any domestic Canadian replacement.

Fighter jets. Canada’s planned order of 88 Lockheed Martin F-35s is under active review, with a Saab Gripen counterproposal on the table. Carney would not say where he personally stood, but named three factors driving the decision explicitly: “differing capabilities, because of strategic dependencies, and because of the economic return” — and specifically wants whichever aircraft is chosen to perform well in the Arctic, tying the decision to Canada’s newly increased defence budget rather than treating it as a straightforward like-for-like replacement.

Potash. This is the sharpest and most immediately consequential of the three, because it is not hypothetical — it is a live dispute Trump himself opened this same week. Canada supplies close to 90% of the potash the United States imports, the key ingredient in fertiliser. Trump said this week he wants to replace that supply with potash from Belarus, a Russia-aligned authoritarian state currently under US sanctions for its role in the Ukraine war. Carney’s counter was not a complaint but a specific proposed deal term: a North American “fertiliser independence” arrangement giving the US and potentially Mexico secured access to Canada’s reserves — the world’s largest — rather than sourcing from a sanctioned state whose supply would have to transit either the Black Sea or the Strait of Hormuz, both active conflict zones this series has covered extensively this month. “I think this is a doable component of the deal,” Carney said, treating it as a solvable negotiating item rather than a grievance.

Underneath all three specifics sat one recurring phrase, offered as the plainest possible explanation for why any of this diversification is happening at all: “There’s certain things, once you’ve seen them, you can’t unsee them.”

A fourth dependency, less concrete but more structural: the dollar itself

Carney used the same interview to restate a view he has held for years, well before this current dispute — that a global financial system wholly dependent on the US dollar is unsustainable — but framed it this time as accelerated rather than merely theoretical. He described a “directional shift” already underway, with countries re-evaluating their exposure to dollar-denominated reserves, and attributed part of that acceleration directly to the Trump administration’s own conduct: its decision to treat economic integration itself as “leverage” against partners rather than as a stable foundation to build on. “It’s the ‘fool me once, fool me twice’ point,” Carney said. “Once you see that, then you start to think, how do I diversify away?” He was careful to add a genuine constraint rather than present the shift as imminent — any real pivot toward a multipolar reserve system remains limited, in his own account, until there is “proper liberalisation” of China’s currency and financial system, a precondition entirely outside Canada’s control and not obviously close at hand. The dollar point matters for this series specifically because it is the one dependency Carney named that has nothing to do with Canada-US relations directly; it is a bet on where the entire international monetary order is heading, made by a former central banker whose career has been built on getting exactly that kind of structural call right before markets catch up.

The paradox this series has to hold honestly

Here is the part a series built on evidence rather than a fixed narrative has to sit with directly, because it complicates the picture Part I left readers with. Part I closed its central section on Carney’s escalating rhetoric — “at war,” “sovereign country,” “he’s speaking the language of war” — building toward an image of a relationship in open confrontation. This interview does not contradict any of those quotes; Carney said all of them and stands by the substance behind them. But in the same Wednesday conversation, he also said he speaks to Trump “frequently,” that they have spoken “a number of times” since the trade talks collapsed last month, and described the actual working relationship in strikingly unemotional terms: “From my perspective, we have a good relationship in the sense of we can pick up the phone and talk to each other about issues. He’s direct. I’m direct in terms of where positions are. It’s much more efficient that way.”

That is not, on reflection, actually a contradiction, though it reads like one at first pass. It is possible to describe a trade relationship as being at war while maintaining a direct, functional communication channel with the person on the other side of it — arguably that channel is more necessary, not less, precisely when the relationship has deteriorated as far as this one has. Carney’s own account of why the two men get along personally despite everything is almost mundane: both prefer directness over diplomatic hedging, which he frames as efficient rather than warm. A series built around Carney’s rhetoric owes it to readers to hold both facts in view rather than letting the sharper quote stand as the final word simply because it made a better headline in Part I.

Carney also made, in the same interview, an argument this series has not yet given him credit for: that Canadian and American interests remain deeply aligned in ways that argue against decoupling rather than for it. His example was the automobile industry — “My entire life, and I’m 61, we’ve had an integrated auto sector” — arguing that breaking that integration to repatriate all production to the United States “doesn’t make sense” as policy, for either country, regardless of who occupies the White House. He was explicit that a mutually beneficial trade deal remains available: “Is there a mutually beneficial trade deal between Canada and the U.S.? Absolutely, absolutely there is one.” Diversification, in Carney’s own framing, is risk management against a specific demonstrated pattern of behaviour, not a strategic decision to sever the relationship itself.

Where he draws the line

The clearest single data point for how far Carney is actually willing to go, as opposed to how far his rhetoric suggests, is energy. Roughly 60% of US oil imports come from Canada, and Canada also supplies substantial electricity to American households and industry — genuinely the most powerful leverage Ottawa possesses in this entire dispute, and the one lever domestic political pressure has pushed him hardest to use. Carney has refused. “If you’re a reliable partner and you supply energy or food to somebody it keeps running, it’s a very, very, very high threshold to change that,” he said. “Never say never, but it starts from a position of, we need to be a reliable supplier in all cases.”

That single refusal tells you more about the actual shape of Carney’s strategy than any of the “rupture” or “at war” language this series opened with. The diversification programme — Starlink, fighter jets, potash, reserve currencies — is aimed specifically at reducing Canada’s exposure to being coerced, not at building leverage to coerce in return. Energy, the one export that would genuinely hurt the United States if withheld, is the one thing he has explicitly declined to weaponise, on the stated principle that reliability itself is the asset worth protecting even in a relationship he has separately, and repeatedly, described using the language of war.

What this adds to the series

Taken together with Parts I and II, the picture that emerges by Wednesday of UN week is more coherent than any single quote from any single day would suggest: a government preparing, seriously and formally, for a worst case it does not expect; building concrete alternatives to specific American dependencies one at a time rather than as a wholesale rupture; maintaining a direct, functional channel to the man whose behaviour prompted all of it; and refusing, on principle, to use the one card that would actually hurt. Whether that adds up to “sovereign country” in the fullest sense Carney’s own phrase implies, or simply to a smaller, more disciplined form of self-protection dressed in bigger rhetoric, is a judgment this series will keep testing as the relationship continues to develop — because on the evidence of this single interview alone, it is clearly not finished testing itself either.
