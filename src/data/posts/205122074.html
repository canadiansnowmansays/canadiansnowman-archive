Postmark: Yizhuang, southern Beijing. A red humanoid crosses the line in fifty minutes flat, faster than any human has ever run the distance. Then it hits a barricade, falls, and waits for its handlers to stand it back up.

On April 19, on a course through an industrial district in southern Beijing, a bright-red humanoid robot named Lightning ran a half-marathon — twenty-one kilometres, thirteen miles — in fifty minutes and twenty-six seconds. As NPR reported, that time was faster than the human world record for the distance: Uganda’s Jacob Kiplimo had run it in about fifty-seven minutes in Lisbon a month earlier, and the robot, built by the Chinese smartphone maker Honor, beat him by nearly seven minutes. More than a hundred robots entered; at least four broke the one-hour mark. Hundreds of millions of people watched the livestream.

It is a genuinely astonishing thing, and this series is pledged to say so before it says anything else. One year earlier, at the inaugural running of this same race, the machines had been a comedy: of twenty-one entrants, only six finished, several stumbled or careened or simply lay down at the starting line, and the winner took two hours and forty minutes — slower than a leisurely human jog. From that humiliation to a world-record pace in twelve months is a rate of improvement that ought to stop anyone in their tracks. The curve this whole series has been tracing — dancing robots, factory robots, battlefield robots, home robots — is right here in one data point, running past the finest human runner who ever lived.

And yet. This postcard is the series finale of its second wave, and I want it to do the hardest thing a wonder-first series can do: hold the astonishment and the skepticism in the same hand, and come out the other side with something truer than either alone. Because Lightning also, near the end of its record run, crashed into a barricade, fell over, and waited for a human handler to pick it up. Both things are true. The machine ran faster than any human ever has, and it could not get up when it fell down. The entire meaning of the robotics moment is in the space between those two facts.

What the race actually proved

Here is where a roboticist’s oldest warning becomes the most useful instrument we have, and it comes from a man who has spent a career puncturing exactly this kind of spectacle.

As Scientific American reported, the veteran roboticist Rodney Brooks — co-founder of the company that gave the world the Roomba, and a career-long skeptic of robot hype — offered the necessary corrective. His objection was not to Lightning’s engineering, which he did not dispute, but to what the race was taken to mean. Lightning did not run a half-marathon under anything resembling human conditions. Its route was premapped — it followed a course it already knew, rather than navigating an unknown one. Its safety was untested — there was no interaction with real crowds, the human runners were kept in a parallel lane to prevent collisions. It had a retinue of support people and vehicles. People, Brooks noted, simply run a marathon; they do not need the track surveyed and cleared and shadowed by a support crew. The robot did.

Brooks has been making one version of this argument for a decade, and it is the single most important idea in this entire series, so let me state it in full: humans conflate performance with competence. When we see a machine perform impressively at one narrow thing, we unconsciously assume it possesses the general competence a human would need to do that thing — and we are almost always wrong. A human who can run a half-marathon can also tie their shoes, navigate a strange city, carry a conversation, and improvise around a fallen barricade. Lightning could do exactly one of those things, on a course it had memorized, and fell down at the one moment reality deviated from the map. The performance was real. The competence it implied was an illusion we supplied ourselves.

Other researchers quoted in the same coverage were gentler but converged on the same point. The race was, they said, primarily a hardware demonstration — a genuine and difficult achievement in the narrow, unglamorous, enormously valuable domain of heat management and structural reliability, keeping a machine running for fifty minutes without overheating or shaking itself apart. Honor’s own engineer said as much, noting the liquid-cooling system adapted from the company’s smartphones and musing that the real payoff would be in industrial applications, not athletics. That is the honest achievement: not a robot that can run, but a robot that can run for a long time without breaking, which is a real and bankable step toward a robot that can work a long shift without breaking. The record time was the headline. The thermal engineering was the news.

The money that outran the machine

If the robot outran the human record, the money outran the robot — and the funding story is where this series’ second wave finds its final and most consequential subject.

As CNBC reported, robotics companies raised 55.8 billion dollars in just the first stretch of 2026, according to the deal-tracking firm Dealroom. The individual rounds are staggering: Germany’s Neura Robotics raised up to 1.4 billion dollars in a single Series C, backed by Nvidia, Amazon, Qualcomm and others, at a valuation around seven billion. Pittsburgh’s Skild AI — which builds not robot bodies but the AI “brain” meant to run any robot — passed two billion in total funding at a fourteen-billion-dollar valuation. Figure, 1X, Apptronik, China’s Unitree and AgiBot heading for IPOs that would value the two Chinese champions together at some thirteen billion. Money is pouring into this field at a rate that even two years ago would have seemed unhinged.

And here the balcony’s particular vantage earns its keep, because a Canadian who has watched a Tesla share price detach from a Tesla’s fundamentals knows how to read a number like this. As market analysts have documented, humanoid-robotics funding behaves like a power-law asset class, not a normal venture market: a single deal can hold a third of all the capital, the top ten deals hold more than ninety per cent, and the aggregate totals are, in the analysts’ own dry phrase, essentially narratives about a handful of winners. Strip out the top five rounds and three-quarters of the money vanishes. This is not a broad, healthy, distributed bet on a maturing industry. It is an enormous, concentrated wager on a very small number of companies, placed by investors who cannot afford to be absent if the thing works and cannot easily tell, from the outside, which of the contestants is Lightning and which is the machine that lies down at the starting line.

The uncomfortable synthesis writes itself. The marathon and the money are the same phenomenon: both are performances that we, the watching public and the investing class, are being invited to read as competence. Hundreds of millions watched a robot run faster than a human and inferred that the robots have arrived. Investors watched the same curve and inferred that the returns will arrive. Brooks’s warning applies to the balance sheet exactly as it applies to the racecourse: a spectacular performance at a narrow thing — a fast run, a viral demo, a doubled forecast — tempts us to assume a general competence, a broad and durable capability, that has not actually been demonstrated. The battery still dies in two hours. The robot still falls at the unmapped barricade. The reliability gap between the lab’s ninety-five per cent and the real world’s sixty per cent is still, as every honest engineer in this field will tell you, the unsolved problem on which all the forecasts depend.

The state behind the spectacle

There is a dimension to both the marathon and the money that a Canadian, alert to how governments move markets, should not let pass — because neither the race nor the funding is a purely private phenomenon, and pretending otherwise misreads the whole picture.

The Beijing half-marathon was not a spontaneous festival of engineering enthusiasm. It was staged in Beijing E-Town — the Economic-Technological Development Area — by a development agency, with the winning company promised municipal orders worth over a million yuan, and it slots directly into the state strategy this series has documented since Part XI: China’s 2026–2030 plan naming embodied AI a national priority, the deployment directive, the standard-setting, the subsidies. The race was industrial policy wearing running shoes, the same way the Spring Festival Gala of Part IX was industrial policy wearing a folk costume. When Honor’s robot crossed the line first, it was not only a company winning a race; it was a state demonstrating, to hundreds of millions of its own citizens and a watching world, that the future it has bet the next five years on is arriving on schedule. The performance was aimed at an audience, and the audience was partly domestic morale and partly foreign investors.

And the money reflects the same state hand. The 55.8-billion-dollar private surge sits atop an even larger public foundation — China alone has committed a state venture-capital guidance fund reported in the range of 138 billion dollars to AI, robotics and allied sectors. The sober institutional forecasts, for their part, keep their heads: Bank of America projects around 90,000 humanoid shipments in 2026 rising toward 1.2 million by 2030 — real growth, but a long climb from a small base, not the overnight transformation the viral clips imply. The consistent message from the analysts who are not selling anything is that the supply chain, not the ambition or the capital, is the binding constraint — and the supply chain, as Part VIII established, runs through China. The marathon and the money and the manufacturing are three faces of one fact: the country that will set the pace of the humanoid century has decided to, and is spending accordingly, in public and in private, on the track and on the balance sheet.

The curve and the cliff

So where, at the end of two waves of postcards, does the balcony finally come down?

On wonder, still — but wonder with its eyes open, which is the only kind worth having. Everything this series has documented is real. The robots genuinely dance, genuinely fold laundry, genuinely haul cargo at Haneda, genuinely capture positions in Ukraine, genuinely run faster than Jacob Kiplimo. The money is genuinely flowing, the supply chain is genuinely Chinese, the price is genuinely collapsing, the home is genuinely being breached. None of it is fake, and the reflexive cynicism that dismisses the whole thing as hype is as wrong as the breathless credulity that takes every demo at face value. Something is happening here, and it is large, and it will reshape the century.

But the shape of the change will be decided in the space between performance and competence — in whether these companies can turn the dazzling narrow demonstration into the boring general reliability that actual work requires, and do it before the 55.8 billion dollars runs out of patience. That is the cliff beside the curve. Every technology that ever mattered had this moment: the point where the spectacular early performance either matured into durable competence or collapsed into a funding winter, and where the fortunes rode on which it would be. The dot-com crash was a power-law market discovering that performance had outrun competence. So, in a smaller key, was every AI winter. The humanoid field is now at exactly that inflection, funded at exactly the scale, and betting — 55.8 billion dollars’ worth of betting — that this time the competence arrives before the patience runs out.

I find, at the end of these sixteen postcards, that I believe it mostly will — that the curve is real enough and the money serious enough and the Chinese manufacturing base deep enough that the humanoid century is coming, unevenly, later than the demos promise and sooner than the skeptics expect, with a Chinese accent and a stranger occasionally behind its eyes. But I hold that belief the way Brooks would want me to: as a bet, not a certainty, with the fallen robot at the barricade kept firmly in view. The machine ran faster than any human ever has. It could not get up when it fell. The future is in that gap, and so is the money, and so — because a series pledged to wonder must also be pledged to honesty — is the possibility that some large fraction of the 55.8 billion dollars is watching a performance and calling it competence.

Sixteen postcards ago, this series set out with a promise: to report the future that is already here, wonder-first but never credulous, from the balcony a Canadian occupies by both temperament and circumstance. What the second wave has shown, from the Spring Festival stage to the Ukrainian front to the Beijing racecourse, is that the future arrived faster and stranger than the first wave imagined — and that the through-line connecting all of it is precisely the gap Brooks named. The dancing robot that cannot work. The marathon champion that cannot stand. The home helper that needs a human inside it. The battlefield machine whose operators beg to keep the final decision. Each is a dazzling performance shadowed by a competence not yet earned, and each asks the same thing of us: to marvel honestly, which means to marvel with the shadow in view. That is harder than pure wonder and harder than pure cynicism, and it is the only stance that will not embarrass us in five years.

That is the whole story of the robotics moment, and it fits on a postcard: they can do astonishing things, narrowly, on a course they have memorized. The question that decides everything — the trillion-dollar question, the one the money is really betting on — is what happens when they hit the barricade that wasn’t on the map. We are about to find out, at enormous scale, with the whole world watching the livestream.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one crossed the finish line first, and then it fell down, and both of those things are exactly the point.
