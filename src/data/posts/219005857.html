The first two tiers of the Reversal Ledger share a reassuring feature. A future president, given time and determination, could undo them without asking anyone’s permission. The executive branch did them, and the executive branch could undo them.

The third tier is different. Fifteen reversals in the ledger were made in a way that puts them beyond the reach of any president. Some were written into statute, mostly through the 2025 budget law. Some were done by emptying an institution of its people and money, which no executive order can refill. And three more, which the ledger files under its fourth tier because of how firmly they are fastened, were struck down by a law designed specifically to stop agencies from trying again. For all eighteen, the road back runs through Congress. That is the slowest road in American government.

What the budget law did

On 4 July 2025 the president signed the budget reconciliation bill that his supporters called the One Big Beautiful Bill. It was long, and among its many provisions were several that reached into the ledger.

It ended the Inflation Reduction Act’s clean energy tax credits on an accelerated timetable. The credit for buying an electric vehicle ended on 30 September 2025. The credits for home energy improvements ended on the last day of that year. Wind and solar projects had to start construction by 4 July 2026 to qualify at all. It removed the fines automakers pay when their fleets miss federal fuel economy standards, which, as the introduction to this series explained, leaves the new standards of 34.9 miles per gallon for 2031, published on 30 September 2026, largely decorative. Twenty-six states and cities sued two days later. It imposed a work requirement on Medicaid: from 1 January 2027, most adults aged 19 to 64 covered through the Affordable Care Act’s expansion must show 80 hours a month of work, study or volunteering, and must renew their coverage every six months. It replaced the Biden administration’s SAVE student loan repayment plan, already blocked by courts, with a new statutory Repayment Assistance Plan from 1 July 2026.

Separately, Congress let the enhanced Affordable Care Act premium subsidies, first passed in 2021, expire on 31 December 2025. The House passed a three-year extension in January 2026 by 230 votes to 196, with Republican votes. The Senate did not take it up.

And in July 2025 Congress approved a rescission package that cancelled the money already appropriated for public broadcasting. The Corporation for Public Broadcasting, created by the Public Broadcasting Act that Lyndon Johnson signed in 1967, voted to dissolve itself and closed on 5 January 2026.

The money that was left to lapse

One item in this tier shows how far the method can reach. On 25 September 2026, five days before the end of the fiscal year, the administration sent Congress a request to cancel $810 million it had already appropriated. Under the Impoundment Control Act of 1974, passed with support from both parties after Nixon refused to spend money Congress had voted, the funds can be withheld while Congress considers the request. Congress had no time to consider it before the money expired on 30 September. The Government Accountability Office regards this kind of “pocket rescission” as illegal, and seven states sued on 1 October. If the money has lapsed, only a new appropriation or a court order can bring it back.

What was emptied

The rest of the tier was done without a new law, by the simpler method of taking away the people and the money.

At the end of 2025, according to figures from the Office of Personnel Management, more than 317,000 people had left the federal workforce that year and about 68,000 had been hired. That is a net reduction of roughly 250,000, or 10.8 per cent of the civilian workforce, in a single year. Most departures were recorded as voluntary, many through the “deferred resignation” offer made in the first weeks of the administration. The Department of Education, created under Jimmy Carter in 1979, has lost about half its staff and is moving its programmes to other agencies under fourteen interagency agreements. It still exists in law, and only Congress can abolish it, but an agency with half its people and most of its programmes elsewhere exists mainly on paper.

The United States Agency for International Development, created under John F. Kennedy in 1961, was folded into the State Department in July 2025. The Environmental Protection Agency’s Office of Research and Development, the scientific arm of an agency founded under Richard Nixon, was closed in July 2025 and replaced by a smaller office, followed by layoffs. Twenty-two mRNA vaccine research projects worth about $500 million were cancelled in August 2025, among them work descended from Operation Warp Speed, the programme Trump himself launched in 2020. National Institutes of Health grants were terminated by the hundred; the Supreme Court let the terminations stand while the claims go to the Court of Federal Claims. Harvard’s research funding was frozen, a freeze a judge has ruled unlawful and an appeals court heard argument on in October 2026, and Columbia paid $200 million to have its funding restored. Voice of America, on the air since 1942, saw its journalists laid off; a judge voided the layoffs in March 2026, but an appeals court has kept them from returning while the case goes on.

Why does this belong in the third tier rather than the first? Because a president can order an agency to start hiring again, but cannot pay for it. Salaries, grants and agencies cost money, and the money is appropriated by Congress. A new president who wanted to restore the EPA’s research capacity, rebuild USAID or reopen Voice of America at its old strength would need appropriations bills to pass both houses. Recreating the Corporation for Public Broadcasting would need a new act of Congress altogether.

The law that forbids a second try

The three items the ledger places beyond even this tier deserve their own explanation, because the mechanism behind them is one of the least understood ratchets in American government.

The Congressional Review Act of 1996 lets Congress overturn a recently finalised regulation by a joint resolution that needs only a simple majority in each house and cannot be filibustered. That makes it unusually easy to use. But it has a second clause, and the second clause is the ratchet. Once a rule has been struck down this way, the agency may not issue a new rule “in substantially the same form” unless Congress specifically authorises it in a new law. Nobody knows precisely what “substantially the same” means, because the courts have never had to say, and no agency has wanted to be the first to find out.

Three reversals in the ledger were made this way. In May 2025 Congress overturned the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s rule limiting the overdraft fees banks can charge. In 2025 it revoked California’s long-standing authority, under a provision of the Clean Air Act dating to 1967, to set its own stricter vehicle emission standards; California is suing over whether the Act could be used for that at all. And in late April 2026, by 50 votes to 49 in the Senate, it overturned the twenty-year ban on copper and nickel mining near Minnesota’s Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness, opening the way for a Chilean-owned company to seek permits. Minnesota has since imposed its own ban on state land.

None of these comes back with the next president. A future Consumer Financial Protection Bureau that wanted to limit overdraft fees again would have to wait for Congress to authorise it. The same is true of the Boundary Waters and, unless California wins in court, of its emission standards.

The ratchet that held

The mechanism in this part is not peculiar to the present administration, and the clearest proof is a Democratic ratchet that worked.

The Affordable Care Act of 2010 was a statute, not an order, and within a few years it had created something more durable than a law: millions of people who depended on it. In July 2017, with Republicans in control of both houses and the White House, the Senate tried to repeal its core and failed by 49 votes to 51. The coverage was the pawl. A programme with a constituency is far harder to undo than one without.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau was designed on the same principle. The Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 funded it from the Federal Reserve rather than through annual appropriations, precisely so that a hostile Congress could not starve it. In 2024 the Supreme Court upheld that design by seven votes to two. That is why, as Part V describes, the present administration tried to idle the bureau by not requesting its money, and why a judge ruled in September 2026 that it could not.

Both sides build ratchets when they can. The question this series keeps returning to is which ratchets are legitimate, and the best answer the ledger offers is the one the ACA illustrates: the ones Congress builds in the open, which then earn their own defenders.

The arithmetic of the Senate

So everything in this part depends on Congress, and Congress has a particular shape.

Most legislation in the Senate needs sixty votes to overcome a filibuster. Neither party has held sixty seats for any meaningful stretch since 2010. That is why so much of modern governing has moved to executive orders, regulations and the one route that avoids the filibuster: budget reconciliation, which needs only a simple majority but is limited to measures that change federal spending or revenue.

This produces an asymmetry worth understanding. What was done by reconciliation can usually be undone by reconciliation. The clean energy credits, the Medicaid work requirement, the automakers’ fines and the ACA subsidies are all money, and a future Congress with narrow majorities in both houses and a president of the same party could restore any of them in a single budget bill, as the Inflation Reduction Act was passed in 2022 and the 2025 law was passed to unwind it.

What cannot be done by reconciliation is building institutions and setting rules that are not about money. Reconstituting the Corporation for Public Broadcasting, re-establishing USAID as an independent agency, authorising a new overdraft rule, restoring the Boundary Waters withdrawal or protecting the Education Department by statute would each need sixty votes, or a Senate willing to change its rules. That is a high bar for a Congress that has struggled to pass ordinary appropriations bills on time.

The election in between

The midterm elections fall on 3 November 2026. Every House seat and a third of the Senate are on the ballot. Nothing in this tier can move without a Congress willing to move it, so the composition of the next Congress will decide whether any of it can begin to be repaired before 2029.

But even the most favourable outcome for the restorers is limited by the presidential veto. A Congress controlled by one party and a president of the other produce, at most, bargains. A full restoration of this tier would need, at minimum, a president and both houses of Congress in agreement, and for much of it a Senate that has changed its rules or found sixty votes. American politics has produced that combination for a few months in the last half-century.

What Congress cannot restore either

There is one more limit, and it is the hardest. Some of what this tier lost cannot be restored by any law.

A quarter of a million federal employees did not simply leave their desks. They took with them knowledge about how programmes work, which contractors deliver, how to inspect a mine or audit a grant, where the files are and why. A new appropriation can pay new people, but the new people will need years to learn what the old ones knew. Researchers who left American universities for Europe or Canada when their funding was frozen may not come back. Rural public radio stations that closed when their federal support ended will not reopen just because the money returns. Clinics abroad that USAID funded, supply chains it maintained and field staff it employed are gone, and the people who depended on them have, in some cases, died during the interruption. Factories planned around the clean energy credits were cancelled, and the ground they would have occupied in electric vehicles and batteries has been taken, very largely, by China.

Congress can repeal a law. It cannot repeal a year.

Lower still

Poe’s prisoner notices, as the blade descends, that the swing grows wider as well as lower. Each pass covers more ground. The third tier of the ledger has that quality. Its reversals were not aimed at one rule or one programme. They removed the money, the staff and the legal authority on which whole fields of government depended.

The next part goes to the bottom of the ledger: the locks that no Congress can open either, because they were fastened by the Supreme Court, by contract, by treaty or by the president’s power to pardon.
