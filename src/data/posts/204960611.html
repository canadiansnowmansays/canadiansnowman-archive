by Stephen Gordon

I count my essays. Every so often the number lands on a year that was already holding something, and I stop and tell you what. This is essay 1,431, and the year it points to is the year they burned Joan of Arc.

I want to be careful here, the way I was careful at 1347, because there is a version of this essay that goes wrong immediately. Joan of Arc was nineteen years old when she was tied to a stake in the marketplace at Rouen and burned alive in front of a crowd, on the thirtieth of May, 1431. There is no joke in that sentence and I am not going to look for one. A teenager was executed by the state and the church acting together, and if that were the whole story, this would be another essay written with my hands still, and I would tell you so at the top and we would sit with it.

But that is not the whole story. That is barely half of it. Because the remarkable, maddening, almost unbelievable thing about Joan of Arc is not how her trial ended. It is that her trial did not stay ended. The verdict changed its mind. And the satire I am reaching for today — because there is satire here, sharp and cold — is not aimed at the girl. It is aimed at the machine that killed her, and then, at its leisure, decided she had been innocent all along, and then, at even greater leisure, decided she had been a saint.

Watch the arithmetic of that. It is the cruellest sum in this entire series.

Here is what happened, in the order it happened.

Joan was a peasant girl from Domrémy who said she heard voices — saints, she said, telling her to drive the English out of France and see the true king crowned. What is not in dispute, whatever you make of the voices, is that she did it. A teenage girl with no military training took the field in a war that had by then been grinding on for the better part of a century — this is the same Hundred Years’ War I wrote about at 1337, the one history rounded down — and she turned it. Orléans was relieved. Charles VII was crowned at Reims with Joan standing beside him. For a moment, an illiterate girl of seventeen was the most consequential person in France.

And then she was captured, and sold to the English, and put on trial — not a military trial, a church trial, for heresy, because the fastest way to destroy her was to prove that the voices came from the Devil rather than God. The court that tried her was staffed by learned men, bishops and theologians, and they conducted a proceeding of great procedural sophistication against a nineteen-year-old who could not read the documents being used to condemn her.

They found her guilty. On the thirtieth of May, 1431, they handed her to the secular authority to be burned, which was the church’s way of killing someone while keeping its own hands technically clean — we did not execute her, we merely found her guilty and passed her along. The distinction mattered to them a great deal. I suspect it mattered not at all to Joan.

So far this is only a tragedy. Here is where it becomes something stranger.

Twenty-five years later, the verdict was overturned.

In 1456, a second trial — a retrial, ordered after the political winds had shifted and it had become inconvenient for the king Joan had crowned to owe his crown to a convicted heretic — examined the first trial and found it corrupt. Rigged. Procedurally poisoned. The men who had condemned her were found to have condemned her wrongly, and Joan of Arc was declared innocent, her name cleared, the fire retroactively deemed a miscarriage of justice.

She had, at this point, been dead for a quarter of a century. The clearing did her no good whatsoever. It did a great deal of good for the reputation of the king who needed his coronation to have been blessed rather than heretical, which is, if you follow the money and the motive, rather the point. The verdict did not change because someone’s conscience finally broke. It changed because the first verdict had become embarrassing to powerful people, and a second verdict fixed the embarrassment. Justice arrived exactly when it was useful and not one year before.

And then — and this is the part that I cannot get past, the part that makes this a Numbers Game essay and not just a sad story — the institution kept going. It did not stop at innocent. In 1920, the Catholic Church canonized Joan of Arc. Made her a saint. The same church whose court had burned her as a heretic in 1431 now held her up, four hundred and eighty-nine years later, as one of the holiest people who had ever lived.

Run the numbers with me, because the numbers are the whole indictment. She was alive for nineteen years. It took the institution twenty-five years to admit it had killed her wrongly. It took four hundred and eighty-nine years to decide she had been a saint the entire time — which means that at the moment they lit the fire, by their own eventual reckoning, they were burning a saint, and it would take them nearly five centuries to work up the nerve to say so.

I have spent this series being sardonic about numbers, and here is a set that earns every bit of it.

Nineteen. Twenty-five. Four hundred and eighty-nine.

Nineteen years to live an entire consequential life and die for it. Twenty-five years for the machine to reverse itself, and only then because the reversal was convenient. Four hundred and eighty-nine years to complete the turn from heretic to saint — the full distance from the stake to the altar, travelled so slowly that every single person who lit the fire, and every person who watched, and every person who could have felt the sting of being wrong, was safely and comfortably dead before the verdict finished changing its mind.

That is the genius of institutional accountability, and I use the word “genius” the way I used it for the dog who learned to open the screen door. The machine is never wrong in the moment. It is only ever wrong in retrospect, at a distance, once being wrong costs nothing. It burns you when you are dangerous and canonizes you when you are safe, and it manages, somehow, to take credit for both — for the rigour of the trial that killed you and for the grace of the sainthood that redeemed you, as though these were two proud moments in a single noble tradition rather than the same institution covering its own tracks across five centuries.

I have watched smaller versions of this. We all have. The figure denounced, destroyed, run out of public life — and then, once safely gone, gradually reclaimed, rehabilitated, quoted approvingly by the very people or the very institutions that did the destroying. The apology that arrives only after the injured party can no longer be embarrassed by accepting it. The statue raised to the person the state once hounded. It is always the same shape. Condemn while dangerous; claim while dead. Joan is only the purest example because the arithmetic is so stark and the reversal so total — from the fire to the halo, with the whole machine insisting, at every stage, that it had been right all along.

So this is essay 1,431, and I have not made a joke at Joan’s expense, because there is none to make. She was nineteen, and she was brave past all sense, and she was burned by men who knew the procedures cold and used every one of them against a girl who could not read.

The joke, such as it is, is on the machine. The joke is that the verdict changed its mind, twice, over five centuries, and expected to be thanked for it both times. The joke is that an institution can burn a saint and canonize a saint and describe both as acts of faith, and that the four hundred and eighty-nine years between the two is not, in the institution’s own telling, an accusation — merely the time it took for the truth to become convenient.

Joan didn’t need the retrial. She didn’t need the sainthood. She has been beyond needing anything since the thirtieth of May, 1431. The reversal was never for her. It was for everyone who came after and needed the story to have a better ending than the one they gave it.

I will see you at the next one, which is lighter, and which is about a machine of a very different kind — one that, for once, made things better instead of merely making them look better in hindsight.

Nineteen. Twenty-five. Four hundred and eighty-nine. Count them again sometime. They add up to exactly how long it takes to be forgiven by the people who should have been asking you for forgiveness.
