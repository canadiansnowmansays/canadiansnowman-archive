Postmark: Shreveport, Louisiana. An Amazon fulfilment centre. 3:47 a.m.

Inside an Amazon fulfillment center. Fleets of autonomous mobile robots (like Proteus) move alongside human workers at all hours — the floor that never sleeps.

At 3:47 in the morning, the Shreveport fulfilment centre does not sleep.

It does not have a shift change at 3:47 a.m. It does not have a coffee break. The Sequoia system — a multi-level containerized inventory structure holding more than thirty million items — does not get tired of retrieving totes. The robotic arm called Sparrow, which uses computer vision and AI to pick individual items from bins and place them in outbound containers, does not lose concentration at hour seven of a twelve-hour shift. The autonomous mobile robot called Proteus — described, with a corporate cheerfulness that is either endearing or slightly ominous, as having lights that form a smile when it navigates around workers — does not notice that it is nearly four in the morning.

Amazon crossed one million deployed robots in July 2025. The milestone was noted in a press release. The company also announced DeepFleet, an AI system that functions as a traffic controller for its robot fleet, improving travel efficiency by ten percent and cutting delivery costs. The one millionth robot did not get a cake.

This is what the factory floor looks like now. Not in the future. Now.

Four Million and Counting

The International Federation of Robotics reported that more than four million industrial robots were operating in factories worldwide as of 2024 — the first time that threshold had been crossed, representing ten percent year-on-year growth. Over five hundred thousand new robots were installed in 2024 alone, the fourth consecutive year that installations exceeded half a million. China accounted for roughly seventy percent of new installations. Europe took seventeen percent. The Americas, including the United States and Canada, took ten.

These are not the robots of science fiction. They do not walk. Most of them do not have heads. They are arms — articulated, programmable, tireless robotic arms bolted to factory floors in automotive plants, electronics assembly facilities, pharmaceutical packaging lines, and food processing operations. They weld, paint, pick, place, sort, pack, inspect, and palletize. They do not call in sick. They do not require health insurance. They do not file grievance procedures.

They also do not, by themselves, explain what is happening. Four million robots is a number. What it represents is a structural shift in the economics of manufacturing that has been underway for decades and is now accelerating faster than most people — and most governments — have absorbed.

What Amazon Built

The Shreveport, Louisiana fulfilment centre, opened in late 2024, is the most automated Amazon facility ever built. It is worth examining in some detail, not because Amazon is a villain in this story — the company’s framing of its robots as worker-safety tools is not entirely dishonest — but because Amazon is the most visible and most advanced example of what the factory floor is becoming, and because the Shreveport facility is essentially a preview of what logistics infrastructure will look like within a decade.

At the centre of the building is Sequoia — the inventory system that holds thirty million items in a structure roughly the size of several city blocks. Items move through the facility in a coordinated sequence involving a cast of robots with names that read like a corporate fever dream: Sparrow picks individual items using AI and computer vision that gives it the versatility to handle over two hundred million unique products. Robin and Cardinal sort and stack packages. Hercules and Titan move inventory pods through fenced-off areas inaccessible to humans. Proteus navigates shared spaces alongside workers. Vulcan — the newest addition, equipped with force sensors and AI — handles items with what Amazon calls “a sense of touch,” reducing the strain of highly repetitive reaching and lifting by keeping human workers in ergonomic power zones rather than stretching to the top and bottom rows of shelving.

Robotic arms and automated systems at work inside Amazon fulfillment centers. From high-speed picking arms like Sparrow (left) to inventory-handling systems like Sequoia (center) and package-sorting robots (right), these tools are transforming warehouses into 24/7 operations that never tire.

Vulcan is worth pausing on. Touch is one of the last sensory frontiers in industrial robotics. The problem of picking an object without knowing exactly what it is, how heavy it is, or how fragile it is — without the tactile feedback that human hands provide unconsciously and continuously — has been one of the fundamental engineering challenges of the field. A human warehouse worker reaches into a bin and her fingers tell her, in milliseconds, that the object is glass, that it is heavier than it looks, that she needs to adjust her grip. Vulcan’s force sensors are an attempt to replicate that feedback loop artificially. The attempt is, for the first time at commercial scale, working.

The Shreveport facility reduced fulfilment processing times by twenty-five percent compared to previous generation facilities. It increased shipping accuracy. Amazon describes this as making workers’ jobs safer, easier, and more productive. Critics describe it as displacement at scale dressed up in the language of wellbeing. Both descriptions are partially true, which is why the factory floor is the most complicated of all the postcard territories.

The Tractor That Drives Itself

A John Deere 9RX autonomous tractor operating in the field. The farmer can monitor from a phone while the machine works — one of the many ways automation is reshaping agriculture

Several hundred miles from Shreveport, in the cornfields of the American Midwest, a different kind of automation is playing out at a different scale.

John Deere has been putting GPS-guided steering systems on tractors since the 1990s. The technology was semi-autonomous — it kept the tractor on course, but a human sat in the cab to supervise. At CES 2025, Deere unveiled the second generation of its fully autonomous 9RX tractor: sixteen cameras arranged in pods providing 360-degree visibility, high-precision GPS accurate to within an inch, machine learning algorithms that allow the machine to adjust to changing field conditions without human input. The farmer can start the tractor from a smartphone, monitor its progress remotely, and attend to other work while several thousand dollars worth of machinery tills the field without anyone sitting in the seat.

This is not a prototype. These tractors are operating on commercial farms. Deere’s goal is a fully autonomous corn and soy production system by the end of the decade — seeding, cultivating, applying inputs, and harvesting, all without a driver. The company has sixty percent of the North American tractor market. When Deere moves, the industry moves.

The labour context matters here as it matters everywhere on the factory floor. A farm in Sonoma County, California, had twenty-seven tractor driver positions open and posted them on job boards. The farm received no applications for weeks. After adopting autonomous tractor systems, the owner updated the listing to seek an agtech operator — someone to monitor and manage the autonomous equipment. The listing noted that video game experience was a preferred qualification. The applications came rolling in. That pivot — from tractor driver to agtech operator — is what the optimists point to when they argue that automation creates new work rather than destroying it. The pessimists note that there were twenty-seven positions and now there is one. Both observations are accurate. The arithmetic is uncomfortable.

The Strawberry Problem

Harvest CROO Robotics strawberry harvester operating in a field. After years of struggling with the delicate “strawberry problem,” machines like this are now reaching commercial viability — capable of replacing crews of dozens of human pickers

Far from the Shreveport facility, in fields in California and Kent and Queensland and Ontario, a different kind of robot is working on a different kind of problem.

The strawberry is, from a robotics perspective, a nightmare.

It bruises easily. It is often partially hidden under leaves. It grows at irregular heights and angles. It comes in colours ranging from almost-red to fully-red, and the difference between the two, which determines whether the berry is ripe, requires a judgment that experienced human pickers make in a fraction of a second based on colour, texture, and context simultaneously. Conventional robotic systems — rigid grippers, fixed cameras, predetermined movement sequences — fail at this task reliably and expensively.

The strawberry problem is why agricultural robotics has lagged behind warehouse robotics by approximately a decade. The warehouse is a controlled environment. The strawberry field is not. Every row is different. Every plant is different. Every berry is different. The variability that makes farming beautiful makes it extraordinarily hard to automate.

And yet it is being automated, because it has to be. Agricultural workers are aging out of the workforce faster than new workers are arriving. In the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and Australia simultaneously, farms that have relied for generations on seasonal human labour are facing shortfalls they cannot fill at any price.

Washington State University researchers developed a strawberry-picking robot that addresses the hidden-berry problem specifically: an AI vision system that combines multiple images of the plant to locate fruit concealed under leaves, then directs a gentle puff of air to move the leaves aside, then uses soft silicone fingers to pluck the berry without bruising it. The University of Essex, in partnership with the jam maker Wilkin & Sons, produced a prototype that picks a strawberry in 2.5 seconds, designed to cost around £10,000 — a fraction of the £150,000 that existing commercial systems command.

In April 2025, Harvest CROO Robotics announced that its automated strawberry harvesting machine had completed field trials showing commercial viability — performance on par with human harvesters, the first time that threshold had been crossed for this particular crop. A single machine can harvest a twenty-five-acre field in three days, replacing a crew of approximately thirty workers.

Thirty workers. One machine. Three days. These are the numbers that make the factory floor postcard the hardest one to write with uncomplicated enthusiasm.

The Labour Question

The honest version of the factory floor story has two simultaneous truths that resist easy reconciliation.

The first truth is that much of what robots are replacing is genuinely bad work. The average Amazon warehouse employee before heavy automation walked between ten and fifteen miles per shift, repeatedly lifting packages of varying weights at unpredictable angles, sustaining injury rates that were measurably higher than the industry average. The strawberry picker crouches in a field for eight hours in summer heat, performing the same motion thousands of times, earning wages that have not kept pace with the cost of living in the communities where they work. The argument that automating this work is doing workers a favour is not entirely cynical.

The second truth is that the argument is not entirely honest either. The jobs being displaced are real jobs, held by real people, with real mortgages and real children and real communities built around the economic activity those jobs represent.

Automotive assembly line with industrial robots. More than four million such machines now operate in factories worldwide, doing repetitive, dangerous, or precision work that humans once performed around the clock.

A 2020 Statistics Canada study found something that complicates the simple displacement narrative: companies that invest in robots also tend to hire more workers, not fewer, because automation enables productivity gains that expand the business overall. The workers they hire tend to do different work — less repetitive, more cognitive, more supervisory, more technical. Employment in those companies increased by nearly twenty percent compared to non-automating peers. This is the optimist’s account of automation, and it is not fabricated. It has happened, historically, with every major wave of technological change, from the agricultural revolution to the industrial one.

The Sonoma County farm that found no tractor drivers found plenty of agtech operators. The skills changed. The work continued. Amazon, for its part, points out that its total human workforce has not declined even as its robot count crossed one million — though critics note that the package volume handled per worker has risen substantially, which means the displacement is embedded in productivity figures rather than headcount. Both framings capture something real. The accounting is complicated precisely because the transformation is ongoing.

The question — the one that no postcard can fully answer — is whether it will happen fast enough this time, and whether the workers caught in the transition are supported while the adjustment takes place. The pace of automation in 2026 is unlike previous waves. The breadth is unlike previous waves. The fact that it is happening simultaneously across warehousing, agriculture, manufacturing, and logistics — all the sectors that have historically absorbed the workers displaced from whichever sector was automating at any given moment — is genuinely new. Previous transitions happened sequentially. This one is happening in parallel, across every sector at once, driven by AI capabilities that are themselves advancing faster than the policy frameworks designed to manage them. The safety net beneath the workers being displaced was not designed for this pace or this breadth.

This is the governance question that The Asimov Gap is better equipped to address than this postcard. What this postcard can do is name the question honestly, without pretending it does not exist.

What the Floor Tells Us

There is a Proteus robot in Shreveport right now whose lights are forming a smile as it navigates around a human worker at 3:47 in the morning.

There is a John Deere 9RX tractor in an Iowa cornfield tilling a row without anyone in the cab, while the farmer monitors it from a phone.

There is a soft-fingered robot in a field in Washington State gently puffing air at strawberry leaves and reaching in to pick the ripe ones.

There are 4.28 million industrial robots on factory floors worldwide, welding and painting and assembling and packaging at speeds and accuracies that human workers cannot match, in conditions — heat, toxicity, repetition, precision — that human workers should not have to endure.

The factory floor has already been transformed. The debate about whether it should be is several decades behind the reality on the ground. What remains is the harder work: figuring out what we owe the people whose livelihoods the transformation has disrupted, and building the institutions capable of answering that question before the next wave arrives.

The robots are not waiting for us to finish the argument. They never were.
