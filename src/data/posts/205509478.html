There is a particular kind of silence that falls over a conflict when a bigger, louder one moves into the room next door. It is not peace. It is not resolution. It is simply that American attention has a compass, and for the first half of this year, the needle pointed wherever the United States Navy actually sailed.

That needle moved four times in less than a year, and it is worth tracing the whole path before landing on where it sits this week. None of this means Washington’s bureaucracy actually stops working on whatever it isn’t looking at — State, the Pentagon, and the NSC run parallel tracks by design, and diplomats juggle more than one file at once. What moves is emphasis: which crisis gets the president’s own attention, the envoys’ travel budget, the White House podium. That is a scarcer resource than the machinery underneath it, and it is the one this essay is tracking. It started, quite literally, with ships. In September 2025, American warships began striking small boats in the Caribbean and, a month later, the Eastern Pacific, on the stated grounds that they were run by drug cartels. By this spring the total had passed 200 dead across more than sixty strikes, with human rights investigators finding no public evidence tying most of the victims to trafficking at all — reporting from the region identified laborers and fishermen making a few hundred dollars a trip among the dead, and at least one strike killed survivors of an earlier strike in a follow-up attack a defense official reportedly ordered in blunter terms than the Pentagon has ever confirmed on the record. Human Rights Watch has called the campaign a pattern of extrajudicial killing carried out without credible legal basis. It built, over four months, into the largest American military buildup in Latin America in a generation, and in January it delivered its actual objective: the capture of Venezuela’s president, Nicolás Maduro, and the installation of a government Washington prefers.

The needle swung again almost immediately, onto Cuba, Venezuela’s dependent neighbor, whose power grid and hospitals have spent the months since grinding toward what the UN’s own human rights office has called near-collapse — infant mortality rising, childhood cancer survival falling, food production down by more than half, medicine stocks reduced to a third of normal, under an American oil blockade that has been called the most severe pressure the island has faced since the missile crisis of 1962. And then, before Havana’s crisis had even finished making headlines, the needle swung a third time, to Iran — and here the metaphor starts to change shape, because Iran is where the navy genuinely mattered again, materially, in a way it hasn’t since. Carrier strike groups moved into the region, the Strait of Hormuz closed to shipping, and a war opened on February 28 with a strike that killed a head of state, consuming the next five months of Washington’s attention, its envoys, and its news cycle.

Venezuela, notably, did claw back a sliver of that attention on its own terms, though not the kind any government wants. On June 24, 2026, a pair of major earthquakes, 7.2 and 7.5 magnitude, struck the country’s north-central coast less than a minute apart, killing more than 3,300 people and devastating Caracas and the coastal state of La Guaira. US Southern Command — the same command structure that had spent the prior autumn striking boats off that same coastline — announced within a day that it was surging naval and air assets into the country to support the rescue effort, at the request of the interim government Washington itself had helped install. It is worth sitting with that for a moment rather than rushing past it: the amphibious ship and the transport aircraft that arrived to help dig survivors out of Caracas rubble were the same fleet, under the same command, that had been firing on fishing boats a few hundred miles offshore only months before. Whatever else one wants to say about that reversal, it produced something Havana, watching its own hospitals fail without any comparable American convoy en route, does not currently have: help. Even so, the earthquake made headlines for days, not months — the compass had already moved on to Tehran, and it did not swing back for long.

This week, the needle swung a fourth time, to Kyiv — and here the metaphor finally breaks entirely, because no American ship is sailing toward Ukraine. What moved instead was bandwidth: the same handful of envoys, the same White House press cycle, the same finite quantity of presidential attention that had just spent five months on Tehran and a week on Caracas rubble. The compass stopped being about where the navy sailed the moment it left the water. It has been, ever since, about where Washington’s diplomats physically are, which is a subtler and in some ways more damning thing to track than ship movements, because a ship at least has to come home eventually. An envoy’s calendar does not. That is where this essay means to stay. But it is worth naming, once, before moving on: the boats are still being struck in the Pacific. Havana is still rationing diesel. Caracas is still adjusting to a government installed by force under cover of a “narco-terrorism” campaign that even friendly analysts now doubt touched the actual trafficking networks. None of those things ended because the cameras left. They simply lost the argument for airtime to whatever came next.

On July 6, 2026, Russia hit Kyiv with 68 missiles and 351 drones in a single night, 23 of them ballistic, none of them intercepted, on the eve of a NATO summit in Ankara. Twelve people were killed and 64 wounded, including two children. It was the second such barrage against the Ukrainian capital in under a week — a Thursday assault had already killed 31 and wounded more than 100. As the New York Times reported this morning, Kyiv’s mayor described crews working through the night at a nine-story apartment block gouged open from the fourth floor down, families watching a crane lift concrete slabs off the rubble of their own neighbors, an unexploded missile sitting in the mud of a courtyard nearby. A woman who lived in that building told reporters she had been woken by the blast; her husband said their own apartment, farther down the hall, was somehow still intact. Outside, another woman watching the rescue said what has become, by now, the only honest thing left to say in Ukraine after four and a half years of this: that no one believes any agreement can actually be reached with the Russian president.

It is worth being honest about a tension in that last sentence and the paragraphs before it. This essay has been skeptical of American power every time it moved south — extrajudicial, installed by force, narco-terrorism as pretext — and is about to spend the rest of its length arguing Washington owes Ukraine more attention, not less. Those two positions can coexist without contradiction: intervention justified by a dubious legal theory against fishermen is not the same category of act as arming a country that was invaded. But the essay should say that plainly rather than let a reader assume American power is suspect everywhere except where it happens to be defending a cause this diary favors. It is not neutral about that distinction. It is trying to be honest about which distinction it’s actually making.

The peculiar arithmetic of this war is that Russia is not winning it. Ukraine’s own campaign — drones sent deep into Russian refineries and production facilities — has produced fuel lines across Russian cities that ordinary citizens have not seen in a generation. Moscow’s ground advance has stalled almost everywhere it is attempted. What Russia has left is the terror weapon: barrages large enough to overwhelm air defense, aimed increasingly at apartment blocks rather than the front line, precisely because it can no longer move the front line itself. President Zelensky went to Ankara this week asking NATO allies for exactly one thing — more Patriot interceptors — because Ukraine’s shortage of that single system is the specific, named reason ballistic missiles are getting through while cruise missiles mostly are not. “The reason is the shortage of interceptor missiles,” he said, about as plainly as a wartime president is capable of saying anything. It is a war of exhaustion in which the side doing the terrorizing is also, unmistakably, the side running out of road — and the side still capable of killing twelve people in an apartment block overnight regardless.

A Narrow Summit for a War That Isn’t

NATO’s leaders gather July 7–8 at the Beştepe Presidential Complex in Ankara, and the alliance has been careful to keep the published agenda narrow: defense investment, defense industrial capacity, and support for Ukraine. Secretary General Mark Rutte has spent months describing his goal for the summit as “NATO 3.0” — an alliance, in his words, “less dependent on the United States” while the United States remains “firmly rooted” in it, which is as polite a way as exists to describe an American administration pushing Europe to take over a war it did not start and cannot yet finish alone. The headline commitment expected out of Ankara is a pledge of roughly €70 billion in military equipment, training, and assistance for Ukraine in 2026, with allies affirming an intent to sustain at least that level into 2027 — funding that will flow almost entirely through European bilateral commitments and EU financing rather than new American contributions. Trump is expected to meet Zelensky on the sidelines, once again promising to end a war his own National Defense Strategy has already reassigned, on paper, to Europe’s care.

The allies have not, as of this writing, been able to agree on Secretary General Rutte’s other proposal: a standing commitment of 0.25 percent of GDP annually to Ukraine, a revived Estonian idea that would guarantee roughly €120 billion a year if adopted. Whether Ankara resolves that argument or simply postpones it again to whatever summit comes after this one is the real business of the next two days, underneath the photographs of leaders shaking hands in front of a very large Turkish flag.

The Bottleneck

There is a structural reason the diplomacy in particular feels thinner than it should, distinct from Ukraine’s own battlefield fatigue: American bandwidth. Washington’s entire diplomatic architecture for this war currently runs through a handful of overstretched appointments: the ambassadorship to Moscow has sat vacant for more than a year, the acting ambassador to Kyiv resigned in April and was never replaced, and the president’s own envoys have spent this month almost entirely consumed by a separate negotiation half a world away, in Doha. Asked why neither man had found time to visit Kyiv itself, Zelensky told CBS something that deserved to outlive the news cycle it was said in: “Unfortunately, we are in this queue of these wars.” Vladimir Putin, for his part, is reported to have told American officials he expects those same envoys back at his table in Moscow once the “hot phase” of that other negotiation concludes — which is as close as a Russian president will ever come to admitting, on the record, that every week Washington’s attention is elsewhere is a week of reduced pressure on him.

That other conflict — the one that opened in February with a strike that killed a head of state, has since produced a ceasefire nobody fully trusts, a funeral that took four months to stage, and negotiations still grinding on in Doha over a strait and a nuclear file — is not this essay’s subject. Nor is Cuba, still rationing diesel eighteen months into a blockade, or Venezuela, still adjusting to a government installed by force, or the Pacific, where boats are still being struck on the same “narco-terrorism” premise that opened this whole sequence back in September 2025. It is enough, for now, to say that all four still exist, that two of them still occupy the only diplomats capable of ending Ukraine’s war faster, and that all four are, in every sense that matters to a Ukrainian family watching a crane lift concrete off their neighbors’ apartment, conflicts the world stopped thinking about the moment the needle swung away from them.

Where That Leaves Ukraine

Four and a half years in, this is a war being fought by an exhausted aggressor against a country that has, by any honest measure, out-thought it — and that is still losing twelve people a night to the one weapon Russia has left that Ukraine cannot yet fully stop. American distraction is not the only reason for that. Ukraine’s own manpower is stretched thin after four and a half years of fighting. Europe has spent a decade underinvesting in the defense industrial base it is now being asked to lead, and NATO’s own allies still can’t agree on a standing funding target for Ukraine, quite apart from anything happening in Doha. More Patriot batteries would raise Ukraine’s interception rate; they would not, on their own, end a war this exhausting for either side. All of that is true, and none of it changes what happened in Kyiv this week. Ankara will produce a declaration, a pledge, a handshake with Zelensky, and then the summit will end and the missiles will not. NATO’s own communiqué language calls its commitment to collective defense “ironclad.” Twelve families in Kyiv this week would be forgiven for wanting a word with a little more weight behind it than that.

There is, it turns out, one piece of Americana that has followed the compass faithfully through every stop on this itinerary without ever actually deploying anywhere. The Village People wrote two songs that matter here, not one. Everybody knows the first: “YMCA,” which the president has made his own with a dance move now as recognizable as anything in American politics, played at the close of nearly every rally he holds, on any continent, for any occasion, war or no war, performed by a man who by most accounts enjoys it with a devotion nearly equal to that of the community that actually made the song famous. But it is the group’s other hit that scored this year, whether or not anyone at the rally noticed: “In the Navy” — because that, and not the dance floor, is where the actual compass pointed. The boats in the Caribbean. The carrier groups off Hormuz. The amphibious ship that reached Caracas after the earthquake. Every needle swing this essay has traced was, quite literally, the Navy, in the Navy, doing exactly what the other song describes. Nobody has ever seen him dance to that one. It doesn’t have a chorus you can move to. It just has ships, and the places they go, and the one place — Kyiv — they never did.

The queue is real. Ukraine has been waiting in it since February. It should not still be waiting in July.
