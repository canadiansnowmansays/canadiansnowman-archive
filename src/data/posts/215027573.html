How dare they.

A governing party sitting on an unfinished war, unpopular tariffs, and a cost-of-living crisis it hasn’t been able to spin away opened its first-ever midterm convention on September 9, 2026 — and spent most of the night not defending any of it, but inflating a narrow left-wing faction into an existential enemy.

Two years of an Epstein scandal that has drawn scrutiny inside their own party’s ranks. An unfinished war with Iran. Grocery and gas prices that have made “affordability” the single word haunting Republican strategists more than any other. Tariffs so unpopular even the candidates running as loyal Trump allies barely mentioned them. That is the governing record Republicans brought into Dallas.

Almost none of it got defended on its own terms.

What filled the space instead: “I call it the Bolshevik Revolution in the Democratic Party,” House Majority Leader Steve Scalise told the crowd. “Let’s make sure that when we wake up the morning after Election Day we know we did everything we could to defeat socialism.”

Two years of governance, and the closing argument was an inflated enemy.

Sit with that phrase for a moment before we go further, because the specificity is the point. Not “too far left.” Not “out of touch.” Bolshevik Revolution — a term that names a specific historical event, a violent seizure of state power, and imports it wholesale onto a party that, this fall, will be running candidates for county commissioner and state senate.

This series has spent twelve parts building a fact-based case, checklist against evidence, for why Trump-era governance meets the historical definition of fascism. Part 11 examined Stephen Miller’s Hobbesian worldview — politics as a war governed by strength and force alone. Part 12 added Carl Schmitt’s complementary framework, courtesy of Brookings’ Jonathan Rauch: politics not as negotiation between factions but as war between enemies, “neither of which can understand the other and both of which feel existentially threatened — and only one of which can win.”

Dallas gave us the clearest live demonstration of the enemies-not-opponents language this series has documented. Not officials implementing policy that happens to fit an authoritarian pattern. The rhetoric itself, spoken from a podium, naming the opposition as an enemy to be defeated rather than a rival to be beaten at the ballot box.

Enemies, Not Opponents

Lisa Lerer reported for The New York Times on September 10, 2026 that speaker after speaker at the two-day Dallas gathering spent more time “whipping up fears about the specter of socialism, communism and even anarchy” than promoting the administration’s record. Democrats running for Congress were, in the words used from the stage, “evil.” They were “communists and anarchists.” They wanted to turn the country into “a socialist hellscape.”

That last phrase belongs to Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who told the crowd that “the radical left is relentless in their pursuit to remake America in the image of every socialist hellscape of the last century — to import the misery of failed workers’ paradises like the Soviet bloc, Caracas and Havana.” Note the construction: American political opponents aren’t described as having bad policy ideas. They are described as an infiltrating force importing misery from abroad — a formulation that doesn’t argue with the other side so much as expel it from the national community altogether.

Senator Tim Scott, chair of the Senate GOP’s campaign arm, went further: “This is a battle for the heart of our nation. The Democrats’ extreme candidates will rip that heart out of our chest. They hate our culture. They hate what it means to be American.” Scott’s own remarks also touched on border policy and tax cuts — he wasn’t purely a fear speech — but even the speakers who nodded at the record spent their real heat on the enemy, not the accomplishment.

They hate what it means to be American. That’s exclusionary language, not policy disagreement — it defines the opposition as standing outside the national community rather than as fellow citizens who see the country differently. It’s worth being precise about what that is and isn’t: this is cultural essentialism, not the blood-and-soil ethnic nationalism this series examined in earlier parts, and the two shouldn’t be collapsed into each other. But cultural essentialism does the same structural work Schmitt’s framework describes — it draws the line at “who counts as one of us” rather than “who has better ideas.” Political rivals don’t hate what it means to be American. Enemies do.

Trump’s own remarks, which ran nearly two hours, built to the same place by a different route. “When you vote in this election, you can tell these far left lunatics and fanatics that America is not for sale,” he told the crowd. “We do not negotiate with communists, with socialists, or with radical left lunatics.” Recent Democratic primary winners, he said, were “the most extreme and dangerous group of candidates” ever to seek office — “radical left communists” who had “overthrown” the party establishment and replaced it with “crazed lunatics.”

We do not negotiate with — that’s not campaign rhetoric about an opposing party. That’s the language of non-recognition, and the real test isn’t whether the sentence sounds like Schmitt. The test is whether the people who said it will treat November’s result as legitimate if they lose it. Dallas doesn’t answer that question yet — nobody on that stage said the election wouldn’t count. But it’s the question this kind of language raises, applied to a domestic electorate that will, in fact, go to the polls in eight weeks and cast ballots the administration is constitutionally obligated to honor.

To be precise about what’s inflated and what isn’t: there is a real leftward shift in some Democratic primaries this cycle, and the speakers in Dallas weren’t inventing it from nothing. Mamdani’s win in New York, Abdul El-Sayed’s Senate nomination in Michigan, and other DSA-aligned victories are genuine data points, and this series has documented elsewhere that Democrats themselves are unsettled about what to do with that wing of the party. But those results describe a faction, not the party. The distortion in Dallas wasn’t that the DSA doesn’t exist — it’s that a real, narrow phenomenon got stretched to cover every Democrat on a ballot, from El-Sayed to a moderate running for county commissioner in a district that will never see a socialist nominee. That’s the move worth naming precisely: not an invented enemy, but an inflated one — real enough at the edges to be believable, expanded enough at the center to justify treating an entire opposition party as existential.

The Vocabulary of 1919 to 1933

None of this is a new tactic, and pointing that out doesn’t require reaching. The specific invocation of “Bolshevik” and “communist” threat as a mobilizing fear has a century-long lineage, and it’s worth walking through precisely because the parallel is instructive rather than decorative.

In Italy’s biennio rosso — the “two red years” of 1919 and 1920 — factory occupations and general strikes across the industrial north convinced landowners, industrialists, and much of the middle class that a Bolshevik-style revolution was imminent. Mussolini’s squadristi did not primarily sell themselves as fascists with a positive governing philosophy. They sold themselves as the only force standing between Italy and Soviet-style collapse. The violence against socialists and trade unionists that followed was justified, again and again, as national self-defense against an existential communist threat — a threat that was real in the sense that strikes and occupations were happening, and wildly exaggerated in the sense that no Bolshevik seizure of the Italian state was actually underway. That’s the closest analogue to what happened in Dallas: a real, bounded phenomenon inflated into a national emergency that justified treating the entire opposition as illegitimate.

Weimar Germany’s own version of Bolshevism-as-threat is worth naming, with a caveat attached rather than left implicit. The Nazi Party’s Judeo-Bolshevism propaganda used the same fear-of-the-left mechanism, but it was never generic anti-leftism — it was a racialized conspiracy theory that ultimately licensed annihilation, a different and far graver thing than what’s in the Dallas transcript. I raise it only to flag the outer edge of where this vocabulary can travel, not because Dallas is anywhere near it. What Dallas does share with both Italy and Weimar is the underlying mechanism, not the destination: point at the left, declare the nation itself at stake, and use the resulting fear to justify treating a normal political rival as beyond the pale.

I want to be exact about what I’m claiming and what I’m not. Nobody in Dallas on September 9 passed an emergency decree. No civil liberties were suspended between the convention’s first and second nights. There was no Reichstag fire, and nothing in this piece suggests one is coming. “Bolshevism” functioned in 1919 the same way it functioned that Wednesday night: as an elastic label, applied not because it accurately described the actual opposition but because it was frightening enough to make any opposition look like an existential threat rather than a normal electoral rival. Scalise reaching for “Bolshevik Revolution” to describe a party running on health care costs and grocery prices is not evidence that Scalise is planning a Reichstag fire. It’s evidence that a century-old vocabulary — fear of the left as a substitute for a defense of the record — is still available, still familiar, and still effective enough to open a national convention with it.

What the Fear Was Standing In For

Here’s the part that should worry a careful reader more than the borrowed vocabulary itself: the fear-mongering wasn’t competing with a defense of the administration’s record. It was substituting for one.

New York Times reporting from the convention floor, bylined by Patricia Mazzei and Shane Goldmacher, noted that most Senate Republicans in competitive battleground races spent their convention time attacking their Democratic rivals by name rather than praising the president who elevated many of them. Senator Jon Husted of Ohio mentioned Trump twice in a five-minute speech — the same number of times he mentioned former President Biden. Michael Whatley, Trump’s own handpicked former RNC chair, now running for Senate in North Carolina, barely mentioned Trump at all, instead spending his time on his Democratic opponent.

Lerer’s own reporting states the quiet part directly: the decision to focus so heavily on vilifying Democrats “amounted to a tacit admission” that Republicans’ best shot at the midterms is painting the alternative as worse — because their own two years don’t hold up to scrutiny. The numbers back that framing. Lerer’s piece itself places Trump, with the exception of Richard Nixon, as the most unpopular second-term president at this stage of his tenure in the history of polling going back to the 1940s. On the issues that were once his strengths — the economy, national security, immigration — he is now underwater.

Every item on the record laid out at the top of this piece got a version of the same treatment from that stage: not silence exactly, but assertion in place of argument. On Iran, Trump’s own reassurance was a promise, not a resolution — gas prices, he said, would be “going down as soon as we win the war with Iran,” a war his own convention was still describing in the future tense. On the cost of living, he told the crowd “I’m bringing the prices way down” and that Democrats “caused it, but we’re fixing it” — an assertion running directly against the affordability numbers this series’ companion series, The Household Ledger, has been tracking since August. Epstein didn’t surface from the podium at all; whatever pressure that scandal is generating outside the hall, it got no acknowledgment from inside it.

What Trump offered instead of a defense of that record was a promise: a $5,000 “Trump Dividend” to every adult American, contingent on Republicans keeping control of Congress — a proposal with no funding mechanism, no enforcement plan for the domestic-spending requirement he attached to it, and a price tag exceeding a trillion dollars. Katie Rogers, also reporting for The New York Times, documented that this is not the first time Trump has dangled unrealized cash to Americans tied to a political outcome — $2,000 tariff-revenue checks and $5,000 DOGE-savings payments have both been floated before and haven’t materialized. The pattern is not new money. It’s the same move as the fear rhetoric, aimed at a different instinct: don’t look backward at the record, look forward at a promise that may never be redeemed.

That’s the tell. An administration confident in its last two years defends them with evidence. An administration that instead spends a national convention inflating a narrow left-wing faction into Bolsheviks, communists, and people who hate America — while dangling a trillion-dollar check it has no plan to pay — is not making an argument about governance. It’s making an argument about fear, because the argument about governance would lose.

The Pattern, Not the Comparison

This series has never claimed that invoking historical fascism means predicting an exact rerun of 1933. The value of the checklist this series has built across twelve parts is that it lets us name specific, recurring mechanisms — enemies-not-opponents framing, scapegoating, cult of personality, rejection of democratic norms — without requiring that every instance rise to Reichstag-fire severity before it counts as evidence. Dallas doesn’t need to be 1933 to belong in this series. It needs to demonstrate, with direct quotes from people currently holding federal office, that the enemies-not-opponents framework Part 11 and Part 12 identified as central to Trump-era governance is not a retrospective label imposed by critics. It’s the language the administration’s own leadership chose to use, from its own podium, on its own terms.

The question this series keeps returning to is whether Americans will recognize the pattern while it’s still just a pattern — words from a stage, not yet policy — rather than waiting for it to escalate into something undeniable. Dallas gave us the words. What comes next is still, for now, up to the voters the speakers spent two nights trying to frighten.
