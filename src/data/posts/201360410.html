Introduction

Welcome to Tomorrow — The manifesto. Why wonder before fear. What this series is, and what it is not. The Asimov connection explained.

The Postcards

📬 From the Garage

Individual inventors, student scientists, makers, and tinkerers. The democratic-invention tradition. The future assembled from off-the-shelf parts.

  • Part I — The Turtle Watcher Burlington, Ontario. A backyard pool. How a fifteen-year-old watched a snapping turtle and reimagined ocean monitoring. Evan Budz, BURT, and the biomimicry instinct.

  • Part XI — The Five-Thousand-Dollar Humanoid. Hugging Face's open-source, 3D-printed robot.

  • Part XIX — The Giant Nut. Combat robotics and its hobbyist-to-aerospace-engineer pipeline.

  • Part XXV — The Puppet Hand That Became a Movement. e-NABLE's open-source 3D-printed prosthetics.

  • Part XXXIII — The Rowboat That Became a Fleet. Open Ocean Robotics — two adventurers who rowed across the Atlantic came home to a Vancouver Island garage and built a solar-powered ocean-monitoring fleet.

  • Part XLI — The Thirteen-Year-Old Who Solved a Problem the Adults Kept Arguing About. Beaverton, Oregon, 2026. Thirteen-year-old Roy Kim built a Peltier-module atmospheric water generator for drought-hit farms — a top-ten national finalist in the 3M Young Scientist Challenge.

🚀 From the Stars

Space robotics, planetary exploration, the lunar economy. The places humans cannot yet go — and the machines beginning to map them.

  • Part II — The Arm That Canada Built From a Toronto clean room to ninety shuttle missions to the Moon. How the Canadarm became the most durable machine in space — and what Canadarm3 will do next.

  • Part X — The Arm Without a Home. NASA shelves the Lunar Gateway eight days before a Canadian flies around the Moon.

  • Part XII — The Trillion-Dollar Rock. Asteroid mining robots earning their living inspecting ship hulls.

  • Part XXVI — The Tow Truck in Orbit. Active debris removal and the Kessler Syndrome.

  • Part XXXIV — The Gas Station in Geostationary Orbit. Northrop Grumman's Mission Robotic Vehicle — the first robot built to refuel a satellite it didn't launch with the fuel tank in mind.

  • Part XLII — The Four-Legged Geologist. University of Basel's Marslabor, 2026. A legged robot learns to read Martian and lunar terrain — gypsum, carbonates, basalt, dunite, anorthosite — semi-autonomously.

🔬 From the Lab

University and institutional research robotics. Biomimicry science. The biology-as-blueprint tradition from da Vinci to the present.

  • Part III — Nature’s Patent Office Three billion years of biological R&D — and the engineers finally reading the filing system. From da Vinci’s ornithopter to gecko adhesives to Harvard’s soft octopus robot to the MIT Cheetah.

  • Part XIII — The Third Frontier. Deep-sea robots and dual-use undersea infrastructure.

  • Part XX — The Rubble That Answers Back. Search-and-rescue robotics since 1995.

  • Part XXVII — The Robot With a Pulse. Biohybrid robots actuated by living muscle tissue.

  • Part XXXV — The Termite's Blueprint. A thousand simple robots, no leader, learn to assemble any shape at all.

  • Part XLIII — The Robot With No Brain At All. UC San Diego's Bioinspired Robotics and Design Lab. A fully 3D-printed, electronics-free, tadpole-inspired swimmer — powered by a pneumatic oscillator and a CO2 canister, with zero onboard circuitry.

🏭 From the Factory Floor

Industrial automation, warehouse robotics, agricultural systems. What is already happening while the humanoid debate continues.

  • Part IV — The Floor That Never Sleeps Four million robots on factory floors worldwide. One million at Amazon alone. The strawberry-picking problem. And the labour question that the wonder lane cannot dodge.

  • Part XIV — Ten Thousand Robots by Christmas. Beijing's deployment directive.

  • Part XXI — The Field That Drives Itself. Autonomous tractors and laser weeding.

  • Part XXVIII — The Demographic Argument. Haneda Airport's humanoid ground crew.

  • Part XXXVI — The House Built Before Lunch. A robot lays a house's walls in a day; a 3D printer beats a conventional apartment build by three months, next door.

  • Part XLIV — The Fifty Trillion Dollar Bet. Fremont, California, 2026. Tesla has shipped zero Optimus units and Elon Musk still calls it the biggest product the company will ever make — because he isn't comparing it to a product at all.

🏥 From the Theatre

Surgical robotics, elder care, rehabilitation, companion machines. The most intimate territory — where robots meet the human body.

  • Part V — The Hands That Never Shake Seventeen million da Vinci procedures. Japan’s PARO seal robot in the dementia ward. Exoskeletons teaching stroke survivors to walk again. And the questions that arise when robots get this close to the human body.

  • Part XV — The Surgeon Who Wasn't There. Teleoperated humanoid surgery in La Jolla.

  • Part XXII — The Needle That Doesn't Cut. Neuralink's transdural implant, Toronto.

  • Part XXIX — The Roommate That Isn't. Companion robots for isolated elders and children.

  • Part XXXVII — The Bone That Learned to Listen. Osseointegrated, neurally controlled bionic limbs — the high-cost counterpart to Part XXV's open-source hand.

  • Part XLVI — The Suit Worn by the Surgeon. Harrow, England, February 2026. A colorectal surgeon wears an exoskeleton through a seven-hour procedure — a robot built to protect the surgeon's body rather than meet the patient's.

⚔️ From the Battlefield

Military robotics, autonomous weapons, drone warfare. The hardest postcards to write. The necessary ones.

  • Part VI — Robots Do Not Bleed Ukraine as the world’s first full-scale robotics war laboratory. Two million drones. A ground robot that held a position for forty-five days. Russian soldiers surrendering to a machine. And the governance gap that no treaty has yet closed.

  • Part XVI — The First Robot War. Machine-on-machine combat in Ukraine.

  • Part XXIII — The Robot on the Beat. Police and security robots.

  • Part XXX — The Ray Gun That Grew Up. Counter-drone directed-energy weapons.

  • Part XXXVIII — The Machine That Clears the Ground It Didn't Break. Humanitarian demining robots in Azerbaijan and Ukraine — the war's own robots, turned to undoing an earlier war's damage.

  • Part XLV — The Box That Launches an Air Force. DARPA's containerized drone-swarm hub, versus China's ATLAS system — a five-hundred-aircraft constellation that ships like cargo.

🏠 From the Kitchen Table

Domestic and companion robotics. Rosie the Robot’s unfulfilled promise — and how close we actually are.

  • Part VII — Where Is Rosie? iRobot’s bankruptcy. The 1X NEO shipping at $20,000. The laundry folding problem that has stumped robotics for sixty years. And why the domestic robot that can fold a T-shirt is closer than most people have noticed.

  • Part XVII — Rosie's Rivals. 1X's NEO and the stranger sometimes behind its eyes.

  • Part XXIV — The Punch Card for Dinner. Moley Robotics' chef-motion-capture kitchen.

  • Part XXXI — The Fold That Finally Arrived, Sort Of. Laundry-folding robots ship at last.

  • Part XXXIX — The Wire Nobody Buries Anymore. Wire-free robotic lawn mowers finally solve the one part of the job that was never the mowing itself.

  • Part XLVII — The Robot That Watches the Litter Box. PETKIT and Whisker's AI-connected pet-care ecosystems — camera-equipped feeders and litter boxes building a per-pet health dashboard, and what an always-on camera in the home actually costs.

🪞 From the Mirror

Humanoid robotics. What it means to build something that looks like us — and what happens when we succeed.

  • Part VIII — The Mirror Problem Boston Dynamics retires hydraulics and ships Atlas to Hyundai factories. China controls ninety percent of global humanoid robot installations. And Masahiro Mori, who charted the uncanny valley at a world’s fair in 1970, died in 2025 having watched it begin to be crossed.

  • Part IX — The Body Electric. Beijing's Spring Festival Gala and its dancing, kung-fu-fighting humanoids.

  • Part XVIII — The Machine That Ran Faster. The marathon-winning robot; performance versus competence.

  • Part XXXII — The Robot in the Mirror. Fremont, Shenzhen, and Spartanburg, 2026. The humanoid race stopped being a highlight reel and became an industrial test — and what it mostly reflects back is us.

  • Part XL — The Hands That Came First. Sanctuary AI, Vancouver — the Canadian humanoid company this series promised, in its own Introduction, to eventually visit.

  • Part XLVIII — The Third Mirror. Germany's Neura Robotics and its 4NE1 humanoid — a genuine third geopolitical entrant beyond the US/China duopoly this series has tracked since Part VIII.

About This Series

Postcards from Tomorrow is a field series — dispatches from the places where the robotic future is actually being built, by real people, with real machines, solving real problems. Each postcard pairs a specific subject with a larger question.

It is a companion series to The Asimov Gap, which asks what rules should govern the machines we are building. Postcards from Tomorrow goes into the field and reports back on what is being built. One is the legislature. The other is the field correspondent.

Tagline: Dispatches from the future that’s already here.

Cadence: Bi-weekly.

The Canadian thread: Canada has a robotics story — from Canadarm to Waterloo’s RoboHub to Sanctuary AI to a fifteen-year-old in Burlington — that is extraordinary and largely untold. It runs through this series as a recurring motif without apology.

A note on scope: each territory above will receive multiple postcards over time. Part I will open From the Garage; future dispatches from that territory will follow as new stories emerge. The series will publish bi-weekly and has no predetermined conclusion. The future keeps generating postcards faster than they can be written.
