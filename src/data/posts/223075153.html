On a recent evening in Greenville, North Carolina, Donald Trump opened his rally by telling the story of Willie Horton, the 1988 crime case that helped sink Michael Dukakis. Thirty minutes later, from the same lectern, to the same crowd, he told it again. He used some of the same phrasing the second time. He got the facts wrong both times.

That single evening contains most of what this series has spent twenty-four instalments trying to count. The repetition is perseveration. The wrong facts are confabulation. The absence of anyone, on stage or in the hall, able to say “sir, you just told that one” is the institutional half of the problem. And the fact that it made no difference to the crowd is the political half.

The Baseline Problem began with a question Lawrence O’Donnell asked on air: if Donald Trump develops dementia, how would anyone know? His point was that Trump’s ordinary behaviour was already so far outside presidential norms that the usual warning signs would be invisible against it. The answer this series proposed was modest. Nobody here is diagnosing anyone. We count patterns instead, and we watch whether they change against his own baseline, not against George Washington’s.

For two dozen essays that counting has been done by hand, one rally and one Truth Social binge at a time. On November 1, 2026, Peter Baker and Dylan Freedman published in The New York Times the most systematic version of that exercise anyone has yet attempted. They covered Trump’s speeches, rallies, interviews, posts and schedules from 2015 to late October 2026, repeating a review they first ran in 2024.

A Disclosure Worth Making

Readers of this Substack know that I write in open collaboration with Claude, Anthropic’s AI model, and say so on the About page. So it is worth noting what the Times disclosed in its methodology section. To find the rambling and incoherent passages, the paper used Claude Opus 5.5 to score more than 6,500 excerpts of Trump’s speeches against a detailed rubric. A passage counted as incoherent only if it scored seven or higher out of ten. The rubric was built from manual review of hundreds of passages and checked for accuracy before it was trusted.

So an essay series written with Claude is now citing a newspaper audit scored by Claude. It is the right use of the tool. Nobody asked a machine whether the president is unwell. The machine read thousands of passages consistently, which is the job a pattern-counter needs done and cannot do alone. The judgement about what the counts mean stays with the humans who sign their names to it.

What the Numbers Say

The Times’ findings break into three groups: what has risen, what has plateaued, and what has not moved at all. All three matter, and an honest series has to report the second and third as carefully as the first.

What has risen. Trump now uses all-or-nothing words like “always”, “never”, “everything” and “totally” 19 per cent more often at rallies than he did a decade ago, and 5 per cent more than in 2024. Clinicians associate that kind of black-and-white language with advancing age and cognitive decline. His swearing has gone from six words per 10,000 a decade ago, to fourteen in 2024, to nearly fifteen now, which the Times notes is consistent with age-related disinhibition. His rallies have doubled in length, from an average of 42 minutes in 2016 to 85 minutes now.

What has plateaued. Rambling or confusing digressions occur about two and a half times as often as in the 2016 campaign. But that rate is roughly the same as in 2024. The big shift happened between his first campaign and his second, and has not obviously accelerated since.

What has not moved. His vocabulary is about the same as it was. So is the reading level of his speech, which sits between fourth and fifth grade. The average modern president speaks at about an eighth-grade level.

Then there are the hours. His public schedule has thinned to 1.3 events per weekday, against 2.1 in his first term. He posts online about 21 times a day, up from 13 in 2016 but down from 34 two years ago. On 30 per cent of the nights of his second term he has posted between midnight and 5 a.m. He has had 240 bursts of ten or more posts within a single hour.

The plateau is the most important finding for this series, and the least likely to make headlines. If the incoherence measure had kept climbing since 2024, the case would look like steady decline. It hasn’t. What has kept climbing are the measures of disinhibition and rigidity: profanity, absolutism, the sheer length of the performance. That is a narrower and more defensible claim than “he is getting worse at everything”. It is also the shape this series has been describing since Part XIX. The filter is going faster than the vocabulary.

The Freaking Genius

In May 2026, the White House physician, Dr. Sean P. Barbabella, released a three-page summary reporting that Trump scored 30 out of 30 on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment and that his cognitive and physical performance were excellent.

The Montreal Cognitive Assessment is a screening test, and a short one. Patients identify drawings of a lion, a camel and a rhinoceros. They draw a clock, repeat words and numbers in order, and name as many words beginning with F as they can in a minute. It exists to flag people who need a fuller workup. A perfect score tells you the floor has not given way. It says nothing about whether the house above it has shifted.

Trump does not describe it that way. A couple of weeks before the Times review, he told a crowd that nobody else could do the test, but he could, because he was “a freaking genius”. In Chillicothe, Ohio, on October 28, he gave an extended account of the tests unprompted. He said he was the only president to pass three of them and that Barack Obama could not have done so.

The Times counted that he raised his cognitive tests without being asked at about half a dozen recent rallies. For a series built on perseveration, a man who keeps returning to the subject of his own cognition is not a rebuttal. It is a data point. People who are untroubled by a question do not usually bring it up six times in a fortnight.

Meanwhile, the letter from Representative Jamie Raskin asking for an independent neuropsychological evaluation, first noted here in Part XIX, remains unanswered. A one-page screening result, announced by the patient’s own employee and celebrated by the patient at rallies, is not an answer to it.

Perseveration: The Husted Loop

The Times documented a fixation that would be comic in a less consequential man. At a rally with Senator Jon Husted of Ohio on October 12, Trump told the crowd he had examined Husted’s body, specifically his backside. Five days later, in Kentucky, he said it again and added that he had never done that with a man before. Three days after that, in North Carolina, he said it again. In Chillicothe, he said it a fourth time.

The specific content is less important than the loop. Four tellings in about two weeks, each delivered as if fresh, each prefaced with the same disclaimer about never having done it before. That is the Willie Horton pattern stretched across a fortnight instead of half an hour. The same fixation spread to other men: Senator Tim Scott, Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s physique, Representative Mike Collins described as “all man”.

His allies say these are jokes, and some of them plainly are meant that way. But a joke is something you remember telling. A story repeated to the same audience on the same night is not a bit.

Confabulation: The Hormone Strait and the Pulitzer Peace Prize

Two of the Times’ examples belong in this series’ running file on word substitution and confident error.

The first is small. Trump has taken to calling the Strait of Hormuz the “Hormone Strait”. This is the waterway at the centre of the Iran war this Substack has tracked for most of the year. One mispronunciation is nothing. The same one, repeated, about the most strategically important body of water in his presidency, is not quite nothing.

The second is better. In Pendleton, South Carolina, in late September, Trump asked the crowd whether anyone knew the name of the peace prize. Then he offered his own answer: the Pulitzer Prize. This is the award he has publicly coveted for two years and complained about losing at nearly every opportunity. He has spoken about it more than almost any other honour on earth, and for a moment he could not find its name.

Confabulation in the clinical sense is not lying. It is the brain filling a gap with something plausible and delivering it with full confidence. “Pulitzer” is a plausible fill for a prize with a famous name. What makes it worth recording is not the slip but the confidence, and the fact that nobody near him corrected it.

Displacement: Norway Pays for Oslo

When the name did come back to him, in Port Huron, Michigan, the result was worse. Having remembered that the Nobel Peace Prize is awarded in Norway, Trump said he would like to ban all immigrants from Norway because he had not won it. He added that Norway “sucks”, and used a vulgarity for the country that echoes the one he used for African nations in his first term.

On September 9, 2026, Tom Nichols argued in The Atlantic that Trump lashes out at friends harder than at enemies when he is frustrated. The pattern ran from the Epstein files to his own party, from Iran to South Korea, and from stalled trade talks to Canada. I banked that argument at the time as a possible new category for this series, distinct from perseveration, confabulation and disinhibition: displacement onto allies.

Norway completes the case. It is a NATO founding member, a reliable ally and a country whose government has no say in who wins the prize. The Norwegian Nobel Committee is appointed by parliament but is independent of the government. Trump’s grievance is with five people in a mansion behind the Royal Palace in Oslo. His proposed punishment falls on five and a half million Norwegians. That is displacement in its purest form: the frustration lands not where it began but wherever it can be expressed safely.

Disinhibition: What No Other President Would Say

Trump said that at an unfenced construction site “only a Hispanic could survive that fall”. He told supporters to “cheat like hell” in the November 3 elections. He said military leakers should be shot. He said he did not care if he was seen as caring more about oil company profits than struggling workers, and then said it anyway, while acknowledging it would make bad headlines. He told a reporter who asked whether he was cancelling a Kansas trip because he was tired that she was disgusting and should get herself into shape.

He also said, without visible irony, that he does not go around mocking people. He said this while using a mocking nickname for James Talarico and a transphobic one for Dr. Annie Andrews.

Disinhibition is not the same as rudeness. Trump has always been rude, and the White House is right that his supporters prize what they call his authenticity. Spokesman Davis Ingle told the Times that the president does not care about being politically correct. The diagnostic interest lies elsewhere: in remarks that work against the speaker’s own stated interest, delivered while he describes, out loud, why they will hurt him. A man who says “I’m going to say it” before saying something he knows will cost him is narrating the failure of his own filter in real time.

Even Trump seems to notice. At a rally in Clarksville, Tennessee, he marvelled that he could say the stupidest things and his crowd would still clap, before quickly adding that he doesn’t say stupid things. It was the most accurate thing he said all night, and he retracted it within a breath.

The Geography of a Tired Man

Two other strands, reported in the final fortnight of the campaign, fit the same picture.

In the closing weeks, the Times review places Trump at rallies in Ohio, Kentucky, North Carolina, South Carolina, Tennessee, Georgia and Michigan, much of it territory he carried comfortably.

Even those states were no sure refuge. On October 26, 2026, Carl Hulse reported in The New York Times that Senator Roger Marshall was under siege in Kansas, that Democrats were running strong in Iowa and Alaska and were close to winning North Carolina, and that independents were within reach of Republicans in Montana, Nebraska and South Dakota. Trump was not visiting safe ground. He was visiting the ground that still felt safe to him. A week later, Marshall lost his seat to Adam Hamilton, the first Democrat to win a Senate seat in Kansas since 1932.

Much of that is ordinary campaign strategy, and the scheduling was not his alone. But it fits a pattern this series has watched since Part XXIII: he goes where the crowds feed him. An 85-minute rally in friendly territory is less a feat of stamina than a form of self-regulation, the one setting where the weave is guaranteed applause. The Times noted that he has been known to nod off in White House meetings but draws energy from crowds. That is the same observation from the other side.

Inside the building, the mood was darker. On October 28, as Katie Rogers reported in The New York Times, the White House facing the midterms was grim and unpredictable. Her account included a president who dictated a 1,300-word post attacking Bill Maher, a daily media intake heavy on what has come to be called AI slop, and a court of favourites, among them Bill Pulte, whose main qualification for influence is access. A man whose information diet is curated by a handful of aides, and padded with machine-made flattery, is a man whose baseline is being set partly by others.

The Test He Wrote for Himself

The most remarkable passage in the Times review is not the Times’. It is Trump’s own.

Speaking to the Republican midterm convention in Dallas in September, he explained what he calls “the weave”. You talk about one thing, he said, then you bring in a ballroom or an airplane or Air Force One, but you always come back to the original topic. Then he went further. Someday, he said, there will come a time when he does the weave and does not get back to the subject. And then, he told the room, “you can say he’s had it.”

It is worth sitting with that. For twenty-four instalments this series has tried to answer Lawrence O’Donnell’s question: against a baseline so unusual, how would anyone know? And the subject has now supplied his own answer, in public, with a specific, observable criterion. The weave that doesn’t come back.

He also stumbled through the very sentence describing the test; the Times transcribed the weave becoming “weave, weave”. That proves nothing. One garbled clause in an 85-minute speech happens to every tired speaker, and this series does not build cases on single moments.

But the Times has now built the instrument he described. It scored 6,500 passages, measured against a decade of his own speech, by a rubric tuned to catch exactly the kind of digression that never finds its way home. The machine does not get tired, does not clap and does not need to be in the room. It found that the digressions had multiplied since 2016 and then held steady since 2024. The profanity and the absolutism kept rising. The vocabulary stayed where it was.

That is not a diagnosis, and nobody honest should call it one. It is a baseline, measured properly for the first time, against which every future rally can now be checked. Stephen Leacock once wrote of a man who flung himself upon his horse and rode madly off in all directions. Trump has told us that one day he will ride off and not come back. Now, at least, someone is keeping the map.
