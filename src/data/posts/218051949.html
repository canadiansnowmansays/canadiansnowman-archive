FROM: The Royal Danish Office of Gentle Clarification, Copenhagen TO: The President of the United States of America RE: Your New Deal (Our Old Deal) DATE: 29 September 2026 CLASSIFICATION: Awkward

Dear Mr President,

First, congratulations. We watched the signing ceremony at the United Nations on 22 September 2026 and we could see how happy you were. You called the agreement long in the making. You were right. It was long in the making. It was, in fact, seventy-five years in the making.

This is the part where we must apologise.

We are sorry. We are so very sorry. We are Danish, so we are apologising quietly, with good posture, but please understand that on the inside we are apologising very loudly.

The deal you just celebrated is the deal you already had, with the annexes filled in and the island finally invited into the room.

On 27 April 1951, a man from the United States and a man from Denmark sat down in Copenhagen and signed something called the Agreement relating to the Defense of Greenland. It let the Americans build bases on Greenland, with Danish agreement, under NATO plans. The Americans built one at Thule. It is still there. You renamed it Pituffik Space Base in 2023, which we thought was a very nice name, and we did not say anything, because it was your turn to have a good idea.

Then, last week, you signed a new agreement that — how do we put this — lets the Americans build bases on Greenland, with Danish agreement, under NATO plans. As the House of Commons Library’s briefing on the deal puts it, the 2026 treaty is an update of the 1951 agreement. The Greenlandic Prime Minister, Jens-Frederik Nielsen, said on the day that it brings the 1951 agreement into 2026. He meant this kindly. We all did.

We did not want to spoil the ceremony. There were flags. There was a pen. Someone had polished the pen.

How Did This Happen?

We have given this a great deal of thought, as we have long winters.

We believe the problem is that the new deal and the old deal look different from a distance. But up close they are like twins. Identical twins. One of them is simply wearing a hat.

It is a very good hat. It says GOLDEN DOME on it. We would not dream of saying anything against the hat.

To be fair to the hat, it came with accessories. The new deal keeps bases belonging to anyone outside NATO off the island unless all three of us agree. It looks harder at investors from outside NATO and the European Union. It promises to stay in force even if Greenland one day decides it has had enough of us, too. These are sensible accessories. We helped choose them. They are still accessories.

A Comparison, Prepared With Love

For your convenience, our officials have prepared a short table. We have printed it in large type and there are no footnotes.

As you can see, the differences are real. Even the unmanned installations have to ask permission now. And Greenlanders were in the room this time, which, to be fair, should have happened in 1951 and is the one thing in this whole business everybody ought to be proud of.

But the owner of Greenland is the same. The rule for changing the deal is the same. The part where America asks and Denmark says yes is the same. The parliaments in Copenhagen and Nuuk still have to approve it before it counts, which is also the same, because that is how it works when a country belongs to the people who live in it.

Perhaps a Sandwich Would Help

In Denmark, we eat a thing called smørrebrød. It is an open-faced sandwich. One slice of rye bread, and on top, something nice: herring, egg, a little cucumber.

Imagine that in 1951 we made you a smørrebrød.

You ate it for seventy-five years. You said very often that it was a terrible sandwich and that you would like to buy the bakery.

Then in 2026, you sent it back to the kitchen. We took the very same sandwich, cut off the crusts, and put one small slice of cucumber on top in the shape of a missile shield.

You said it was the best sandwich in the history of sandwiches, maybe ever, and that nobody had ever made a sandwich like it.

We said thank you.

We are sorry.

Or Perhaps Some Bricks

We are also the country that invented LEGO, so allow us one more try.

Every year, we sell the same little plastic bricks in a new box. The bricks click together exactly the way they did in 1958. The box has a new picture on the front. Children are delighted. Parents know. Nobody says anything.

You have bought the new box, Mr President. Inside are the bricks you already owned. The finished model looks very much like the old one, with two extra towers in the south and east.

You may keep the box.

Some Things We Did Not Give You

In the interest of Danish honesty, which is like regular honesty but in a nicer sweater, we should also mention the things that are not in the box.

Greenland is not in the box. The Greenlanders are not in the box. The price tag you once talked about is not in the box. You said afterwards that America would have permanent control over security and all other needs, and we can see why the word “permanent” would feel good, and we did not want to correct you on the day, because you had just been given a pen.

But “all other needs” is not in the box either. We looked. Twice. We turned the box upside down.

A Word from Our Friends in Canada

Before closing, we took advice from our friends in Canada, who are the world’s leading authority on saying sorry. Canadians apologise when you bump into them. They apologise to the door when it closes on them. They have also, like us, spent the better part of two years being told by you that they would make a lovely addition to the United States. So we asked them how best to apologise to you. They thought about it for a long time, very politely, and then said: “Sorry, but no.”

We found this very helpful. We have written it down.

In Closing

We want you to know that we are not laughing at you. We are laughing near you, which is a Scandinavian tradition, and it is how we survive January.

You have not failed. You have, in fact, done something very rare in diplomacy. You have taken an agreement that worked, threatened for a year and a half to throw it away, frightened a small and friendly country half to death, strained the alliance that has kept your own people safe since before you were old enough to vote, and then, at the very end, signed the thing you started with, with a new hat on it.

In your country you have a book called The Art of the Deal.

In our country we would like to publish a sequel. We are calling it The Art of Making the Same Deal Again. It is quite short. Most of it is photocopied from 1951.

With our warmest regards, our deepest apologies, and a small plate of smørrebrød, crusts removed,

The Kingdom of Denmark (the people, mostly; the government remains very busy being polite)

P.S. Please do not ask to buy the sandwich. It is not for sale. It was never for sale. It is, and we cannot stress this enough, a sandwich.
