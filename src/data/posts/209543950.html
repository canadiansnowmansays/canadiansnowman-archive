The Copyright Wars has, so far, been a story told through dockets: Bartz’s settlement numbers, Cruz’s holdout opt-outs, the fight in Boston over whether Sony and Universal get to add 61,026 more recordings to the case against Suno. All of that remains the load-bearing legal drama, and none of it resolves before 2027 at the earliest. But a New York Times explainer on AI music this month is worth a detour, because it surfaces two things the court filings don’t capture: how the industry is already living with AI music regardless of how the lawsuits land, and how much of the current mess has nothing to do with training-data theft at all.

The Deal That Didn’t Need a Verdict

While Sony and Universal fight Suno in Boston, Warner Music has done something more interesting: it made a deal. Warner’s catalog — Madonna, Bruno Mars, Neil Young among others — is now available to Suno on an opt-in basis, meaning any Warner artist can choose to let the service train on and generate music styled after their own work. It’s a bet that controlled, compensated AI access beats prohibition, and it’s the first real test of whether “Madonna-style birthday song” commands a premium over generic “’80s synth-pop” once an actual artist’s name and blessing are attached.

It also exposes the fault line the lawsuits can’t fully resolve on their own. Suno’s own system, when asked to imitate Neil Young directly, redirects the prompt toward “similar styles” rather than the artist by name — an internal guardrail against exactly the publicity-rights and copyright exposure Warner’s deal is designed to license around. That guardrail exists because the legal question of whether imitating a specific artist’s voice or style infringes rights of publicity, separate from copyright entirely, remains genuinely unsettled — a second legal track running alongside the training-data fight this series has focused on.

The Split Among Artists Isn’t Close to Settled

It would be convenient for this series’ thesis if artists were unanimous in opposition. They are not. Timbaland has introduced a virtual artist, TaTa Taktumi, whose vocals were partly AI-generated on a track he says was otherwise 80 to 85 percent human-made — a framing clearly designed to preempt exactly the criticism this docket has been tracking. Grimes has likewise signaled openness to experimentation. Against that: an open letter from the Artists Rights Alliance, signed by more than 200 major acts including Billie Eilish, Norah Jones, and Stevie Wonder, calling unchecked generative AI in music an “assault on human creativity.”

That split matters for how this series should read the Bartz settlement and the Suno litigation going forward. This is not a monolithic industry suing a monolithic technology. It’s a fractured industry — labels suing on behalf of catalogs while some of their own signed artists experiment enthusiastically with the same tools — which makes any eventual settlement or verdict a partial answer at best. Even a plaintiff’s win in Boston will not stop Timbaland or Grimes from doing exactly what they’re already doing, because they have separately, individually consented to it.

The Bigger Scam Isn’t the One in Court

The detail most worth pulling out of the Times piece is Deezer’s. The French streaming service reports roughly 90,000 AI-generated tracks uploaded to its platform daily, and — this is the number that should reframe the whole conversation — as many as 85 percent of the AI tracks that actually get clicks are fraudulent, generated by scammers and then driven to plays by bot armies to harvest royalty payouts from the same finite pool that’s supposed to pay human artists.

This is a different problem from anything Bartz v. Anthropic or the Suno case addresses, and arguably a more urgent one in the near term. The training-data lawsuits are about whether AI companies owed something to artists whose work trained the models. The streaming-fraud problem is about AI-generated content actively siphoning money away from real artists right now, today, regardless of how any lawsuit resolves — because the industry’s own royalty-pool math treats a bot-farmed play identically to a human one. Grammy-aligned industry groups have begun pushing for mandatory AI-content labeling as a partial fix, but labeling doesn’t stop bot clicks from diluting the pool; it only tells a human listener what they’re hearing, assuming they bother to check.

Where This Leaves the Series

None of this changes the trajectory the Boston and New York cases are on — April 9, 2027 remains the earliest either court is likely to rule on the underlying fair-use question, per the last docket update. But it does mean this series should widen its aperture slightly. The training-data fight is the marquee legal battle, and it’s the one with a trial date attached, so it will keep anchoring this sub-series. But the more immediate damage to musicians right now may be coming from a source with no court date at all — a royalty system built for a world where every stream represented a human listener, now flooded with fraudulent plays that no lawsuit currently pending addresses.

Asimov never wrote a robot story about royalty fraud, but the underlying failure is a familiar one from this whole series: a system built on an assumption — that a play is a person — quietly stops being true, and nobody updates the system until the damage is already showing up in someone else’s paycheck.
