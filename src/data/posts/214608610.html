by Stephen Gordon

The count arrives at 1,623, and it hands me the entry this whole series has quietly been building toward without my quite admitting it — because 1,623 is the year the most important book in the English language was assembled out of loose paper by two men who are not the author, seven years after the author was dead, and I have come to believe that those two men, and not the genius whose name is on the spine, are the true patron saints of everything I do here.

Their names were John Heminges and Henry Condell. Hold them for a moment, because almost nobody does. They were actors — working members of the King’s Men, the company for which William Shakespeare wrote and in which he held shares — colleagues of his, ordinary professional players who trod the boards beside him for two decades and then, when he died, did the single most consequential act of bookkeeping in the history of literature. They counted his plays. They gathered them, sorted them, corrected them, and put thirty-six of them between two covers, and in doing so they saved half of them from vanishing off the face of the earth.

I need you to feel the size of that word, vanishing, because this is the part that stops me cold every time.

When Shakespeare died in 1616, he had made no arrangement to preserve his plays. Why would he? A play, in 1616, was not literature. It was working stock — a script, the property of the company, written to be performed, not read. Roughly half of his plays had been printed in his lifetime, in cheap single editions called quartos, some of them decent, some of them garbled pirate versions taken down from memory by someone in the audience. But the other half had never been printed at all. They existed only as manuscripts and playhouse copies — a few fragile stacks of paper, in a London made of wood, in an age of plague and fire. And on that list of the never-printed, the plays that existed in 1622 as nothing more than loose paper one careless candle away from oblivion, are these: Macbeth. The Tempest. Twelfth Night. Julius Caesar. As You Like It. Measure for Measure. Antony and Cleopatra. The Winter’s Tale.

Read that list again and understand what it means. If Heminges and Condell had shrugged — if those two ordinary men had decided, as ordinary men usually do, that the effort was too great and someone else would surely see to it — then “tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow” does not exist. “We are such stuff as dreams are made on” is never written down that anyone keeps. “The quality of mercy is not strained” survives, because that one happens to be in a play they’d printed early, but “if music be the food of love, play on” does not. Half the sky of the English language simply never rises. We would know Shakespeare as a talented playwright of the middle rank, author of Romeo and Juliet and Richard III and a handful of others — good, but not the summit, because the summit is disproportionately made of the plays that only just made it onto the boat.

This is a Numbers Game essay, and here is the number-idea, and it is one I have circled for two thousand essays: a work that is not counted into permanence does not survive on merit. It survives because someone kept the ledger. Merit is necessary and it is nowhere near sufficient. The universe does not preserve genius automatically; it preserves what somebody troubled to write down, copy, and store. I made this argument at essay 1,519, standing over the shut notebooks of Leonardo da Vinci — the greatest observer of his age, who published essentially nothing, and whose discoveries therefore changed almost nothing for centuries because they sat in a drawer. Shakespeare was very nearly Leonardo. He was a man who poured the summit of human expression into loose paper and then walked away from it, indifferent to its keeping. The difference — the entire difference, the reason one of them remade the world and the other became a beautiful footnote to what-might-have-been — is that Shakespeare had a Heminges and a Condell, and Leonardo did not. Genius supplies the work. Someone else, almost always, supplies the survival.

Regular readers of this series will feel a bell ringing here, and I want to ring it deliberately, because it is the same finding arriving from the far side of the map. At essay 1,835 I told you about young Darwin at the Galápagos, who nearly lost the discovery of the century because he failed to label his finches by island — and whose theory was salvaged only because his less brilliant shipmates had, out of plain habit, tagged their specimens properly. I called it the Covington protocol, after the servant who filled in the column the genius left blank. The First Folio is the Covington protocol raised to the highest power in human culture. Heminges and Condell were not Shakespeares. They were the men who filled in the column he left blank — who did the dull, devoted, uncelebrated clerical labour of gathering and ordering and preserving, without which the genius above them would have evaporated. Every great count in these files, I have found, required someone like them: the keeper, the tagger, the one who wrote it down. History files the work under the genius. It survives because of the clerk.

Consider what the labour actually was, because it was not small. For seven years they worked at it. They hunted down the good manuscripts and set them against the bad quartos, trying to establish, play by play, line by line, what the man had actually written — the first great act of editing in English, performed by two amateurs on a friend’s remains. They organised the thirty-six plays into the three categories we still use: Comedies, Histories, Tragedies. They arranged the financing and the printing of an enormous, expensive volume — a folio, the large and serious format reserved for Bibles and works of weight, itself a quiet argument that these playhouse scripts deserved to sit on the shelf beside scripture. And they wrote a short address to the reader, which contains the single most human sentence in the history of publishing. Explaining why they had done it, these two actors said they had gathered the plays only “to keep the memory of so worthy a friend and fellow alive as was our Shakespeare.” Not for money. Not for the ages. For their friend. The most important book in the language exists because two men did not want their mate to be forgotten. I have read that line more times than I can count, and it has never once failed to move me, because it is the purest statement I know of the thing underneath this entire series: that counting, in the end, is how we refuse to let what we love disappear.

And the numbers of the book itself keep the theme going, so let me close the ledger properly. They printed, the scholars estimate, around seven hundred and fifty copies of the First Folio in 1623. Of those, roughly two hundred and thirty-five survive today — a survival rate that would have been far worse without the format’s very bulk and dignity, which made people keep it rather than use it up. And each surviving copy is now among the most valuable printed objects on earth; one sold a few years ago for just under ten million American dollars. Turn that over. At essay 1,440 I celebrated Gutenberg for making the rare common — for taking the scarce, precious, hand-copied book and rendering it endless and cheap. The First Folio ran the machine in reverse and proved the other half of the same law: make a thing that matters, print it in the hundreds, let four centuries pass, and the common becomes rare again, and then priceless. The same pages that Heminges and Condell printed to save Shakespeare from oblivion are now guarded in climate-controlled vaults precisely because they succeeded. The book that was made to keep the memory alive is now itself the memory being kept.

I owe you one more figure from inside the volume, because it completes the accounting and it is the line everyone half-remembers without knowing where it comes from. Heminges and Condell, needing to introduce their friend’s collected works, secured a commendatory poem from the greatest literary name then living — Ben Jonson, Shakespeare’s rival, his friend, his sharpest and most competitive contemporary, a man who had once said Shakespeare lacked art and would happily have buried him under a better epitaph than praise. And Jonson, given the chance to file the definitive verdict at the graveside, did the generous thing, and he did it in the language of time and reckoning. He wrote that Shakespeare was “not of an age, but for all time.” That phrase — worn so smooth by four centuries of quotation that we forget it was ever fresh — is a claim about the count. An age is a number; all time is the refusal of the number. Jonson was saying that this man’s ledger would never close, would go on being audited and found solvent by generation after generation with no final entry. And here is the quiet joke the Numbers Game cannot resist: Jonson could only make that boast, and be right, because two actors had just spent seven years making sure there would be a book for all time to read. “For all time” is not a property of the plays. It is a property of the plays plus the folio — genius plus preservation, the work plus the keeping. Jonson supplied the prophecy. Heminges and Condell supplied the paper that let it come true. Prophecy without a printer is just a compliment at a funeral.

Dior has no patience for the theatre and would have made a poor Elizabethan — she does not care for crowds, standing, or being shushed, and the groundlings’ pit would have driven her under a bench. But she understands preservation perfectly, in her way, because she is herself a keeper of things against loss: she has buried, in the small patch of earth behind the townhouse, a archive of items she considers too important to be trusted to the surface world — a particular bone, a weatherproofed toy, a rawhide of evident significance — each one interred, catalogued in whatever ledger a husky keeps, and revisited. She is running her own First Folio out there, a collected works of everything she has loved and refused to let vanish, printed in earth. I watched her check on it this morning, nose down, taking inventory, confirming the count. The impulse is exactly Heminges and Condell’s, stripped to its animal root: this mattered to me, and I will not let it be lost. Everything else — the folios, the archives, the two thousand essays, the memory of a worthy friend — is that impulse wearing better clothes.

The count moves on, as it does, and it moves toward a much darker relationship between a man and the truth he had counted. The next landmark is essay 1,633, and it is one of the grave ones. Heminges and Condell printed a dead friend’s words to keep them alive; ten years later, in a room in Rome, a living man will be made to kneel and take his own true words back. This series has just shown you the count kept out of love. It is about to show you the count denied under threat. Bring nothing that breaks.

Two actors, seven years, thirty-six plays, seven hundred and fifty copies, and one sentence about a friend. Half of Shakespeare reached us on their devotion alone. The genius wrote the plays. The keepers wrote the future.

See you at 1,633, when a man is ordered to un-write what he knew.
