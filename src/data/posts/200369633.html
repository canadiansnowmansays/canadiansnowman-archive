On Monday afternoon, Eamon Javers of CNBC asked the President of the United States about reports that Iran might suspend the negotiations to end the war the President had launched.

“I don’t care if they’re over, honestly,” Trump replied. “I really don’t care. I couldn’t care less. If they’re over, they’re over.”

Eleven weeks earlier, Trump had been asked the same question in a different form. Could he get bored of fighting Iran? “There’s nothing boring about this,” he said.

This week he found out he could.

Between those two declarations: a 38-day bombing campaign, thousands dead, the Strait of Hormuz closed for three months, the global economy destabilised, eurozone inflation at a two-and-a-half-year high, American inflation at a three-year high, two of the world’s three major central banks now contemplating rate hikes, 13 American service members killed, and a ceasefire that has been violated so many times the word has stopped meaning anything. The man who launched the war has decided the negotiations to end it are, by his own admission, very boring. The foreign minister of the country he is fighting predicted this in print.

Karim Sadjadpour of the Carnegie Endowment, writing in The Atlantic this week, named the structural problem more clearly than anyone has “America under Trump is the attention-deficit superpower, pinballing from isolationism to interventionism in Venezuela, Iran, and Cuba, having hollowed out the State Department. The Islamic Republic is an obsessive-compulsive revolutionary state — a regime with a half-century fixation on resisting America, rather than advancing the welfare of its own people. Fighting America is not the regime’s policy; it’s the regime’s identity.”

His thesis is the one no one else has been willing to write: “Washington needs a deal, but Tehran needs an enemy.” America seeks resolution. Iran is committed to revolution. A monopoly on focus can outlast a monopoly on power. That is the war in a sentence.

And Sadjadpour found the smoking gun: in Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi’s 2025 diplomatic memoir, the man now leading Iran’s negotiating team wrote out the playbook. “The Iranian negotiation style is generally known in the world as the ‘bazaar style,’ which means continuous and tireless bargaining. This method is a process of interaction that requires great patience and time. He who gets tired and bored quickly will lose.”

Iran’s foreign minister put the strategy in print. The American president just confessed, on camera, to the failure mode the strategy was designed to trigger.

And then, on Tuesday, the American secretary of state confirmed the diagnosis under oath (CNBC)." → Luke Fountain, CNBC, June 2, 2026 Testifying before the Senate Foreign Relations Committee, Marco Rubio conceded the structural problem in a single, devastating sentence: “Talks with Iran are not like talks with Switzerland. They’re very different. They require the use of intermediators, unfortunately.” That is the secretary of state of the United States, in sworn testimony, telling the Senate that the war his administration started cannot be ended through ordinary diplomacy — and that his president’s frustration at the pace is therefore a category error. Sadjadpour identified the structure. Araghchi published the playbook. Rubio has now confirmed it from the witness stand. Three voices, three perspectives, one diagnosis. The only person in this story who has not understood the diagnosis is the man whose decisions depend on understanding it.

To understand how Trump arrived at “very boring,” consider what happened in the eight days before he said it.

Wednesday, May 27: Mediators told the White House that Iran was comfortable with the latest framework. Iran said yes. The deal Trump’s team had spent weeks negotiating was, finally, in shape to be signed (New York Times).

Thursday, May 28: Vice President JD Vance told reporters: “It’s hard to say exactly when or if the president is going to sign. I can’t guarantee that we’re going to get there.” The vice president of the United States, on the record, hedging the signature of his own president on the war his own president launched.

Friday, May 29: Trump spent two hours in the Situation Room with his top aides. He emerged with no announcement.

Saturday, May 30: U.S. forces struck radar and command-and-control sites at Goruk and on Qeshm Island. The third round of “self-defense strikes” in eight days.

Sunday, May 31: The administration leaked that Trump had toughened the terms of the deal he had just received Iran’s agreement on. His stated objection: the unfrozen funds provisions reminded him too much of Obama’s Pallets of Cash — which is to say, of the very framework his own team had engineered to avoid that comparison. The unnamed official offered the leak’s preferred spin: the tougher terms were “potentially intended to speed up the process by putting pressure on Iran to accept the framework already sent.” The same official conceded in the next sentence that “any changes to the document, known as the memorandum of understanding, could mean additional delays.” The cited rationale and the predicted outcome contradicted each other in adjacent paragraphs.

Monday, June 1, morning: Iran fired two ballistic missiles at U.S. forces in Kuwait. Both intercepted. Kuwait’s foreign ministry called it “a dangerous escalation and a direct assault on the security and stability” of the country. It was the third week in a row Kuwait — a sovereign nation, U.S. ally, not a party to this war — had been targeted because of where the U.S. was launching strikes from.

Monday afternoon: Trump told CNBC that the negotiations were “very boring” and “I couldn’t care less.”

Monday evening: Trump posted on Truth Social that he had spoken to Netanyahu and “indirectly with Hezbollah” and announced: “Israel will not attack them, and they will not attack Israel.”

Monday night: Netanyahu issued a statement that made no mention of a ceasefire, vowed to continue Israeli operations “as planned in southern Lebanon,” and added that the threat to strike Beirut “remains.”

Tuesday, June 2: Israeli forces continued striking southern Lebanon. A new evacuation order was issued for Nabatieh. Secretary of State Marco Rubio testified before the Senate that an interim deal “could happen today, it could happen tomorrow, it could happen next week.” Senator Cory Booker observed that the war Rubio kept insisting was “over” was, in fact, still being fought.

Tuesday afternoon: Trump posted on Truth Social that reports of a breakdown in talks were “Fake News” and that conversations had been going on “continuously, including four days ago, three days ago, two days ago, one day ago, and today.” Less than twenty-four hours after telling a national television interviewer that he couldn’t care less if the talks were over, the president of the United States declared that they had never stopped — and counted backwards on his fingers, in public, to prove it. The Monday confession was either a lie or the Tuesday rebuttal was. There is no third reading.

Wednesday, June 3: Iranian drones struck Terminal 1 of Kuwait International Airport New York Times, killing a civilian and injuring others. Flights were suspended. Iran also fired missiles at U.S. Fifth Fleet headquarters in Bahrain. Wednesday morning: Trump told the New York Post that Iran's supreme leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, was personally involved in the negotiations and that "I hope to meet with him." Monday: "I couldn't care less." Tuesday: "continuously, four days ago, three days ago." Wednesday: "I hope to meet with him." Three days, three positions, the same war. The supreme leader the United States and Israel tried to kill on day one and have not seen in public since March is now the man the president of the United States hopes to meet personally. Three months ago this was unconditional surrender. Today it is a hoped-for handshake with a man in hiding.

This is what a president looks like when his own playbook bores him.

David Sanger of the New York Times, who has covered the Iranian nuclear programme for twenty years, was direct about the meaning of Trump’s CNBC interview. “There are reasons to doubt that Mr. Trump is truly so unconcerned.” Gasoline prices have soared. An Exxon-Mobil executive recently predicted they could go far higher. Republicans are watching their constituents’ fury at the war. And the Times noted, with the dry precision of an obituary, that Trump “has professed indifference about a range of issues that carry deep political consequences for him, including the midterm elections and the financial situation that Americans are facing.”

There it is. The press corps’ verdict: Trump’s serial “I don’t cares” are no longer a negotiating posture. They are the posture. Midterms, gas prices, the financial situation of ordinary Americans, the war he started — he has declared, in turn, that he is uninterested in each of them. Sanger does not say the word detachment. He doesn’t need to.

But even Sanger’s careful framing missed the most damning line. In March, when an aide warned that the President might lose interest in the conflict, Trump dismissed it: “Somebody said today, they said, ‘oh, well, the president wants to do it really quickly. After that, he’ll get bored.’ There’s nothing boring about this.”

It took eleven weeks. Araghchi predicted it in his memoir. The press is documenting it in real time.

What the structural collapse of the deal looks like from above is one thing. What it looks like from below is another.

In Dahiya, the southern suburb of Beirut that Netanyahu threatened to strike on Monday and Trump claimed to have saved hours later, the residents are the only people in this story whose lives actually hang on what each capital announces.

“I lost count of how many times I’ve evacuated,” said Zahra Khomasi, 43, sitting in her car with her 14-year-old son and 9-year-old daughter on Monday. She fled in 2024. She fled in March. She returned in April. She fled again Monday. “We’ve somehow become used to this.”

“I don’t believe in this cease-fire,” said Batoul Hassan Srour, 47, in a shelter in Aramoun north of Beirut. “We heard this many times, but we need action, not just talk.”

“These cease-fires are like anesthetic shots,” said Saada al-Qadi, 50, who had fled to a relative’s apartment in the mountains. “I see no true cease-fire on the horizon.”

Moussa Hariri, who lives in the centre of Dahiya, sent his wife and children to a shelter on Monday. He refused to leave. “None of this really concerns me anymore. I would rather die in my own bed. I don’t want to leave Dahiya.”

“What sort of cease-fire allows bombing the south but just not Beirut?” asked Abbas Mousa, 45, from the coastal city of Sidon.

“All theater,” said Abbas Fahd, 45, in Nabatieh, after the Israeli evacuation order. “We want a cease-fire, but we do not believe the war is likely to end anytime soon.”

Lebanon’s President Joseph Aoun, in a statement issued as his own capital was being threatened, offered the dignity of a small country facing a larger one over its corpse: “Some regrettably consider negotiation to be surrender. It is not that, nor is it a concession. It is a solution to stop wars with the least possible harm.”

The president who could end this war by saying so finds it boring. The president of the country being bombed in the process is reminding everyone what negotiation actually is.

Israel’s reaction to Trump’s social media diplomacy was its own revelation.

Yair Lapid, leader of Israel’s parliamentary opposition, called the country under Netanyahu “a total vassal state.” Naftali Bennett, the prime minister’s top political rival, said the government had “lost control over Israeli sovereignty.” Even Itamar Ben-Gvir, the far-right minister of national security in Netanyahu’s own coalition, posted: “Mr. prime minister, you said a strong prime minister says ‘yes’ to the president of the United States, when possible, and ‘no,’ when needed. This is the time to say ‘no’ to our friend, President Trump.”

A coalition cabinet member, on social media, publicly instructing the prime minister to defy the American president. The man who began this war alongside Trump three months ago has now been sidelined from the negotiations to end it, contradicted in real time by Truth Social posts that claim to speak for his government, and humiliated by his own coalition for accepting the arrangement.

Sadjadpour predicted this too. Iran has demanded that any deal end the fighting on all fronts, including Lebanon. Netanyahu has refused. The deal cannot accommodate both demands. So the deal cannot be signed. So Trump posted a fiction of one and Netanyahu walked it back six hours later. The Israeli prime minister, having lost his American backing on Iran, has nothing left to negotiate with except the war he is still fighting in Lebanon. He will fight it for as long as he is permitted to.

Inside a damaged hospital at the site of an Israeli strike in Tyre, Lebanon, on Tuesday.Credit...Daniel Berehulak/The New York Times

Christina Goldbaum of the Times captured the underlying logic in Beirut Tuesday morning: a durable Lebanon truce would benefit Trump and Hezbollah, both of whom could claim victory. It would hurt Netanyahu, who would emerge from months of fighting with little to show. The Israeli prime minister is now in the strange position of being the principal obstacle to a peace his American patron has announced.

On Monday afternoon, while Trump was on CNBC declaring himself bored, the United Nations Security Council convened an emergency session at France’s request.

The vote was effectively 14-1. France, Britain, Russia, China — all four other permanent members — called on Israel to withdraw from southern Lebanon and refrain from attacks. The United States, alone, defended Israel and blamed Iran and Hezbollah. France’s ambassador called Israel’s campaign “a major strategic mistake.” Britain’s deputy ambassador called it “reckless and disproportionate escalation.” Lebanon’s ambassador said the Council’s repeated failures had “emboldened the perpetrator to commit the very same crimes time and time again.”

Three months into the war, the United States is diplomatically isolated on its own war. Russia and China are on one side of a Security Council debate about American military conduct. Britain and France are on that same side. America is alone. This is the country that, eleven weeks ago, was supposed to have orchestrated an unconditional surrender. It is now arguing on behalf of an ally that its own closest partners describe as committing a strategic mistake.

While Trump found the negotiations boring, the economic consequences of the war he cannot end were being added up by central bankers and international institutions.

Eurozone inflation hit 3.2 percent in May — the highest reading in two and a half years. The European Central Bank is expected to raise interest rates at its meeting next week. This is the second major central bank now publicly contemplating rate hikes because of the Iran war. Last week, the U.S. Federal Reserve’s preferred inflation gauge hit a three-year high, and two Fed governors publicly stated they were “prepared to raise rates.”

On Wednesday, the OECD added the third pillar New York Times. Global growth, the Paris-based organisation projected, would slow from 3.4 percent in 2025 to 2.8 percent this year. G20 inflation would average 4 percent in 2026 and 3.1 percent in 2027 — higher than the OECD’s own forecast just two months ago. American growth would fall to 2 percent this year and 1.8 percent next. Eurozone growth would be cut nearly in half to 0.8 percent. And the report’s most damning sentence: “The economic consequences are likely to be felt for some time even after its resolution.” The war is not just expensive to fight. It is expensive to end. And it will go on being expensive long after the last memorandum of understanding is signed — if one ever is.

Two of the world’s three major economic blocs are about to tighten monetary policy because of a war that cannot end because the president who started it has decided the negotiations to end it bore him. Mortgages get more expensive. Car loans get more expensive. Credit cards get more expensive. Small businesses borrow less. Hiring slows. And somewhere in the cost of borrowing imposed on every American, European, and the developing world, is the price of a presidential attention span.

This is where Sadjadpour’s analysis turns from incisive to chilling. The Islamic Republic, he argues, has only made major compromises twice in its 47-year history. The first was the 1988 decision to end the Iran-Iraq War — a concession Ayatollah Khomeini compared to “drinking poison.” The second was the 2015 nuclear deal with the Obama administration. In both cases: overwhelming pressure, a viable diplomatic exit, and crucially, no demand that the regime change its revolutionary identity.

Trump’s proposed deal, as currently structured, demands all three changes — abandon nuclear weapons ambition, abandon proxy networks, abandon hostility to America and Israel — in exchange for “hundreds of billions of dollars in Persian Gulf investment that could make Iran ‘one of the richest countries in the world.’” Trade revolution for prosperity. The Islamic Republic has never done that and is not going to start now. Sadjadpour, with devastating economy: “The regime’s defiance resembles the strategic victory of Monty Python’s Black Knight.” Limbs lopped off. Insists it is merely a flesh wound. And no government willing to immiserate its own population can be quickly broken by a blockade, even one costing it $450 million a day.

And the war’s deepest, ugliest legacy may be the lesson Iran has drawn from it. A former Iranian official told Sadjadpour that Tehran “retains the knowledge and now has the will to build nuclear weapons in short order.” Iraq, Libya, and Ukraine gave up their weapons programmes and were subsequently invaded or carved up. North Korea kept its weapons and survived. The Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps reportedly maintains as many as 30 underground “missile cities,” some likely built with North Korean assistance, some buried deeper than the nuclear facilities the United States already destroyed.

Sadjadpour’s image will stay with me: “Like Gaza, Iran is becoming a place where the authorities and their weaponry thrive underground while citizens languish aboveground.”

The war launched to ensure Iran would never have a nuclear weapon has, by every available indication, made Iran more determined to acquire one. The thirteen Americans who died at Arlington died for an objective the war has made farther, not closer.

And so to the question that hangs over the next ninety days, and the next ninety after that.

If Trump signs the deal his team negotiated, he will be signing something his hawks have already called a “nightmare for Israel” and a “disastrous mistake.” If he toughens the terms further to placate them, Iran will reject the toughening and continue to bargain in the bazaar style its foreign minister published a memoir about. If he abandons the deal entirely, he returns to a war that has driven inflation to three-year highs, isolated America at the Security Council, and made his own coalition’s hawks denounce him for not finishing what he started. If he continues to drift between these positions while declaring himself bored, gas prices climb, the bond market resets, and Iran builds the bomb the war was supposed to prevent.

Trump cannot sign the deal he produced. Trump cannot abandon the deal he produced. Trump cannot live with the war he started. The president has discovered, in the late innings of a war he chose, that none of his options are acceptable, and his solution is to announce that he finds the situation boring.

“I really don’t care. I couldn’t care less. If they’re over, they’re over.”

Stephen Leacock, who looms over much of what I write, once observed that an indignant man is rarely a man of action. An indifferent man, presiding over a war that has killed thousands, is something else entirely. Sadjadpour’s verdict, in the closing line of his Atlantic piece, is one I cannot improve on: “America seeks resolution. Iran is committed to revolution.”

And the president of the country seeking resolution? He just told a television interviewer he was bored of seeking it.

The foreign minister of the country committed to revolution? He warned us this would happen, in a book published last year.

He who gets tired and bored quickly will lose.
