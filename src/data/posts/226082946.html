On the afternoon of Sunday, 22 November 2026, two days after the United Nations climate summit in Antalya was supposed to have finished, its president, Turkey’s environment minister Murat Kurum, put the final text to the room. It did not survive the afternoon. The European Union rejected it. So did Britain, the Pacific island states, Egypt and a long line of the countries with the most to lose from a warming planet. Kurum told the delegates there was nothing more to be done and no new text would be offered, and he left the stage while some of them were still speaking. When the UN’s climate chief, Simon Stiell, tried to take the floor, Kurum waved him away.

So ended COP31, the longest climate summit in the history of the process, with nothing to show for it. As Chico Harlan and Lisa Friedman reported from Antalya for The New York Times, it was the first time the annual talks had broken down over a final agreement since Copenhagen in 2009, and only the third failure in three decades.

The sticking point was not money, though money was a problem too. It was a phrase. A group of oil producers led by Saudi Arabia, with Russia alongside, would not accept any mention of fossil fuels, which are the main cause of global warming. The countries that wanted the phrase would not accept a text without it. Nobody could vote, because the process does not vote.

This series has spent seven essays on a Security Council where one permanent member can stop the other fourteen. The previous dispatch, from The Hague, showed a court that can finally name a crime and then has to ask that Council to do something about it. This one comes from a conference hall on the Turkish Riviera, and it shows a problem this series knows well, in a place where most people would not think to look for it. You do not need a permanent seat to wield a veto. You only need a rule that says everyone has to agree.

What the room was fighting over

The fight had been building all fortnight. On Monday, 16 November, the presidency published an 18-page draft “cover decision”, the main political statement of the summit. According to an Agence France-Presse report carried by Al Jazeera on 17 November, it called for a phase-out of fossil fuels and for wealthy nations to reach net zero by 2030. A European official called the positions of a bloc that included Saudi Arabia and India “obviously unacceptable”.

By Thursday, 19 November, the fossil fuel language had gone. The Greek daily To Vima reported that the next draft no longer mentioned coal, oil or gas at all. Chris Bowen, the Australian minister running the negotiations, explained that this was a summit about implementation. Kurum argued that a target to electrify the world economy by 2035 already implied moving away from fossil fuels.

The language had been hard won. As Joshua Partlow wrote for The Atlantic, diplomats spent more than twenty years at these talks before agreeing to mention fossil fuels at all. Glasgow in 2021 settled for a “phase down” of coal. Dubai in 2023 went further, with a pledge to “transition away” from fossil fuels that organisers called the beginning of the end of the fossil fuel era. As the Times noted, no final agreement at the three COPs since has mentioned them directly. The Antalya drafts also dropped references to rich countries’ historical responsibility for the warming.

The text that finally went to the room on Sunday was not empty. According to La Voce di New York, it called on countries to raise electricity’s share of global energy use from about 23 per cent to 35 per cent by 2035. It called for tripling adaptation finance for poorer countries, without saying tripled from what. It acknowledged that warming would overshoot the Paris limit of 1.5 degrees, and it urged faster work on technology to capture carbon and pull it out of the air. Saudi Arabia and the Arab Group had pressed for that last item, arguing that safe temperatures could not be held without it. Critics called it a licence to keep burning oil. Johan Rockström, director of the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research, told The Atlantic that even the electrification target, which would need a more than fourfold speed-up, would not stop warming of 2 degrees or more. He called it a “minor and insufficient consolation”.

What it did not do was say “fossil fuels”. More than 130 countries had wanted the words. Darragh O’Brien, Ireland’s climate minister, speaking for the EU, said the bloc would not accept a COP that greenwashed fossil fuels. It was, he said, “unconscionable” to have a climate pact that made no reference to them.

The veto by another name

The UN climate convention was signed in 1992 and its parties first met in 1995. In the thirty-one years since, they have never managed to adopt the rule in their own procedures that would let them vote. So the conference works by consensus, and consensus in practice means that a decision passes only if nobody objects loudly enough to stop it. A single delegation with enough nerve can sink the text.

Bowen put it plainly after the collapse. As Al Jazeera reported in its round-up of reactions on 22 November, he said some parties had insisted fossil fuels be mentioned while others would veto any mention of them. He used the word himself. To the BBC, he described the positions as “unbridgeable red lines”. Russia’s delegate, Sergei Kononuchenko, said his country could not agree to any formulation on fossil fuels. That is not a negotiating position. It is a veto, cast in the open.

Readers of this series’ essay on the veto will recognise the shape. The Security Council’s veto was put into the Charter at San Francisco on purpose, so that no great power would ever be dragged into a war it did not want. The cost was paralysis whenever one of them had something to protect. The climate process has the same cost without even that excuse. Nobody designed a veto into it. It arrived by default, because the parties could never agree to take it out.

This series has been here before, too. Its first essays described how the League of Nations required unanimity for almost every important decision, and how that rule helped turn the League into a debating society that could not stop Japan in Manchuria or Italy in Abyssinia. The men who drafted the UN Charter learned from that. They gave the General Assembly majority voting and confined the unanimity problem to five permanent seats. The climate conference has spent three decades quietly rebuilding the League’s rule.

Rockström called unanimity the process’s “real Achilles’ heel” in an online post quoted by the Times, and accused a handful of countries of deliberately putting spanners in the wheel. Former Vice President Al Gore, who was in Antalya for the opening days, had already called it “insane” that any single country could block the whole process, and suggested supermajority voting. After the collapse he went further, in a statement on X: “It is outrageous that a handful of petrostates have veto power over any mention of fossil fuels.”

Even the man who runs the process conceded the point, carefully. Simon Stiell told reporters he would seek advice on “areas of potential reform” to the consensus system. Asked by the BBC whether consensus still worked, he gave the answer every defender of the Security Council veto has given since 1945: it is the process we have, and it got us to Paris. Then he admitted it had fallen short, and called the question of reform “a live conversation”. In the language of UN officials, that is close to a confession.

It is worth being careful here, because the rule cuts both ways. On 19 November, To Vima reported that Panama had sent the presidency a letter, signed by its environment minister Juan Carlos Navarro, threatening to block any outcome that did not put science at the centre and cap oil production. Panama is a small country, and in a majority vote its threat would mean little. Under consensus it carried real weight. The same rule that let Saudi Arabia strip out fossil fuels gave Panama the power to hold out for more. In Antalya, in the end, it was the EU and the vulnerable states who said no. Belgium’s climate minister, Jean-Luc Crucke, said it was not the end of multilateralism, “but there has to be courage to say no.” A consensus rule protects whoever is willing to walk away. That is its defence, and it is also its flaw. This series will come back to that argument in its essay on the veto as a shield.

Who was in the room, and who was not

The United States did not send a delegation, for the second year running, according to Nature World News. The Times reported participants’ view that other countries had taken harder positions in the vacuum Washington left. The country that helped broker the Paris Agreement was instead using trade policy to sell fossil fuels abroad.

And while the negotiators sat in Antalya, Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin met at a lakeside villa in Shenzhen and agreed to “increase the production and supply” of petroleum. Together, the United States, China and Russia make up three of the world’s four largest emitters. None of their leaders came to the climate summit. According to The Atlantic, the only G7 leader who did was Britain’s prime minister, Andy Burnham. When a leader appeared, the cameras crowded in; when the leaders left, so did the press, and television crews worked among empty seats and rows of vacant press tables. “It’s just not cutting through,” one climate editor said.

The running of the summit did not help. Under a compromise reached in Brazil a year earlier, Turkey hosted the conference while Australia chaired the negotiations. As the talks ran past their deadline, delegates found it hard to tell who was in charge. Groups drifted in and out of back rooms, and the presidency never called all the nations together to settle the matter. In the closing plenary, Kurum had to apologise publicly to Ghana’s Zanetor Agyeman-Rawlings, speaking for the African Group, after she accused him of cutting her off. Xolisa Joseph Ngwadla, a negotiator from Botswana, told the Times that the process was “basically mismanaged”. According to The National, Kurum had said that same Sunday morning that only “minor requests” stood in the way. Hours later he was complaining that some countries had got hung up on a single word or phrase. That word was the cause of the problem the whole conference was meant to solve.

The world outside the hall

None of this happened in a quiet year. Two months before the summit, the United Nations acknowledged that the world would fail to hold warming to 1.5 degrees. A summer heat wave in Europe had killed some 50,000 people. In August, a glacier collapse in Nepal unleashed floods that killed at least 1,455 people and left more than 5,000 missing. Nepal says it needs about $5 billion to rebuild, and the UN’s loss and damage fund has set aside $20 million. A super El Niño was driving record ocean temperatures, and, as Mike Pearl reported for Gizmodo, the World Health Organization declared a state of emergency on the summit’s first day, citing heat, flooding, wildfire smoke and malnutrition. The World Meteorological Organization reported that global temperatures from January to September had averaged 1.47 degrees above preindustrial levels. “There is no end in sight,” its secretary-general, Celeste Saulo, said.

The countries closest to the water were the bluntest. The Marshall Islands said it had been let down on all sides and would not trade its future for the benefit of fossil fuel interests. Lynda Tabuya, Fiji’s climate minister, told the Times that fossil fuel producers were banding together no matter which part of the world they came from. António Guterres, the Secretary-General, said he was “deeply disappointed”.

Not everyone blamed the oil producers alone. Antwi-Boasiako Amoah, who chairs the African Group of Negotiators, told the BBC the fossil fuel fight had been “over-hyped” and had buried Africa’s demand for adaptation finance. The countries arguing over the phrase, he said, were all producers, exporters, importers or users of fossil fuels. “It was hypocrisy.” The collapse took the text’s finance promises down with it, along with whatever new help for adaptation and disaster recovery the poorest countries might have got this year.

What a collapse leaves behind

“No deal” sounds worse than the legal reality. The Paris Agreement still stands. So does every decision taken at earlier summits, including the Dubai language about transitioning away from fossil fuels. Several narrower decisions were adopted in Antalya before the end, among them one on a “just transition” for workers in high-carbon industries. The next summit, COP32, will be held in Addis Ababa in November 2027, with Ethiopia’s foreign minister, Gedion Timothewos, as its president. What failed was the political statement meant to show the world that governments were responding to the breach of 1.5 degrees. Instead the world has its answer: they could not agree to name the cause.

The countries that want to move faster are no longer waiting. The Times reported that nations favouring wind, solar and other clean energy were working more and more outside the formal UN system, through their own conferences and domestic pledges, and that Denmark, Spain and Portugal had announced new fossil fuel phase-out plans in November. Readers of this series’ essay on Korea will know this pattern too. When the Security Council froze in 1950, the Assembly built Uniting for Peace to get around it. A coalition of the willing can act without everyone’s consent. What it cannot do is speak for everyone, and in climate, everyone’s emissions count.

Copenhagen in 2009 was the last collapse, and the world went on to Paris six years later. The Hague in 2000, the one before that, was suspended and patched up in Bonn the following summer. There is a version of this story in which Antalya becomes another stumble on the road to something better. There is another in which it marks the point where a forum that could only move as fast as its most reluctant member finally stopped moving.

The question at the heart of this series is whether the Parliament of Man, as Tennyson imagined it, can work when its members disagree about what is at stake. In Antalya, nearly 200 countries could not agree to write down the words “fossil fuels”. That is not because most of them did not want to. It is because the rules let a few of them say no.
