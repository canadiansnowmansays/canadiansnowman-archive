This is the floor.

⚖️ Serious — one of the gravest. It is about the first time a countdown was used in earnest, and about the invoice posted in 1905 and paid in 1945, and about two ledgers of the dead in my own country’s north that still do not agree. There is no wink. The dog stays out of it.

I count my essays, and long ago, at essay 1313, I wrote a sentence about dread — about the way a catastrophe can be booked long before it arrives, so that by the time you see it coming there is nothing left to do but arrive at the floor. I promised then that the series would quote that sentence back to itself when it reached its gravest numbers. It has reached one. Essay 1,945 is the year the war ended, and the year the exchange rate a patent clerk posted forty years earlier was cashed over two cities in Japan.

A count from the cinema

Begin with a small thing, because the small things in this series always turn out to carry the large ones.

The countdown — ten, nine, eight, down to zero — is not an ancient ritual. It is a piece of theatre, and we know roughly where it was staged. In 1929, the German director Fritz Lang made a silent film called Frau im Mond, Woman in the Moon, about a rocket journey to the Moon, and for the launch sequence he had the seconds counted backward on title cards, so that the audience would feel the tension rising toward a moment of release. Historians of the device have found earlier fictional precursors, as they always do, but Lang’s film is the one that put the countdown into the world’s imagination. It was invented, in other words, as a way of sending people to the Moon.

Its first use in earnest was something else.

At 5:29 in the morning on July 16, 1945, on a stretch of New Mexico desert the scientists had named Trinity, the physicist Samuel Allison read the final seconds of the countdown over the loudspeakers, and at zero the first atomic bomb in history exploded. The light was seen hundreds of kilometres away. The men who watched it reached, many of them, for scripture or for silence. A count that a film-maker had imagined to carry human beings toward the Moon was used for the first time, for real, to detonate a weapon.

Back at essay 1,914, I promised you that beyond the hardest numbers lay the best morning of the century — a July night in 1969 when the counting ran backward, out loud, toward the only deadline in these files that humanity ever set for its best self and kept. That essay is already written and waiting further down the count. I did not know, when I wrote it, how much it depended on this one. The countdown was born for the Moon in 1929, used to make a bomb in 1945, and given back to the Moon in 1969. Twenty-four years separate the two countdowns. In those twenty-four years the same ten numbers were made to mean the worst thing and then the best thing human beings had ever done. Read the two essays together. They are one essay, about one count, and which way it is pointed.

The invoice

At essay 1,905 I wrote about the exchange rate: that mass and energy are a single column in two currencies, and that a single gram of matter, at Einstein’s posted rate, holds the energy of roughly twenty thousand tonnes of explosive. I said the rate would wait for someone to make the exchange. On August 6, 1945, someone did.

The bomb dropped on Hiroshima that morning converted less than a gram of its matter into energy. Less than a paperclip. That was the whole of the transaction. Three days later, on August 9, a second bomb was dropped on Nagasaki. Japan announced its surrender on August 15, and signed it on September 2, and the Second World War — which had killed, by estimates that run from about seventy to eighty-five million people, the largest death toll in human history — was over.

Now the count of the two cities, and you will know by now what kind of count it is. At Hiroshima, something like seventy to eighty thousand people died on the day of the bombing, and by the end of 1945 the toll had reached perhaps ninety thousand to a hundred and forty thousand. At Nagasaki, perhaps forty thousand died at once, and sixty to eighty thousand by the year’s end. They are ranges. They are ranges because the cities’ own records burned with the cities, because thousands of the dead were Korean forced labourers and Allied prisoners whom no one was counting, and because the bomb went on killing after the war was over — through burns, through injuries, and through radiation sickness that appeared weeks, months and years later. The survivors, the hibakusha, were counted for the rest of their lives, and some of them are still being counted now. The armistice of 1918 stopped the clocks at eleven and did not stop the one in my Great-Uncle Art’s chest. The surrender of 1945 did not stop the one in theirs.

I will not argue here whether the bombs were necessary. Serious historians have argued it for eighty years, with the numbers on both sides — the lives a land invasion of Japan would have cost, the lives the bombs did cost — and the argument is itself a kind of ledger that has never balanced. I will only enter, plainly, the thing this series exists to enter: that the whole transaction was less than a gram in each city, at a rate posted in three pages by a clerk who spent the rest of his life wishing it had not been cashed.

The ore from Great Bear Lake

And now my own country’s page in this ledger, which most Canadians have never been shown.

Some of the uranium in the Manhattan Project came from Canada — from a mine called Port Radium, on the shore of Great Bear Lake in the Northwest Territories, just below the Arctic Circle. The ore was dug, bagged in burlap sacks, and moved by barge and portage and rail on its long journey south. And much of the carrying, on the river and lake routes, was done by Dene men from the community of Déline, who loaded the sacks on their backs for a few dollars a day. Nobody told them what was in the sacks. Nobody told them about the dust that settled on their clothes and went home to their families, or about the camps where the sacks were stacked, or about anything at all.

In the years that followed, Déline came to be called the Village of Widows, because so many of the men who had carried the ore died of cancer. The community counted its dead and drew its conclusion. And then, in 2005, a study commissioned by the federal government concluded that the men’s radiation exposure had been insufficient to cause their cancers. So there are two ledgers of the same men, and they do not agree. The community’s count says the ore killed them. The government’s count says it did not. A negotiator for Déline put the honest position as well as it can be put: they could not deny that the men might have died of the radiation, and they could not prove it either way.

You know this kind of entry by now. It is 1,857’s two ledgers of the dead, it is 1,918’s range, it is the oldest dispute in the series: whose count of the dead is believed. What I will add is the strangest detail I found, and the one I cannot stop thinking about. In 1998 a delegation from Déline travelled to Hiroshima. They went to express their sorrow to the people of the city — for their part, unknowing, in carrying the ore that became the bomb. A community that had been told nothing went across the world to apologise for what it had not been told.

There is one more thread, because the lattice of this series will not let me leave it out. Two years after the war, my relation Hugh Keenleyside became Commissioner of the Northwest Territories — the territory from which that ore had come. I do not know what he knew of Port Radium; the record I have does not say. I note only that the family’s paperwork, and the country’s, keep turning up on the same pages.

The other count of that summer

There is a second document from 1945 that belongs beside the bomb, and it is the reason this essay does not end at the floor.

On June 26, 1945 — three weeks before Trinity, before the public knew such a weapon could exist — the representatives of fifty nations gathered in San Francisco and signed the Charter of the United Nations. Its opening words are a count of a kind, too: We the peoples of the United Nations, determined to save succeeding generations from the scourge of war, which twice in our lifetime has brought untold sorrow to mankind. Twice in our lifetime. The men and women who wrote that had counted the wars, and the number they reached was two, and they were trying to keep it from becoming three.

So the same summer produced the weapon that could end the counting altogether, and the institution designed to make it unnecessary. They arrived within weeks of each other. I have written a whole series about that institution, its successes and its failures, partly because Hugh spent eight years of his life inside it. I will say only this, here: the ledger of 1945 has two columns, and both of them are still open. Eighty-one years on, the bomb has not been used in war again. That is a count, too — the longest run of zeros in the history of the weapon — and it is the most important zero this series has ever recorded. It has to be kept every single day by someone, and it can only be lost once.

The closer

So 1945 is the floor. A count invented for the Moon was used to make the light at Trinity. A rate posted in 1905 was paid in less than a gram, twice. Two cities were left as ranges, a village in the Canadian north was left with two ledgers that will not reconcile, and fifty nations signed a document trying to keep the number of world wars at two. The war ended. The counting did not.

The count moves on, and it moves to the year the British Empire began to leave the place it had governed longest — and to the line a lawyer drew across a subcontinent, in five weeks, from a census, without ever having set foot there. The next landmark is essay 1,947, and it is the year a border was drawn as a body count. I will write it as this series writes the grave ones.
