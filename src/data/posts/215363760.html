At 2 p.m. on Wednesday, October 28, 2026, six days before Americans vote, the Federal Reserve chose to wait. Six weeks after raising rates for the first time since 2023, its policy committee held them steady at 3.75 to 4 percent, by a vote of 10 to 2. The next morning, the government reported that the economy had grown at a 3.4 percent annual rate over the summer, and that the Fed's preferred measure of inflation had risen 3.6 percent over the year to September. A chairman who had promised to finish the job decided, for one meeting at least, that the job could wait. To see why that matters to a household, start where this story started, six weeks earlier.

At 8:30 on the morning of September 11, 2026, the Bureau of Labor Statistics released the number the entire apparatus of Washington had spent the preceding week bracing for. It did not move. Consumer prices rose 3.4 percent over the past year — the same figure as the month before, a 0.4 percent monthly climb that came in exactly where forecasters expected. Core inflation, the measure that strips out food and energy to track what’s structurally sticky, rose 0.3 percent for the month and 2.4 percent for the year — slightly hotter than forecast, and just hot enough to matter.

As Colby Smith reported for the New York Times that morning, the number carried outsized weight because the Federal Reserve’s rate-setting committee was due to meet September 15 and 16, and a report this stubborn made a quarter-point increase look close to inevitable. Futures markets, Joe Rennison noted the same day, were already pricing in roughly 70 percent odds of a hike before the number landed. By the eve of the meeting, that figure had climbed to 90 percent. On the afternoon of September 16, it became certain: the Fed raised its benchmark rate a quarter point, to a range of 3.75 to 4 percent, the first increase since July 2023. All twelve voting members supported it — a striking reversal from July, when three regional bank presidents voted against Warsh for holding rates steady.

This series has spent eleven parts documenting the distance between what Washington says about the cost of living and what a household actually experiences at the register. This instalment follows the two Fed meetings that bracket the end of the campaign: the September hike, which is now a matter of record, and the October pause, whose cost will not be known until well after the ballots are counted.

The same morning also produced a second data point worth pairing against the first. As Tony Romm reported for the Times on September 12, the University of Michigan’s consumer sentiment survey dropped sharply that month. Prices have now risen faster than hourly wages, year over year, for five straight months — not a one-month blip, but a household watching its paycheque lose a race it has already lost five times running. The September report made it six, and wage growth itself slowed to 3 percent, down from nearly 4 percent a year earlier. Oren Klachkin, a financial market economist at Nationwide, named the disconnect: healthy macro numbers sitting beside a population that feels none of it, because none of it changes what a receipt says.

A Chairman With No Good Options

Kevin Warsh inherited the Federal Reserve chairmanship with a mandate this series has tracked from the start: bring inflation back to the 2 percent target without becoming the man who sank a Republican president’s midterm by raising the cost of borrowing weeks before the vote. Those two goals collided on September 16, and Warsh chose the target over the president.

His own words at the Fed’s Jackson Hole conference in August set the trap he then walked into: he needed to be confident inflation was moving toward the Fed’s objective “clearly and at sufficient speed,” or the Fed would have “work to do.” The September data never showed that movement. By his own stated standard, Warsh had work to do — and on September 16 he did it, over the explicit and repeated objection of the man who appointed him.

That objection was never subtle. Trump had threatened to halt a broad swath of U.S. trade unless the Fed cut rates. Cutting was never under serious consideration inside the building. Washington got the opposite of the president’s demand, delivered unanimously, with National Economic Council director Kevin Hassett telling reporters beforehand that the administration would “respect the process” and saying little more afterward. Whether Trump follows through on his trade threat remains an open thread, one this series will track separately from the household-cost story it is telling here.

What September 16 Actually Did

The Fed didn’t just raise rates; it told the country, via its quarterly projections, that this was the first of more to come. Eighteen of the nineteen policymakers who submitted forecasts expected at least one more quarter-point increase before year’s end. Inflation is projected to end the year at 3.7 percent by the Fed’s preferred measure, and the 2 percent target isn’t projected to arrive until 2029. The September inflation report then raised the stakes for October: within hours of its release on October 14, investors had raised the odds of an October increase from roughly 20 percent to 44 percent.

In the end, the Fed held. As Colby Smith reported for The New York Times on October 29, ten officials voted to keep rates where they were. Two wanted a quarter-point increase: Beth M. Hammack, president of the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland, and Lisa D. Cook, the governor President Trump has tried to fire. The official the president has tried to remove was one of the two who voted to make borrowing more expensive before the election. Warsh insisted the midterms played no part. He pointed instead to longer-term inflation expectations, which still show faith that the Fed will eventually reach its target; to an economy he described as strong but not overheating; and to tighter credit conditions, including costlier financing for A.I. companies, which he read as a sign that policy was working. He also made clear that the pause was not a stop. He declined to describe financial conditions as tight, and he spoke of needing a new kind of monetary medicine and of working out the right dose. Investors expect the next increase in December.

Warsh himself declined, for the second straight quarter, to submit his own rate forecast, calling the dot plot a form of forward guidance that ties the Fed’s hands. The irony is hard to miss: a chairman appointed in part to restore public trust has made his signature reform the withholding of the one document that would let households and markets check his math.

The Morning After

The data released the next morning did little to support the case for patience. As Ben Casselman reported for The New York Times, gross domestic product grew at a 3.4 percent annual rate in the third quarter, up from 2.2 percent in the spring and the fastest pace in a year. Consumer spending grew at 3.8 percent. Business investment grew at 12.7 percent and accounted for more than half of all growth, much of it poured into A.I. data centres. Residential investment, the category that includes building homes, shrank for the sixth time in seven quarters.

That is the economy this series has been describing, seen from above. Spending is strong, but households are paying for it out of their savings. They saved just 4.1 percent of their after-tax income in the third quarter, the lowest rate in nearly four years, and after-tax income adjusted for inflation fell in September. Diane Swonk, chief economist at KPMG, described an economy that feels less like a rising tide than “some people in yachts and some people with no boats at all.” The yachts are stock portfolios lifted by A.I. enthusiasm. The people with no boats are living on wages that have fallen behind prices over the past year.

The inflation numbers arrived the same morning. As Aruni Soni reported for The New York Times, the Personal Consumption Expenditures index, the Fed’s preferred gauge, rose 0.5 percent in September alone and 3.6 percent over the year. Core prices, which exclude food and energy, rose 3.1 percent. Personal income grew just 0.2 percent for the month, down from 0.3 percent in August. Prices rising faster and income rising slower: that is the household ledger in two lines, delivered the morning after the Fed decided it could afford to wait.

The Windfall

The same week’s data had a second column, and it ran the other way. Since the United States and Israel began striking Iran on February 28, Americans have spent an extra $147 billion on gasoline and diesel, according to Brown University’s Climate Solutions Lab. That is more than $1,100 per household. Much of that money has a forwarding address. An analysis by The New York Times of more than 50 large American oil producers and refiners found they earned over $130 billion in profit between April and September, against less than $47 billion in the same months of 2025. Exxon’s third-quarter profit more than doubled, Chevron’s nearly tripled, and Valero’s rose more than sixfold. None of them plans to drill or refine their way to lower prices. Valero told analysts its surplus cash would ultimately go to its owners, which usually means dividends and buybacks. John Catsimatidis, chief executive of United Refining and a longtime Trump ally, said he might spend some of his on a new corporate plane, since at 78 he could hardly wait.

That is the household ledger in two columns: a family paying roughly $1,100 more to fill the tank, and a balance sheet growing stronger for the same reason. The administration’s answer has been to deny the first column exists. At a rally in Lubbock on Tuesday, the President said gas prices had fallen. According to AAA, they were still more than a dollar a gallon above their level on Inauguration Day, as The New York Times reported. Days after calling the affordability crisis a myth, Kevin Hassett told Fox Business that frustration over particular prices was a legitimate feeling, but that an economist must look at the aggregate. Obfuscate, in two moves. Families don’t buy the aggregate. They buy gasoline.

The Last Time the Fed Waited

Claudia Sahm, a former Fed economist now at New Century Advisors, put the risk plainly to Colby Smith: “Waiting is not costless.” The Fed has learned that twice in living memory, and both times the bill landed on households.

The first lesson came under Arthur Burns, Fed chairman from 1970 to 1978. Burns understood inflation as well as anyone, but under steady pressure from Richard Nixon, who wanted easy money ahead of the 1972 election, he kept rates lower than the numbers justified. Nixon was re-elected in a landslide. Inflation, made worse by the 1973 oil embargo, reached about 12 percent by the end of 1974. The Fed then tightened, eased again as soon as unemployment rose, and repeated the cycle through the rest of the decade. Each round taught businesses and workers to expect more inflation, and those expectations became part of the problem. By March 1980, consumer prices were rising 14.8 percent a year.

Paul Volcker, who took over in 1979, ended it the only way left. He pushed the federal funds rate toward 20 percent. The cost was two recessions in three years and unemployment of 10.8 percent by late 1982. For households, the damage showed up where it is showing up now: the average 30-year mortgage rate peaked above 18 percent in October 1981. A family buying a home that year paid for a decade of waiting.

The second lesson is recent enough that most voters lived through it. In 2021, as prices began to climb after the pandemic, Jerome Powell’s Fed called the rise “transitory” and kept rates near zero while still buying bonds. Powell retired the word in November 2021, but the first increase did not come until March 2022, when inflation was already above 8 percent. It peaked at 9.1 percent that June. To catch up, the Fed raised rates by three-quarters of a point at four straight meetings, the fastest tightening since the Volcker years. Mortgage rates more than doubled in less than a year, from about 3 percent to above 7 percent. A generation of would-be buyers was locked out, and that lockout had only begun to ease in February 2026 when the war with Iran pushed rates back up.

Warsh knows this history better than most. He was among the sharpest critics of the Fed’s 2021 delay, and that criticism was part of the case for making him chairman.

None of this means a six-week pause is a repeat of 1972 or 2021. September’s hike was unanimous, and the Fed has signalled more to come. Richard Clarida, a former Fed vice chair now at Pimco, considers one or two more increases after December plausible, depending on whether inflation slows. Matthew Luzzetti of Deutsche Bank expects increases in December and March, and possibly another in the spring if inflation stays sticky. But the warning signs are the ones history taught the Fed to watch. Sahm notes that companies push through more price increases in January than in any other month, which could make early 2027 the next pressure point. And markets showed a hint of doubt on the day of the decision: short-term Treasury yields fell while long-term yields rose. That pattern suggests investors expect the Fed to move more slowly than it says, and worry that inflation will last longer as a result. For households, it matters directly, because mortgage rates follow long-term yields. If the Fed’s patience is read as softness, the 7.4 percent mortgage described below could get worse, not better.

The Part of the Story That Hasn’t Hit the Data Yet

Ben Casselman, live-blogging the September 11 release, made a point worth centring: that CPI report described August, a month when oil traded around $80 a barrel and gas averaged roughly $4 a gallon. By the eve of the Fed meeting, oil was back near $100. By September 16, Gregory Schmidt reported, regular gasoline had climbed to $4.37 a gallon — a 47 percent rise since the war with Iran began — with diesel at $6.31, up 68 percent. None of that shows up in CPI until the following month’s print, which meant the October number was likely to look considerably worse than the one that triggered the hike. It did. The September report, released October 14 and examined in Part XI, showed prices up 3.7 percent on the year, with gasoline up 34 percent.

Trump told supporters at the Dallas convention that gas prices would fall “right after the election.” They had not obliged by mid-October: gasoline neared $4.50 a gallon in late September, and on October 14 AAA still put the national average at $4.33, more than a dollar above a year earlier. As Ben Casselman noted for the Times on October 18, the national average has now sat above $4 a gallon for most of the past six months, and record diesel prices are threatening to work their way into the price of food. Kate Gordon, a former senior Department of Energy adviser, put the stakes plainly in earlier reporting cited by this series: energy shocks hit hardest among the roughly half of Americans living paycheque to paycheque — a war of choice arriving as a heating bill weeks before they vote.

Winter has its own line item. As Aruni Soni reported for the Times on September 18, heating oil — the fuel about 3 percent of American households rely on, concentrated in the Northeast and Alaska — is up roughly 60 percent since the war began on February 28. In New York, the average hit $6.14 a gallon the week of September 14, against $3.70 a year earlier: two-thirds more for the same gallon of the same fuel. Jonathan Robidoux, a Long Island renter who spent $1,200 on heating oil last winter, is one of roughly 4.79 million households facing the choice of buying now or gambling on a price drop before the tank runs dry. Futures for December delivery, at $4.61 a gallon on September 17, suggest the market expects some relief by midwinter. This series has watched that bet fail once already: fuel prices fell after the June ceasefire announcement, then climbed straight back when the fighting resumed in July.

Natural gas, which heats roughly 47 percent of American homes, is actually cheaper wholesale than a year ago, and electric heat is up a comparatively modest 5 percent. The pain is concentrated, not universal — which is exactly why it will be invisible in a national CPI print and entirely visible in the mailboxes of Northeastern households in the days after they vote.

What the Rate Hike Actually Costs a Household

It is worth being precise about the mechanism, because the Fed’s rate and the rates a household pays are not the same number. As Tara Siegel Bernard reported for the Times on the day of the decision, most consumer products tied directly to the Fed’s move — credit cards, personal loans, small-business loans — are pegged to the prime rate, which tracks the federal funds rate closely, and cardholders can expect the increase within a couple of billing cycles. Michele Raneri of TransUnion noted that the near-term hit to a minimum payment is small but compounds for anyone carrying a revolving balance.

Mortgages work differently. Thirty-year mortgages track the 10-year Treasury yield, not the Fed’s overnight rate — which is why mortgage costs had been climbing for weeks before the vote, driven by the bond market’s own verdict on the deficit, the war, and the AI borrowing boom. In the week of the decision, Freddie Mac put the average 30-year fixed rate at 6.76 percent, up from 6.35 percent a year earlier, still just short of the “at or above 7 percent” trajectory the Mortgage Bankers Association’s Michael Fratantoni had been forecasting. The 10-year yield touched 5.04 percent the day before the decision, then eased to roughly 4.95 percent once it landed. At the time, analysts read that dip as investors rewarding the Fed for following through.

The reward did not last. Within two weeks the sell-off had resumed, and this time it was global. On October 1, as Eshe Nelson reported for The New York Times, the 10-year yield reached as high as 5.34 percent, its highest level since 2002. French 10-year yields approached 5 percent, and Brent crude climbed back above $100 a barrel, even though more oil is now leaving the Middle East than at any point since the war began. It did not stop there. By October 23, Brent had reached $116.72 a barrel — up 60 percent since the war with Iran began and its highest level since April — while the national gas average held at $4.29 a gallon according to AAA, still well above the $4 threshold it has sat above for most of the past six months. The causes analysts listed read like a table of contents for this series: the war, inflation that refuses to come down, heavy public debt, an AI investment boom that props up growth while adding to the supply of bonds, and the expectation that central banks are not finished. The Fed, the European Central Bank and the Bank of Japan all raised rates in September, and traders expect each to do so again before year’s end.

The household bill for that sell-off arrived a week later. As Gregory Schmidt reported for The New York Times on October 8, 2026, Freddie Mac’s average 30-year fixed rate reached 7.4 percent that day, up from 7.28 percent the week before and the highest since November 2023. The 10-year yield had climbed again, to 5.36 percent. Fratantoni’s forecast, slightly ahead of the data in mid-September, had arrived.

The more telling number is the one from before the war. In February 2026, just before the strikes on Iran, mortgage rates had dipped below 6 percent, and economists were talking about housing finally becoming affordable again. Kara Ng, a senior economist at Zillow, told Schmidt that from 7 percent the road back to affordability is a long one. Nancy Vanden Houten of Oxford Economics estimates that two and a half to three million people have been priced out of the market altogether. Even households that clear the rate face a second line item: the average monthly property insurance payment for single-family mortgage holders hit a record $209 in June, nearly 10 percent of monthly housing costs, against 7.5 percent a decade ago. Between February and October 2026, a war, a deficit and a bond market that no longer trusts either added nearly a point and a half to the American home-buyer’s mortgage rate — before the Fed’s next decision has even been made.

That episode also settles one of this series’ open questions: whether Scott Bessent’s bond-buyback programme ever gained real traction. It has not. The Treasury’s $5.2 billion self-purchase in early September did little to move yields. For about two weeks, it looked as though Warsh had done what Bessent’s interventions could not. October suggests the bond market’s worries were never only about the Fed’s resolve. They were about the war, the deficit, and a government that keeps finding new things to borrow for, and no single rate hike answers any of those. The president opposed the hike, the hike helped for a fortnight, and the forces pushing borrowing costs back up are largely ones his own policies set in motion.

There is a darker possibility underneath. Much of what is propping up growth is the A.I. building boom, and on October 16 Torsten Slok, chief economist at Apollo Global Management, warned that if investors lose faith in it, the economy could be left in “a stagflationary environment in which growth slows even as price pressures persist.” That is the one outcome a rate hike cannot fix, and the one a central bank fears most.

The Five Thousand Dollar Problem

At the party’s midterm convention in Dallas, Trump vowed to distribute a $5,000 cheque to every American adult if Republicans hold Congress on November 3 — a pledge Tony Romm’s reporting frames as a bump to economic activity that could further fuel inflation. Brett Loper of the Peter G. Peterson Foundation put the cost at between $1 trillion and $1.2 trillion depending on which of two Times pieces you read; the direction matters more than the figure. Stacked on an already-$40-trillion debt, that money would likely, in Loper’s words, “lead to greater inflation and higher interest rates” — the same higher interest rates now showing up in every mortgage quote in the country.

This is worth sitting with under the TACO framework this series has used since Part I. Threaten: a trade war reignited against Canada, tariffs prepared against dozens more countries. Accommodate: a headline pledge of direct cash to voters, timed to the week before a rate decision those same voters will feel in their mortgages. Capitulate: Trump’s own quiet admission, the same week, that oil prices “probably would not fall until right after the election.” Obfuscate: a promise from the same podium that gasoline will fall “below $2 a gallon,” a figure no credible forecast supports, made while his own administration prosecutes a war against one of the world’s major oil producers.

A government cannot fight a war that spikes the price of oil and credibly promise gas at a price not seen in over a decade. Those two positions were never in tension. They were in outright contradiction, and September and October 2026 supplied the receipts.

Two Numbers, Same Household

A higher federal funds rate does not stay in the Fed’s boardroom, and that mechanism connects this essay back to Part VI, “The Cost of Borrowing to Get By.” It raises the cost of credit card balances already at record levels, and of auto loans in a used car market still running well above its prepandemic trend. The bond market, meanwhile, has added its own surcharge to every mortgage written since. The September 16 decision has been framed, correctly, as a monetary policy question. It is also a second, simultaneous tax on exactly the households this series has spent eleven parts describing — layered on top of the tariffs, the war-driven fuel prices, the winter heating bill, a 7.4 percent mortgage, and a $5,000 pledge that even the president has admitted cannot produce relief before voters decide.

The voters who will settle that question have already been heard from. “I won on groceries,” Trump told NBC News in 2024. As Tim Balk, Ann Hinga Klein, Kevin Williams and Sam Easter reported for The New York Times on October 14, 2026, his own voters in four Senate battleground states are now divided over the grocery bill. Carolyn Calcutt, a retired teacher in Michigan, expects prices to improve “as things improve in Iran.” Angel Hartmier, a single mother of three in Ohio who cast her first-ever ballot for Trump in 2024, is less patient: “I did it for the first time and it all went sideways.” She is unsure she will vote again. Rebecca Guerrette, a retired nurse in Maine, says she probably will not. In a midterm, the votes a party should fear losing most are the ones that simply stay home.

None of this resolves cleanly before Americans vote on November 3. It doesn't need to, to matter. September showed that the story this series has told since Part I — a government promising relief on a timeline the underlying economics will not support — had been independently corroborated by the institution in Washington with the least political incentive to lie about it, when the Fed raised rates over the president's objection. October added a harder question. The Fed has paused, says the election had nothing to do with it, and signals that it will resume in December. It may be right. But Arthur Burns also believed he could afford one more season of patience, and Jerome Powell believed the price spike would fade on its own. Each time, the bill for waiting came due later, and households paid it: in mortgage rates, in lost jobs, and in prices that never came back down. Whatever voters decide on November 3, the Fed meets again on December 9, and the numbers it will face are already in.
