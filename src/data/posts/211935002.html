by Stephen Gordon

In the spring of 1588, the largest invasion fleet Western Europe had ever assembled gathered at Lisbon under the title la Grande y Felicísima Armada — the Great and Most Fortunate Fleet. This is esFsay 1,588, and it is about the most expensive adjective in naval history. I want you to look at that name the way this series has taught you to look at everything: as an entry in a ledger. “Great” was arithmetic, and defensible — around 130 ships, some 30,000 men, the accumulated treasure of an empire on which the sun did not set. “Most Fortunate” was something else. “Most Fortunate” was a result, posted to the books before the venture sailed. The fleet’s own name contained its outcome, declared in advance, in the superlative. The sixteenth century had invented many things by 1588. With that name, it invented counting your chickens at imperial scale.

Legend and English propaganda would later prefer the title Invincible Armada, which is even better for my purposes and slightly worse for history’s, so let the record show: the Spanish said Most Fortunate. The enemy said Invincible, mostly afterward, mostly with a smirk. Either way, the adjective sailed first, and the ships followed it.

The plan behind the adjective was King Philip II’s, and it had the particular quality this series has learned to fear most in plans: it was made far from the water. Philip governed his global empire from a desk — famously, from a small office in the Escorial, annotating documents in the margins late into the night, a bureaucrat-king ruling by memorandum. The Armada was a memorandum. The fleet would sail up the Channel, rendezvous off Flanders with the Duke of Parma’s veteran invasion army, escort it across to England, depose the heretic queen, and restore the faith. On paper the arithmetic was crushing. The plan’s difficulties — that the rendezvous had no deep-water port, that Parma’s barges could not survive contact with Dutch flyboats, that two forces moving on different timetables would have to meet on open water in fighting season — were raised by the men who would have to execute it, and were answered from the desk, as such difficulties usually are, with the observation that God would provide. God’s role in the venture had been assigned in the planning documents. He had, so far as the archives reveal, not initialled them.

The man ordered to command it, the Duke of Medina Sidonia, is one of history’s great reluctant executives, and I write as a former executive who sat through my share of launches that the front office had already named Most Fortunate. The Duke, appointed after the death of Spain’s great admiral Santa Cruz, wrote back to the king begging off, pleading — by the accounts that survive, and we handle private correspondence with the usual tongs — inexperience at sea, poor health, seasickness, and debts. It is the most honest self-assessment in this entire series and it availed him nothing. The empire had counted its ships; it had not budgeted for a commander who counted his own limitations accurately. He was sent anyway. The one man attached to the Most Fortunate Fleet who declined to predict his own performance was overruled by the men who had predicted everything.

What followed, in outline, is famous, and the outline is not where the counting lesson lives, so I will be brief. The Armada entered the Channel in late July in its great crescent formation and was harried up the coast by an English fleet that was faster, handier, and shooting at longer range. The crescent held — as a defensive formation it was genuinely formidable, and the English captains, Drake among them, found it maddeningly hard to crack. What cracked it was not gunnery but the oldest weapon in the naval ledger: at midnight on July 28, off Calais, the English sent in fireships — eight vessels, expendable, ablaze — and the Armada, anchored and packed close, did the arithmetic of wooden ships and fire in about four minutes and cut its cables. The formation that a week of battle could not break was broken by the threat of eight burning hulks, most of which touched nothing. I file that beside every panic this series has covered: the damage was done not by the fire but by the sudden, simultaneous recalculation.

Scattered, the fleet was mauled off Gravelines on July 29 — the one real battle of the campaign — and then the wind pinned it northward, away from Parma, away from the rendezvous that was the entire point, and the Duke faced the decision that turns this essay grave for a paragraph, because men are about to die in quantity and this series does not round men. The only way home was up: north around Scotland, out around Ireland, down the open Atlantic — thousands of kilometres of hostile water in ships shot through, short of provisions, their anchors abandoned at Calais. It was September and October, and the autumn gales of 1588 were, by the reckoning of the time, extraordinary. Ship after ship went onto the Irish rocks. Of the roughly 130 that sailed, something like a third to a half never returned — the tallies dispute one another, as tallies of catastrophe do — and of the thirty thousand men, perhaps half died, the enormous majority of them not in battle at all but by wreck, drowning, starvation, and disease on the voyage home. England, for the record, lost not one ship. The Most Fortunate Fleet was defeated seven parts by weather and logistics for every one part by gunfire. The adjective had picked a fight with the North Atlantic, and the North Atlantic, which has never once predicted its own behaviour, won on the audit.

The English understood exactly what had beaten the Armada, and said so in metal. The medal struck to commemorate the victory carried the inscription Flavit Jehovah et dissipati sunt — God blew, and they were scattered. Consider how remarkable that is as an accounting document. The victors, with every incentive to claim the credit, posted the decisive entry to the weather. It is the second time in three essays that this series has caught a sixteenth-century power crediting the correct ledger — Charles V’s globe for Elcano was the first — and I dwell on it because the habit did not survive into the era I usually cover, where the wind itself would by now have been informed that it was merely finishing what the leadership started.

But before the medal cools, I owe you the entry the medal left off, because this series does not close a ledger with the victors’ line alone. England won, and then England did not count her own. In the weeks after the fighting, with the Armada limping north, the English fleet lay in its home ports with typhus and dysentery moving through the crews, and the sailors of the victorious navy began to die — by the accounts of their own commanders, in the thousands, faster at anchor than the Spanish had managed to kill them at sea. The queen’s government, its treasury drained by the mobilization, was slow with the men’s pay; Lord Admiral Howard, to his lasting credit, wrote in protest and spent from his own pocket to feed and discharge dying men, remarking in his correspondence — we handle it with the standing tongs — on the misery of seeing the sailors who had saved the kingdom perish in its streets. So the full accounting of 1588 reads like this: Spain declared its fortune in advance and lost half its men to the sea; England credited the wind in metal and lost thousands of its men to the ledger’s oldest trick, which is that the victory gets a medal and the payroll gets deferred. Two empires, two counting errors, one campaign. The men, as usual, appear in neither name.

Here, then, is the idea at the centre of this essay: certainty is a counting error, and it is the only counting error that is committed before the count. Every other mistake in this series happened during or after the tally — the monk slipped, the log drifted, the epicycles piled up. The Armada’s error was complete before the first sail was bent. To call the fleet Most Fortunate was to book the revenue before the sale, to enter the outcome in the results column while it still lived in the future, where outcomes are stored beyond the reach of any empire’s arithmetic. And I would ask you to notice the asymmetry that makes this error so durable: the fleet really was great. The counting of ships, guns, and men was accurate. You can be entirely right about the inventory and entirely wrong about the result, because the inventory is yours to count and the result is co-authored — by the enemy, by the sea, by the September you cannot see from the spring. The modern superlative operates on the same books. Every venture announced as the biggest, the greatest, the most tremendous ever seen is running the Armada’s accounting: inventory declared as destiny, the adjective dispatched ahead of the fleet, and the weather — there is always weather — not yet consulted.

Dior reviewed the campaign with me on the lakeshore this morning and identified the Spanish error immediately, because it is the one error she has never committed. Dior has chased perhaps four thousand squirrels in her career and caught none, and her record on this is unblemished by prediction: she has never once designated a pursuit Most Fortunate in advance. Each squirrel is approached as an open question, sincerely, at full speed. She is the only empiricist in the household. The fleet sailed with its result in its name; she sails nameless at every squirrel, and so has never lost anything but the squirrel. There is a business book in her method, and she has declined to write it, on the grounds that publishing your method is itself a form of counting in advance.

The count moves on, as it does — and it moves, next, out of this century entirely. The next landmark is essay 1,605, and it is a double: the year one man tried to blow up a parliament and another published a book about a gentleman at war with windmills, and the wrong one of the two is remembered as the madman. The seventeenth century opens with a bang that failed and a novel that didn’t.

The fleet was great. The fortune was pending. The books never confuse the two; only the names do.

See you at 1,605 — remember, remember.
