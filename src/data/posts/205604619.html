On October 1, 1949, Mao Zedong stood on the rostrum above the Gate of Heavenly Peace — the Tiananmen, the same gate where the emperors of the Ming and Qing dynasties had issued edicts — and announced the founding of the People’s Republic of China to a crowd of perhaps 300,000 people assembled in the square below.

“The Chinese people have stood up,” he declared.

The sentence is perhaps the most resonant in modern Chinese political history. It was not primarily a statement about communism, or about the defeat of Chiang Kai-shek, or about the specific political system the new government intended to build. It was a statement about a century of humiliation — the unequal treaties, the foreign concessions, the occupied throne room, the indemnities — and about the fact that the country that had signed those treaties and paid those indemnities and watched those throne rooms occupied was not going to do so anymore.

Mao was a brutal man who presided over policies that killed tens of millions of his own citizens. He was also the leader who ended a century of foreign humiliation and gave China a functioning central government for the first time since the fall of the Qing. Both things are true, and the Chinese Communist Party’s legitimacy rests substantially on the second truth even as it suppresses discussion of the first.

The moment of the proclamation matters for understanding contemporary China in ways that go beyond the historical. Xi Jinping’s entire political project — the Long Return — is built on that moment. The century of humiliation ended in 1949. The restoration of China’s rightful place began. The question Xi is answering, with every Five-Year Plan and every territorial claim and every quantum communication satellite, is: how far has the restoration progressed?

The Civil War that produced the proclamation had ended less than a year before.

Chiang Kai-shek had emerged from the Second World War with American equipment, a large army, and international recognition as the legitimate government of China. Mao had emerged with a political base in northern China and an army hardened by years of guerrilla warfare. The Americans, who had supplied Chiang throughout the war, attempted to broker a coalition government. The negotiations failed because neither side intended to share power.

The Civil War resumed in full in 1946. Its outcome was decided more quickly than most observers expected, for reasons that continue to be studied in military academies worldwide.

The Nationalist army was larger and better equipped. It also had a fundamental political problem: it controlled the cities, and the cities were experiencing hyperinflation so severe that workers were being paid in wheelbarrows of currency. The Nationalist government had financed the war against Japan partly by printing money, and the chickens had come home to roost. Urban populations who had welcomed the Nationalists as liberators in 1945 were alienated by 1947 by an economic chaos that the government seemed unable to address. The rural population, which had spent the war years under Communist administration that had implemented land reform — redistributing land from landlords to peasants — had a concrete reason to support the Communists.

The People’s Liberation Army crossed the Yangtze River in April 1949. Nanjing, the Nationalist capital, fell without significant resistance. Shanghai, the great commercial city, fell in May. Canton fell in October. Chiang Kai-shek evacuated the government, the KMT gold reserves, and a substantial portion of China’s museum collections to Taiwan, where the Republic of China continues to exist as a separate political entity — the unresolved question that the Formosa series of this section addresses in full.

By October 1, the mainland was effectively under Communist control. The proclamation from the Tiananmen rostrum was not a beginning. It was a recognition of something that had already happened.

The first years of the People’s Republic were, by the standards of what followed, relatively successful.

Land reform — the redistribution of land from landlords to peasants — was implemented nationwide between 1949 and 1953. The process was violent. Landlords were subjected to “struggle sessions,” public meetings at which peasants were encouraged to denounce them for past exploitation. Many were executed. The estimates of deaths during the land reform period range from several hundred thousand to two million, and the party has never produced a definitive accounting. But for the hundred million peasant families who received land they had previously worked as tenants or labourers, land reform was the most tangible benefit the revolution delivered.

The Korean War, which began in June 1950 when North Korea invaded the South, pulled China into its first major military conflict as a new state. When United Nations forces — commanded by Douglas MacArthur and dominated by the United States — advanced toward the Yalu River on the Chinese border in October 1950, Mao made the decision to intervene that most of his advisors considered suicidal: he sent the People’s Volunteer Army into Korea against an American force with air superiority, modern artillery, and nuclear weapons.

China did not win the Korean War. It fought the most powerful military force in the world to a standstill along approximately the same line where the war had begun, and that standstill became the ceasefire line that exists today. For a country that had just spent a century being defeated by foreign military forces, the Korean outcome — whatever its human cost, which was enormous — was understood as a demonstration that the new China could fight.

The war cost an estimated 180,000 Chinese dead, possibly more. Among them was Mao’s son, Mao Anying, killed by an American airstrike in November 1950. Mao was told. He lit a cigarette, sat in silence, and went back to work. Whatever the man’s personal qualities, his capacity for will in the face of catastrophic personal loss was not in doubt.

The alliance with the Soviet Union, formalised in the Sino-Soviet Treaty of February 1950, brought Soviet advisors, Soviet industrial equipment, and Soviet institutional models to China. Between 1950 and 1960, the Soviets assisted in the construction of more than 150 major industrial facilities, provided the blueprints for a planned economy, and trained thousands of Chinese engineers, scientists, and military officers. The first Chinese nuclear weapon, detonated in 1964, used a design derived from Soviet assistance provided before the alliance collapsed.

The nuclear test deserves a moment’s attention in the context of this series. China tested its first atomic bomb in October 1964 — fifteen years after the founding of the People’s Republic, in a country that had spent those fifteen years recovering from civil war, fighting in Korea, and enduring the Great Leap famine. The test was conducted at Lop Nur in the Xinjiang desert, using a uranium-235 implosion device. It made China the fifth nuclear-armed state on earth, after the United States, the Soviet Union, Britain, and France. The announcement was accompanied by a statement that China would never be the first to use nuclear weapons and would pursue complete nuclear disarmament — a posture it has nominally maintained, while building an arsenal that has grown significantly under Xi. The test was, above all, a message: the country that had spent a century being carved up by foreign powers with superior weapons now had the most consequential weapon in human history. The Century of Humiliation was not going to happen again.

The alliance collapsed because Mao and Khrushchev could not tolerate each other. Mao regarded Khrushchev’s 1956 denunciation of Stalin as ideologically disastrous — if Stalin could be wrong, the principle of infallible party leadership was in danger, and Mao was not prepared to accept that danger. Khrushchev regarded Mao’s revolutionary adventurism as reckless, particularly after Mao’s statements during the 1957 Moscow conference suggesting that a nuclear war might be survivable for China if it was catastrophic for the capitalist world. The Soviets withdrew their advisors in 1960, taking their blueprints with them, in some cases tearing unfinished machinery from the factory floors.

China completed its industrialisation without them, which is to say: at enormous human cost.

The Great Leap Forward, announced in 1958, was the policy that defines Mao’s legacy in the judgment of any honest accounting.

The intention was to accelerate China’s industrialisation and agricultural collectivisation simultaneously, through a mobilisation of mass human energy that would make up for the country’s lack of capital. Steel production was the symbol: Mao set targets that required every village to build backyard furnaces and melt down whatever metal was available — cooking pots, farm tools, door hinges — to meet the quota. Peasants were organised into communes that were supposed to produce agricultural output far beyond historical precedent. Local officials, under extreme pressure to report success, fabricated statistics. The fabricated statistics flowed upward through the system. Mao, receiving reports of miraculous harvests, ordered grain exported to the Soviet Union to service debts while the countryside that had supposedly produced the harvests was starving.

The famine that followed, from 1959 to 1961, killed somewhere between 15 and 55 million people, depending on the methodology used and the political orientation of the historian applying it. The range reflects both genuine uncertainty and the political sensitivity of the question in China, where the death toll has never been officially acknowledged. The most careful scholarly estimate, by demographer Judith Banister, suggested 30 million excess deaths. Frank Dikötter’s more recent archival research, drawing on provincial records, suggests a figure closer to 45 million.

These are numbers that require a moment to absorb. The Great Leap Forward killed more people than died in the First World War. It killed more people than the Jewish Holocaust. It killed more people than any other famine in recorded history. It killed them not through enemy action or natural disaster but through a government policy based on ideological enthusiasm, falsified statistics, and the institutional impossibility of telling Mao Zedong that his plan was not working.

The inability to tell the emperor his plan was wrong — the same vulnerability that had allowed the Taiping Rebellion to metastasize while the Qing court pretended it wasn’t happening — had returned in Communist form, and it was killing tens of millions of people.

Mao survived the Great Leap Forward politically, though he briefly retreated from the day-to-day management of the economy. Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping implemented the pragmatic policies that ended the famine: allowing peasants to farm small private plots, reducing commune sizes, importing grain. The crisis eased by 1962.

Mao spent the early 1960s watching the pragmatists repair the damage his policy had caused and concluded, apparently without irony, that they were becoming dangerously revisionist. The Cultural Revolution — which will receive its own extended treatment in the next part of this series — was his response: a second mobilisation of revolutionary mass energy, this time directed not at industrial production but at the enemies within the party who were, in Mao’s analysis, restoring capitalism under socialist slogans.

The targets of the Cultural Revolution included virtually every person who had the technical education, professional expertise, or institutional knowledge to run a modern society. Teachers, doctors, engineers, scientists, and party officials were sent to the countryside for “re-education” — physical labour, self-criticism sessions, and the abandonment of any skill or knowledge that could be characterised as bourgeois. Universities closed for four years. The educational and professional infrastructure that China would need to modernise was systematically dismantled in the name of preventing the revolution from being betrayed by people who knew too much.

Deng Xiaoping, the pragmatist who had helped end the famine, was purged twice during the Cultural Revolution. He spent years in internal exile, doing factory work, before being rehabilitated and purged again. His political survival, and eventual triumph, is the subject of Part VII of this series.

What does the Mao period, honestly assessed, mean for the Long Return?

It means that the restoration Xi Jinping is pursuing had to begin from a country that Mao left profoundly damaged. The institutional knowledge destroyed by the Cultural Revolution had to be rebuilt. The economic distortions of twenty-five years of planned economy had to be unwound. The international isolation that Mao’s revolutionary foreign policy had produced had to be reversed. The generation that had been sent to the countryside instead of to university had to be replaced.

It also means that the legitimacy of the Long Return rests on a foundation that cannot be fully examined. The party that is restoring China’s greatness is also the party that ran the Great Leap Forward. The system that built the quantum networks is the same system that prevented anyone from telling Mao his plan was failing while millions starved. The institutional incapacity to speak truth to the emperor — the incapacity that the Empress Dowager’s court had demonstrated in 1900 and Mao’s court demonstrated in 1959 — has not been structurally resolved. Xi Jinping’s consolidation of power, his elimination of rivals and flattening of the institutional checks within the party, represents its recurrence in a new form.

The friend in Shenzhen who stopped replying to my political essays understood this. He did not need to articulate it. He simply went quiet.
