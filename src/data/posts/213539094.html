Stephen Gordon September 4, 2026

Nine hours before this essay went to draft, a listener could have opened a podcast feed and heard something that sounded, for a moment, like satire. Two women’s voices, clipped and urgent, telling a phone caller to reach a senator named Chris and demand he “stop healthy millionaire,” and to call a man named Tom and tell him to “lower costs for Michiganders.” The audio was rough. The grammar was rougher. Neither ad identified who had paid to put those words in front of voters, and neither was required to, because neither ad was, in the eyes of the law, a political advertisement at all. They were issue ads. They were about healthcare costs and grocery prices. They just happened to name a sitting senator, run in the weeks before an election, and vanish the moment the polls closed.

This is the maze. We have spent eleven essays walking its corridors — from the Tillman Act’s timid first prohibition, through Sidney Hillman’s invention of the political action committee, through the soft money arms race and Citizens United and the slow strangulation of the Federal Election Commission. Every part of that history was, in its way, a story about a door closing and a window opening somewhere else. This addendum exists because a window opened again, and the New York Times confirmed August 31, in an investigation by Shane Goldmacher and Theodore Schleifer, what the maze’s architects have known for some time: the money did not slow down. It found a new corridor, and it is running through nearly every contested race in the country at once.

The Times reviewed advertising data, campaign-finance filings, and tax records and arrived at a number that should, by now, feel familiar in its shape if not its size. About a billion dollars in anonymous money is moving through the 2026 midterm landscape — and the Times was careful to call this a low-end estimate, since its own tally counts only what is publicly traceable: television and digital ad buys, and donations to super PACs from nonprofits. It cannot count the direct mail, the paid influencer content, or the door-knocking operations that dark money groups also fund, because those activities leave no comparable paper trail at all. The Brennan Center’s own tally of the last full cycle, 2024, put dark money at $1.9 billion — nearly double the previous record, itself a jump from the cycle before that. Since Citizens United opened the gate in 2010, these groups have spent at least $4.3 billion trying to shape who governs the country. Nick Penniman, the head of the reform group Issue One, put the scale of the current moment in a phrase that will likely outlive this essay: it is, he told the Times, “enough money to manipulate a lot of politicians and to put a lot of poison in the blood of the American political system.”

The Times’ investigation — echoing ground we covered in Part IX — identified three doors into the maze, and it is worth walking through them again with sharper numbers, because the mechanics have not changed even as the amounts have grown. The first is the simplest: give money to a nonprofit that has no obligation to name you. A 501(c)(4) can spend on politics so long as politics is not, by an unenforced and unenforceable standard, its “primary purpose.” The Times identified the four biggest dealers in this door — nonprofits allied respectively with House Republicans, Senate Republicans, House Democrats, and Senate Democrats, each sharing office space and staff with a sibling super PAC, essentially offering donors a one-stop shop for concealment. Together those four groups account for more than $460 million in hidden-source spending this cycle, and their direct ad spending alone has grown from $166 million in the 2022 midterms to more than $242 million this time around — a fifty percent jump in a single cycle.

The second door routes that same anonymous money into a super PAC, which must disclose its donors — except that its donors are, by design, other dark money groups, so the disclosure discloses nothing. There is no meaningful cap on the size of these internal transfers. Some of the country’s highest-profile super PACs, the Times found, are now funded entirely by nonprofits that answer to no one.

The third door is the one that has grown the most interesting since we last wrote about it: the pop-up super PAC, formed weeks before an election, spent down entirely, and left to disclose its donors only after the votes are already counted. The mechanism exploits a specific, almost administrative gap in the law — the final pre-election disclosure deadline falls three weeks before voting, meaning any group formed after that date can spend freely and secretly through Election Day, with a disclosure obligation that arrives too late to matter. We noted this tactic in Part IX as a structural possibility. This cycle it has become something closer to an industry, and the case studies are, even by the standards of this series, remarkable.

Consider the pop-up super PAC called Lead Left. Through the spring of 2026 it spent more than $3.1 million supporting a slate of unambiguously progressive candidates in two Democratic primaries — the kind of spending that looked, on its face, like an act of solidarity from the party’s left flank. On June 20, 2026, weeks after those primaries had already been decided, Lead Left filed the disclosure it had been permitted to delay, and the money trail led not to a progressive donor but to a Republican-aligned group called Conservative Americans PAC. The Times traced that money one layer further back than we could when this addendum was first drafted: Conservative Americans PAC was itself the recipient of $37 million from a nonprofit called the American Prosperity Alliance, formed by Republican operatives and donors who had spent 2022 watching Democrats meddle in G.O.P. primaries and decided, this cycle, to return the favor. The Alliance spawned at least five pop-up committees under names built to sound like they belonged to the other side — Lead Left PAC and Progressive Champions PAC among them — using limited liability corporations and post-office boxes to further obscure who was actually behind the ads. Two people briefed on the group’s finances told the Times, on condition of anonymity, that its funding leaned heavily on corporate interests, including the pharmaceutical industry.

The Alliance’s network did not confine itself to symbolic mischief. In Maine’s Second District, Republican operatives used the network to spend nearly $500,000 defeating the Democratic candidate they considered strongest — a state senator named Joe Baldacci — in a race for the seat being vacated by the retiring Representative Jared Golden. The vehicle they used, a pop-up called Real Change PAC that disclosed no donors until after the election, ran an ad accusing Baldacci of being “dark money’s choice.” The accusation was, in the narrowest technical sense, projection: the ad calling Baldacci a creature of dark money was itself paid for entirely with dark money. Baldacci lost by several hundred votes. “It’s the ultimate in deception,” he told the Times afterward. In Texas, the same broader network tried, and failed, to elevate a Democratic Senate candidate whom the candidate’s own party had publicly denounced as antisemitic — an attempt to hand Republicans the weakest possible general-election opponent, in the same spirit as the maneuvering against Baldacci.

Not every use of the tactic runs in that direction, and not every use of dark money buys the outcome it is purchasing. Three races illustrate both halves of that sentence at once. In Michigan, Representative Haley Stevens received roughly $62 million in support from six groups — a mix of dark-money nonprofits, heavily dark-money-funded super PACs, and pop-ups — running against Dr. Abdul El-Sayed in the Democratic Senate primary. Thirty million of the money working against El-Sayed came from the American Israel Public Affairs Committee’s political operation alone. El-Sayed won anyway, and delivered a line on election night that belongs in the same file as Penniman’s and Weiner’s: “The power of our many is greater than the power of their money.” In Minnesota, Representative Angie Craig’s Senate primary bid was backed by a super PAC almost entirely funded by a nonprofit that had not existed before December of the prior year — freshly minted money for a freshly announced candidacy. She lost. In Texas, Senator John Cornyn lost his primary despite roughly $43 million in dark money spent on his behalf, including expenditures by a group led by former Governor Rick Perry. And in Kansas, an obscure Democratic Senate candidate received a wave of secret money running at roughly one hundred times what he had on hand in his own campaign account in the closing stretch — a ratio that makes the phrase “his own campaign” almost decorative.

No single race captures the system’s full anatomy as completely as Maine’s Senate contest, where Susan Collins is seeking re-election in a race that could decide control of the chamber. Nearly $75 million in dark money — from both sides at once — has been spent in a state with fewer than 900,000 registered voters, which works out to more than eighty dollars in secret spending for every voter in Maine. The spending is not confined to television. A Republican-aligned nonprofit called One Nation has sent Collins voters at least ten separate mailers, thanking her, among other things, for her work on behalf of veterans, firefighters, and cancer research.

But the single detail that does more to illustrate the “who benefits” question than any dollar figure in this essay belongs on its own. Collins chairs the Senate Appropriations Committee, which directs federal spending — placing her in an unusually small circle of lawmakers whose goodwill special interests have particular reason to cultivate. A nonprofit called Stronger America Inc., which has spent more than $11 million on ads supporting Collins and no one else, hosted a dinner for her in July at a Washington townhouse owned by the technology company Oracle. Voters were never told the money existed. Collins, evidently, knew exactly whose table she was sitting at. Oracle, whose building regularly hosts such dinners, did not respond to the Times’ request for comment, and neither did Collins’s campaign.

Enforcement, or the visible lack of it, is where this series has always ended up, and the specifics have hardened since Part VII into something worse than the “structural deadlock” we described then.

The Federal Election Commission currently has two of its six seats filled. The Commission lost its policymaking quorum on May 1, 2025, when Commissioner Allen Dickerson’s term expired and was not renewed, and lost even its bipartisan minimum function on October 3, 2025, when Commissioner James Trainor resigned. President Trump sent two nominations to the Senate on February 11, 2026, to refill the empty seats; as of this writing they remain unconfirmed. A two-member Commission cannot open new investigations, cannot approve settlements, and cannot issue the advisory opinions that let political operatives know, in advance, whether a maneuver is legal. It can publish educational pamphlets. It cannot enforce the law those pamphlets describe. Dan Weiner offered the plainest possible summary to the Times: “The F.E.C. has never been a cop on the beat. But at the moment they can’t even enforce routine traffic violations.” Sean Cooksey, the Commission’s former chairman and a former counsel to Vice President Vance, offered the case for the defense — that anonymous political speech has a lineage running back to the Federalist Papers, and that “there is important political value in allowing people to associate and speak anonymously, especially when they have unpopular opinions.” It is a real argument, made by people who are not cynics, and it deserves to be recorded here rather than waved away — though it is worth noting that the Federalist Papers were published under pseudonyms whose authors were, eventually and by design, revealed, which is precisely the step that never arrives in the modern version.

The IRS, the other nominal watchdog in this arrangement, did not respond to the Times’ request for comment, and the Commission itself declined to comment as well — which is, in its own small way, as telling a data point as any dollar figure in this essay.

The agency is not merely underfunded; it has been redirected. It lost close to a third of its workforce during the broader downsizing of the federal government, and the administration has moved to install political allies within its criminal-investigative division while weakening the role IRS lawyers play in those investigations, with the new stated priority being domestic terrorism and “illicit finance,” not the disclosure violations of political nonprofits. Even where lawyers are inclined to comply, the incentives point toward delay rather than transparency: the Times reports that nonprofit lawyers are routinely advising clients not to file comprehensive disclosures of this cycle’s activity until mid-November 2027 — more than a full year after the midterm elections have been decided, and comfortably past the point where the information could change a single vote.

There is one more thread worth pulling before this addendum closes, because it belongs to the future of this series as much as its past: the maze has a new occupant. The AI industry — barely a presence in our earlier accounting of the super PAC ecosystem — has become one of its most aggressive players. A super PAC called Leading the Future, funded chiefly by OpenAI’s Greg Brockman alongside venture financiers Joe Lonsdale and Andreessen Horowitz, went three-for-three in Republican primaries in Texas and North Carolina this spring, and has committed $125 million to establishing a federal regulatory framework favorable to its backers. A rival vehicle, Public First, funded with $20 million from Anthropic, spent competitively in at least one of the same races. Readers of the Asimov Gap will recognize the shape of this immediately: an industry that spent its first years insisting it needed no regulation at all now spending nine figures to write the regulation it prefers, through the same anonymous architecture that dark money nonprofits, oil companies, and pharmaceutical interests built before it. The maze does not discriminate by sector. It only asks whether you have enough money to enter, and whether you would prefer not to be seen while you are inside. And it is not only the AI industry lending the maze new tenants: the Times’ analysis found the Koch family network responsible for more than $50 million in undisclosed spending on the right, a trio of environmental nonprofits responsible for a comparable sum on the left, and Mr. Trump’s own allied nonprofit, Securing American Greatness, which has spent at least $44 million since early last year, sending nearly $25 million of it to super PACs and airing roughly $19 million in ads boosting the President and congressional Republicans directly.

What Michigan, Minnesota, Texas, and Kansas add up to, taken together, is not proof that dark money doesn’t work — the Baldacci and Collins cases argue exactly the opposite — but proof that it doesn’t always work, which is a more interesting and more useful fact than either the cynic’s or the reformer’s version of this story usually allows. It echoes the argument we made in Part X, when this series first proposed that reform is a choice rather than an impossibility. If voters are, in fact, growing more resistant to advertising once they learn who paid for it, then dark money’s greatest asset is not persuasion. It is timing. It works because the unmasking happens after the votes are counted, not before — sometimes fourteen months after, per the lawyers advising clients on when to file. Close that gap, even partially, even through nothing grander than faster disclosure deadlines, and the maze’s most effective weapon becomes a liability instead. Representative Chris DeLuzio of Pennsylvania, who has sponsored legislation to close the pop-up loophole specifically, put the underlying suspicion plainly to the Times: “If you are trying to hide who your donors are, you are probably trying to hide some nasty stuff.”

Nobody has closed that gap. The FEC lacks the votes to try. The IRS has been pointed elsewhere. The nonprofit, the super PAC, and the pop-up PAC keep doing precisely what Part IX said they were built to do, and the dots keep disappearing, and the game goes on. Part X ended with a note about what reform could look like, and it is worth recording, for the sake of the record this series is quietly building, that the attempt has not been abandoned even as the FEC sits dark. A version of the DISCLOSE Act was reintroduced in 2026, carrying the same basic architecture it has carried since it first failed to pass in 2010: require any organization spending more than $10,000 on federal elections to promptly disclose its donors, close the foreign-money loopholes that Citizens United left ajar, and put a floor under the maze that the Court built. It has not passed. It is not likely to pass in this Congress. But its repeated reintroduction, cycle after failed cycle, is itself a data point — proof that the argument for transparency has not gone away just because the institutions meant to enforce it have.

We will be back before this cycle ends. There is, I suspect, more maze to walk — and, if Michigan and Maine are any guide, more than one way for voters to walk out of it.

Super PACman is an ongoing history of money in American democracy. The series began with the original sin and asks the question that every generation of American reformers has faced and none has finally answered: does the promise mean what it says?
