by Stephen Gordon

I count my essays. The number climbs, it lands on a year that already means something, and I stop and tell you what.

This is essay 1,492, and you already know the year. Everyone knows the year. It is possibly the single most famous date in the Western calendar, drilled into schoolchildren on two continents in a rhyme so sticky it has outlived most of what it was meant to teach: In fourteen hundred and ninety-two, Columbus sailed the ocean blue. You knew that before you knew almost anything. It may be the first date you ever memorized.

And that is exactly the problem with 1492. It is the most famous year in the calendar and one of the least understood, because the rhyme did its job too well — it planted one event so firmly that the other two things that happened that same year, in that same country, at the hands of the same two monarchs, fell out of the popular memory entirely. We remember the ship. We forgot the harbour it sailed from, and who was crowding it, and why.

This whole window of essays has been sailing toward this number. Let me tell you what actually happened in 1492 — all of it — and then let me tell you the detail I found while writing this, the one that stopped me cold, because it is the kind of thing this series exists to notice, and I had never once heard it in my life.

Three things happened in Spain in 1492. They were not coincidences. They were the same project, wearing three faces.

The first was the fall of Granada. On the second of January, 1492, the last Muslim ruler of the emirate of Granada handed the keys of the city to Ferdinand and Isabella, and the Reconquista — the nearly eight-hundred-year Christian campaign to take back the Iberian peninsula from Muslim rule — was complete. Spain was, for the first time, a single unified Christian kingdom. This was the triumph. This was the thing the monarchs had spent their reigns pursuing, and they had it at last: one country, one crown, one faith.

The second thing followed directly from the first, because “one faith” is a phrase with a body count. On the thirty-first of March, 1492 — twelve weeks after Granada fell — Ferdinand and Isabella signed the Alhambra Decree, ordering every Jew in Spain to convert to Christianity or leave the country by the end of July. Not to leave with their wealth: they were forbidden to take gold or silver. Just to leave, or to stop being who they were. A community that had lived in Spain for more than a thousand years — that had, in better centuries, helped make Spain the most learned kingdom in Europe — was given four months to abandon its faith or abandon its home. Somewhere between one and two hundred thousand people chose exile. Many died on the roads and the boats trying to reach anywhere that would have them. Some, according to the accounts, were murdered by brigands who believed the refugees had swallowed their gold to smuggle it out.

And the third thing. On the third of August, 1492 — three days after the expulsion deadline — a Genoese sailor named Cristoforo Colombo, sailing under the Spanish crown, cast off from the port of Palos with three ships and a theory that the Indies could be reached by sailing west. He was wrong about the theory, spectacularly wrong, wrong about the size of the earth and what lay on the far side of it, and his wrongness carried him into the history books because he blundered into two entire continents that Europe did not know were there. He never accepted what he’d found. He died insisting he had reached Asia. But the rhyme is right about the date: in 1492, he sailed.

One year. A triumph, a cruelty, and a discovery — signed by the same two monarchs, funded from the same treasury, driven by the same conviction that the world could be sorted into those who belonged and those who did not.

Here is the detail. Here is the thing I did not know until I sat down to write essay 1,492, and I have read a fair amount of history, and it undid me a little.

Columbus kept a diary. It survives, at least in summary, and it opens — the very first entry, the frame for the whole voyage that would remake the world — with Columbus himself noting that in the same month the monarchs gave him his orders to sail west, they issued the edict expelling the Jews from Spain. He writes it down himself. He knew. The two events were not separated in his mind by centuries of textbook editing the way they are in ours. They were the same month. He set them side by side on the first page, the expulsion and the expedition, because to him they were simply two things the Crown was doing that season.

And then this, which I have not been able to shake since I read it: by several accounts, Columbus’s departure was delayed — the harbour at Palos, and the nearby ports, were clogged with ships. Jewish ships. The vessels carrying the expelled, the hundred thousand and more driven out by the July deadline, crowding the same waters Columbus needed to leave from. He is said to have had to wait, and push his sailing back a day, because the sea lanes out of Spain were full of the people Spain was throwing away.

Sit with that image, because I cannot stop sitting with it. The most famous departure in the history of the Western world — the voyage that opens the modern age, that every schoolchild memorizes — had to wait its turn behind a fleet of refugees. The discovery could not leave the harbour until the cruelty had finished sailing out of it. They were, quite literally, in the same water on the same tide. And history remembered the one ship and forgot the hundred, remembered the man sailing toward a new world and forgot the thousands sailing away from the only home they had, on the same current, in the same days, expelled by the same signatures that funded him.

I said, back at 1485, that I would speak of Spain’s terrible year more soberly when the count reached it, and here I am, and here it is, and I find I do not want to be glib about any of it — not even about Columbus, who is usually the easy target.

Because the standard modern move is to knock Columbus off his pedestal, and there is plenty to knock: he was a poor governor, a brutal one by the accounts of his own contemporaries, and the world he opened became, for the people already living in it, a catastrophe on a scale that dwarfs even the Alhambra Decree. All of that is true and none of it is my subject today. My subject is smaller and stranger: it is the way a single year can hold a triumph and a discovery and an atrocity all at once, funded from one purse, and then hand all of its fame to just one of the three, and let the other two sink out of memory like coins into dark water.

We built a nursery rhyme around 1492. We did not build one around the Alhambra Decree. And the reason, I think, is not conspiracy but something more ordinary and more troubling: the discovery flattered us, and the expulsion did not. One of these stories is about setting out, boldly, toward a new world. The other is about a Christian kingdom throwing out a hundred thousand of its own people for praying wrong. A civilization chooses which of its numbers to memorize, and it tends to choose the ones it can be proud of, and 1492 is the proof — the same year, the same country, the same monarchs, and only one of the three events made it into the rhyme.

So this is essay 1,492, the one everyone thinks they know, and the one this whole window has been sailing toward since a fallen city closed the road east at 1453 and sent Europe looking, desperately, for a way around.

They found the way around. It ran west, and it left from a harbour crowded with the ships of the expelled, and it opened a new world by the same signatures that had just closed an old one. Granada fell, and Spain was whole. The Jews were cast out, and Spain was, by its own lights, purified. And Columbus sailed, three days late, past the last boats of the people his own patrons had exiled, toward a continent he would never understand he had reached.

Three things, one year, one number. The rhyme remembers a third of it. This essay is for the rest.

I will see you at the next one. It won’t be as famous. Almost nothing is. But I have learned, doing this, that the famous numbers are often the ones we understand the least — that a date can be memorized by millions and still be mostly forgotten, if we only ever learned the part that made us proud.

Fourteen hundred and ninety-two. He sailed the ocean blue. And the harbour he sailed from was full of people being sent to die, and nobody wrote a rhyme about them, and that — the choosing, the remembering, the forgetting — is the whole reason I count.
