Part XXV reported the most systematic count of Trump’s speech yet attempted: a New York Times audit of a decade of rallies, which found his profanity and absolutism still rising and his rambling steady since 2024. It ended with the test Trump set for himself, the weave that does not come back. The audit was published two days before the country voted. On election night the counting resumed, live.

Most nights produce one or two data points. The hours on either side of the polls closing on 3 November 2026, when Republicans lost the House, the Senate and a list of governorships, produced a ledger. What follows is that ledger, in roughly the order it was recorded, with the usual caveat. A bad night is not a diagnosis. An 80-year-old man watching his party collapse on live television is entitled to be angry. The question this series asks is not whether he was upset. It is whether the shape of his reaction matches the patterns already logged, and whether he seemed aware of what he was saying.

The closing argument: perseveration

The last full day of the campaign set the tone. On 2 November Trump gave the final address of the cycle beside the Reflecting Pool on the National Mall, the renovation project that Part XX of this series examined. That part logged his claim that the pool’s failed lining was the work of vandals, a story his own officials could not support.

He told the story again. Alexandra Petri, writing in The Atlantic, timed it: twenty minutes into the speech, he had still not moved on from the pipes. He held up prepared slides. He mused that “like the human being, grass has a life also.” He repeated the account of vandals with box cutters and knives. There were no such vandals.

In clinical shorthand this is perseveration: returning to the same subject, often the same phrases, after the moment for it has passed. The Reflecting Pool keeps returning to this series because it keeps returning to him. The occasion this time was the most important speech of a midterm campaign that he had personally asked voters to treat as a referendum on himself.

Michael Scherer and Ashley Parker, in The Atlantic, catalogued the wider rally material of the final weeks: stories that ran for hours about rounded posteriors (the Husted loop that Part XXV logged), strong hands, “Dumocrats,” transgender swim meets, the Vietnam War and the blue paint he had reapplied to the pool. A Republican consultant told them that by then the White House was “not even trying.”

Election morning: confabulation

On the morning of 3 November Trump called in to Fox & Friends. “Inflation is solved,” he said, as The New York Times reported, although consumer prices had risen 3.7 per cent over the year to September. He described the previous night’s strike on Iran’s Qeshm Island as “a minor attack… Not a big war,” and added that when you talk about war, “this isn’t really war.”

This series has used the term confabulation carefully. It does not mean lying, which involves knowing the truth. It means producing confident statements that do not match the record, apparently without registering the mismatch. A president can spin inflation figures; all of them do. Declaring a problem “solved” on the morning voters are going to the polls angry about it is a different thing. So is describing an eight-month war, which has driven up the price of everything his voters buy, as not really a war.

Early evening: displacement onto allies

As voting drew to a close, Trump watched Fox News, and turned on it.

According to Michael M. Grynbaum in The New York Times, he posted three attacks on the network within 95 minutes. He called its polling “the worst” and claimed its ratings were down “because they are AGAINST me!” He called Jessica Tarlov, the liberal co-host of “The Five,” “DUMB AS A ROCK.” He texted her co-host Greg Gutfeld with instructions on how to rebut her, and Gutfeld read the text on air. Shown the message, Tarlov suggested he “just press stop,” as if it were spam. At 7 p.m. Trump posted a 184-word denunciation of Fox for covering “Trump Deranged Dumocrats.”

Part XXV added displacement onto allies to this series’ categories, on the strength of Norway and a pattern Tom Nichols identified in The Atlantic in September: when frustrated, Trump lashes out harder at friends than at enemies. Fox News is the friendliest major network in America and the place where his political career began. On the night of the worst defeat of his presidency, it was his first target.

The text to the studio: disinhibition

The Gutfeld episode belongs in a second column as well. A president texting a cable host during a live broadcast, with debating instructions, on the afternoon of election day, while Americans are still voting, is a small thing. It is also exactly the kind of small thing this series logs as disinhibition: the loss of the filter between impulse and action. In the same category belong the rally aside, recorded by Scherer and Parker, that Republicans who did not vote would “go to hell,” and the closing-week remarks about the physique of an Ohio senator.

Mid-evening: the two positions

Then came the post that defines the night. As the losses mounted, Trump wrote on Truth Social that he had not been on the ballot, and that if he had been, Republicans “would have won IN A LANDSLIDE!”

For two months the central message of his campaign had been the opposite: pretend I am on the ballot. Jonathan Lemire noted in The Atlantic that the post contradicted the core of his own campaign. That alone would be unremarkable. Politicians reposition after losses all the time.

What makes it a data point is that the two positions were held simultaneously and without apparent friction. He was on the ballot for the purposes of turnout. He was off the ballot for the purposes of blame. This series has seen the pattern before, in the “smart president, dumb president” line logged in Part XXI. A healthy political operator moves from one position to another. Trump appeared to hold both at once.

Not everyone in his party accepted the off-ballot version. Peter Meijer, the Michigan Republican who voted to impeach Trump after 6 January and lost his next primary for it, told McKay Coppins of The Atlantic that his condolence calls outnumbered his congratulations about ten to one. Asked about the claim that Republicans lost only because Trump was not on the ballot, he lost patience. “This was an anti-Republican wave,” he said, “and he’s the leader of the Republican Party.”

Late evening: the counterfactual

Shortly before 10:30 p.m., Trump gave The New York Times a phone interview while watching the results. He said he was “absolutely willing to accept those results” and would work with Democrats. He said he had no regrets about his endorsements. Then, looking at South Carolina, where his chosen candidate Darline Graham had just lost the seat of her late brother, he said: “I see that Darline lost. Wow, that’s amazing. How do you think Lindsey would have done if he ran?”

It was not the first time he had asked that kind of question. Even before the polls closed, according to Shane Goldmacher, Jonathan Swan and Maggie Haberman of The New York Times, Trump had asked someone privately: “If we never went into Iran, what do you think would happen in the midterms?” During the campaign he had told aides that the party’s troubles reflected well on him: “It says something very nice about me that they just can’t win without me.”

Lindsey Graham died in July. Part XIX logged Trump’s ad-libbed remarks at the funeral. On election night the question was not grief. It was substitution: the defeat reframed as a hypothetical win by someone else, and posed to a reporter as a genuine question.

Near the end: flattening

The last entry is the hardest to categorise. Before the night was out, Trump told Fox’s Bret Baier: “It’s just a point in time, Bret. Just a point in time. It’s the way it is. There was a mood in the air, I guess.”

Set against the rest of the evening, the tone is the most striking thing. Six hours earlier he was attacking a Fox co-host in capital letters. By the end he sounded almost detached, the repeated phrase and the trailing “I guess” doing most of the work. This series does not read too much into a single remark at the end of a long night. It records the swing.

The morning after: the beam

The ledger was supposed to close at midnight. Trump added to it the next day.

His first public appearance after the election, at midday on 4 November, was a half-hour tour of the half-built White House ballroom. According to The New York Times reporters Shawn McCreesh, Erica L. Green and Chris Cameron, who covered it live, he did not mention the midterms. He talked about “upgrading trash island,” a golf course on the Potomac. He talked about the marble: “The owner of the quarry didn’t want to sell it, but he liked me.” He talked about the floor plan: “If I put the kitchen on top, you would lose three walls.” After more than ten minutes, Fox News cut away. No cable network was carrying him.

When reporters finally asked about the election, he said his Reflecting Pool speech had not been an election speech at all but a “ribbon-cutting” of sorts. Asked about Vice President JD Vance’s post that morning blaming the war in Iran for the defeat, he said: “Well I think it’s largely true.” He also said: “As far as that war is concerned, I would have done nothing different.” Then he stopped taking questions. “Watch the beam go up,” he said, and walked away. Pointing at the ceiling, he told no one in particular: “Now envision chandeliers.”

Asked whether he was worried about impeachment, he said: “No, not even a little bit.” Of the renovations: “For me, it’s relaxing.” Of the war: “If I had to do it again, I’d have to do it again.” At 6:33 that evening he posted again, not about the election but about the clocks. “It’s so dark outside,” he wrote, complaining that the Senate had failed to make daylight saving time permanent and that the sun had set in Washington at 5:06 p.m. He had not wanted to raise it before the election, he explained, “because I didn’t want to have certain Senators lose their Race, in a landslide.”

The morning after the worst night of his presidency, the subject he returned to was the same one he had closed his campaign with: a construction project. And he agreed that the war had cost his party the election while insisting he would fight it again. This series has a column for each.

The ledger

What he would not say

Five days on, Peter Baker of The New York Times, who has covered five presidents whose parties lost midterms, wrote that Trump has sounded “like a man in denial.” The word needs care, because on the narrow facts he has denied very little. He told the same newspaper that he was “absolutely willing to accept those results.” He called Vance’s verdict on the war “largely true.” From the man who spent four years refusing to concede 2020, those are not small sentences.

What he has refused is the meaning of the result. George W. Bush called 2006 a “thumping.” Barack Obama called 2010 a “shellacking.” Bill Clinton, after 1994, was reduced to arguing that the Constitution gave him relevance. Baker notes that Trump has offered no humility and little recognition of public discontent. His own vocabulary for the worst night of his presidency was “a point in time” and “a mood in the air.” The nearest thing to an acknowledgement came from the White House, which posted online: “TRUMP IS STILL YOUR PRESIDENT.”

The days since have followed the same line. He signed a steel beam for the ballroom and flew to Florida to talk about the space programme, and Baker, watching independently of this ledger, found him flat and perhaps a little dazed. Then the tone reversed. On Friday he named Stephen Miller as chief of staff, and on Saturday he offered Dana White, the head of the Ultimate Fighting Championship, the job of press secretary.

There are two ways to read this, and they lead to different places. A man who understands what happened and refuses to give anyone the satisfaction of hearing it is pursuing a strategy, however unattractive. A man for whom the information does not fully land belongs in this series. The record so far supports neither cleanly. Before the polls had closed he was privately asking what would have happened had he never gone into Iran, so he can see the cause. Asked the next day, he said he would have done nothing different. He can apparently identify what cost him the election and cannot carry that knowledge one step further. Whether that is stubbornness or something else is the question a committee chair will soon be able to ask.

A week later the same question came up about something else entirely. Asked on 9 November whether he had known, when he flew to China in May, that Nvidia’s Jensen Huang was in a relationship with a suspected Chinese spy, Trump said: “No, I didn’t hear about it then.” The New York Times promptly reported that Marco Rubio had briefed him aboard Air Force One on the way, just before Huang boarded, and that he may have been told the year before. David A. Graham, writing in The Atlantic, read it as indifference: a president who ignores any intelligence that contradicts his instincts. This series asks the narrower question it asked about the election. Either he remembered the briefing and chose to deny it, which is politics, or it never fully registered, which is the pattern above. Of the affair itself, he offered that Huang “probably has an excuse.”

What the night adds

None of these behaviours is new to this series. What is new is their density. Seven entries in little more than a day, across five categories, is the most this series has recorded in so short a span. Three more followed the next day.

And the stakes have changed. From January the House and Senate will be controlled by a party that can hold hearings and issue subpoenas. The neuropsychological evaluation that Representative Jamie Raskin asked for, logged in Part XIX and never answered, can now be requested by a committee chair rather than a minority member. The questions this series has been asking from outside will soon be asked by people with the power to compel an answer.

The baseline keeps moving. On 3 November it moved in public, on live television, on the one night every camera in the country was pointed at the result. He told the country to pretend he was on the ballot. They did. What they saw afterwards is now part of the record.
